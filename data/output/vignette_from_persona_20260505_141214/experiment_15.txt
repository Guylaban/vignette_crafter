fast food restaurant, where the work was fast, noisy, and tightly scheduled. Before the trauma, he
had been known by supervisors as steady and efficient, someone who kept his head down and did what

happened to him physically, but as something he saw unfold in front of him, with the kind of
abruptness that left him feeling powerless and stunned. The hour was fixed in his mind, and even now
the same time of day could pull him back into it with a jolt of unease, as if the air itself had
turned heavy again. When that hour came around, he often noticed himself looking up, pausing
whatever he was doing, and becoming aware of how closely the present seemed to resemble the past.

In the weeks and months that followed, Karim found that loud noises set him on edge in a way that
startled him because it seemed to happen before he had time to think. A tray crashing in the
kitchen, a fryer lid slamming down, a customer’s sharp laugh near the counter, or a sudden shout
from across the dining area could leave him tense and scanning the room. He became especially
watchful for unexpected sounds, as though he had to catch them before they caught him. At work,
where blenders whirred, timers beeped, and metal pans clattered throughout the day, he often felt
his body bracing for something he could not name. The restaurant’s pace also made delays hard for
him to tolerate. If a drive-thru order took longer than expected or a delivery was late, he would
assume that something bad was happening somewhere out of sight. He knew the thought was extreme, yet
it came to him immediately and with conviction, and he would find himself watching the clock,
listening harder, and growing more distressed as the minutes passed.

Karim also came to feel that safety existed only in a very limited number of places. He described
this in practical terms: a small corner of his apartment, a particular seat in the staff break area,
one section of the bus route he used to get to work. Outside of those few places, he felt exposed
and on guard, as if he had to be ready for something to go wrong. This narrowed his life in quiet
but significant ways. He stopped lingering after shifts, declined invitations from coworkers, and
preferred to go straight home rather than make any unnecessary stops. Even in familiar environments,
he remained alert to where he could stand, where he could sit, and how quickly he could leave. Over
time, the world seemed less like a place he moved through and more like a series of locations he had
to assess for danger.

Being criticized or corrected became another potent trigger for him. In the restaurant, a manager’s
brief comment about a missed step in an order, or a coworker telling him he had done something the
wrong way, could hit him with disproportionate force. He would go quiet, feel his face heat, and
become acutely aware of the need to control his response. Karim said that criticism now carried a
sharp edge, not because he disliked feedback, but because it could suddenly make him feel singled
out and vulnerable in a way that was hard to shake. He tried to hide how unsettled he became, though
the effort itself left him exhausted. He had come to believe that he needed to keep himself
contained and careful, since even small remarks could leave him feeling as though he were no longer
on solid ground.

By the time he sought help, Karim was still working, but the strain of living this way had become
increasingly hard to manage. He was tired of measuring every place by how safe it felt, tired of
bracing at unexpected sounds, and tired of spending so much energy trying to get through ordinary
workdays without being thrown off by criticism, delays, or the hour of the day that had become
loaded with memory. What brought him in was the sense that his world had grown too narrow, too
vigilant, and too governed by the expectation that something bad might be about to happen. He said
he wanted to feel less trapped by his own reactions, though what he feared most was that he would
never again feel fully at ease anywhere outside those few places he had learned to trust.
