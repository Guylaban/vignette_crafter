Arun was 31, a South Eastern Asian American judge who had built his adult life around order,
composure, and long hours spent in a quiet courthouse where people expected him to stay steady no
matter what was said in front of him. He had been married for four years, and until recently his
days had followed a predictable rhythm: early mornings at home, the commute to the courthouse,
hearings, paperwork, and the careful discipline of leaving work pressure behind when he came home.
That rhythm was shattered during a natural disaster that struck his community with sudden force. He
had been at home when the weather turned severe, and what he remembered most was the frightening
sequence of moments rather than a full narrative. In his mind, the event arrived in separate
pictures: the sky darkening in a way that felt wrong, the sound of wind and impact, the scene of
damage and confusion afterward. He described those memories as mostly images, as if each moment were
frozen in place, and when they came back to him they did not feel distant or symbolic but completely
real across all his senses at once. The smell, the noise, and the sight would return together so
vividly that for an instant he felt back in the middle of it, with the same fear in his body and the
same sense that things could break apart without warning.

In the weeks and months that followed, Arun found that certain ordinary cues could pull him straight
back into those moments. Ambulances were especially difficult for him. When he heard one pass the
courthouse or saw it stopping outside a building, his attention locked onto it immediately, and his
body reacted before he had time to collect himself. He also became highly aware of weather
conditions that resembled the day of the disaster. A gray sky, heavy wind, or the humid pressure in
the air could make him tense, scanning outside and comparing the present moment to the memory he did
not want to summon. Even the smell of a hospital or antiseptic could catch him off guard. He had
once walked into a medical building for a routine appointment and been hit by that sharp, clean
odor; he had to stop for a moment, because it carried him back to the emergency atmosphere
surrounding the disaster and made the memory feel suddenly near again. These experiences were not
abstract reminders for him. They were specific, embodied moments in which he felt the past arrive in
the present.

Arun tried hard to keep the memory out of his mind. He found himself making a conscious effort not
to think about the trauma, pushing it away whenever it surfaced. He also began avoiding relaxing.
What had once been his way of recovering after work—sitting quietly, letting his shoulders drop,
reading at home, or lying back on the couch—started to feel unsafe to him. In those unguarded
moments, he became more aware of how easily his mind could drift toward the disaster, and he came to
believe that relaxing meant lowering his defenses at a time when he should stay alert. Instead of
resting, he kept himself occupied, sometimes sitting rigidly through the evening. Going out also
became harder. He found himself avoiding leaving home unless he had to, and over time his world
narrowed. The more he stayed in, the more his wife noticed that plans were being canceled, errands
were being postponed, and he was choosing the smallest possible route through the day.

At work, he remained functional on the surface, but the courthouse was not free of reminders. When
emergency vehicles passed nearby, the sound could cut through the quiet formal setting and pull his
attention away from the bench and toward the memory of the disaster. He made every effort not to
show this. As a judge, he felt pressure to appear calm and unimpressed by disturbance, and he
managed that role carefully, but the strain showed in how much energy it took to keep his mind on
the present. At home, his wife saw that he was no longer able to settle into ordinary evening life
in the way he used to. What had once been shared downtime now felt difficult to him, especially if
it involved being still or going out together.

Arun eventually sought help because the effort of holding everything in had become exhausting. He
was tired of reacting so strongly to weather, sirens, and the smell of hospitals, tired of the way
the memories could return with such force, and tired of shaping his life around avoiding the next
unexpected reminder. He hoped to regain some sense that he could live normally again, sit with his
wife without feeling on guard, and move through the city without bracing for a sudden return of the
disaster in his mind. What frightened him most was that his world had become smaller than it used to
be, and he feared that if things stayed this way, he would remain trapped in the same few images and
sensations that had followed him ever since the storm.
