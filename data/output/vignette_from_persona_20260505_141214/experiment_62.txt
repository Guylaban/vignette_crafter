Samuel was 45 and worked long days as an agricultural worker, spending most mornings and afternoons
outdoors and then returning in the evening to the shared routines of the home he kept with his
partner. He had grown up in Africa, in a household where fear could enter a room quietly and then
suddenly become impossible to ignore. As a child, he had witnessed domestic violence between adults
in the family home, an event that left a lasting mark on how he moved through the world. What he
remembered most was not a single dramatic scene but the helplessness of standing close enough to see
and hear what was happening, knowing that something was terribly wrong and still feeling unable to
step in. Since then, he had carried a painful conviction that he should have done more. In the years
that followed, he often returned to the same thought with a hard, self-reproaching certainty: if he
had been braver, if he had called out, if he had intervened, perhaps the outcome would have been
different. He described this with shame, saying he could not forgive himself for not stopping what
happened.

Over time, Samuel had come to see himself as a different person from the boy he once was. He said he
used to be happy, but now he felt “always miserable,” a phrase he used without exaggeration, as
though it had become part of his identity. He also spoke with sadness about what the experience had
done to his belief in other people and in life more generally. He had lost faith in people being
safe or dependable, and even when others tried to reassure him, he found it nearly impossible to
accept their words at face value. For Samuel, reassurance did not settle anything; it seemed to
bounce off a part of him that remained unconvinced and watchful. That mistrust had become especially
noticeable in everyday settings where he could not control who was near him. He made a point of
never being alone with another person if he could avoid it. At work, this sometimes meant keeping to
the edges of the field crews, staying where others could see him, and choosing tasks that allowed
him to remain in open view rather than step into enclosed spaces with a single coworker. At home, he
became careful about positioning himself near doors or open areas, and he preferred not to be
isolated in a room with someone else unless he could help it. He did not describe this as fear in
the moment so much as a rule he had come to live by.

Samuel also tried hard not to think about what he had witnessed, but the effort itself showed how
often the memories pushed toward the surface. He said he could bring himself to the edge of the
memory and then force his mind away, only to find the same images and feelings returning later,
sometimes when he least expected them. He would catch himself going over the old scene again,
especially at night, and there were stretches when his thoughts turned toward revenge, replaying
what he wished he could have done to the person who had caused the violence. He did not present this
as a plan so much as a grim and repetitive preoccupation, a way of circling around helplessness
without ever reaching relief. These private arguments with himself often intensified when he was
alone in the dark or when the house was quiet, because nighttime felt less safe to him than
daylight. He said that when evening came, he became more alert to sounds outside and inside the
home, listening for movement, checking, and scanning his surroundings in a way he did not during the
day. In daylight he could work, talk, and function more easily, but after dark he felt as if the
world changed character and became harder to trust.

By the time he sought help, these patterns had been present for years and had begun to shape his
relationships and daily routines in concrete ways. His partner had noticed how he avoided being
alone with others and how difficult it was for him to relax when visitors were present. Samuel
himself was tired of living as if danger might appear at any moment, and he was troubled by how
little comfort he could take from the people around him. He had arrived wanting relief from the
constant watchfulness, the night-time unease, and the bitter self-judgment that continued to follow
him. More than anything, he hoped to feel safe enough to be near other people without guarding
himself so fiercely, and he was afraid that if nothing changed he would remain trapped in the same
mistrust, misery, and regret that had already shaped so much of his life.
