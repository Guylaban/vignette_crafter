Marisol was 24, a single Latin American woman who had moved through her warehouse shifts in a large
distribution center on the edge of town with a quiet efficiency that her supervisors had once
praised. Before the traumatic event, she described herself as someone who kept to herself at work,
spoke when necessary, and preferred to finish her assignments without drawing attention. That
changed after the day she was at the shopping district downtown when a bombing struck near the plaza
where she had stopped after work. She remembered the sound first, then the sudden violence of people
running in every direction, the sharp movements of bodies turning and dropping, and her own body
reacting before she could think. In the aftermath, there were stretches of the event that she could
not retrieve no matter how hard she tried, even when family members asked her to tell them what had
happened. She knew the sequence in broad outline, but some parts remained blank, and when she tried
to speak about it she became angry and frustrated, irritated with herself for not being able to put
the experience into words. At times, the anger returned when the memory surfaced unexpectedly; she
would tense, clench her jaw, and feel a hot, immediate rage at whoever had done this, and at the
fact that ordinary life had been interrupted so violently.

In the weeks that followed, Marisol found herself moving through the world as though danger might
break through at any moment. She had come to believe that the world was a dangerous place, and that
she could no longer trust her own body to keep her safe. She described feeling on guard all the
time, as if she had to monitor everything around her before allowing herself to relax. In practice,
that meant scanning every room and every walkway for exits, watching people’s hands, and noticing
who moved quickly or unexpectedly. Sudden movements by other people put her immediately on guard: a
coworker stepping out from behind a shelf, a cart rolling too fast around a corner, someone turning
abruptly in her direction. At those moments, she would feel her body go rigid and her attention
narrow to the possibility of something happening again. She became especially uneasy at night, when
the warehouse was darker, the parking lot quieter, and the drive home felt longer than it had
before. During evening shifts she found it harder to feel safe, and she counted the hours until
daylight, when the same streets seemed at least more visible. She also noticed that she had become
jumpy and easily startled; loud noises, a door slamming, or someone calling her name too suddenly
could make her flinch before she had time to compose herself.

Marisol’s ways of getting through the day changed in small but consequential ways. At work, she
avoided eye contact with coworkers and with unfamiliar people who came through the warehouse,
keeping her gaze lowered to her tasks or fixed on the floor. She explained that looking directly at
others made her feel exposed, as if she might miss a sudden movement or not notice trouble coming.
She also began to deliberately try to leave her body or blank out when the memory or fear came too
close. In those moments she would stare ahead without really seeing, letting her mind drift until
the feeling passed, because being fully present felt unbearable when her body seemed so unreliable.
She could spend long stretches of a shift moving boxes, checking labels, and keeping herself busy,
while still feeling half detached from what was happening around her. The habit of constant scanning
followed her everywhere, including the parking lot after work and the grocery store on the way home,
where she would keep checking the aisles and entrances instead of focusing on what she needed.

The changes affected her relationships as much as her work. She had less patience for being
approached unexpectedly and found herself pulling away from casual conversation. Friends commented
that she seemed distant, but she did not know how to explain that she was trying to keep herself
braced for whatever might happen next. What frightened her most was the sense that she could not
fully trust her own reactions or her own body; even when nothing was happening, she felt compelled
to remain alert, as though lowering her guard for a moment would invite another catastrophe. She
sought help when she realized how exhausting it had become to live in a state of constant vigilance,
especially with the night shifts and the long walks to her car after dark. She came to treatment
hoping to feel safe enough to go through an ordinary day without scanning every corner, and fearing
that if she continued as she was, the world would keep feeling like a place where danger could erupt
without warning.
