Mai Nguyen was 29 and had built her life around precision, discipline, and the expectation that she
would stay composed under pressure. As a combat pilot, she was used to long hours on base, the
constant routines of briefing rooms, simulators, and aircraft hangars, and the expectation that she
could keep her focus even when the work was demanding. What changed her was not a crash in her own
aircraft or an enemy encounter, but the death of someone she loved. The event had come without
warning and with a finality that left her reeling: one moment she was speaking with him and
expecting the conversation to continue in the ordinary way, and later she was facing the fact that
he was gone. In the days that followed, she kept returning mentally to the scene, as though her mind
were stuck on the moment the loss became real. She described the memory as if it were a film that
played over and over, and when she tried to picture what had happened, it came back in sharp, still
fragments, almost like looking at photographs laid out in front of her. That replaying was not
something she controlled; at times a detail in her surroundings—a phrase, a tone of voice, a change
in the atmosphere around her—could bring the full memory back without warning.

In the beginning she tried hard not to think about it. Mai would push the memory away whenever it
surfaced, telling herself to get back to work, to keep moving, to stay focused on the next task. She
also became careful about what she was willing to hear from others. If someone at the squadron
started talking about loss, about families left behind, or about people who had lost loved ones, she
found herself wanting to step out of the conversation. Those themes felt especially sharp to her,
not because she wanted sympathy but because they seemed to open the door to everything she had been
trying not to face. She also felt distinctly uncomfortable in her own body when the memory was near,
as if the unease itself were a signal that something was wrong and about to break through. Over
time, she found herself avoiding situations in which other people might remind her of the event.
Friends from work would mention the loss in a casual or well-meaning way, and she would go quiet or
change the subject. Their reminders felt intrusive, as though they were pulling her back into the
worst moment of her life before she was ready.

Mai’s life narrowed in ways she did not expect. As a single woman, she had once assumed she would
eventually form a new relationship or at least make new friendships easily through work, but after
the loss she became wary of starting anything new. She did not want to explain herself, did not want
the topic to land on what had happened, and did not trust that other people would know how to handle
it. She had come to believe that she could not rely on other people in the way she once had, and
that belief made closeness feel risky. When others reached out, she held them at a distance. She
remained functional on the surface, showing up for her duties and keeping her work performance
intact, but she was much more alone in the spaces outside of work.

At the same time, her sense of herself changed in a painful way. Mai was haunted by the thought that

not describe this as a passing regret; it sat with her as a fixed, self-condemning conclusion. Even
when she tried to reassure herself that the loss had not been something she could have controlled,
the thought returned with force. The memory itself often came back with such clarity that it felt
less like remembering than reliving, and that immediacy made it hard for her to keep her footing
when the reminder was close at hand.

By the time she sought help, the strain was visible in the way she lived around the memories rather
than through dramatic disclosure. She was tired of spending so much energy trying not to think,
tired of feeling pulled into the image of the event whenever someone spoke too openly about

person she had been. She hoped, cautiously, that treatment might help her face the memory without
being overwhelmed by it, and that she might learn how to move through daily life without feeling so
certain that something was wrong with her, that other people could not be trusted, and that she had
failed in the one moment she believed mattered most.
