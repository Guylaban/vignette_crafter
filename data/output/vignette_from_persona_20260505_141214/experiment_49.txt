Matiu, a 63-year-old publisher of Oceanic heritage, came to treatment after more than a year of
living with a sense that danger could return without warning. He and his wife had built a quiet life
in their home, and in many ways he had kept functioning: he still went to work, still handled
manuscripts and meetings, still moved through the routines of a long marriage. Yet since the
traumatic event, he had become someone who entered each room as if he needed to account for every
possible exit. The event itself had been a man-made disaster, sudden and chaotic enough that, even
months later, he could recount it in a manner that felt less like telling a story and more like
reopening a scene. When he spoke of it, his recollection was not diffuse or hazy; it came with
sharp, vivid fragments, mostly as pictures in his mind. He could still see the layout, the faces,
the movement, the moments of confusion and alarm. He described how the memory would force its way in
when he did not want it to, arriving uninvited in the middle of an ordinary day.

In the weeks after it happened, Matiu noticed that certain cues brought him back to those scenes
with uncomfortable immediacy. He dreaded waking from nightmares because the first few seconds after
opening his eyes were full of residue from the dream, a lingering certainty that the threat was
still near. Those mornings were especially hard if he woke with his heart already racing, not yet
sure where he was. Talking about the event was another potent trigger. When colleagues or friends
asked careful questions, or when someone at work mentioned the incident in the context of public
discussion, he felt the images close in again. He became aware of the smell of alcohol as
particularly unsettling. A whiff of it from a person passing too close, or in a room where drinks
had been served, could bring back the old alarm with striking force. He knew the scent was ordinary,
but to him it had become fused with the memory of the disaster.

Matiu responded by arranging his life around vigilance. At work, he preferred to sit with his back
against a wall so he could see the room and keep track of who came and went. In meetings, he would
choose the seat nearest the edge or against the perimeter without drawing attention to it, though to
him the reason felt obvious and necessary. He also found himself constantly scanning his
surroundings for signs of threat. Whether he was in his office, at a café, or in a waiting room, his
eyes moved before his attention settled, checking doors, corners, and the faces of strangers. He
avoided seeing friends, not because he no longer cared about them, but because social gatherings
seemed to increase the chances of being exposed to alcohol, unexpected conversations about the
event, and the strain of having to appear composed while he remained alert beneath the surface. Over
time, that avoidance narrowed his world. Friends stopped asking as often, and he noticed the quiet
distance that had grown where easy companionship used to be.

Even at home, Matiu no longer felt fully at ease. He described being on guard inside his own house,
as if the walls could not entirely keep danger out. Ordinary sounds made him jump: a door closing
too sharply, a dropped object, a sudden ring from another room. He was aware that the reaction was
stronger than it used to be, but he could not stop himself from tensing and turning toward the
noise. He felt jumpy and easily startled in a way that left him embarrassed, especially because
people around him would barely react. His wife noticed that he was always looking around and that he
seemed to settle only when he had checked and rechecked his environment. Though he remained
committed to his work, the effort of holding himself together in public and then coming home still
watchful left him weary.

By the time he sought help, it was not because the memories had disappeared; they remained clear,
detailed, and capable of breaking through at any moment. Rather, he had become tired of living as if
every room required vigilance and every ordinary sound might mean something more. He wanted relief
from the nightmares that left him unnerved on waking, from the smell of alcohol that could bring the
scene back with such force, and from the narrowing of his social life that had crept in as he
avoided friends and kept himself braced. What brought him in now was the fear that this level of
alertness had become his new normal, and the hope that he might again move through his home, his
work, and his relationships without feeling that he had to keep watch all the time.
