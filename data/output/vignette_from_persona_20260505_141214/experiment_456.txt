interrupted when a man-made disaster struck in a public place she knew well, turning an ordinary day

the blast came without warning, a sharp bang that seemed to split the air and freeze everyone in
place. What stayed with her most was not only the sound itself but the immediate sense that the
world had become unsafe in an instant, that something human-made had erupted into the middle of life
and could do so again at any time. From that day on, the memory of the event remained vivid enough
that even hearing a loud bang in another context could pull her back into the same startled dread.

In the weeks and months afterward, Amina found herself responding to the world as though danger
might be hidden in ordinary moments. She became acutely unsettled by explosion or bang sounds,
whether they came from construction outside her synagogue, a dropped object in a kitchen, or
something sudden on television. She also noticed a disturbing sense of being watched, as if eyes
were on her even in familiar places. That feeling could arise while she was walking through her
neighborhood or sitting in her office, and she would have to work to keep her composure while
continuing with her responsibilities. News about violence or disasters was especially hard to take
in. When a related story came on the radio or appeared on her phone, she would often put it aside
quickly, not wanting the images or headlines to settle into her mind. She had come to expect that
hearing about similar events could leave her tense for the rest of the day.

Amina’s day-to-day life gradually narrowed around efforts to keep herself from being reminded of
what had happened. When friends, congregants, or colleagues mentioned the event or something
similar, she would try to change the subject or leave the room, and afterward she often felt drained
and irritated with herself for being unable to sit through the conversation. At home, she avoided
sex with her husband, not because she no longer cared for him, but because intimacy left her feeling
exposed in a way she could not tolerate. She described a need to stay in control of her body and
surroundings, and sexual closeness felt too vulnerable, too likely to let in feelings she was trying
to keep contained. Over time, this created distance in the marriage that she found painful to
acknowledge, though she did not know how to explain that the problem was not her husband but the
sense of danger that followed her into private moments.

reassurance and understand it intellectually, but it did not reach the part of her that remained on
alert. Nighttime was especially difficult. During the day she could sometimes keep herself occupied,
but after dark she became more watchful and found it harder to settle. She checked doors, listened
for unusual sounds, and remained sensitive to the possibility that something could happen again when

abrupt noises before anyone else seemed to react. She could not stop mentally assessing whether

measuring herself against what she believed she should have been able to change. At the same time,
she had come to believe that she had to be especially careful because you never know what can happen
next. That belief was not abstract; it shaped how she walked into buildings, how she sat in
meetings, and how she moved through evenings at home. She also found herself thinking that nothing
good could happen to her anymore, as if the disaster had marked a line beyond which ordinary hope no
longer applied to her life. These thoughts sat alongside a persistent sense of apprehension that she
described as exhausting but inescapable.

By the time she sought help, Amina was still functioning in many of her roles, but she was doing so
