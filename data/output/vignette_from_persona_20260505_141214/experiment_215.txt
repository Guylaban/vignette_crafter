Daniel Reyes had been a school counselor in a public middle school in the suburbs of a large North
American city for six years when the accident happened, a role that fit the patient he had always
thought of himself as being: steady, patient, good in a crisis. He was married, well-liked by staff
and students, and used to being the person others came to when something had gone wrong. The trauma
that later reorganized his life occurred on an ordinary afternoon while he was driving home after
work. A sudden moment of inattention at an intersection led to a collision that seriously injured
another driver, and from the first report onward Daniel was faced with the fact that his own action
had been the immediate cause. The details of the scene remained fixed in him with a force that was
not limited to memory. In the days after, the police came, questions were asked, and the word
“serious” attached itself to the injury in a way he could not shake. He began to avoid police
altogether, crossing streets early or changing routes if he saw a cruiser parked near the school or
outside a store, because being near them brought back the fear of being asked again to explain what
he had done.

In the months that followed, Daniel found that stress itself became a trigger. Staff meetings,
paperwork deadlines, noisy afternoons in the counseling office, even the pressure of being asked to
handle multiple student crises could send him abruptly back to the accident scene. Medical
procedures were another potent cue. When he had to undergo a routine medical examination, the smell
of the clinic and the sight of gloves and equipment were enough to make his chest tighten and his
body go rigid, as if he were once again watching the injured person being assessed and not yet
knowing how badly they were hurt. He noticed that certain smells could bring everything back with
startling immediacy. The odor of antiseptic or rubber, or other related smells he encountered in
medical settings, would arrive before he had time to prepare, and he would feel suddenly trapped in
the original moment, his stomach dropping as if the day of the crash had opened up under him.

What Daniel described most vividly was how the memory came back. It did not feel like something that
had happened and ended; it felt present, as though the event was unfolding in front of him again. He
could recall the crash, the sound, the impact, and the aftermath across all his senses at once, so
fully that for a moment he would be disoriented by the ordinary room around him. He knew what
happened, but when he tried to explain it he often found he could not find the words. In sessions he
would start, stop, and restart, saying only that it was “the worst thing” and then falling silent,
because the experience resisted being shaped into language. At times, the recollection was
accompanied by strong physical reactions: his heart would pound, his body would tense, and he would
feel flushed and shaken when something reminded him of it. The emotional tone was equally intense, a
heavy mix of fear, horror, anger, guilt, and shame that could arrive without warning and sit in him
for hours.

Daniel had come to believe that he could not trust himself to do the right thing. Before the
accident, he had seen himself as careful and responsible; afterward, he looked back on his own
choices with suspicion and self-reproach, convinced that the event happened because of the way he
acted. He repeated to himself that if he had been more attentive, more disciplined, more worthy of
the role people expected from him, none of it would have happened. Over time he began to speak about
his life as though it had been destroyed by the trauma, not in a dramatic or theatrical way, but
with a flat certainty that his future had narrowed around one irreversible mistake. He stayed
mentally occupied all the time, keeping himself busy from morning until night, filling gaps with
work tasks, errands, news, and scrolling on his phone so there would be less room for thinking about
the event. When silence came, or when he was alone at night, the memory pressed in harder, and he
would lie awake needing to stay alert, unwilling to let himself drift into sleep quickly because
sleep felt like a surrender to whatever might come next in his mind. His marriage had become quieter
and more strained, not from open conflict but because he was so often elsewhere in his thoughts, and
his wife had begun to notice how often he seemed tense, distracted, and unreachable.

By the time he sought help, Daniel had exhausted his usual ways of getting through the day. He came
to treatment because the effort of staying occupied, avoiding police, and steering clear of
reminders had become difficult to maintain while continuing to work in a school full of
unpredictable stressors and constant demands. He was most afraid that one wrong moment, one lapse in
attention or one unexpected reminder, could bring back the full force of the crash or prove that his
judgment could never be trusted again. At the same time, he was hoping for some way to live with the
memory without feeling as though he were back at the intersection every time it surfaced.
