Martin had built his life around movement. At 56, he was still known in his community as a dancer, a
man who taught, performed, and spent much of his day in rehearsal studios, theaters, and crowded
event spaces where music and bodies filled every corner. He was married, and until the event that
altered him, he had thought of himself as someone who could move through the world with poise and
control. That changed on an evening he still tried not to picture. He was driving when he failed to
stop in time and his car struck another vehicle. In the confusion that followed, he understood
immediately that the other person was badly hurt. The image of the damaged car, the suddenness of
the impact, and the knowledge that he had been the one behind the wheel remained fixed in his mind.
What stayed with him most was not only the sound and the violence of the collision, but the sense
that a single moment had broken the life he had expected to continue.

In the weeks after the accident, Martin found himself repeatedly pulled back to the scene in his
mind, especially when he encountered certain songs. Music, which had once been central to his work
and pleasure, became unpredictable; a familiar tune could suddenly bring back the whole sequence of
the crash with a force that made him feel unsteady. He also became intensely unsettled in crowded
spaces and places with too much stimulation. In rehearsal studios, theaters, and public gatherings,
where overlapping sound and movement once felt natural to him, he now felt overwhelmed and on edge,
as though the environment itself might tip into danger. When he saw cars that had been hit or badly
damaged, even in passing, the sight struck him hard and left him visibly shaken. He could not look
for long without his mind filling with the accident again, the image arriving with the force of
something happening in the present.

Martin responded by trying hard not to think about what had happened. He would push the memory away
when it surfaced and keep himself busy rather than allow his mind to settle on it. He also began
avoiding the place where the event happened and anything near it, taking different routes and
arranging his days so he would not have to pass that stretch of road. Over time, he found himself
dwelling on how he used to be before the event, returning in thought to the version of himself who
moved through life without hesitation and who felt dependable in his own eyes. These comparisons
left him with a sense of diminished self-respect that he found difficult to escape. He told himself,
over and over, that he would never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened. He
believed he should have protected others and failed. In his most painful moments, he felt he had
failed in his most important role in life, and this belief sat heavily on him, especially because
his identity as a dancer had long been tied to discipline, attentiveness, and the care of others in
shared space.

As months passed, the problem did not soften into the background. Martin had difficulty
concentrating, both at work and at home, and he noticed that he could not settle into tasks the way
he once had. He remained on guard much of the time, unable to relax even when he knew he was in a
safe place. At night he struggled to fall asleep because he felt he needed to stay alert, as if
letting his guard down might allow something terrible to return. His sleep became a time of
vigilance rather than rest. These changes began to affect his work and relationships in practical
ways. In rehearsal he was less present, more likely to lose track of instructions or music cues, and
he found certain spaces more difficult to tolerate than before. His world grew smaller, shaped by
the effort required to avoid reminders and to keep his thoughts from turning back to the accident.

By the time he came for help, Martin was exhausted by the strain of living this way. He had reached
a point where the songs, the crowded rooms, the damaged cars on the road, and the route near the

by memories. What frightened him most was the possibility that he would remain trapped in this
version of himself, unable to forgive what he had done and unable to return to the life he had once
known.
