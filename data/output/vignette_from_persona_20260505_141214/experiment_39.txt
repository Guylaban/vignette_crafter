At 68, Minh Tran had spent most of his adult life in courtrooms, and even after decades as a public
defender he still moved through the day with the careful, measured habits of someone who knew how
quickly ordinary situations could turn dangerous for other people. Widowed and living alone, he came
to treatment after nearly two years of increasingly difficult mornings, nights, and workdays since
the sudden death of his wife, Lan. The event had happened in a place that still felt unreal to him:
not at home, not in a hospital, but at a bus stop near their neighborhood when she was struck and
killed in a random act of violence while Minh was a short distance away. He had seen only pieces of
it. What remained were disconnected fragments that never settled into a complete sequence: Lan
stepping ahead of him, the sound of voices rising, the sharp crack of impact, people shouting, his
own body moving before he could think, then later kneeling beside her and realizing she was gone.
When he tried to remember it, the details arrived out of order and without continuity, as if his
mind had broken the scene into separate shards that would not fit together.

Since then, Minh had found that memories of that day could arrive with a force that made him feel as
though he was back at the stop again. At times he would suddenly hear, in his own head, bits of what
he had heard in those moments—the screech of tires, the frightened yelling, the urgent sounds of
people calling for help—despite being alone in his apartment or sitting at his desk in the office.
Those intrusions were usually followed by an abrupt wave of grief that felt larger than the present
room could hold. He would have to stop whatever he was doing, lower his head, and wait for the
feeling to pass, though it rarely passed quickly. He described those moments as being overwhelmed by
the full fact of her absence all over again, not just sadness but a deep, wrenching grief that
seemed to hit without warning.

Over the months after Lan’s death, Minh’s days began to narrow around a constant sense that danger
could appear anywhere and at any moment. He had become vigilant toward other people in a way that
was new for him, scanning faces, posture, and movement when he walked to the courthouse, stood in
line at the grocery store, or waited at intersections near the bus stop where Lan had died. He
noticed strangers more intensely than he had before and found himself studying who looked “off,” as
though he had to identify risk before it could reach him. Even brief changes in the environment
could set off a false alarm in his body: a sudden shout in the hallway outside his office, a car
backfiring on the street, or someone approaching too quickly behind him made him jerk, tense, and
turn as if he were in immediate danger even when nothing was actually wrong. At home, these
reactions were joined by repeated checking of the doors and windows. Before bed he would move from
room to room, testing the locks more than once, then return to check them again after climbing under
the covers. If he heard an unexpected sound at night, he would get up and repeat the sequence, not
because he believed he could truly control what might happen, but because not checking felt
unbearable.

Minh also found himself avoiding anything that resembled the circumstances of the loss. He stopped
watching films or television programmes that involved accidents, assaults, or sudden death, and he
avoided reading news stories about similar events. A report on the radio or a scene on a screen
could leave him shaken for the rest of the day, so he chose silence over the possibility of being
pulled back into those images. Even his work was affected in small but concrete ways. As a public
defender, he had always been accustomed to hard cases and difficult clients, but now he noticed that
crowded court corridors and the unpredictable movement of unfamiliar people heightened his sense of
watchfulness. He could still do his job, though with more effort and less ease, and he sometimes
chose to eat more than usual during breaks or late at night, not because he was hungry so much as
because the act of eating gave him something immediate and blunt to focus on when his mind felt full
of fear, horror, anger, guilt, and shame. He did not speak easily about those feelings, but they
were present in the way he held himself and in the way he avoided places and stories that reminded
him of what he had lost.

Minh sought help only after noticing how exhausted he had become by living in this constant state of
alertness, checking, bracing, and withdrawing. What finally brought him in was the realization that
he was spending more time trying not to be frightened than actually living, and that his grief for
Lan kept returning with the same force whenever the memory broke through. He came to treatment
hoping for some way to sit with the memories without being torn apart by them, and afraid that if
nothing changed he would remain forever watchful, alone in his apartment, still waiting for danger
at every door.
