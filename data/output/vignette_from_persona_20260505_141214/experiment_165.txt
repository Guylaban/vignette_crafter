Mina Tran was a 19-year-old hairdresser of Southeast Asian background who came to treatment after
nearly a year of living with the aftermath of a road accident in which she had accidentally caused
serious injury to another person. On the day of the event, she had been driving with a passenger
when her attention was split for a moment and the car struck another vehicle. The details she could
not shake were not abstract to her; they came back as sudden pictures of the impact, the other car
hit by accident, and the sound that followed, especially any loud noise that seemed to cut through
the room and drag her straight back to that instant. Since then, she had found herself jolted by the
slam of a door in the salon, the sharp bang of equipment dropped on the tiled floor, or any
unexpected crash from the street outside. Even when she knew she was safe, her body reacted as
though danger had returned, and she described a constant inability to relax that made rest feel
impossible.

In the hours and days after the accident, Mina kept replaying the scene in fragments. At times she
could picture it with painful clarity, as though she were staring at a sequence of still images: the
cars, the angle of impact, the moment everything went wrong. At other moments, she could recall what
happened but felt strangely numb, as if it had happened to someone else, to a stranger she had been
forced to watch from a distance. Then, without warning, the numbness would give way to overwhelming
grief. What struck her most was not only what she had seen but what she felt she had done. She was
convinced that she should have protected the other person and that she had failed. That belief
settled into the way she spoke about herself, with a painful certainty that she could not undo what
had happened and could not make herself the kind of person who would have prevented it.

Mina began avoiding anything that made the event feel close. She stopped driving entirely and would
not ride as a passenger either, turning down lifts from her boyfriend and arranging her life so that
she could get everywhere another way. Over time this narrowed the shape of her days. She worked as a
hairdresser, and the salon became one of the few places she could still tolerate because it was
familiar and predictable, but even there she entered each morning scanning the exits as soon as she
walked in, noting where she could get out if something happened. She found herself listening for
sudden sounds, her shoulders tightening at every bang or screech. When the calendar moved toward the
date connected to the crash, she became especially guarded and tried to keep busy so she would not
have to sit with the memory or the anniversary of what she had done. Those dates felt dangerous to
her, not because she believed anything specific would happen, but because she feared being pulled
back into the same helplessness and grief.

In the months after the accident, Mina also noticed how she behaved around people whenever she felt
even slightly threatened. She became overly compliant, quick to agree, apologetic in a way that
seemed almost automatic. At work, if a client sounded irritated, she would soften her voice, rush to
please them, and make herself smaller rather than risk conflict. In her relationship, she worried
that asking for too much might drive people away, and beneath that was a deeper conviction that she
could not rely on other people anyway. The accident had left her with a sense that bodies could fail
at any moment, including her own. She spoke of her body as if it were not dependable, something she
could not fully trust to keep steady or respond correctly. That feeling sharpened whenever she
noticed her heart racing or her muscles tensing after a loud noise or a sudden image of two cars
colliding.

By the time she sought help, Mina was exhausted by the range of places she had to monitor herself:
the salon, the street, the inside of a car, any room where she entered and immediately looked for an
exit. She had grown tired of living in a state of alertness that never fully turned off and of
carrying the grief of the accident in a way that surfaced whenever she remembered it. What brought
her to treatment was the growing sense that her world had become too small and too carefully
managed, and that she was frightened of spending the rest of her life organized around that one
moment. What she hoped for, though she said it cautiously, was to be able to remember the accident
without collapsing into shame or numbness, and to move through ordinary life without feeling as if
danger were always one loud noise away.
