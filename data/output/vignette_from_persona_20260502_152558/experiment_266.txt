Arthur presents as a quiet, stoic man, his 76 years etched into the deep lines around his eyes. He
has worked as a cashier at a small neighbourhood grocery store for the past decade, a job he took
after his wife, Eleanor, passed. The trauma that brings him to therapy, however, is far older,
rooted in a life he left behind in Europe fifty years ago. He speaks of it in a flat, distant tone,
as if narrating a story about a stranger. He can describe the precise colour of the sky and the make
of the car, a small, rattling sedan he was driving with Eleanor beside him. But when he recounts the
ambush on that country road—the sudden eruption of gunfire, the shattering of the passenger-side
window—he feels nothing at all. This detached recall, where the memory unfolds like a silent film,
is a stark contrast to how the event lives in his body day to day.

The trauma rarely returns as a full narrative. Instead, it is a series of stark, intrusive images
that ambush him without warning. It is the spiderweb of cracks spreading across the glass, or the
bright, impossible red blooming on Eleanor’s sleeve. These visual memories are so potent that for a
moment, he feels he is right back in that car, the engine stalled and the smell of cordite in the
air. This terrifying sense of reliving the event immediately ignites a profound sense of threat. His
heart hammers in his chest, and the quiet aisles of the grocery store suddenly feel menacing. In
these moments, he is certain that the world is a fundamentally dangerous place, a conviction that
has calcified over the decades. He finds himself watching customers with an intensity that unnerves
them, tracking their hands and their movements, constantly on guard for the first sign of harm.

This ever-present sense of threat has, over time, reshaped his life into a series of careful
negotiations with fear. The most significant of these is his absolute refusal to get behind the
wheel of a car, or even be a passenger. The intrusive images are most powerful there, and the
feeling of being trapped inside a metal box is unbearable. This avoidance, born from the terror of
his memories, has become a cage. His daughter, who lives two towns over, has begged him to visit his
grandchildren, but the thought of the car journey makes him physically ill. This strategy of
avoidance only serves to strengthen the original memory; with no new, safe experiences in a vehicle,
the ambush remains his only salient reference point. The avoidance also feeds his hypervigilance.
Because he feels safest at home, the world outside becomes, by definition, more threatening,
justifying his need to remain super-alert and watchful.

His world has shrunk to the small radius he can walk, a reality that has profoundly eroded his sense
of self. He looks at his life and sees a man who cannot do the simple things others take for
granted, confirming his deeply held belief that he is no longer the capable, strong person who once
built a new life for his family. This appraisal, in turn, makes the traumatic memories more
accessible; feeling weak and helpless in the present primes him to recall the ultimate moment of
helplessness from his past. The strategies he uses to manage his fear have become a feedback loop of
despair. He spends long hours wondering why this happened to him, a circular quest for meaning that
only deepens his conviction that he has lost his faith in any kind of benevolent order to the world.
He actively pushes away moments of joy or contentment, finding them dissonant and unsafe; feeling
good is a luxury he cannot afford, a lowering of the guard that might invite disaster.

The constant vigilance is utterly exhausting. At night, he lies awake for hours, unable to sleep
because his body remains on high alert, listening for any sound that might signal danger. This
chronic state of threat has cemented his negative appraisals of the world. After decades of feeling
unsafe, he has come to believe that people are fundamentally untrustworthy and that life is little
more than a series of potential ambushes. It was his daughter’s tearful ultimatum—that if he could
not find a way to come to them, he might lose touch with his grandchildren altogether—that finally
brought him to seek help. Arthur fears he is running out of time, that he will die in his small
apartment, a prisoner of a single afternoon fifty years ago, forever watching the world for a threat
that has already come and gone.
