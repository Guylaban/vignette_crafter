no release valve. The incident itself had been jarringly mundane at its start. He was driving home
from a job site, his truck laden with tools and off-cuts of cedar, when he saw the flashing lights
in his rearview mirror. He’d made an incomplete stop at a sign a few blocks back. But the routine
traffic stop spiraled. The officer’s tone was aggressive, his questions rapid-fire. Confused and
increasingly nervous, Bao fumbled for his registration. When a second officer appeared at his
passenger-side window, unseen and unheard until he tapped on the glass, Bao flinched violently. That
was all it took. He was pulled from the truck, slammed against the door, and cuffed. The delay, the
sudden escalation from a minor mistake to physical force, the feeling of being trapped and unable to
see the men behind him, had rewired his sense of safety.

In the weeks that followed, the world began to feel like a series of potential ambushes. Bao found
he was unable to relax, even at home with his wife, Mai. A pervasive, humming anxiety settled deep
in his bones. At his workshop, he rearranged his entire setup, moving his primary workbench so that
it was flush against the back wall, allowing him an unobstructed view of the large bay door. He told
his partner it was for better light, but the truth was he couldn't bear the thought of anyone
walking in behind him. This constant, exhausting vigilance only intensified his belief that danger
was imminent. When a lumber delivery was thirty minutes late, he didn't just feel impatient; he was
consumed by a certainty that something terrible had happened—a catastrophic accident, a violent
confrontation. He would call the supplier repeatedly, his heart pounding until he heard a
dispatcher’s bored voice on the other end.

This relentless sense of threat became an intolerable state to live in. To manage the feeling of
being constantly unsafe, Bao began turning inward, searching for a reason. He would spend hours
replaying the traffic stop, convinced it was his fault. *If only I hadn’t flinched. If I had
answered more clearly. If I had just found the papers faster.* This pattern of self-blame became a
punishing, circular ritual he hoped would give him a sense of control, a rulebook for avoiding
future disaster. When the rumination grew too loud, and the effort to avoid thinking about the event
felt like holding a beach ball underwater, he started smoking cannabis before bed. It was the only
thing that seemed to quiet the noise and blunt the edges of his hyper-awareness, allowing him a few
hours of dreamless sleep.

But these strategies, meant to bring relief, only tightened the trap. The endless self-blame
reinforced his conviction that he was fundamentally unsafe, that a single misstep on his part could
invite catastrophe. This belief left him feeling even more vulnerable and on guard than before. The
cannabis that helped him sleep left him feeling foggy and slow the next morning, a cognitive haze
that made him feel less capable of detecting and responding to danger, which in turn amplified his
baseline anxiety and made it impossible to ever truly relax. His attempts to push the memory away
only made it return with more force, ambushing him when he least expected it. The very things he was
doing to feel safe were making the world feel infinitely more threatening.

The strain began to show everywhere. His focus at work was shot, and he’d made a costly measuring
error on a set of custom cabinets. Mai was growing distant, frustrated by his inability to be
present with her and worried by the cloying smell of smoke that now clung to his clothes most
evenings. It was after a particularly sharp argument, where she told him she felt like she was
living with a ghost, that Bao finally made an appointment. He came to therapy feeling exhausted and
ashamed, terrified that he was losing his grip and would eventually push away the one person he had
left to trust. More than anything, he wanted to feel like he could sit in his own living room, with
his back to the door, and believe he was truly home.
