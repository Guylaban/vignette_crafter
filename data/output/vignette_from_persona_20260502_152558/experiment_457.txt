Anja had always defined herself by her body’s strength. For fifteen years as a firefighter, it was
her most trusted tool—reliable, resilient, and under her command. That changed eight months ago with
the sepsis. It began as a simple infection that spiralled with shocking speed, landing her in the
ICU, intubated and immobilised. She remembers not a sequence of events, but a series of terrifying
snapshots: the relentless beeping of machines she couldn't see, the metallic glint of the IV stand
by her head, and the sharp, sterile smell that seemed to coat the inside of her throat. Most of all,
she remembers the feeling of absolute helplessness, of her body fighting a war without her consent,
leaving her a prisoner in her own skin.

Now, that feeling of helplessness ambushes her in daily life. The world has become a minefield of
reminders. At the fire station, the smell of antiseptic wipes used to clean equipment is enough to
make her heart hammer against her ribs and her muscles clench, as if bracing for an impact. This
sudden, overwhelming sense of danger can be triggered by the most innocuous things; seeing a needle
during a station blood drive or even a medical drama on television sends a jolt of raw adrenaline
through her system, leaving her body buzzing with an alarm that has no off-switch. She feels
constantly on edge, her posture rigid, scanning rooms not for fire hazards, but for anything that
looks vaguely medical.

The memories themselves are not stories she can tell. When they come, they arrive without words, as
pure, physical sensations. She will be sitting at her desk, and suddenly her back will arch
slightly, her breath catching in her chest, as if she were once again immobilised in that hospital
bed, the ventilator tube pressing down. The terror that accompanies these moments is identical to
what she felt in the ICU—absolute, profound, and certain of imminent death. This visceral
reenactment is so unbearable that she has developed powerful, automatic reflexes to shut it down.
The instant she feels the memory’s edge, she forces her mind elsewhere, reciting engine maintenance
protocols or mentally mapping out the streets in her district. She has learned to steer clear of any
situation, even happy ones, that might provoke a strong emotional response, fearing any intense
feeling could knock down the door she keeps so firmly bolted against the past.

Over the months, these protective strategies have become a prison. Her deliberate avoidance of
anything medical has paradoxically made her feel more vulnerable. By refusing her annual physical
and avoiding doctors, any minor bodily sensation—a cough, a headache, a muscle ache—is no longer
just a nuisance but a potential sign of catastrophic illness, feeding the constant tension in her
shoulders and gut. Her body is no longer a tool but a potential traitor, and her avoidance only
amplifies the sense that it is perpetually on the brink of failing her again.

This state of high alert has, in turn, made her exquisitely sensitive to the very things she tries
to avoid. Because she is constantly scanning her environment for threats, she notices every
ambulance siren, every hospital sign on the freeway, the faint smell of disinfectant in a public
restroom. Her hypervigilance ensures that the world is always presenting her with triggers, which
then sends another surge of panic through her, reinforcing the need to stay on guard in a cycle that
leaves her exhausted.

The more Anja pushes the memories away, the less they feel like the past. Her refusal to put words
to the experience, even to her husband, has kept the trauma frozen in time. It remains a chaotic
jumble of terrifying sensations, un-sequenced and unprocessed, which makes it feel like it could
happen again at any moment. This has carved a silence in her marriage and forced her to take a desk
job at the station, a daily humiliation that reminds her of the capable person she used to be.

It was the notice of a mandatory department-wide physical that finally brought her to therapy. The
deadline is in two months, and the thought of undergoing a medical examination feels as terrifying
as running into a burning building without her gear. She feels trapped between the certainty of
losing the job that has been her identity and the paralyzing fear of the procedure itself. Anja is
terrified that she is permanently broken, but a small part of her hopes she can one day feel safe in
her own body again.
