Dev, a 50-year-old animator, presented for treatment eighteen months after his daughter’s successful
battle with acute lymphoblastic leukemia. While his daughter, Priya, is now in remission and
thriving, Dev finds himself trapped in the sterile, terrifying world of the hospital where she was
treated. The trauma began not with the diagnosis, but with the first lumbar puncture. He remembers
the sharp, clean smell of antiseptic wipes as he and his wife, Anjali, held their six-year-old down.
He can still feel the ghost of her small body struggling against his, a memory of profound
helplessness that has become the bedrock of his distress. During the long months of treatment, he
would often sleep alone in a nearby hotel to allow Anjali to rest at Priya’s bedside. The
unfamiliar, silent rooms became echo chambers for his fear, a space where every possible
catastrophic outcome played out in his mind until dawn.

The relief he expected to feel when Priya’s treatment ended never arrived. Instead, a quiet,
vigilant anxiety settled in. Now, the triggers are everywhere. A routine physical for his work’s
insurance policy, for instance, is no longer a simple appointment. The moment the notification email
arrived, Dev was plunged into a detailed mental rehearsal of the worst-case scenario. He imagined
the doctor’s grave expression, the discovery of a hidden illness in his own body, and the subsequent
horror of leaving his daughter fatherless after everything she had endured. He spent two days caught
in this cycle of imagined disaster, a strategy he believed would prepare him for any potential
shock, but which left him exhausted and unable to focus on his animation deadlines. This pattern has
become his default response; any trigger connected to medical settings or being alone immediately
activates this frantic, internal preparation for the worst.

These strategies, meant to provide a sense of control, have instead sensitized him to the world,
making his triggers more potent and frequent. His constant rehearsal of disaster scenarios has made
him hypervigilant to his own body, so the thought of a doctor's office—and its pervasive antiseptic
smell—now feels physically unbearable, a place where his worst fears are most likely to be
confirmed. He also finds himself ruminating for hours, not about a person, but about the disease
itself, scouring medical journals online in a futile attempt to find a cause, an environmental
factor, something he can blame and mentally "get even with." This obsessive search keeps the
memories of the hospital raw and immediate, ensuring that the scent of hand sanitizer at the grocery
store is enough to bring back the cold dread of the oncology ward. This heightened sensitivity is
compounded by his constant need for reassurance, asking Anjali multiple times a day if Priya looks
pale or if he himself seems unwell, a behaviour that is visibly straining her own recovery.

The consequences of this self-reinforcing cycle are beginning to cascade. Anjali, who endured the
same trauma, is exhausted by having to constantly manage his anxiety. "She's healthy, Dev," she told
him last week, her voice gentle but frayed. "We have to live in the now." But he feels anchored to
the past. The most immediate crisis is professional. A major animation conference is approaching, a

daughter fought so hard to have back.
