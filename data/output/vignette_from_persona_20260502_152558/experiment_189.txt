Imani has lived for twenty years in the quiet foothills of the Blue Ridge Mountains, a place whose
dense forests and deep silences once reminded her of the peaceful parts of her childhood home. At
72, she still works as a forester, a role that has defined most of her adult life. It was in a
different forest, half a world away, that her life was irrevocably fractured. The trauma did not
come from the wildness of nature, but from the methodical violence of men. She was in her small
village home when the raid began. The explosion that tore apart the neighboring hut was not just a
sound but a physical force, throwing her from her bed to the dirt floor. The world went from silent,
sleeping darkness to a hell of fire and screams in an instant. In the chaotic, flickering light, she
saw them: armed men moving with a terrifying quietness between the burning structures. Their
suspicious, darting glances made it clear that every shadow, every villager, was a potential threat
to them. Imani lay frozen, the suffocating blackness of the night punctuated by the predatory
movements of those silent figures.

In the years after she came to the U.S. as a refugee, Imani believed the quiet solitude of her work
would be a balm. Instead, the forest became a place of echoes. Her attempts to manage the fear that
lived inside her began subtly. She started by leaving a television on through the night, the
meaningless chatter a barrier against the profound silence that reminded her of the moments before
the attack. Soon, this wasn't enough. She developed a ritual of checking the locks on her small
cabin, then the windows, then scanning the perimeter of her property from the porch, her eyes
tracing the dark edge of the tree line. This constant search for threat, meant to reassure her,
instead began to amplify her fear.

Her strategies for safety have ironically made her world more dangerous. The hypervigilance she
practices during her daily surveys in the forest has trained her senses to find menace everywhere.
She no longer sees a fellow hiker on a distant trail; she sees a person acting suspiciously, their
pace and posture immediately interpreted as a prelude to ambush. This intense focus on potential
human threats makes her acutely sensitive to the forest's natural sounds. The sharp crack of a
falling branch or a hunter's distant shot no longer registers as a normal part of her environment;
it is the explosion, the beginning of the attack, and her body reacts before her mind can catch up.
By constantly scanning her surroundings for signs of threat, she has primed her mind to find them,
turning the familiar woods into a landscape of triggers. Her vigilance ensures she is never at
peace, as every rustle in the undergrowth or unexpected silhouette confirms her deepest fear: that
the danger was never left behind.

The consequences have been profound. Her professional reputation for meticulous observation has
curdled into obsessive scanning that leaves her exhausted and unproductive. She avoids the more
remote parts of the national forest she is meant to steward, her world shrinking to a few familiar,
easily monitored trails. Colleagues have noted her jumpiness and her reluctance to work near dusk.
The solitude she once cherished now feels like a vulnerability. Waking up in the middle of the
night, as she often does, plunges her immediately into the suffocating, moonless dark of the past.
The familiar shapes of her own cabin become indistinguishable from the village huts, and she is
certain she can hear the soft footfalls of men outside her window.

It was after a particularly bad week, when the sound of a nearby quarry blast sent her scrambling
into a ditch on the side of a fire road, that Imani finally sought help. She had lain there for
nearly an hour, heart hammering, unable to convince herself it wasn't a mortar shell. The
realization that she was losing her grip not just on her peace, but on her reality, was terrifying.
She came to therapy not asking to forget the past, but with a simple, aching hope: to be able to
stand in the quiet of her own forest at nightfall and see only the stars.
