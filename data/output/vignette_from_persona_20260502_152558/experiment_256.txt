in a photograph he couldn't turn away from. The trouble began on a Tuesday night in March, walking
home from a late parent-teacher conference. Two figures emerged from the alley beside his apartment
building. One shouted at him to stop, the sound sharp and ugly in the quiet street. Before he could
process the demand, the other man grabbed him from behind, an arm hooking around his throat and
twisting his head to the side. The searing pain in his neck was immediate as he was forced to the
ground. He remembered the rough texture of the pavement against his cheek and the static-like image
of his wallet being pulled from his back pocket. The police who arrived later seemed procedural and
distant, their presence feeling more like an indictment than a comfort.

In the weeks that followed, the world became a minefield of reminders. Any sudden, loud noise—a car
backfiring, a student having a meltdown in his classroom—would instantly bring the memory flooding
back. It wasn't a story he recalled, but a series of high-definition snapshots: the glint of a
streetlamp on a belt buckle, the frayed cuff of a jacket, the specific pattern of cracks in the
pavement. Each time these images appeared, a hot wave of rage would wash over him, so intense it
made his hands shake. He felt a profound fury at his own helplessness, which was almost immediately
followed by a crushing sense of guilt. He believed he should have done something different—run
sooner, fought back, not taken that route home.

This self-blame began to curdle into a more permanent set of beliefs. Ethan became convinced that he
was fundamentally inadequate, that someone stronger or smarter would not have gotten into that
situation in the first place. This core belief that he had failed a basic test of survival meant he
could never let his guard down again. The world, once familiar, was now a place of constant,
imminent threat. He found himself unable to relax, even in his own apartment, and his body was
perpetually tense, as if bracing for another attack. At school, a place that had once been his
sanctuary, he developed a compulsive need to scan for all the exits the moment he entered any room,
from the staff lounge to the gymnasium. His heightened sense of threat also made him more sensitive
to the shouting that was an inevitable part of his workday.

The feedback loop was relentless and self-sustaining. His conviction that he was inadequate and had
to be on guard at all times (**Negative Appraisals → Threat**) fueled a pervasive sense of danger
that followed him everywhere. This feeling of imminent threat, in turn, made the photographic
memories of the assault more vivid and frequent (**Threat → Memory**). When the snapshots did
intrude—the shout, the grip, the pavement—they felt less like memories and more like they were
happening all over again, which only intensified his feeling of being unsafe in the present moment
(**Memory → Threat**).

This cycle tightened its grip over the months. The constant state of alert confirmed his belief that
he was right to be terrified, reinforcing his sense of inadequacy for not being able to simply "get
over it" (**Threat → Negative Appraisals**). His negative self-appraisals also coloured the memory
itself; when he recalled the event, his mind focused exclusively on his own perceived failures, the

memory, but for the photographs in his mind to finally lose their power, to become flat and distant
so he can feel safe in his own life again.
