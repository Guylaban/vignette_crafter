Elena was fifty-two when Hurricane Isabel tore through her coastal town, and five years later, the
storm still felt more present than the past. She remembers the water rising with unnatural speed, a
churning, grey mass that swallowed the street, then the yard, then the first floor of her home. The
memory itself feels disjointed, like a poorly edited film belonging to someone else. She can recall
the sight of her neighbour’s rooftop being submerged and the wrenching grief of knowing Isabel, the
woman who had lived there for thirty years, was gone, but she cannot connect these images to her own
life. The woman who clung to her own roof rafters for hours, nauseated by the smell of saltwater and
gasoline, feels like a stranger.

Her nights are the worst. Elena has come to dread the quiet of her apartment, where every creak of
the building or gust of wind sounds like the prelude to disaster. This constant state of high alert
means she rarely sleeps for more than an hour or two at a time. Lying awake in the dark, already
tense, the sound of a dripping tap can become the relentless advance of floodwater, triggering a
cascade of physical sensations. A wave of nausea, identical to the one she felt on the roof, will
wash over her, a potent reminder that brings the formless dread of the hurricane rushing back. This
feeling of imminent threat has shrunk her world to the four walls of her apartment, the only place
she feels a semblance of safety.

The triggers are everywhere. As a physical therapist, she used to find the hydrotherapy pool a place
of healing for her patients. Now, the sight of the large body of water makes her stomach clench.
When a patient recently mentioned their granddaughter, also named Isabel, Elena was seized by such
an intense, image-less wave of grief that she had to excuse herself from the room. These moments,
when the full emotional intensity of the trauma returns without a clear picture, are the most
terrifying. In response, she has learned to force her mind blank, to stare at a point on the wall
and deliberately try to leave her body until the feeling passes. It is this coping mechanism that
allows her to function, but it leaves her feeling hollowed out and disconnected. This deliberate
dissociation is one of several strategies she has developed; she also finds herself dwelling for
hours on the person she used to be—a woman who loved sailing, who wasn't afraid of the rain, who
could hear a name without flinching.

This constant retreat, both mental and physical, has created a powerful, self-sustaining cycle. By
dwelling on her past self, Elena inadvertently keeps the grief of her loss at the forefront of her
mind, ensuring the memory remains raw and painful. Her refusal to go out, born from a need for
safety, has made the outside world feel more menacing, sensitizing her to triggers she might
otherwise tolerate; the sight of a puddle in the clinic’s parking lot can now feel as threatening as
a rising tide. The memory itself seems to generate its own triggers; recalling the event brings back
the physical sickness, and that nausea then acts as a fresh catalyst for the memory. The more she
tries to escape the feeling through dissociation or avoidance, the more powerful the memory becomes
when it inevitably breaks through.

Elena sought therapy after her daughter, visiting from out of state, found her sitting in silence in
her darkened living room, unable to go to the grocery store because it had started to rain. Her
daughter’s alarm was a mirror to her own despair. Elena confessed that she felt trapped in a loop
she couldn't see a way out of. She no longer feels like herself, but like a ghost haunted by a
memory that isn't even hers. Her greatest hope is to one day feel the sun on her face without
scanning the sky for storm clouds, to hear the name Isabel and remember her friend’s smile instead
of the churning grey water.
