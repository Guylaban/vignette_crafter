Omar, a 56-year-old social media manager, described the last eight months as living in a city made
of glass, where every surface reflected a danger only he could see. The trauma occurred during a
downtown protest he had attended not as an activist, but as an observer, filming on his phone for a
personal blog. He remembered the festive, hopeful energy souring in an instant. The memory was never
a full narrative, but a series of violent sensory snapshots: the percussive pop of tear gas
canisters, the panicked surge of the crowd, and the searing, chemical burn as a police officer
sprayed him directly in the face. He recalled the acrid taste filling his mouth and throat just
before he fell, the weight of others trampling him, and a profound, gut-wrenching shame for
scrambling away, leaving his phone and his principles behind on the pavement.

In the weeks that followed, these fragments began to intrude on his life. The memories were potent
and physical; when they came, he could taste the pepper spray as vividly as if it were happening
again, a phantom bitterness that would send a jolt of raw terror through him. This sudden, intense
feeling of threat would leave him breathless and disoriented, his heart hammering against his ribs.
The rage that accompanied these moments was just as powerful, a white-hot fury at his own
helplessness that left him shaking. He began to feel that the world was not the predictable, mostly
safe place he had always believed it to be. Instead, he saw it now as a fundamentally dangerous
place, where violence could erupt without warning.

This new conviction that the world was a minefield began to dictate his every action. At his
marketing agency, a job that required him to be constantly engaged with online media, he found
himself unable to scroll through news feeds. He lived in perpetual fear of stumbling upon a video of
police or protests, so he avoided all media that wasn't pre-screened by a junior colleague. This
belief in pervasive danger also kept him in a state of exhausting hypervigilance. He felt he could
not relax, even at home with his wife, because he was certain that people were not what they seemed
and that threat could emerge from anywhere. He started to distrust the motives of colleagues, then
neighbours, seeing potential betrayal in every unexpected glance or change in tone.

His persistent state of high alert only reinforced his avoidance. Feeling constantly unsafe, he
began mapping escape routes wherever he went—the grocery store, the cinema, even his in-laws’ home.
In work meetings, he would manoeuvre to sit with his back to a wall, his eyes constantly scanning
the room and the hallway beyond. He had also begun to avoid any situation that might provoke strong
feelings, positive or negative. He turned down invitations to his nephew’s boisterous birthday party
and stopped listening to the classical music he once loved, all in an effort to maintain a flat,
numb emotional state he mistook for safety.

This constant effort to suppress and avoid had unforeseen consequences. By deliberately pushing the
memory of the protest away the moment it began to form, he ensured it never had the chance to be
fully processed. It remained a collection of terrifying, sensory-laden fragments, its power
undiminished. This avoidance also cemented his negative beliefs about the world. By shielding
himself from any news related to activism or law enforcement, he never encountered information that
might challenge his view that all such encounters were doomed to violent chaos, which only deepened
his conviction that others could not be trusted. The constant feeling of being on guard served as
daily proof for his conclusion that the world was a dangerous place.

Omar finally sought therapy after his manager confronted him about his declining performance and his
refusal to engage with current events, a core requirement of his job. His wife had also grown
distant, telling him she felt she was living with a ghost. He came to the first session looking
exhausted and defeated, explaining that what frightened him most was the belief that he had
irrevocably broken something within himself on that day—that he had acted in a way that went against
his own values by running, and that the safe, trusting person he used to be was gone for good.
