felt she was “wearing thin.” For decades, she had managed the echoes of her past, but now they were
the only sounds she could hear. The trauma occurred over forty years ago in her home country, a
place and time she rarely speaks of. During a period of intense civil conflict, soldiers had raided
her apartment building in the middle of the night. She remembers the splintering crash of a
neighbour’s door, followed by an explosion that threw her from her bed. Huddled in a closet, she had
watched through the slats as a young soldier, his face illuminated by the hallway light, stormed
past. She never forgot his eyes or the specific dark green of his uniform. She eventually immigrated
to the United States, building a career and raising a family, believing she had left that night
behind her.

In recent years, however, the past has become relentlessly present. Her life in a quiet American
suburb is now governed by the threat she escaped long ago. This cycle often begins on her commute to
the publishing house. The sight of a police officer on a street corner is enough to trigger it; the
uniform, though different, carries the same terrifying authority. Her heart begins to pound, and the
conviction that violence is imminent becomes absolute. She is no longer a respected editor on her
way to work, but a young woman hiding in the dark, certain she is about to be found. This spike in
her sense of threat is so powerful and immediate that she will often get off the bus a stop early or
duck into a shop, waiting until the perceived danger has passed, her mind racing with images of the
soldier’s face. The sudden bang from a construction site can send her reeling, the sound
indistinguishable in that first moment from the grenade that shook her building.

This pervasive sense of danger has, in turn, reshaped her perception of the world, making her
hypervigilant for the very cues that torment her. Because she is so certain that she is unsafe, her
mind is in a constant state of search. Walking through the city, she scans crowds, not for friends,
but for a ghost—the face of the soldier she saw for only a few seconds. Every face with a similar
structure seems to hold a flicker of his cold expression, and each potential sighting reinforces her
belief that the threat is not just a memory, but a present and active danger that has followed her
here. This feeling intensifies dramatically after dark. She cannot believe she is safe in her own
bed, a fact her ex-husband and grown children have tried to impress upon her for years. Their
reassurances feel hollow, naive. At night, she finds herself waking multiple times, her body rigid
with alertness, compelled to check the locks on her doors and windows. The ordinary night sounds of
her neighbourhood become signs of intrusion, and the darkness outside her window feels like a
hostile presence waiting to get in.

The reciprocal loop between these triggers and her sense of threat has now tightened into a
stranglehold. The more she scans her environment out of a feeling of constant threat, the more
likely she is to notice a uniform or a face that resembles the soldier's. Each trigger she
encounters then provides visceral, terrifying proof that her vigilance is not only necessary but
insufficient, which in turn drives her to be even more watchful. Her world has become small and
treacherous. She has declined invitations to social events she knows will have fireworks and has
started working from home more often to avoid the unpredictability of her commute. The final

building. The daily, percussive banging has kept her in a state of near-constant panic for three

could be fixed, but with the desperate, fading hope that she might one day feel safe enough to fall
asleep without first checking every lock twice.
