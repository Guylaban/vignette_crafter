Mateo had always lived in his body. For sixty-two years, it was his instrument, his voice, his home.
As a dancer and choreographer, he commanded it with a grace that defied his age. But four months
ago, on his way to a dress rehearsal, that home was violated. A truck ran a red light, and the world
dissolved into the deafening shriek of tires and the percussive, final crunch of metal. He remembers
the sound more than anything, a visceral auditory scar that has refused to fade. The details remain
a blur, a chaotic sequence he has never been able to arrange into a story. When he tries, the words
fail him, as if the memory exists only in sensation, not language.

In the weeks that followed, Mateo tried to force a return to normalcy, but his body, once a source
of pride, had become a source of terror. The world felt peppered with ambushes. A car backfiring on
the street would send a jolt of horror through him, instantly conjuring the sound of the crash. This
raw, physical sense of threat would drag the memory back with it, not as a cohesive narrative, but
as a sensory fragment—always that terrible sound, accompanied by a wave of shame so profound it felt
like a physical sickness. He felt ashamed of his helplessness, pinned in the driver's seat, a
passive victim. The shame was the worst part, and it was this feeling, more than the fear itself,
that drove him to desperate measures. He began trying to push the thoughts away, a constant,
exhausting effort to police the borders of his own mind. At night, he would stay up until three or
four in the morning, watching old movies until he was too tired to dream, terrified of what awaited
him in the dark.

These strategies became a damaging ritual. His attempts to suppress the memory only seemed to give
it more power; the auditory fragments would erupt without warning, louder and more startling than
before. When the memories did break through, he would deliberately try to leave his body, to blank
out and feel nothing, staring at a wall in his studio until the world went fuzzy. This deliberate
dissociation, meant to be an escape, only fractured the memory further, making it feel even more
alien and unspeakable. The failure of these efforts fed a growing belief that he was fundamentally
broken. Staying up all night left him frayed and jumpy, which he interpreted not as exhaustion, but
as proof of his flawed character. He had come to believe the accident happened because of who he
is—too proud, too focused, someone deserving of punishment.

existence, making him see danger everywhere. The belief that he is powerless to stop bad things from
happening has transformed his dance studio, once a sanctuary, into a space of constant threat-
monitoring. He finds himself unable to focus on his students, instead scanning the exits as soon as
he enters, plotting escape routes from a room he has known for twenty years. His body is coiled with
a tension that makes his art impossible. When a young dancer recently touched his arm to ask a
