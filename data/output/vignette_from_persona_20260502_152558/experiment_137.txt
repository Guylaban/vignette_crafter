Sarah had spent twenty years turning precious metals and raw stones into small, wearable pieces of
art. Her jewelry shop, with its velvet-lined trays and the gentle hum of the polishing wheel, was
more than a business; it was her sanctuary. That changed six months ago. The man came in just before
closing, lingering by the silver chains. She remembers the cloying sweetness of his cologne and the
glint of a cheap, oversized watch on his wrist. Then, there are gaps. The next thing she can recall
with any certainty is the cold, hard feel of the linoleum floor against her cheek and a profound,
terrifying silence. Some moments from that evening are seared into her mind with hyper-vivid
clarity, while others are a complete and terrifying blank, a void she finds herself instinctively
pulling back from whenever her mind drifts too close to its edge.

In the weeks that followed, her sanctuary became a cage. Every customer who walked through the door
felt like a potential threat. Sarah found herself constantly scanning for exits the moment she
entered any building, her body tensing if a stranger stood too close in the grocery line. She soon
realized the only place she felt even a sliver of safety was in her small apartment above the shop,
with the door double-bolted. The world outside had become a landscape of imminent danger. This
pervasive sense of threat seemed to prime her mind for the past; her hypervigilance would dredge up
sudden, unwanted pieces of the assault, like the image of his watch flashing under the shop lights,
which would appear without warning and then vanish.

These fragments of memory became potent triggers for her present-day terror. A glimpse of a similar
watch on a passerby’s wrist would instantly bring on a wave of debilitating nausea, the same
sickness she felt that night. That sudden, visceral feeling of being ill was enough to convince her
that she was in immediate danger, sending her heart racing and her eyes darting toward the door,
ready to flee. The constant state of high alert, in turn, made her more susceptible to these
triggers; her frayed nerves meant that even a customer speaking a little too loudly could now feel
like a direct physical threat, sending her retreating to the back room.

The triggers did more than just ignite fear; they reinforced a deep sense of shame. Hearing a story
on the news about someone losing a loved one would plunge her into a state of self-loathing, a
conviction that she was now fundamentally contaminated. This feeling of being dirty was often
accompanied by the thought that her reactions were proof she was a “lousy coper,” that a stronger
person would have moved on by now. This belief that she was both broken and incompetent only
amplified her sense of vulnerability. If she was this weak and damaged, she reasoned, then she was
utterly defenseless, which made the presence of any stranger feel exponentially more threatening.
The world felt dangerous precisely because she had come to believe she was no longer equipped to
exist in it.

Over the months, these cycles tightened their grip, choking the life out of her career and her
spirit. The nausea, the intrusive images, and the overwhelming fear became a constant hum beneath
the surface of her days. She started keeping the shop door locked during business hours, only
buzzing in customers she recognized, a practice that drove away new and old clients alike. The
vibrant joy she once found in her craft was replaced by a hollow, gray numbness. She felt, in her
own words, “dead inside,” an empty shell going through the motions in a world that had lost its
color.

What finally brought Sarah to therapy was seeing him. Last Tuesday, she was looking out her shop
window and saw the man who assaulted her walking casually on the other side of the street. The sight
of him triggered a complete and paralyzing freeze. She couldn't move, couldn't breathe, couldn't
think. It was the terrifying realization that she was living in a prison of her own making, while he
walked free, that broke through her numbness. She is here now, not with any clear sense of hope, but
with an urgent fear that if something doesn't change, the feeling of being contaminated and broken
will be permanent, and she will never again feel safe enough to create something beautiful.
