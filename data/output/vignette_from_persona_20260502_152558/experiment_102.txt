downtown office on a rainy Tuesday evening, she had been momentarily distracted by her phone’s
navigation while trying to find a faster route to meet her husband, Sameer. A young man, a
university student with dark, curly hair wearing a bright green raincoat, had stepped into the
crosswalk from between two parked cars. Priya remembers the screech of the tires on wet pavement and
the sickening thud of impact. The young man survived, but with severe injuries that permanently
altered the course of his life. For Priya, that single moment became the start of an unending
internal loop of horror and self-recrimination.

In the weeks following the accident, the world became a minefield of reminders. Her own workplace
was the most potent, as the building’s location was inextricably linked to the trauma. The sight of
any young man with a similar build or hairstyle to the victim could trigger a cascade of panic, a
jolt of ice water through her veins. In response to these frequent triggers, Priya began to engage
in a series of desperate mental and behavioural maneuvers to control her distress. She started by
refusing to drive, a decision that quickly expanded to being a passenger in any vehicle, forcing her
to rely on public transportation or her husband. At her desk, after a triggering encounter with a

wireframes but meticulously dissecting the moments before the accident. She would dwell on every
possible alternate path: if only she had left work five minutes earlier, if only she had taken the
main road, if only she hadn't glanced at her phone. She also tried to forcefully suppress the
intrusive images of the accident, believing that if she could just push them out of her mind, she
could regain some semblance of control.

These strategies, however, did not bring relief; instead, they tightened the trap. Her constant
