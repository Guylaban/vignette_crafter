agent. The memories do not play like a film; they surface as a series of stark, still photographs in
his mind: the face of another prisoner, eyes wide with terror; the glint of a boot on the concrete
floor; the rusted bars of the cell door. When these images arise, he is often overwhelmed by a wave
of pure, undifferentiated emotion so powerful he has to physically brace himself, shutting down the
memory before it can fully form.

The triggers are insidious and everywhere. Initially, he believed he could manage them, walling off
the experience. But the world kept echoing the cell. Walking through the crowded terminals of the
airports he once navigated with ease now provokes a visceral sense of danger. Surrounded by the
press of bodies, he feels a primal certainty that an attack is imminent, and he finds himself
scanning faces, watching hands, profiling strangers as he moves through the concourse, convinced he
can spot the threat before it materializes. This constant vigilance has bled into his home life; an
unusual silence in the house at night no longer feels peaceful but pregnant with menace, compelling
him to get up and check that every door and window is bolted, a ritual he might repeat three or four
times before dawn.

The sensory details of the trauma are the most potent ambush. He was recently preparing for a flight
when the scent of jet fuel mixed with the damp morning air, and in an instant, he was back in the
suffocating darkness of the cell, smelling the stale sweat and damp earth. The memory brought with
it not just a photograph but a physical sensation of dread, the same gut-wrenching panic that
something terrible was about to happen. This feeling of immediate threat, born from the memory, sent
him back through the cockpit pre-flight check three times, his hands shaking as he confirmed the
latch on the cockpit door was secure.

order. The intense threat perception, in turn, dredged up a memory: the photographic image of a
guard laughing while striking another prisoner. The memory itself then became a new trigger, making
him so exquisitely sensitive that the ordinary sound of a baby crying two rows back felt like a
physical assault, another harbinger of the suffering he had witnessed.
