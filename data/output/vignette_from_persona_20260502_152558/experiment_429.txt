Kaelen had always found the world a place of vibrant, chaotic beauty, something to be captured on
canvas. At 71, he still spent his days painting, his hands steady, his eye for color as keen as
ever. That all changed nine months ago. He was at an outdoor art fair, his own stall filled with
landscapes of the Pacific islands of his youth, when the first explosion tore through the cheerful
chatter of the crowd. He was knocked from his stool by the panicked surge of people, the smell of
cordite and fear instantly overwhelming the scent of oil paints and popcorn. He remembers the rough
scrape of the cobblestones against his cheek and the sight of an overturned fruit cart, its contents
staining the ground a terrible red. He lay there, curled into himself, for what felt like an
eternity, listening to the screams and the sirens that followed.

In the months since, the memory of that day has refused to fade. It is not a hazy recollection but a
series of vivid, high-definition snapshots that force their way into his mind without warning. The
memory is not just mental; it is physical. When an image of the chaos surfaces, Kaelen feels his
body involuntarily reenact the trauma, his shoulders hunching as if to protect his head, his muscles
tensing into the same tight crouch he held on the ground. These sensory-rich intrusions have
profoundly reshaped his understanding of the world. The memory of utter helplessness and the sight
of people running for their own lives has convinced him that he cannot rely on other people in a
crisis. More fundamentally, the raw violence of the memory has taught him that the world is a deeply
dangerous place, and that his own aging body is no defense against it; he feels he can no longer
rely on himself.

This new set of beliefs—that the world is hostile and he is defenseless—has dictated his every
action. The conviction that he is unsafe has led him to retreat from the very life that once
sustained him. He no longer goes out. The bustling streets he once loved to sketch now seem filled
with hidden threats. His downtown studio, once a sanctuary, sits unused. He has his groceries and
art supplies delivered, the brief interaction with the delivery person his only contact with the
outside world for days at a time. Internally, he works tirelessly to maintain a state of detached
calm, believing that any strong emotion is a sign of weakness, a crack in his fragile defenses. This
constant effort to control his emotions is exhausting. He also calls his niece, Leilani, three or
four times a day, asking the same questions: Had she heard any news? Was everything alright? He is
seeking a reassurance that he is okay, a confirmation of safety that evaporates almost as soon as
the call ends.

These strategies, meant to keep him safe, have only served to imprison him further. By staying
inside, he never has the chance to see that the world can be ordinary, even boring. His self-imposed
isolation gives the intrusive memories more space to bloom. The immense effort he pours into
suppressing his feelings often backfires, making the memories burst through with even greater force,
as if a dam has broken. This failure to control his mind only reinforces his belief that he can't
rely on himself, that he is fundamentally broken by what happened. Each time he calls Leilani for
reassurance, the fleeting relief is followed by a wave of shame at his own dependency, further
cementing the painful appraisal of his own inadequacy. His world has shrunk to the size of his
apartment, a feedback loop where the memories prove the world is dangerous, his avoidance confirms
this belief, and the resulting isolation makes the memories more potent.

It was Leilani who finally insisted he seek help. During their last call, her voice was strained
with a mixture of love and exhaustion. "Uncle," she had said gently, "you can't live like this. Your
paintings are gathering dust. You are gathering dust." Kaelen knows she is right. The canvases in
his apartment stand blank, and his once-vibrant palette of colors has been replaced by the gray
walls of his self-made prison. He has come to therapy not with a grand hope of reclaiming his old
life, but with a small, specific prayer: to one day feel safe enough to walk to the art supply
store, to feel the sun on his face, and to choose a new tube of cadmium yellow without his body
preparing for a disaster that has already passed.
