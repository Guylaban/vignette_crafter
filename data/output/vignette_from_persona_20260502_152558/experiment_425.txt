wife passed, his work as a nanny for a young family provided a gentle rhythm to his days, a way to
fill the quiet house with the sounds of life. That was before his youngest daughter, Linh, was
rushed to the hospital with a fever that spiraled into a terrifying, week-long battle in the
intensive care unit. Though Linh made a full recovery nearly a year ago, the man who sat in the
clinic waiting room was not the same one who had walked into that hospital. He was, in his own
words, someone he no longer recognized.

The trauma did not live in him as a coherent story. He found he could not tell a clear narrative of
what happened, not even to himself. Instead, it existed as a series of sensory ambushes, more vivid
and real than his memory of breakfast that morning. It was the high-pitched, rhythmic beep of the
machines, the acrid smell of antiseptic, and the sight of Linh’s small body overwhelmed by tubes and
wires. These were not memories of the past; when they came, it felt like it was happening right now,
the panic and helplessness as fresh and sharp as they were in that sterile, windowless room. The
sheer, agonizing vividness of these moments drove him to do anything to keep them at bay. At work,
if the toddler he cared for developed even a slight cough, Bao would immediately launch into a loud,
distracting game or turn on cartoons, his mind racing to build a wall against the intrusive images
he knew were waiting to flood in.

This constant, frantic effort to suppress his thoughts and distract himself only seemed to keep the
memories fragmented and potent. Because he never let himself assemble the pieces into a whole story
with a beginning, middle, and an end where his daughter survived, the fragments retained their full
power. His refusal to think about the trauma ensured it never became a part of his past, but
remained a perpetual, threatening present. This cycle of unprocessed memory reinforced his deepest
fears about himself. Each time a flashback broke through his defenses, it served as fresh evidence
that he had been permanently changed for the worse. The calm, capable father he had once been was
gone, replaced by this frightened stranger who couldn't even handle a common cold.

fundamentally broken, the idea of seeking medical or psychological help felt pointless. He had also
developed a profound distrust of others since the ordeal; he couldn’t shake the memory of the

were not what they seemed. This conviction made him avoid his own doctor and isolate himself from
friends who tried to ask how he was doing. Instead, his strategy was internal: to keep his mind
occupied at all times. The silence in his apartment after work was unbearable, a vacuum that would
immediately fill with the sounds of the ICU. He kept the television on from the moment he woke until
he fell into a fitful sleep, the noise a necessary shield.

reminder. He was brought to therapy not by his own choice, but by Linh’s gentle insistence. She had
