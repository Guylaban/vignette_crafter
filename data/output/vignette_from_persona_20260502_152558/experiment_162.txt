Eleanor, at seventy-six, possessed a mind once renowned for its meticulous precision. As a
radiologist, she had spent a lifetime finding clarity in shadows, tracing the subtle architecture of
the human body on film. Now, her own inner world was a place of impenetrable fog and sudden,
blinding flashes of terror. The change began eight months ago, following a trip to visit extended
family in a country that descended into violent, regional conflict while she was there. She and her
husband were caught in the city during a week of intense street fighting before their evacuation.
The event she cannot speak of, the one that lives not in words but in the rigid set of her jaw,
happened at a makeshift checkpoint. A young, nervous soldier, his face slick with sweat, had forced
her from their car, shouting in a language she didn't understand. He’d pushed her to her knees in
the dusty road, the rough fabric of his uniform scratching her arm, his rifle pressed against her
neck. The memory is not a story she can tell; it is a physical sensation of grit in her teeth and a
wave of shame so profound it feels like drowning. When these fragments surface, she feels an
intense, scalding humiliation for her own paralysis in that moment.

This wordless, shame-soaked memory dictates her present. Any unexpected, sharp noise—a car
backfiring, a dish clattering in the kitchen—can bring back the feeling of the dusty road, and with
it, a surge of absolute certainty that she is in mortal danger. This feeling transforms her quiet
suburban street into a landscape of imminent threat. A delivery driver approaching the door is not a
courier but an unknown man whose intentions are suspect. She finds herself constantly scanning her
surroundings, her eyes darting between windows, her body tensed for an attack that her rational mind
knows is not coming. The world outside her home has become a map of potential ambushes, so she has
stopped going to any unfamiliar places. The new cafe her husband suggests, the library exhibit her
friends are visiting—all are rejected. She cannot assess the danger, and the thought of being caught
off guard again is unbearable.

The persistent sense of threat has, in turn, reshaped her memory of the trauma. When she is already
on high alert, jumpy from a knock at the door, the fragmented images from the checkpoint become more
vivid and harder to push away. The feeling of danger acts like a magnet, pulling the worst moments
to the surface. This vicious cycle, where the memory fuels her fear and the fear brings back the
memory, has driven her into a state of managed retreat. To escape the overwhelming feelings of
terror and shame that ambush her, Eleanor has constructed a fortress of avoidance. She spends
upwards of six hours a day on her tablet, lost in the predictable patterns of sudoku and matching
games. This digital absorption is a shield, preventing any stray thought or feeling, good or bad,
from taking hold. She has become adept at avoiding not just the memory, but emotion itself.

Her strategies for survival have come at a cost, calcifying the trauma. By spending her days in a
state of digital distraction, she starves her mind of the processing time it needs. The memory,
never confronted, remains a raw, non-verbal horror, impossible to place into a narrative of the
past. Her husband’s gentle attempts to get her to talk about it are met with a frustrated shake of
her head; she literally cannot find the words, and the effort itself brings the shame rushing back,
which in turn sends her reaching for her tablet. This deliberate avoidance of feeling also
reinforces her perception of threat. By refusing to plan for the future—even for something as simple
as a weekend trip—she implicitly confirms her belief that the world is too dangerous to make
commitments in. Her life has shrunk to the size of her living room, a space she can control, and her
avoidance of the outside world means she never has the chance to learn that a stranger walking down
the street is just a person, not an impending disaster.

The loop is now almost seamless. A glimpse of a uniform on the news channels a feeling of threat,
which makes the memory of the young soldier's face intrude. The shame of that memory is so acute
that she immediately retreats into her phone for hours, avoiding her husband’s concerned gaze. This
escape prevents any processing of the event, leaving it as potent as ever, ready to be triggered
again. It was after she refused to attend her grandson’s college graduation, terrified by the
prospect of the unfamiliar campus and the crowds, that her husband finally insisted she seek help.
Eleanor agreed, not out of hope, but out of a deep fear that the sharp, analytical mind she once
took for granted was being erased, leaving only a terrified shell who could no longer distinguish
shadows on a film from the ghosts in her own head.
