Sarah, a 30-year-old bank teller, traces the beginning of her current struggles back to a home
invasion six months prior. The event itself is not a coherent narrative in her mind but a series of
searing sensory fragments. She remembers the unnatural quiet of the house just before, the feeling
of being watched from the darkened hallway, and the low, calm voice of the man who held her captive
for several hours. His anger was never loud; it was a controlled, terrifying force that preceded the
worst of the pain, and his constant, silent observation made her feel less like a person and more
like an object. The assault had a profound sexual component, twisting what should have been intimate
into an experience of pure terror.

In the weeks that followed, the memories began to intrude without warning. It was never just a
thought; when the memory came back, it felt completely real, a full sensory reliving of the event.
At her teller window, the sudden silence between customers would make her feel a suffocating sense
of danger, as if something terrible was about to happen, and the memory of the intruder’s quiet
watchfulness would flood her senses. This constant feeling of threat left her perpetually on edge;
the pneumatic tube’s hiss or a dropped coin would trigger a violent startle response, making her
jump and her heart hammer against her ribs. The feeling of being unsafe could itself bring the
memory back, trapping her in a loop where the fear and the memory fed one another.

This overwhelming cycle has reshaped her life. The memories are so emotionally consuming that her
primary impulse is to stop them, to do anything to escape. In the middle of a transaction, if the
memory returns, she will immediately drop her gaze, unable to make eye contact with the customer in
front of her. This avoidance, however, only makes her more hypervigilant and sensitive to being
watched, reinforcing the very trigger that brought the memory back. At night, she wakes frequently
to check the locks and windows, her body convinced she is not safe. The memories have also made her
exquisitely sensitive to her triggers; after a flashback, even a calm expression of frustration from
her husband feels like a precursor to violence, sending a jolt of terror through her.

To cope with the near-constant state of threat, Sarah has developed strategies that offer fleeting
relief but ultimately tighten the cycle. After a particularly difficult day of feeling watched and
jumpy at work, she spends hours and hundreds of dollars on compulsive online shopping, the brief
thrill of the purchase a welcome distraction. In the quiet of the evening, she finds herself going
over and over the details of the trauma, a desperate attempt to make sense of it, but this
overanalysis only serves to bring the sensory details back with greater intensity. These coping
mechanisms have become their own source of distress. The mounting credit card debt from her spending
is a new source of tension with her husband, and her avoidance of eye contact has been noticed by
her manager, who has commented on her seeming "disengaged" with customers.

The most painful consequence has been the erosion of her marriage. Intimacy has become impossible.
Any sexual situation is now a potent trigger, bringing back the full horror of the assault. Her
husband’s attempts to be close feel threatening, and she recoils, leaving him confused and hurt. It
was after she flinched away from his touch last week, seeing the deep pain in his eyes, that she
finally made an appointment. Sarah came to therapy because she feels she is losing everything that
once made her feel safe and loved. She is terrified that she is permanently broken, and her most
fervent hope is to one day be able to sit beside her husband without feeling like she is in danger.
