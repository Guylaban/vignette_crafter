protector in her community. At seventy-one, she was the veteran everyone looked up to, steady and
unflappable. That persona shattered six months ago, on a warm Saturday evening at a family barbeque
in her own backyard. Her grandson, celebrating a new job, had had too much to drink. Leilani smelled
the beer on his breath when he came to hug her goodbye and told him not to drive. They argued, his
youthful pride clashing with her authority, and he had stormed off to his car. She was still
standing on the porch, frozen between her roles as grandmother and officer, when she heard the
screech of tires and the sickening, final sound of impact just two blocks away.

The memory of that night never comes to her as a linear story. Instead, it lives in her as a
collection of sensory fragments. Her memories are overwhelmingly visual—brutal, high-definition
snapshots of the crumpled driver-side door, the shattered glass glittering on the asphalt under the
streetlights. These images are so clear and detailed that she can recall the specific pattern of the
crack in the windshield. Sometimes, when the house is quiet, the memory is auditory; she hears the
percussive crunch of metal, a sound that seems to vibrate deep in her chest, followed by her own raw
scream. Because these fragments are so vivid and intrusive, her mind has learned to treat the world
as a minefield of potential reminders. The sight or sound of anything that echoes that night has
become an immediate and potent trigger for her distress.

This hypervigilance has made her job, once a source of pride, a source of constant torment.

self-blame. As she looks at the damaged vehicles, the thought is immediate and absolute: *The event

fundamentally helpless, unable to stop bad things from happening to the people she loves most.
