Bao had spent forty years as a public defender, navigating the darkest corners of human behavior
with a steady hand and a belief in due process. But six months ago, the darkness had breached the
walls of his own home. It came in the form of a shattered door frame and a man whose face he can
only recall in disjointed, nightmarish flashes. He remembers the splintering crack of the wood—an
auditory fragment that now plays on a loop in the silence of his study—and the overwhelming
helplessness as he watched his wife, Lien, fall. The memory never comes as a whole story, only as
these cruel, disconnected pieces: the sound, a glint of metal, the scent of his wife’s perfume
suddenly vanishing. Whenever these fragments surface, they bring not just terror, but a tidal wave
of grief so profound it feels like he is drowning all over again.

This grief and the fragmented horror of the memory have become the engine of his daily life. The
recollection of the intruder's face, however fleeting, compels him to scan every person he passes on
the street, in the grocery store, and especially in the courthouse, searching for a similar set of
eyes, a similar threat. This constant vigilance is exhausting, turning his familiar world into a
landscape of potential assailants. The profound sorrow that accompanies the memories often drives
him to the kitchen late at night, where he eats standing at the counter, not for hunger, but for a
moment of distraction from the pain. He can no longer watch the news or his old crime dramas; any
depiction of violence feels like a direct, personal threat, forcing him to turn off the television
and sit in a silence that is soon filled by the sounds from his own memory.

His sense of pervasive threat is constant. Bao has come to believe that danger is not a possibility,
but an inevitability he failed to prevent once and will surely fail to prevent again. This feeling
often manifests as a jolt of pure panic, a false alarm where a car backfiring or a book falling off
a shelf sends him into a state of high alert, his heart hammering as if the home invasion is
happening in that very moment. In these moments of intense fear, the fragmented memories are often
triggered, pulling him back into the worst night of his life. The auditory recall of the door
breaking is most common, a sound that seems to echo in the house and confirms his certainty that he
is unsafe. The world feels fundamentally dangerous, and he is awash in negative emotions—not just
fear, but a deep, abiding shame for having been unable to protect Lien.

This ever-present sense of threat dictates his actions, shrinking his life into a series of rigid,
protective rituals. His work, once a source of pride, is now a source of constant anxiety. The
courthouse, filled with strangers, has become a place of intense hypervigilance. He finds himself
watching the gallery instead of the witness, his focus fractured. At home, he repeatedly checks that
the doors and windows are locked, sometimes getting out of bed three or four times to rattle the
deadbolt he had installed, each check a desperate attempt to quiet the fear. But these very actions
only seem to amplify his anxiety. The cold metal of the lock under his hand reinforces the belief
that an imminent threat lies just outside, and the act of checking itself often triggers the memory
of the original door splintering. In this way, his efforts to feel safe only serve to remind him of
how unsafe he truly is, tightening the loop of fear and memory until it feels impossible to breathe.

It was a mistake at work that finally brought him to seek help. Lost in scanning the courtroom for
threats, he missed a key piece of prosecution testimony, a detail his paralegal had to point out to
him later. The realization that his vigilance was not only failing to make him safer but was
actively compromising the work he had dedicated his life to was a profound blow. Bao arrived at his
first session looking exhausted, admitting quietly that he no longer feels in control. He is
terrified the memories will never stop, but his deepest fear is that they are a warning—that he is
doomed to fail again, and that the quiet, empty house he can no longer bear is the price of that
failure.
