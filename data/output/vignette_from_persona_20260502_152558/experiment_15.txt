unsafe. The primary driver of his distress, the engine of his fear, is the way ordinary moments now

leaving him perpetually on edge.

This transformation of neutral cues into imminent threats is most acute at his job, the very place

seemed to morph into the assailant’s, and a wave of terror washed over him. He was certain he was

confused and concerned. Any form of criticism, no matter how small or gently delivered, now triggers

minute late, he doesn’t feel impatient; he feels a rising panic, certain that the delay means
something terrible has happened up the road and that he is trapped, exposed. The approach of
midnight, the time the robbery occurred, brings a palpable sense of dread each day. As the clock
ticks closer, he feels the walls of his apartment closing in, the only place he feels any semblance
of safety. He finishes his shift and rushes home, locking and bolting the door behind him, his work
uniform still on. He has stopped answering calls from his family and the few friends he has left. He
came to therapy because his manager told him he needed to get help or risk losing his job. More than

belief that the moment he lets his guard down, the violence will happen all over again.
