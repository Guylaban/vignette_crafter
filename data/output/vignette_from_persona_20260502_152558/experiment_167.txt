Lien, an eighty-year-old religious educator at her community’s Buddhist temple, has carried the
silence of her trauma for over six decades. The event itself lives in her not as a coherent
narrative, but as a collection of sensory shards, disconnected and overwhelmingly vivid. She
remembers the smell of damp earth as she hid, the sudden, searing heat as the thatch of her family’s
home caught fire, and the rough grip of a hand that shoved her to the ground in the chaos. Vast
stretches of that night are simply gone, a black void her mind refuses to enter. What remains of the
memory feels profoundly alien, as if she is watching a film about a stranger’s suffering. Yet
paradoxically, these fragments feel more real to her than the memory of her own wedding day; the
orange glow of the flames is more present than the face of her husband sitting across the breakfast
table.

This fractured, intrusive quality of the memory has made her world a landscape of potential threats.
The scent of turned soil from the temple’s community garden can send a jolt of panic through her,
and the sight of a candle flame during a ceremony feels like a physical blow. The memory of being
violently shoved has made any unexpected touch intolerable; when a student brushes past her in the
hallway, her entire body seizes up. These triggers immediately activate a series of desperate, well-
worn strategies for control. A whiff of damp earth from an open window will send her on a circuit of
the house, checking and re-checking every lock, a ritual she performs dozens of times a day. The
constant threat of the memory breaking through has led her to fill every waking moment with
activity. She keeps her mind occupied at all times, a fortress whose walls are built of prayer
lists, lesson plans, and the endless, silent recitation of sutras.

Her interpretation of the event has become the engine of her suffering. She has come to believe that
there is something intrinsically wrong with her, a fundamental weakness that made the event happen.
This deep sense of personal fault taints the memory itself, preventing it from ever being integrated
as something that happened *to* her rather than something that *is* her. This core belief of being
inherently damaged also fuels her avoidance. The certainty that she is flawed makes her feel
perpetually unsafe, which in turn drives the compulsive need to check the locks and maintain a state
of hypervigilance. She has not made a new friend in decades, convinced that anyone who got too close
would eventually discover the permanent humiliation she carries inside her. She feels her body,
which froze in terror that night, is unreliable and can no longer be trusted to keep her safe.

These behaviours, meant to protect her, have instead become a cage, tightening around her over the
years. The constant mental distraction ensures the memory remains unprocessed, preserving its
fragmented and alien quality. Each time she avoids a new social connection at the temple, it
reinforces her belief that she is uniquely broken and must be kept separate. This relentless cycle
of avoidance and mental escape has, over time, made her even more sensitive to her triggers; by
treating the world as a source of constant danger, her nervous system has learned to agree. A
fragment of memory—the heat on her skin—will surface, and in response, she will retreat into her
rituals of checking and distraction, which only serves to keep the memory powerful and unprocessed.
Her strategies have deepened her conviction that she is a humiliated, broken person, and this belief
makes the memories even more toxic when they do intrude.

It was an embrace from her seven-year-old grandson that finally brought her to therapy. He had run
up behind her and thrown his arms around her waist in a surprise hug. Lien had screamed and
recoiled, a reaction of pure terror that left the boy in tears and Lien shaking with a shame so
profound it felt like a physical illness. The sight of his frightened face made her realize that the
fortress she had built to contain her past was now walling her off from her own family. She came to
her first session asking not for a cure, but for a way to "be a proper grandmother," terrified that
the poison from that long-ago night would continue to seep out and harm the people she loves most.
