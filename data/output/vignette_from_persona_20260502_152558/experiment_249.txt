Anong presented as a woman hollowed out by exhaustion, her small frame tense and her eyes constantly
scanning the room. At fifty-two, she had worked at the same fast-food restaurant for over a decade,
a place of familiar routines and noises that had, until six months ago, felt like a second home. The
flash flood that tore through her neighborhood changed everything. It came in the middle of the
night, a sudden roar that she first mistook for a truck on the highway. The memory of it never
arrived as a clear story, but as a series of violent, disconnected snapshots: the shriek of twisting
metal as the retaining wall gave way, the sight of her husband’s face illuminated by a flickering
flashlight just before the power died, the sensation of icy water rising around her ankles in the
pitch-black living room. When these fragments force their way into her mind, the raw terror is so
immense that she feels a physical need to shut down, to stop the memory before it can form a
complete picture.

In the months since, Anong has come to believe that the world is fundamentally unsafe and that she
is powerless within it. This deep-seated conviction that she can't stop bad things from happening
has become the lens through which she views everything. Because she feels utterly helpless, every
unexpected sound is a potential catastrophe. At work, the sudden clatter of a dropped tray or the
deep rumble of a delivery truck backing up to the loading dock sends a jolt of panic through her,
her heart pounding as if the roar of the floodwaters has returned. This sense of pervasive threat is
most acute after sunset; she finds it almost impossible to feel safe at night, lying awake for
hours, listening to the house settle, certain that disaster is about to strike again.

Her intrusive memories feed this constant state of alert. A sudden downpour during her commute can
trigger a vivid fragment of water bursting through the window frame, and for a moment, she is no
longer in her car but back in the dark, suffocating house. The overwhelming fear from the memory
bleeds into the present, leaving her trembling and hyper-aware of every sound for hours afterward.
This cycle also works in reverse. Her baseline of high alert makes her exquisitely sensitive to
triggers, ensuring the memories are never far away. The shrill beep of the fry timer at work, a
sound she has heard thousands of times, can now be enough to plunge her back into the chaos, the
high-pitched alarm mimicking the sound of the smoke detector shorting out in the flood. The long,
quiet hours she spends unable to sleep at night become a fertile ground for these unwelcome images
to surface, replaying the worst moments in an endless, fragmented loop.

This vicious cycle has cemented a deeper, more painful set of beliefs about herself. Each time a
memory overwhelms her with raw, unprocessed terror, it serves as proof for her deepest fear: that
she will never be able to feel normal emotions like joy or peace again. She interprets the emotional
intensity of her trauma recall not as a symptom, but as her new, permanent reality. This belief
makes the memories themselves even more terrifying, something to be fought off at all costs, which
only ensures they remain fragmented and potent. The experience has left her feeling profoundly
alienated, as if she is living behind a wall of glass, watching the rest of the world go on. She
feels she no longer belongs anywhere—not at work, where she startles at every noise, and not even at
home with her husband, whose attempts to comfort her feel like they are coming from a great
distance.

The cumulative effect has been devastating. Sleep-deprived and perpetually on edge, she has made
several mistakes at her job, earning a gentle warning from her manager. At home, she has withdrawn
from her husband, unable to explain the chasm that has opened between them. It was after he found
her sitting awake in the dark living room for the third night in a row, simply staring at the
window, that he insisted she seek help. Anong agreed, not with any real belief that she could be
fixed, but with the quiet, desperate hope that someone might help her find a way to simply survive
the night.
