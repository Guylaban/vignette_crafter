graphic designer, finding a quiet satisfaction in the clean lines and ordered logic of her craft.
That sense of order was shattered six months ago, on the night the creek swelled into a raging
river. They were having a glass of wine, listening to the storm, when the water came. It wasn’t a
leak; it was an invasion. The first sign was the groan of the French doors in the living room, and
then the explosive slam as the frame gave way and a wall of black water tore through their home. The
smell of mud and pine was instantly overwhelmed by the sharp scent of the whiskey bottle that
shattered off the sideboard. In the chaos, she lost sight of Robert, a terror so absolute it
eclipsed everything else.

In the weeks that followed, staying in a small, borrowed apartment, the memory of that night was a
confusing fog. Sometimes, Eleanor could recount the events with a chilling detachment, as if
describing a film she’d seen long ago, feeling utterly numb to the horror of it. At other times, the
memory was so extreme, so outside the bounds of a normal life, that it felt fundamentally unreal, a
story too dramatic to have possibly happened to her. This sense of disbelief was a fragile shield.
Her conviction that the world was a safe, predictable place had been destroyed. She now saw it as it
truly was: a place of random, brutal danger where everything could be lost in an instant. This
belief that the world was fundamentally unsafe made the memory feel less like a past event and more
like an ongoing revelation; it wasn't over, it was simply waiting to happen again.

This new understanding of the world governs Eleanor’s days. Any sudden noise, especially the sound
of a door opening or closing, is no longer a neutral event. It is a signal of immediate threat, a
direct echo of the moment the flood breached their home. Hearing a door slam in the hallway of their
new apartment complex sends a jolt through her, instantly confirming her belief that safety is an
illusion. Talk of loss on the television news, even stories of strangers in distant places, feels
personal, a reminder of the universe’s deep and abiding injustice. These triggers don't just
frighten her; they validate her worldview, proving that her vigilance is not paranoia but simple,
painful clarity.

Firm in her belief that chaos is always imminent, Eleanor has developed routines to keep it at bay.
She spends hours dwelling on how the flood could have been prevented, replaying the weeks leading up
to it, searching for a decision she could have made differently that would have saved their home.
This rumination feels like a duty, a way to mentally correct the profound unfairness of what
happened. Her primary battle, however, is fought with bleach and cleaning cloths. She cleans their
small apartment compulsively, organizing and reorganizing drawers, scrubbing surfaces until her
hands are raw. It is a desperate attempt to impose a small pocket of order on a world she knows to
be dangerously out of control.

Her attempts to manage her internal world are just as relentless. When the memory of the flood
surges up—the roar of the water, the splintering wood—it feels so intensely present that she has to
physically brace herself. To escape the feeling that it is happening right now, she immediately
throws herself into a task, trying to actively erase the images from her mind by focusing on the
precise alignment of books on a shelf or the meticulous cleaning of the grout in the bathroom. But
these strategies only seem to strengthen the memory’s hold. The effort to suppress it makes it feel
fragmented and volatile, liable to burst forth without warning, while the constant rumination keeps
the details sharp and immediate. This, in turn, has made her even more sensitive to her
surroundings; the constant internal rehearsal of the trauma primes her to react to the slightest
sound, making the slam of a cupboard door feel as threatening as the French doors giving way. Her
world has shrunk to the size of their apartment, a space she endlessly tries to control while
feeling besieged from the outside.

It was Robert who finally convinced her to seek help. He found her one afternoon in her new, smaller
studio, not working on a design project but wiping down her already immaculate computer monitor for
the third time that hour, her body rigid with tension. She had stopped taking on new clients, unable
to focus. He had gently put his hand on her shoulder and told her, "I'm still here, El. But I feel
like I lost you in the water, too." The truth of his words broke through her rigid defenses. Eleanor
is terrified that this state of high alert is permanent, that the feeling of safety is a memory from
a life that no longer belongs to her. More than anything, she hopes to one day sit in a room with
her husband and hear a door close without waiting for the world to come crashing in.
