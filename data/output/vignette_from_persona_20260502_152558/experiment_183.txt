Sarah had always seen her work in social ministry as a calling, a way to be present for people in
the chaos of their lives. Now, chaos was the one thing she could not bear. Six months ago, on a
rainy Tuesday afternoon driving back from a home visit, a truck ran a red light and T-boned her
sedan. The moments that followed were a blur of shattering glass, the shriek of metal, and a sudden,
crushing pressure. She remembers the acrid smell of the airbag and the terrifying inability to move
her legs, pinned as she was against the dashboard. In those minutes before the paramedics arrived, a
profound sense of helplessness washed over her; her body was not her own, and there was nothing she
could do but wait.

In the weeks that followed, this sense of helplessness metastasized into a set of rigid beliefs. She
was certain that she could not stop bad things from happening to her. What had occurred was deeply
unfair—the other driver was texting, a fact that seemed to get lost in the insurance paperwork—and
nothing had been done to right the wrong. This conviction that the world was unjust and she was
powerless within it began to poison her perception of safety. Even when friends assured her she was
fine, she found it impossible to believe them. This core belief that she was fundamentally unsafe
drove a constant, exhausting vigilance. In any new space—a church meeting room, the grocery
store—her eyes would immediately dart around, cataloging the exits before she could even sit down.

This pervasive sense of threat is now ignited by the slightest provocation. The screech of tires on
a TV show or the sight of mangled cars on the evening news sends a jolt of terror through her. Even
feeling physically unwell—a headache, a touch of nausea—becomes a trigger, instantly transporting
her back to the feeling of her broken body trapped in the car. Her belief that her body is
unreliable makes her exquisitely sensitive to these internal states, turning any physical discomfort
into a harbinger of disaster. In response to these triggers, her world has contracted dramatically.
The primary casualty has been her work. The thought of getting behind the wheel of a car, or even
being a passenger, is intolerable. This avoidance of driving means she has had to transfer all her
clients who require home visits, a source of deep shame and a confirmation of her own helplessness.

Her strategies for safety have become a prison. The constant feeling of being under threat compels
her to avoid any situation that might escalate it, which for her, now includes almost everything.
She avoids driving, situations that put her body under any stress like carrying heavy donation boxes
at the ministry, and even tries to avoid relaxing, as letting her guard down feels like an
invitation for disaster. These avoidance behaviours, born from her negative appraisals of the world
and herself, only serve to amplify her fear. By never driving, she never has the chance to learn
that she can be safe in a car, so the imagery of a crash remains a potent trigger. Her avoidance of
physical exertion has made her so de-conditioned that normal bodily sensations now feel alien and
threatening, reinforcing her belief that her body can't be trusted.

The cycle has become ruthlessly efficient. A trigger, like feeling a cold coming on, confirms her
appraisal that her body is unreliable. This appraisal heightens her sense of ambient threat, making
her jump at the sound of the office phone ringing. This state of high alert makes avoidance feel not
like a choice, but a necessity. She calls in sick, avoiding the stress of work and the perceived
danger of her own body, which in turn sensitizes her further to the next trigger. Her life is now
circumscribed by these efforts to feel safe, efforts that only reinforce her conviction that she is
in constant danger. It was after she had to turn down a promotion because it would require travel
between different ministry sites that Sarah finally made an appointment. She feels trapped not by
wreckage of metal, but by the architecture of her own fear, and she is desperate, for the first time
in a long time, to find an exit.
