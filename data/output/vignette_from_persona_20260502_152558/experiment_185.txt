Elena has worked at the same downtown bank for fifteen years, a quiet and watchful presence behind
the teller’s window. At fifty-five, she moves with a careful economy of motion, her gaze constantly
drifting from the customer in front of her to the doors, the street outside, and the faces of those
waiting in line. She sought therapy after an incident last month. A courier dropped a heavy box of
coins, and the sharp, echoing bang sent her diving behind the counter. The subsequent warning from
her manager was gentle but firm, and it was the final confirmation of a fear she had long held: the
world she had built to keep herself safe was beginning to crumble.

The event that reshaped her life happened over thirty years ago, when she was a young woman in her
home country, then in the grip of a brutal civil war. She was in a bustling city market, her hand
resting on the coarse, scratchy burlap of a sack of lentils, when the car bomb detonated. The memory
is not a story she can tell. It exists in her mind as a chaotic collage of sensory fragments: the
world-ending roar, the sudden pressure in her ears, the rain of dust and debris, and the specific,
rough texture of the burlap under her fingertips just before the world dissolved into noise and
heat. She remembers the silence that followed the blast, then the screaming. When she tries to
explain what happened, the sequence shatters; she loses the thread, unable to connect the moments,
leaving her with a feeling that it was all so extreme, so impossible, that it couldn't have actually
happened to her.

This fractured, unreal quality of the memory is precisely what makes it so potent. Because it lacks
a clear beginning or end, it feels less like a past event and more like a permanent, ongoing threat
that could reignite at any moment. This conviction turns her teller’s station into a guard post. The
sudden thud of a rubber stamp or a car backfiring on the street outside is never just a noise; it is
a direct trigger that sends a jolt of adrenaline through her body. Her heart begins to pound, a
familiar and exhausting physical reaction that immediately launches her into a state of high alert.
In those moments, the sound brings with it a sensory echo of the market—the smell of dust, the
feeling of grit in her mouth—and her focus snaps from her work to survival. She begins scanning the
lobby, studying the body language and faces of every person, searching for the slightest sign of
hostile intent.

This constant state of threat assessment has, over decades, cemented a set of core beliefs. Living
with her nervous system perpetually braced for impact has convinced Elena that the world is
fundamentally a dangerous place. Her hypervigilance is not a choice but a duty she must perform to
stay alive. This exhausting work of scanning her environment also makes her more likely to notice
potential triggers; her attention is immediately snagged by the texture of a canvas tote bag or a
conversation about loss overheard in the queue. When a customer tearfully mentions a recent funeral,
the words bypass sympathy and land as a cold, hard confirmation of her own internal emptiness, a
feeling that nothing in her life holds any true meaning anymore.

The cycles feed on each other, tightening their grip over time. The intrusive memory fragments fuel
her sense of current danger, and that sense of danger reinforces her belief that she must always be
on guard. After a night of particularly vivid, disjointed nightmares, the memory leaves her so raw
that the next day at work, every slamming door feels like a direct assault, making her even more
likely to be startled. This feedback loop has cost her dearly. Her marriage ended years ago, worn
down by a tension her ex-husband could no longer bear. She has slowly withdrawn from friendships,
unable to relax or feel present.

The warning at work was not just about her job; it was a sign that her defenses were no longer
containing the damage. Elena came to therapy because she is terrified of what will happen when they
fail completely. She doesn't speak of recovery, but of a more modest hope: to one day feel the sun
on her face without scanning the rooftops for a threat, and to believe, even for a moment, that she
is truly safe.
