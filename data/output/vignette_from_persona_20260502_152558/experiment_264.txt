theatre. For fifteen years as a surgeon, his world was one of control, of problems that could be cut
away, of bodies that could, with skill and focus, be repaired. That sense of order was incinerated
over the course of a single weekend six months ago. He and his wife had been staying in a remote
cabin when the wildfire came. It moved with an unnatural speed, a roaring beast that consumed the
forest. What he remembers most are the sounds of trees cracking like gunshots and the thick, acrid
smell of burning pine that clung to everything, a smell that felt like the world itself was being
rendered down to ash.

In the weeks that followed, the fire came home with him. The city was full of traps. A car
backfiring on the street outside his home would instantly plunge him back into the chaos, not as a
full narrative, but as a sensory fragment: the percussive blast of a propane tank exploding at a
nearby property. These moments, which could be triggered by any loud, sudden noise, were not just
frightening; they brought with them a profound and overwhelming grief for a world that had felt safe
just moments before the fire. He felt as though he was only remembering parts of what happened, with
the rest of it lost in a fog of smoke and panic, a sensation that was deeply unsettling for a man
who prided himself on memory and meticulousness.

The memories themselves became a source of threat. After a flashback, often arriving without warning
while he was driving or trying to read, he would be left raw and hyper-alert for hours. The
lingering image of the orange sky would prime his senses, and he would find himself subtly sniffing
the air for the slightest hint of smoke, his ears straining to catch any sharp noise that might
signal a repeat of the danger. This vigilance made it almost inevitable that he would encounter
another trigger, locking him into a cycle where the memory of the threat and the cues for that
threat began to feed one another, making his world feel progressively smaller and more dangerous.

This escalating sense of siege began to bleed into his work. During a routine cholecystectomy, the
familiar smell of the cauterizing pen—a sharp, burnt scent he had registered thousands of times
without a thought—suddenly became the smell of the fire. A wave of panic washed over him. Though his
hands remained steady, his focus fractured. He finished the procedure on pure muscle memory, his
mind a world away. That evening, he went straight for the whiskey, pouring a glass to blunt the
sharp edges of his anxiety. He then spent hours scrolling through his phone, a frantic attempt to
distract himself from the echoes of the day. This pattern became his default response; whenever the
world ambushed him with a trigger, he would retreat into alcohol or a flurry of mindless activity to
push the experience away.

alcohol to escape, the more he reinforced the feeling that the memory was an incomplete picture, a
series of terrifying snapshots he couldn’t assemble into a coherent story. His wife noticed his
withdrawal, the way he would sometimes initiate sex with a desperate, detached urgency, using the
physical sensation as another form of release from the tension that was constantly thrumming beneath
his skin. The intimacy they had shared for over a decade began to feel like another casualty of the
fire, leaving him more isolated with the fragments of his memory.
