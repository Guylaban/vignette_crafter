Elena, a 59-year-old physical therapist, came to therapy describing a lifetime of anxiety that had
recently become unbearable. For decades, she had managed what she called her "nervous disposition,"
but now found herself exhausted by the constant vigilance required to get through a day. She spoke
of her childhood in a hushed, detached tone, as if recounting a story she’d read about someone else.
The memories of her stepfather’s abuse were not a coherent narrative but a collection of sensory
shards: the scent of stale cigarette smoke, the creak of a specific floorboard outside her bedroom
door, the particular shade of yellow on the kitchen wallpaper. She often felt the story was too
extreme to have been real, a lurid fiction her mind had invented, yet the fragments would surface
without warning, sharp and disruptive.

These sudden pieces of memory were the starting point for the cycles that dominated her life. At her
clinic, a male patient’s heavy footfall in the hallway could trigger an image of that yellow
wallpaper, and in an instant, the sterile treatment room would feel charged with danger. This surge
of threat was immediate and overwhelming; she would feel a primal urge to position herself in a
corner, her back pressed firmly against the wall so she could see every entrance and exit. She began
to see potential threats in every stranger who approached her, her body tensing as if bracing for an
impact that never came. The fear was so potent that it would often bring back other fragments of the
past, the feeling of being trapped in her office conjuring the memory of being cornered in her
childhood kitchen.

The distress from these moments of intense threat would follow her home, a humming, frantic energy
that demanded an outlet. To quell the panic, Elena would turn to a rigid set of rituals. Some
evenings, she would eat until she felt a dull, leaden numbness, the physical fullness a temporary
distraction from the emotional void. More often, she would begin to clean. She would scrub floors
and organize cupboards with a frantic, compulsive energy, believing that if she could just impose
perfect order on her physical environment, she could regain a sense of control over the chaos she
felt inside. This need for safety extended into the night, where she would wake multiple times to
check the locks on her doors and windows, her sleep a shallow and interrupted affair.

Her strategies, designed to keep her safe, had become a cage, reinforcing the very memories she was
trying to escape. When a memory fragment would intrude, her immediate response—to eat, to clean, to
organize—served to numb the associated emotion and push the thought away. This constant suppression
prevented the memories from ever being fully processed. By never allowing herself to sit with the
feelings, she ensured the memories remained disjointed and alien, perpetually feeling as if they had
happened to a stranger. This, in turn, made them more frightening when they did break through, their
raw, unprocessed nature fueling the sense of imminent threat that kept her perpetually on guard. Her
world had gradually shrunk; she avoided any potentially stressful social situation, from
neighbourhood gatherings to professional development conferences, which had left her feeling
profoundly isolated.

It was a quiet comment from her grown daughter that finally prompted her to seek help. "Mom," she
had said gently, "your house is beautiful, but it feels like no one is allowed to live in it. You
never just sit." The observation landed with painful accuracy. Elena realized she had spent nearly
sixty years with her back to the wall, both literally and figuratively. She is terrified of
confronting the memories, convinced that their full force would destroy the fragile order she has
built. Yet, the exhaustion of maintaining that order has become its own kind of destruction. Her
greatest hope, she admitted tearfully in her first session, is to one day be able to sit on her own
couch, in the middle of the room, and simply feel at peace.
