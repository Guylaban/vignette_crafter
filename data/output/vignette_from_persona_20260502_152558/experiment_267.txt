Rohan, a twenty-year-old writer with a quiet intensity, traces his current state of paralysis back
to a Tuesday afternoon six months ago. He was at a workshop, deeply engrossed in editing a short
story, when he realized he was late to pick up his younger sister, Priya. He remembers the flicker
of annoyance, the quick text message telling her to get a ride with her friend Sarah. He remembers
the relief of not having to rush. That relief is now the memory that haunts him most, because an
hour later, his phone rang with his mother’s frantic voice. There had been an accident. By the time
he reached the hospital, Priya was gone. The world, as he knew it, ended in the sterile quiet of a
waiting room, under the hum of fluorescent lights.

In the months that followed, the initial shock curdled into a set of rigid, terrifying beliefs.
Rohan became convinced that the world was not just indifferent, but actively malevolent and
unpredictable. His prior assumptions about fairness and safety felt like a cruel joke; his beliefs
about how life was supposed to work had been utterly shattered. This pervasive sense of a dangerous
world meant he could never truly be at ease. Even in his own apartment, a space once dedicated to
creative focus, he remains in a constant state of high alert, unable to relax. He spends his nights
staring at the ceiling, his mind racing, and the slightest noise from the street—a car door
slamming, a distant siren—causes him to jolt upright, his heart hammering against his ribs as if
expecting another catastrophic phone call.

This profound conviction that disaster is always imminent has reshaped his life. He has come to
believe that any happiness is fleeting and that making plans is simply an invitation for fate to
intervene. As a result, he systematically avoids committing to anything in the future. Invitations
from friends to concerts or weekend trips are met with vague excuses. The idea of buying a ticket
for an event weeks away feels reckless, like tempting the same cruel randomness that took his
sister. To manage the unceasing anxiety, he calls his parents multiple times a day, the
conversations circling the same desperate script: "Are you okay? Is everything alright?" He is
seeking reassurance not just for their safety, but for his own precarious sense of stability,
needing someone else to confirm, just for a moment, that the world is holding together.

His avoidance, meant to protect him, has instead become a cage, reinforcing his darkest conclusions
about himself and the world. By turning down every social invitation and refusing to plan beyond the
next twenty-four hours, his world has shrunk to the four walls of his apartment. This self-imposed
isolation serves as daily proof for his belief that he is no longer the happy, engaged person he
once was, but someone fundamentally broken and miserable. Each time he dodges a reminder of
Priya—changing the radio station when her favorite song comes on, taking a long detour to avoid
their old park—he confirms to himself that the grief is too big, that he is too weak to handle it,
and that the only sane response to a hostile world is to hide from it.

These very strategies, designed to reduce his distress, perversely keep his nervous system on a
knife's edge. The constant vigilance required to avoid grief triggers means a part of his mind is
always scanning his environment for potential threats, leaving him perpetually tense and exhausted.
Because he never allows himself to experience a planned event that goes well or to sit with a memory
of his sister without falling apart, he never gets any evidence to contradict his fear. His mind and
body remain primed for a catastrophe that has already happened, trapped in the immediate aftermath.
This state of constant, low-grade panic has made sleep a near impossibility, further fraying his
ability to cope during the day.

Rohan sought therapy after his agent, concerned by months of silence, sent an email asking if he was
alright. He has stared at the unopened message for a week, the notification a symbol of a
professional and creative life that is slipping through his fingers. He confessed that he hasn’t
written a single new word since the accident. He is terrified that engaging with the world again
will only lead to more pain, but he is even more terrified of this empty, silent life becoming
permanent. He wants, more than anything, to believe in the future again, but he no longer remembers
how.
