Sofia sits rigidly in the chair, her gaze fixed on a point just over her therapist's shoulder. It
has been eight months since the music festival, and she still cannot find the words for what
happened. She knows the facts—the explosion, the screaming, the stampede—but when she tries to
describe it, the narrative crumbles, leaving only aphasic gaps and a hot, consuming rage that feels
more real than the memory itself. The attack happened at night, and the darkness now feels predatory
to her.

She had gone to the festival with friends, losing herself in the music under the open sky. The first
blast felt like a physical blow, a concussive force that threw her to the ground. In the chaos that
followed, she remembers the frantic, unpredictable movements of the crowd as people scrambled over
one another. She recalls a feeling of absolute helplessness, of her own body refusing to respond,
frozen amidst the terror. There are large sections of the event she simply cannot access, black
holes in her memory that her mind refuses to enter, no matter how hard she prods.

In the weeks that followed, a set of rigid beliefs began to crystallise. Sofia had come to believe
that the world was a profoundly dangerous place and that she had to be on guard at all times to
survive. This conviction settled deep in her bones, leading her to feel perpetually on edge, her
body coiled tight and ready to react. At her job in the warehouse, the sudden clang of falling metal
or the unexpected rumble of a forklift makes her jump so violently she feels a jolt of pain in her
neck. This constant, simmering sense of threat is exhausting, particularly at night, when every
creak of her apartment building sounds like an approaching footstep.

Her beliefs about the world’s danger dictate her every action. Convinced that threat is everywhere,
she constantly scans her environment, her head on a swivel as she walks the warehouse floor,
checking exits and monitoring the movements of her coworkers. She avoids making eye contact, a
behaviour that has made her colleagues see her as aloof and has drawn a concerned comment from her
supervisor about her being “distracted.” These very strategies, meant to protect her, only reinforce
her fear. The relentless scanning confirms her appraisal that danger is imminent and that
hypervigilance is essential for survival. By keeping her body in a state of high alert, the scanning
makes her even more susceptible to being startled, feeding the very sense of threat she is trying to
manage.

The fragmented memories are a powerful engine for this cycle. When an image from that night breaks
through—a flash of fire, the look on someone’s face—it brings with it that familiar, searing rage.
The raw emotion immediately triggers a spike in her perceived threat, making her heart pound as if
the danger were present and real. In these moments, she feels an overwhelming urge to either
intensify her scanning, trying to locate the source of a non-existent danger, or to deliberately
dissociate, to “blank out” and retreat from a world that feels too sharp and hostile.

The feedback loop is now a constant in her life. A sudden movement from a coworker in her peripheral
vision sends a jolt of alarm through her, a direct echo of the panicked crowd. This sharp sense of
threat can, in turn, trigger a memory fragment—the feeling of being trapped, the rage—which then
compels her to stare at the concrete floor, deliberately trying to leave her body until the feeling
passes. Her attempts to cope have become the architecture of her prison, each strategy reinforcing
the beliefs that fuel her anxiety. This has taken a toll; the warning from her supervisor has made
her terrified of losing her job, one of the few remaining structures in her life. She has come to
therapy not with a clear goal, but with a desperate, quiet plea: she is tired of being so afraid and
wants to know if it is possible to ever feel safe in her own body again.
