Kenji, a 27-year-old journalist, presented for therapy six months after leaving an abusive
relationship. The final assault had been the worst. An argument that started on the city bus
escalated when they got home. His ex-partner, Mark, had shoved him against the wall, the impact
knocking the wind out of him, before striking him repeatedly. Kenji remembers the smell of stale
beer on Mark’s breath and the shock of pain radiating through his ribs, a physical echo that has
outlasted the bruises. He had stayed silent, a strategy he’d learned over months of trying to de-
escalate Mark’s unpredictable rage. He left the next day and never went back.

Initially, Kenji felt a surge of relief, but it was quickly replaced by something more insidious.
The memories of that night, and others like it, began to intrude without warning. They weren't like
memories at all; they felt like re-enactments. When an image of the assault surfaced, he would feel
so overwhelmed by a tidal wave of shame and terror that he would have to stop whatever he was doing,
bracing himself against a phantom ache in his side. The sheer vividness of these experiences—feeling
the pain as if it were happening right now—cemented a profound and devastating set of beliefs. He
had come to see himself as fundamentally inadequate, someone whose weakness had invited the abuse.
This conviction, paired with the raw memory of betrayal, solidified into a core belief that people
simply can’t be trusted. These appraisals began to reshape his world, making him hyper-aware of
potential threats. Because he was certain that any man could turn on him without warning, being
alone with men became an unbearable source of anxiety. The memory of the argument starting on the
bus had also poisoned public transport for him, transforming a simple bus ride into a potent
trigger.

This new, threat-saturated reality has made his work as a journalist nearly impossible. Last week,
while conducting a one-on-one interview in a small office, the subject, a friendly city councilman,
leaned forward suddenly to emphasize a point. The abrupt movement sent a jolt of panic through
Kenji. Instantly, he was back in his old apartment, bracing for a blow. The councilman’s face
blurred, replaced by Mark’s. He managed to stammer an excuse about feeling unwell and fled the room,
leaving the story unfinished. In the aftermath of such moments, Kenji’s mind turns inward. He walks
home, skipping the bus, and spends the evening replaying the failed interview, dwelling on what he
has done wrong, each thought a confirmation of his professional failure. He pushes down the hot
surge of anger at himself, refusing to let it surface, and avoids eating, the gnawing emptiness in
his stomach a familiar, almost comforting, distraction from the turmoil in his head.

This pattern of self-blame has only deepened his conviction that he is inadequate, a belief now so
entrenched it feels like a fact. This certainty that he is uniquely flawed fuels a pervasive sense
of threat that follows him everywhere. He has come to believe you can never know who will harm you.
At night, he finds himself waking several times to check that the doors are locked, even though his
new partner, who is gentle and supportive, is sleeping beside him. When his partner is late coming
home from work, Kenji’s mind doesn’t entertain traffic; it leaps straight to catastrophe, a
certainty that something terrible has happened. Even when his partner arrives safely and offers
reassurance, Kenji finds it impossible to believe he is truly safe. This constant, exhausting
vigilance serves as its own kind of proof; if he feels this unsafe all the time, he reasons, then
the danger must be real, and he must be too weak to handle it.

He finally sought therapy after his editor put him on notice for the missed deadline, and his new
partner gently expressed concern about his weight loss and constant anxiety. Kenji feels trapped in
a loop he can no longer control. He is terrified that he is permanently broken, that he will lose
his career and drive away the first kind person he has been with in years. More than anything, he
wants to be able to sit in a room with another person and not be waiting for the moment the violence
begins.
