dry land, the constant beeping of machines, and the pervasive, sharp smell of antiseptic that seemed
to cling to everything. She survived, but the electrician who walked out of the hospital was not the
same woman who had walked in. Now 54 and divorced, Mai has come to therapy because her world has
become a map of invisible threats, and the safe zones are shrinking with each passing month.

body. Before her mind could even form a thought, her heart was hammering against her ribs, her palms
were slick with sweat, and the air in her own lungs felt thin and useless. She felt a powerful,
primal urge to flee. This immediate, overwhelming physical reaction to such triggers has become a
central feature of her life, a signal from her body that danger is imminent, even when her rational
mind knows it isn't. In that moment at work, she abruptly stood up, mumbled an excuse about needing
a tool from her truck, and spent ten minutes outside, forcing herself to dissociate, to tell herself
this was happening to someone else, a character in a movie, not her. This response, a trigger
launching her directly into a desperate attempt to cope, has become an exhausting, reflexive
pattern.

Mai now lives in a state of constant, low-grade tension, her body perpetually braced for the next
assault. She describes it as being a live wire herself, humming with a current of anxiety that never
fully shuts off. This pervasive sense of threat dictates her choices. When her body feels so keyed
up, the only logical response, it seems to her, is to eliminate any possible source of activation.
She has stopped going to her annual physicals, the thought of a doctor’s office with its familiar
antiseptic smell being enough to make her feel dizzy. As an electrician, she now turns down any jobs
located in hospitals or clinics, a decision that has cost her significant income. She spends long
evenings caught in a loop of counterfactual thinking, imagining the life she would have if she’d
never gotten sick—a life where she was still married, still fearless on the job, still able to
simply breathe without vigilance.

This web of avoidance and mental escape has, over time, only amplified her fear. By treating every
medical cue as a legitimate danger to be avoided, she has reinforced her body’s belief that these
situations are truly life-threatening. The thought of a routine check-up, once a minor
inconvenience, now looms in her mind as a catastrophic event, which in turn keeps her body on even
higher alert. This self-reinforcing cycle feels inescapable. The more her body screams danger, the
more she avoids; the more she avoids, the more dangerous the world seems. Her constant rumination on
what might have been also keeps the trauma fresh, making the upcoming two-year anniversary of her
hospitalization feel less like a date on a calendar and more like a looming threat she is powerless
to stop.

It was a near-miss with an ambulance last week that finally propelled her to seek help. Stuck in
traffic, she watched one wail past, and the subsequent panic attack was so severe she had to pull
over, convinced she was having a heart attack. She sat in her truck for an hour, shaking, lost in
the terrifying thought that her body was betraying her all over again. Mai is exhausted by the
constant vigilance. She wants to be able to work without fear, to hear a person catch their breath
without her own body preparing for a fight for survival. More than anything, she wants to feel at
home in her own skin again, to trust the simple, life-giving rhythm of her own breathing.
