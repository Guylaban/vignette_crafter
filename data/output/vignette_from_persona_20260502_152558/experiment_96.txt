Minh had spent forty years of his life in motion, first as a refugee himself and then, for decades,
as a dedicated aid worker, his small van a familiar sight delivering supplies and comfort to newly
arrived families. At seventy-two, he was supposed to be slowing down, but the work was his life.
That life, however, had ended six months ago on a highway overpass. He was driving a young family to
a medical appointment when a repurposed utility truck swerved violently into their lane. The world
dissolved into a concussive roar and a flash of white light. Minh remembers nothing more, only
disconnected snapshots that ambush him now: the glint of sun on shattered glass suspended in the
air, the crimson stain blooming on the passenger seat, the high-pitched shriek of metal. Sometimes,
when the house is quiet, he can still hear that shriek, an auditory scar that feels more real than
the memory of his own wife’s voice.

In the weeks that followed, these fragments began to govern his life. The intrusive images of the
wreck, so vivid and sharp, triggered a terrifying, primal conviction that the danger was not over;
it was imminent and everywhere. Sitting in his living room, a picture of the twisted metal would
flash in his mind, and his body would respond as if the crash were happening again. His heart would
pound, and a cold sweat would break out as he scanned the street outside his window, certain he was
being watched. He began to feel that being superalert was the only thing keeping him safe. This
constant state of watchfulness, in turn, made the world a minefield of triggers. The rumble of a
garbage truck down the street was no longer just a sound; it was the prelude to an explosion, a
sensory cue that instantly brought back the sound of the blast and the image of the shattered
windshield. The loop was complete and merciless: the memory ignited the threat, and the feeling of
threat made the memory inescapable.

The memories also fueled a quiet, corrosive self-assessment. He would sit for hours replaying the
moments before the attack, convinced his decision to take the highway instead of the side streets
was the fatal error. The thought, *I should have known, I put them in danger*, became a constant,
punishing refrain. This self-blame was so consuming that he stopped driving altogether. The driver’s
seat of his own car felt like an accusation. At first, his wife, Lien, was understanding, but as
weeks turned into months, his refusal to even be a passenger became a significant strain. Any
attempt on her part to discuss it, however gently, was met with his immediate retreat. The chaos and
violence of the memory had left him with an intense fear of any confrontation, and he would simply
leave the room rather than face her worried questions.

His world has now shrunk to the four walls of his house and the small garden out back. This is the

living in a world that had become as small and as frightening as the final, terrifying moments
