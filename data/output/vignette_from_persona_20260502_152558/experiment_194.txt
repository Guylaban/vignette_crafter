Sarah, a 35-year-old counselor, presented for treatment six months after a motor vehicle accident.
The incident occurred on a rainy Tuesday evening as she was driving home from her private practice.
Distracted for a moment by a message on her car's display, she hadn't seen the pedestrian stepping
into the crosswalk until it was too late. The sound of the impact, the sight of the young man on the
pavement, and the look in his eyes just before the ambulance arrived became the defining moments of
the trauma. She was not physically harmed, but the pedestrian suffered life-altering injuries. In
the immediate aftermath, Sarah’s memory of the event was a chaotic blur of sensory impressions. She
could recall the slick blackness of the road and the rhythmic sweep of her windshield wipers, but
the critical seconds were missing, replaced by disconnected images that refused to form a coherent
story.

In the weeks that followed, this fragmentation of memory became a source of profound distress.
Whenever a piece of the event would surface—the glint of the streetlights on the wet asphalt, the
specific shade of the man’s jacket—it brought with it an overwhelming wave of grief and horror. This
intense emotional reaction, rooted in the memory itself, drove her to develop strategies for
containment. She began to try hard not to think about what happened, pushing the images away the
moment they appeared. This conscious suppression extended into her physical world; she started
taking a much longer route home from her office to avoid the intersection, and the sound of a
distant siren would cause her to physically flinch and cover her ears.

The core of Sarah's suffering, however, was anchored in her powerful negative appraisals of herself
and her role in the event. She had come to believe with absolute certainty that the accident was
entirely her fault, a direct result of her momentary lapse in attention. This crushing self-
blame—the conviction that she had failed in her fundamental duty to protect others—fueled a
pervasive and constant sense of threat. The world no longer felt safe because she herself was the
danger. This belief system manifested as a state of relentless hypervigilance; she was perpetually
on guard, scanning her environment for potential threats, her body locked in a state of high alert.
Sleep offered little respite, with nights spent staring at the ceiling, her mind replaying her
failure. The guilt and shame were a constant, heavy presence, coloring every interaction and
draining all meaning from a life she had once found fulfilling.

These powerful feelings of threat, in turn, dictated her behavior. The exhaustion of being
constantly watchful made her shrink her world. At her counseling practice, she found it increasingly
difficult to do her job. Making eye contact with her clients, a cornerstone of her therapeutic work,
became unbearable. She was certain they could see the failure she felt, the terrible thing she had
done, reflected in her own eyes. This avoidance of eye contact, driven by her fear and shame, began
to create a distance between her and the very people she was meant to help. Her constant state of
arousal also kept the traumatic memory from settling; her sleeplessness and anxiety seemed to
prevent the disconnected pieces from ever forming a narrative she could process, leaving them as
potent, intrusive fragments.

The strategies Sarah employed to manage her distress only served to maintain it. Her strenuous
efforts to suppress thoughts of the accident ensured the memory never had a chance to be integrated,
leaving it raw and powerful. This avoidance, while providing fleeting relief, subtly reinforced the
danger, as she never learned that the sound of an ambulance was just a sound, not an imminent
catastrophe. The ongoing feelings of fear and guilt also seemed to confirm her negative beliefs; the
fact that she felt so awful was, to her, proof of her own unforgivable negligence.

Sarah finally sought therapy after her husband found her sitting in her car in their driveway,
unable to get out after hearing an ambulance pass their street. She feels trapped in a relentless
cycle of guilt and fear, believing she is unworthy of peace. While a part of her is desperate to
escape the torment, she is terrified that truly confronting the memory will only confirm what she
already believes: that she is a monster who deserves to suffer.
