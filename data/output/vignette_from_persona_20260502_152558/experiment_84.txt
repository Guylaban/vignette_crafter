Kofi had spent a lifetime mastering the language of the body. As a dancer, even at sixty-eight, he
possessed a grace and physical confidence that belied his age. But that was seven months ago, before
the collision. Now, his world had shrunk to the four walls of his apartment, and his body felt like
a stranger’s, an unreliable vessel he no longer knew how to command. The accident itself lived in
him not as a story but as a collection of sensory shards: the blinding glare of headlights in his
rearview mirror, the high-pitched squeal of tires on wet asphalt, and a shattering sound that seemed
to come from inside his own head. When he tried to deliberately assemble these pieces into a
coherent narrative, they would scatter, leaving him with unnerving gaps and a profound sense of
incompleteness.

These gaps in his memory became fertile ground for self-recrimination. He felt that if he couldn't
remember the whole event, he couldn't be sure he hadn't done something wrong, some fatal misjudgment
in that split second before impact. This uncertainty solidified into a deep-seated conviction that
he could no longer trust his own instincts. The fragmented nature of the memory also left him
perpetually braced for impact; without a clear beginning, middle, and end, the trauma felt
unfinished, capable of recurring at any moment. This feeling was most acute when he heard sudden,
loud noises—a car backfiring, a dish dropping—which would instantly transport him back to the
terrifying chaos of the crash, his heart pounding in his chest. The memory’s refusal to form a
coherent story meant that the threat it contained was never properly filed away as “past.”

His negative beliefs about himself and the world began to dictate his experience of the present. The
conviction that his own body was fundamentally unreliable meant that every ache or moment of
stiffness, once a familiar part of a dancer’s life, now felt like a harbinger of catastrophic
failure. This pervasive sense of physical vulnerability bled into a broader hypervigilance. He also
harboured a deep bitterness toward the paramedics who took nearly thirty minutes to arrive, a delay
during which he lay trapped and helpless. This experience cemented a belief that the systems meant
to protect him were broken, and now, any unexpected delay—a late bus, a slow-moving queue—triggered
a surge of anxiety, a feeling that something terrible was unfolding just out of sight. His self-
doubt made him hesitant to even try to recall the accident, as if probing the memory would only
confirm his own inadequacy in the face of the event.

This triad of a fragmented memory, a constant sense of threat, and corrosive self-doubt drove Kofi
into a systematic retreat from the world. Believing he couldn't do the right thing and that his body
might fail him at any moment, he began to avoid any situation that felt unpredictable. He stopped
going out. The dance studio, once his sanctuary, became a place of imagined peril. He couldn't bear
to see the people he used to socialise and rehearse with, certain they would see his fragility. His
life, once a fluid performance, became a rigid exercise in risk management. This withdrawal was a
direct response to the world feeling like a minefield, a place where his fractured memory offered no
reliable map and his own judgment could not be trusted.

The strategies meant to keep him safe, however, only amplified his fear. His apartment, once a place
of rest, became a listening post. The more he stayed inside, avoiding the world, the more attuned he
became to the sounds from the street, each one amplified and menacing. He arranged his furniture so
he could always sit with his back to a wall, his eyes scanning the door. His self-imposed isolation
didn't diminish the threat; it concentrated it, confirming his belief that danger was everywhere,
just waiting for him to let his guard down. By avoiding old friends, he lost the very connections
that might have reassured him, leaving him alone with his terrifying thoughts.

It was the profound loneliness that finally brought Kofi to therapy. His life had become a waiting
game, a small, quiet existence devoid of the movement, music, and community that had once defined
him. He came to his first session not with a clear hope for recovery, but with a desperate,
whispered question: was there any way to trust his own body, and the world, again?
