Bao is a man who has learned the art of stillness. At seventy-six, his movements are deliberate and
economical, skills honed over decades of quiet labor in warehouses across the city. He is known for
his reliability, a man who keeps to himself, stacking pallets and operating the forklift with a
focused calm that his younger colleagues mistake for simple old age. They do not see the vigilance
behind his eyes, or understand that his quiet is not one of peace, but of containment. For Bao, the
past is not a distant country; it is a physical presence that can ambush him without warning, a
ghost that has followed him from the mud-soaked village of his youth in war-torn Cambodia.

He was only a teenager when the soldiers came. The memory, when it returns, is never fragmented. It
is terrifyingly complete, a perfect, vivid recollection. He remembers the shouting, the smell of
damp earth and sweat as he was thrown to the ground, the crushing weight of a boot on his back, and
the rough grip of hands binding his wrists. He remembers the absolute certainty that he was about to
die. This detailed memory is the source of the triggers that now litter his daily life. The scent of
rust and damp cardboard in the warehouse can suddenly become the smell of the pit he was held in. A
loud, angry voice from a supervisor berating another worker can echo the soldiers’ shouts. But it is
the feeling of being held, of his movement being restricted, that is the most potent key to
unlocking the past.

For years, Bao managed to keep the memories at bay. They were a low hum beneath the surface of his
new life in America. But the cycle began in earnest a few months ago. A younger colleague, seeing
Bao stumble near a loading dock, grabbed his arm firmly to steady him. The sudden, restraining touch
was all it took. In an instant, Bao was no longer in a brightly lit warehouse but back in the
darkness of his village, the weight on his back, the rough hands on his skin. The memory was so
overwhelming that he cried out and stumbled away, leaving his co-worker confused and concerned. He
had to leave work for the day, overcome by a wave of terror and shame so powerful he could not
speak. This incident demonstrated the direct and powerful path from a present-day trigger to the
full re-experiencing of his past trauma.

That single flashback seemed to leave a door open that Bao can no longer close. The vividness of the
memory has, in turn, made him acutely sensitive to the very triggers that summon it. He now lives
with a constant, low-level dread, scanning his environment for anything that might set it off again.
The memory of being pinned to the ground makes him flinch from the well-meaning hand of a colleague
and causes him to feel a spike of panic when he has to squeeze between two tall stacks of inventory,
the feeling of being physically hemmed in becoming almost unbearable. The memory of the soldiers’
confrontation makes him leave the breakroom whenever voices are raised in argument. The past now
dictates his present, with the memory itself acting as a constant reminder of what to fear, making
the world feel like a minefield of potential triggers.

This vicious circle now defines his days. Just last week, while moving a pallet of damp goods, the
specific olfactory trigger—that mix of mildew and earth—caught him. The memory returned without
preamble, a sudden and total sensory immersion. He was back in the pit, the fear as fresh as it was
sixty years ago. He felt the overwhelming urge to flee, but his body was frozen, his hands gripping
the forklift controls until his knuckles were white. He had to stop, right in the middle of the
aisle, closing his eyes and breathing through the panic until the warehouse sounds slowly filtered
back in, replacing the echoes of the past. The episode passed, but it left him shaken and exhausted,
the boundary between then and now feeling dangerously thin. It is this exhaustion, this sense of
being relentlessly hunted by his own mind, that has finally brought Bao to therapy. He fears he is
losing the quiet control he has guarded for so long and hopes, after a lifetime of silence, to
finally find a way to lay the ghosts of his past to rest.
