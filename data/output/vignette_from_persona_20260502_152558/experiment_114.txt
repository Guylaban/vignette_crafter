supposed to be observing a trafficking ring, but he was identified, grabbed from a darkened alley,
and thrown into a windowless room. He remembers the scratch of the burlap sack over his head, the
cold bite of zip ties on his wrists, and the low, indistinct murmur of his captors’ voices from the
other side of a heavy steel door.

The event itself remains a blur, a collection of terrifying fragments rather than a coherent
narrative. Adebayo struggles to articulate the experience, even to himself. He tells his therapist
he knows what happened, that he can list the sequence of events, but when he tries to describe the
feeling of it, he is unable to find the words. The memory doesn't live in his mind as a story.
Instead, it lives in his ears. While trying to complete paperwork at his desk, he will suddenly hear
the sharp, metallic click of the bolt sliding shut on that basement door, a sound so vivid he
flinches, expecting to find himself back in the dark. At other times, a wave of pure, sickening
terror will wash over him without warning or context. There is no image attached, just the raw,
physical sensation of helplessness and dread he felt while tied to a chair, leaving him breathless
and disoriented in the middle of his own kitchen.

These intrusive, fragmented memories have become the central threat in Adebayo’s life, and his days
are now organized around a desperate effort to control them. The auditory flashbacks and surges of
emotion are so unpredictable and overpowering that his primary response is to try to force them out
of his mind. He spends hours trying hard not to think about the trauma, a constant, exhausting
battle of mental suppression that only seems to make the intrusions more frequent. Because the
terror is so strongly associated with the blackness of the basement, he has come to see all darkness
as dangerous. He no longer takes night shifts, a significant problem for a detective, and refuses to
leave his apartment after sunset, cutting himself off from friends and social events. The memory of
being restrained has poisoned all physical contact. When his girlfriend, Imani, reaches out to

being bound. He sees the hurt and confusion in her eyes but feels powerless to stop the reaction.

drawn concerned questions from his supervising officer, threatening the career he worked so hard to
build. His relationship with Imani is fraying under the strain of his withdrawal and his inability

of what happened in that basement. He finally sought help after Imani found him one night standing
rigidly in the living room, every light in the apartment blazing. He had been woken by the sound of
the refrigerator’s compressor kicking on, his mind instantly translating it into the hum of the
warehouse’s faulty generator. He is here because he is terrified of losing everything that once
defined him—his job, his relationship, his own sense of safety—to a memory he can neither fully
recall nor ever seem to escape.
