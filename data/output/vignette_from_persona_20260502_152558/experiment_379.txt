Mark, a 49-year-old architect, presented for treatment six months after witnessing a violent
assault. The incident occurred during a client meeting at a downtown coffee shop. He had been
admiring the cafe’s structural design when a loud argument erupted at an adjacent table. It
escalated with shocking speed. One man produced a knife, and in the ensuing struggle, the other was
badly wounded. Mark remembers the scene in disjointed flashes: the glint of the blade under the
track lighting, the acrid smell of burnt coffee mixing with a coppery, metallic scent, and a
profound wave of nausea that left him feeling paralyzed in his chair. He was certain there was
something he should have done, a way he should have intervened, but he had remained frozen until
security arrived.

In the weeks that followed, Mark found he couldn't piece the memory together into a coherent story.
When his wife asked what happened, he found he couldn't find the right words, the sequence felt
jumbled and confused, leaving him frustrated and silent. This difficulty in articulating the event
began to curdle into a deeper conviction that there was something fundamentally wrong with him, some
intrinsic fault in his character that had caused him to freeze. This sense of personal failure made
his reactions to reminders feel not just involuntary but shameful, amplifying their power. He began
to feel intensely isolated, as if he were living behind a pane of glass, watching the rest of the
world move on while he was stuck replaying a film with all the scenes out of order.

The world, once a place of blueprints and possibilities, now felt laden with threat. His conviction
that he was fundamentally helpless meant that the world now seemed predatory, a belief that fueled a
constant, exhausting vigilance. When entering any room, even his own familiar office, his first
instinct was to scan for all the exits. At restaurants with his wife, he became irritable and tense
unless he could find a table where his back was against a wall, allowing him to survey the entire
space. This state of perpetual high alert seemed to prime his mind for the past; any sudden noise,
like a colleague dropping a stack of papers, could tear a hole in the present, through which a
fragment of the memory would intrude. Living in this state of constant threat scanning also made him
exquisitely sensitive to his surroundings; he would notice the scent of coffee from a block away, or
flinch at a scene of an argument on a TV in a storefront window.

This hypervigilance created a vicious, self-sustaining cycle. Any trigger, however small, would
immediately confirm his sense of imminent danger. The smell of dark roast coffee brewing in the
office kitchen would instantly put him on high alert, his muscles tensing as his eyes darted to the
nearest exit. That same smell would also splinter his concentration, pulling up a confusing flash of
a man's shocked face and a dark stain spreading on the floor. Because these memories came back not
as a narrative but as a sensory assault—a smell, a feeling of nausea, a silent image of violence—the
threat felt immediate, as if the violence were about to erupt again right in his own office. This
feeling of reliving the event, of the past collapsing into the present, only reinforced his need to
remain on guard, tightening the loop of fear.

The consequences have been significant. He avoids client meetings in cafes, a staple of his
profession, making excuses that are beginning to wear thin with his partners. His marriage is
strained by his irritability and his refusal to go to crowded places. The sense of being set apart
from others has become a self-fulfilled prophecy as he withdraws from the very people who could
offer support. He finally sought therapy after an incident at work where a dropped tray in the
cafeteria caused him to cry out and duck for cover, drawing stares from his colleagues. The mixture
of shame and the stark realization that he could not control these reactions brought him to his
first appointment, hoping to find a way to stop feeling so broken and to quell the certainty that
something terrible is always about to happen.
