Leilani, a forty-six-year-old forensic analyst, presented to the clinic six months after the death
of her husband, David. She described a pervasive sense of being "stuck in a fog," unable to connect
with her life or work, and haunted by the day he died. The accident had been sudden, a violent
collision on the interstate during a rainstorm. Leilani was in the passenger seat. She remembers the
event not as a continuous sequence, but as a series of stark, silent photographs burned into her
mind: the glint of headlights through the rain-streaked windshield, the impossible angle of David’s
head against the shattered side window, the surreal pattern of blood on his white shirt. When these
images surface, they are often accompanied by the phantom sound of screeching tires and the
sickening crunch of metal, an auditory echo that cuts through any silence. The sensory fragments are
so potent that when they arrive, she feels a wave of emotion so overwhelming she has to stop
whatever she is doing, bracing herself against a wall or gripping the edge of a table until it
subsides.

In the months following the accident, Leilani developed a profound conviction that her life, as she
knew it, was over. The future she and David had planned was gone, and with it, she felt all meaning
had been drained from her existence. This bleak appraisal of her life began to shape her every
action. She had once found a quiet satisfaction in the meticulous, ordered world of her forensics
lab, but now the work felt hollow and pointless. Believing her life was already destroyed, she saw
no reason to maintain the pretense of normalcy, and she began calling in sick, unable to face the
concentration her job demanded. At home, she started restricting her food intake; the gnawing
emptiness in her stomach felt like an appropriate physical manifestation of the void inside her.
This sense of hopelessness also seemed to magnetize the trauma itself; her conviction that nothing
good was left in the world made it feel as though her mind was constantly scanning for proof,
serving up the snapshot memories of the crash as if to say, "See? This is all that's real now."

Her attempts to cope only seemed to pull her deeper into the distress. To fend off the memories that
ambushed her in the quiet of the evening, Leilani began delaying sleep, forcing herself to stay
awake scrolling through her phone until two or three in the morning, hoping to fall into an
exhausted, dreamless state. But the strategy consistently backfired. The profound fatigue from lack
of sleep and poor nutrition only weakened her defenses, making her more vulnerable to the intrusive
images and sounds during the day. The memories would break through with even greater intensity,
leaving her shaken and disoriented. Furthermore, each day she avoided work, and each meal she
skipped, served as confirmation of her deepest fears. The growing pile of unopened mail, the
concerned but unanswered texts from colleagues, and her own reflection in the mirror were all
evidence for the conclusion she had already reached: her life was irrevocably broken. Her avoidance
wasn't protecting her; it was proving her right.

Leilani’s husband’s family had tried to support her, but she found herself pulling away, convinced
that no one could understand the finality of her loss. She had lost her faith not just in a higher
power, but in the basic predictability and safety of the world. Now, facing a formal warning from
her supervisor at work and feeling increasingly isolated, she has sought therapy. She expressed a
deep fear that this state of numb, haunted detachment is permanent, that the person she was died in
the car with her husband. Her primary hope is simply to "feel something else," though she is
terrified that engaging with the memory will only destroy what little is left of her.
