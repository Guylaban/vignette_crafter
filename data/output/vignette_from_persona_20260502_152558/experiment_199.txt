a high-definition film of a familiar living room, the pattern on the sofa, and a betrayal that
dismantled her sense of safety in the world. The memory is not something she recalls; it is
something that happens to her, forcing its way into her mind with vivid, excruciating detail.

In the weeks that followed, Keilani began to construct a new set of rules for living. The core
belief that settled deep in her bones was that she was now defective, that something within her was
fundamentally broken. This conviction, that "somebody else would have stopped it," became the lens
through which she viewed everything. This sense of personal failure made any internal state of
discomfort feel like a confirmation of her brokenness, a trigger for further distress. The belief
that she was no longer the capable woman she had been fueled a constant, humming sense of threat;
her body felt perpetually tense, coiled and ready for a danger she couldn't name but felt
everywhere. This feeling of being on high alert meant she was always watchful, scanning rooms and
faces, which in turn made even moments of simple happiness with her husband feel reckless and
unsafe, turning relaxation itself into a new kind of trigger.

Her days are now governed by a suffocating cycle of reactions. A phone call from a relative is
enough to trigger a jolt of alarm that makes her body feel instantly on guard. The memory of the
assault can surge from this, vivid and uncontrolled, leaving her breathless in her own kitchen. To
manage the intrusive images and the feeling of imminent danger, she has developed strategies that
offer fleeting relief but ultimately deepen the trap. She eats, often to the point of physical pain,
using the discomfort as a distraction from the memories. This physical fullness then becomes its own
trigger, a new source of discomfort that confirms her sense of being broken.

The woman who once thrived at the center of her community now lives on its margins. At church
functions, a requirement of her job, she stands near the exit, her mind mapping escape routes rather
than listening to the sermon. The constant sense of being on alert compels this behaviour, but
standing there, poised for flight, only reinforces the belief that she is in danger, keeping her
body tense and watchful. Before leaving the house, she applies a thick layer of makeup, a mask she
feels is necessary to hide the defectiveness she is certain everyone can see. This ritual of
applying the makeup, however, often brings back flashes of the memory of that night, her own
reflection becoming a portal to the past.

The connections have become a self-sustaining engine of distress. A trigger—seeing a man who vaguely

physical threat, the feeling of her body being on high alert. This sense of threat demands a coping
strategy, so she retreats to her car and eats until she feels sick. The strategy of overeating leads
to physical discomfort, which acts as another trigger, which feeds her belief that she is broken,
which makes her more sensitive to threat, which makes her more likely to be triggered by the
