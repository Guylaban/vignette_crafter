Kofi had always been a man who found comfort in structure. As a curriculum designer, his days were
spent creating logical, sequential pathways for learning, a process he found deeply satisfying. But
for the last seven months, his own mind had become a place of chaos and alarm, a landscape he no
longer recognized. The shift began on a Tuesday night in late autumn, driving home from a late
meeting. The sudden wail of a siren and the blinding flash of red and blue lights in his rearview
mirror were the prelude. It was a case of mistaken identity, a car that matched the description of
one involved in a robbery. But the facts that emerged later did nothing to erase the terror of being
ordered out of his car by shouting officers, the cold press of metal as he was cuffed, or the
profound helplessness of that half-hour on the side of the road before the mistake was sorted out
with a cursory apology.

In the weeks that followed, the world began to feel fundamentally unsafe. Kofi found himself
constantly on guard, his body humming with a nervous energy he couldn't discharge. Walking through
the open-plan office where he worked, he would scan the faces of his colleagues, cataloging every
exit and potential threat. He’d sit with his back to the wall in the cafeteria, a habit he couldn't
explain. His wife, Amara, noticed he’d begun flinching at the sound of the doorbell, his entire body
tensing as if braced for impact. A car backfiring on their street sent him diving for cover behind
the sofa, his heart hammering against his ribs in a wild, painful rhythm. He was living in a state
of perpetual, high-stakes anticipation, waiting for a danger that never fully arrived but always
felt imminent.

This constant state of physical and emotional alert became the gateway to the trauma itself. When a
sudden noise or a glimpse of a police car on the street would send a jolt of adrenaline through him,
the feeling of that night would wash over him. It wasn't a coherent memory, not a film he could play
in his head. Instead, it was a sickening wave of pure, unattached dread and helplessness that would
leave him breathless and sweating. In those moments, when his body was screaming danger, he would
try to grasp the memory, to make sense of what was happening to him, but the emotional overwhelm was
so total that his mind would just shut down. He had to stop, to push it away, to focus on his
breathing until the feeling subsided. When Amara would find him pale and trembling and ask what was
wrong, he found he couldn't form the words. The event lived in his muscles and his racing heart, not
in a story he could tell her or himself. This inability to articulate it only made him feel more
isolated in his terror.

The intrusion of these states began to cripple his professional life. His job required long periods
of quiet concentration, but the office had become a minefield of triggers. A dropped book, a shouted
conversation down the hall, the squeal of a chair being pushed back too quickly—each was enough to
startle him violently, derailing his train of thought for an hour or more. His productivity
plummeted. He started wearing noise-canceling headphones constantly, which isolated him from his
team and led to missed conversations and a quiet reprimand from his manager. He felt a deep shame
about his inability to control his own reactions, a failure of the very logic and order he so
valued.

The breaking point came last week. Amara had been trying to get his attention from the other room
and, getting no response, came up behind him and gently touched his shoulder. Kofi yelled out and
spun around, knocking a lamp off the end table, his eyes wide with a terror she had never seen up
close. Seeing the fear and hurt on her face as he stood there, panting and unable to explain the
violent reaction, was more painful than the fear itself. It was in that moment he knew he could not
go on like this. He came to therapy not with a clear story to tell, but with a desperate hope to
quiet the alarms constantly ringing inside his own body, to find the words for the wordless horror
that held him captive, and to once again feel safe in his own life.
