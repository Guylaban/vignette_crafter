Sister Agnes had lived within the stone walls of the convent for thirty-five years, her life
governed by the comforting rhythm of the Liturgy of the Hours. Now, six months after the accident,
that rhythm felt like a cage. The trauma was not her own, but that of Sister Therese, the bright
young novice she had been mentoring. They had been returning from an errand at the parish office,
walking along a familiar street on a Tuesday afternoon. Agnes remembers the conversation
vividly—they were discussing the readings for the upcoming Mass—and then the memory splinters. There
are flashes of searing clarity: the improbable blue of the sedan, the screech of tires that seemed
to tear the air, the sight of Sister Therese’s white wimple coming to rest on the grimy asphalt. The
moments that followed are a complete blank, a void from which she emerges only in the memory of the
ambulance’s wail.

In the quiet of her cell, the questions that arose in the initial shock soon hardened into
convictions. She came to believe the accident was her fault, a direct consequence of her
inattention. She had been talking, gesturing, not watching the traffic as she should have. This core
belief—that she had failed in her most basic duty to protect the young woman entrusted to her
care—began to dictate her life. Convinced of her own dangerous unreliability, she started to alter
her daily routines. She could no longer walk the three blocks to the parish office, as the route
required crossing the intersection where Sister Therese was killed. The mere thought of the place
was enough to bring on a wave of nausea. This avoidance, born of a profound sense of self-blame, was
her first secret penance.

The memories themselves became the enemy. Something as simple as the sound of a car braking suddenly
outside the convent walls could instantly bring back the full, vivid horror without warning, as if
it were happening all over again. The image of the crumpled wimple would sear through her mind, and
she would have to grip the side of her desk to stay upright. To prevent these ambushes, she began to
avoid anything that might remind her of Sister Therese. She quietly removed a small photograph of
the two of them from the common room bulletin board and found she could no longer tend the roses the
novice had so loved in the cloister garden. The avoidance was a desperate attempt to control the
intrusive, fragmented recollections of the event.

Over the months, these strategies have become a self-reinforcing trap. By never allowing herself to
walk past the intersection, she has never had the chance to see it as just a corner where people
safely cross every day. The site remains frozen in her mind as a place of pure threat, ensuring that
the traumatic memory retains all its original power and horror. Each time she takes the ten-minute
detour to avoid it, her actions serve as an unspoken confirmation of her own damning judgment: "I
failed here. I cannot be trusted here." This reinforces the crushing belief that she is
fundamentally unreliable, a danger to those around her.

Her relentless self-blame also keeps the memory sharp and accessible, preventing it from fading into
the past. She constantly sifts through the seconds before the impact, interrogating her own actions,
searching for the precise moment her inattention sealed the novice’s fate. This constant mental
review ensures the traumatic details remain vivid and easily triggered. In response, a new, more
private behavior has taken root. Before crossing any street now, even the quiet lane inside the
convent grounds, she performs a rigid mental ritual, reciting a specific sequence of prayers three
times over. It is not a prayer for peace or guidance, but a frantic, superstitious plea to prevent
another catastrophe, a magical belief that if she performs this penance perfectly, she can atone for
her failure and keep everyone else safe.

It was her spiritual director who finally insisted she seek help, noticing her growing isolation and
the haunted look that rarely left her eyes. Sister Agnes’s faith, once a source of boundless
comfort, now feels like an impossible standard she can never meet. She has come to therapy not
because the grief is too much, but because she is terrified that she has become a person who cannot
be relied upon, by her community or by God.
