fractured his life. The assault occurred in a hotel room, perpetrated by an older, well-regarded
colleague he had admired. After a day of panels, the man had invited Samuel to his room to discuss a
research paper, locking the door behind them. The details of the hours that followed were a blur of
disconnected sensory fragments, but the memory of the deadbolt clicking shut, the feeling of being
trapped, and the complete loss of control were seared into his mind. This experience formed the
bedrock of his current distress, making his own therapy office, once a sanctuary, feel like a
potential trap.

In the weeks following the assault, a pervasive sense of shame settled over him. Samuel became
convinced that his trauma was visible to everyone, a stain on his character that others could see
just by looking at him. He felt acutely exposed, especially around other men. This deep-seated
belief that he was permanently marked and fundamentally helpless began to poison his perception of
the world. Because he had come to believe he was incapable of preventing bad things from happening,
his body remained in a constant state of high alert. He found himself unable to relax, even in his
own apartment, and sleep became a nightly battle against the need to stay vigilant. The simple act
of sitting in a cafe became an exercise in threat assessment; he could not settle unless his back
was firmly against a wall, with a clear view of the entire room and all its exits.

This state of perpetual, exhausting vigilance only served to confirm his worst fears about himself.
The fact that he was unable to switch off the alarm, that his body hummed with a tension he could
not release, felt like proof of his own brokenness. The constant scanning of his environment
reinforced his conviction that he was utterly helpless, a man whose future had been stolen. Each
night he lay awake, listening for nonexistent threats, the darkness would amplify his belief that he
had no future, that his life as he knew it was over. This feedback loop was relentless: the more
unsafe he felt, the more hopeless he became, and the more hopeless he became, the more he
interpreted the world as a place of imminent danger.

His hypervigilance made him exquisitely sensitive to reminders of the trauma. Because he was always
scanning for danger, the presence of other men, especially in enclosed spaces, became almost
unbearable. This was most acute in his professional life. The thought of being alone in his office
with a male client, a routine part of his job for over a decade, now triggered a wave of panic. He
began finding excuses to keep the door ajar, a breach of confidentiality that filled him with
professional shame. The belief that he was exposed and helpless made these situations feel not just
uncomfortable, but life-threatening. He started rescheduling or referring away male clients, a
pattern his colleagues began to notice. When a news report or casual conversation touched on sexual
violence, the words would land like physical blows, sending him spiraling back into the certainty
that he was fragile and unsafe.

The cycle has now ground his life to a halt. His practice, once a source of pride, is faltering. He
avoids social gatherings and has cut off contact with most of his male friends. The isolation has
become profound. Samuel finally sought therapy after a panic attack during a session with a new male
client, where the simple act of the client shifting in his chair caused him to flinch so violently
that he had to end the appointment abruptly. He sits in the therapy room now, his back pressed into
the corner of the sofa, terrified that articulating his experience will make it more real. His
greatest fear is that he is right—that he is permanently broken—and his only hope is to one day feel
safe enough to close a door without feeling the walls closing in.
