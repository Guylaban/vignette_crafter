Mateo, at seventy-five, had always prided himself on his meticulous nature. As a quality assurance
engineer, his job was to find flaws, to anticipate system failures before they happened. It was a
mindset that had served him well for decades, but it had turned on him in the nine months since the
fire. The wildfire had come on a Tuesday, whipped into a frenzy by the hot, dry Santa Ana winds. He
and his wife, Elena, had only minutes to evacuate. He remembers the acrid smell of burning pine, the
sky glowing a terrifying orange, and the roar that sounded like a freight train cresting the hill
behind their home. In the panic, he’d frozen for a crucial minute, debating whether to grab the
photo albums, a delay he was now certain had almost cost them their lives. They escaped, and their
house was miraculously spared, but the sense of safety was gone.

Now, the world is a minefield of reminders. A neighbor’s weekend barbecue can send a plume of smoke
over the fence, and instantly, Mateo’s heart begins to hammer against his ribs. The scent doesn't
just remind him of the fire; it feels like the fire is happening all over again. This sudden,
overwhelming physical sense of danger sends him into a spiral of thought. He retreats to his study,
ostensibly to work, but instead spends hours replaying the evacuation, dwelling on the single minute
of his hesitation. He meticulously lists every single thing he believes he did wrong, every
preventative measure he could have taken in the years prior. He tries to push the images out of his
mind, a strategy he’s learned is futile; the more he shoves them away, the more vividly the memory
of the flames licking the ridge seems to burn behind his eyes.

This constant internal battle has left him in a perpetual state of high alert. He feels jumpy and
watchful, his body coiled tight with an anxiety that never fully recedes. The slightest unexpected
noise—a car backfiring, a dish dropped in the kitchen—makes him flinch violently. This state of
hypervigilance means he is exquisitely tuned to potential threats. He is the first to notice a
change in the wind, the first to smell the distant campfire of a hiker, the first to catch a news
report about a brush fire hundreds of miles away. Because he is always on guard, he is constantly
finding new triggers that others miss, each one confirming his belief that danger is imminent. This
feeling of being physically on edge is intolerable, and his mind immediately seeks a familiar,
painful refuge: ruminating on how the entire disaster could have been prevented if only he had been
smarter, faster, better.

The cycle feeds on itself, tightening its grip over the weeks and months. The more he dwells on his
perceived failures, the more agitated and startled he becomes, his body reacting as if the threat is
no longer a memory but a present reality. This internal rehearsal of the trauma makes him even more
sensitive to his surroundings; after an afternoon lost in self-blame, the sight of a particularly
red sunset through his office window, so similar to the smoke-filled sky of that day, can feel as
menacing as an approaching siren. One afternoon, while testing a software build, a hot, dry wind
began to rattle the windowpane—the exact weather from that day. A wave of panic seized him, his
breath catching in his throat. He abandoned his work, his mind immediately flooded with thoughts of
his own culpability, a desperate attempt to control the past because the present felt so
terrifyingly out of his hands.

His life has shrunk. Elena’s attempts to have friends over for dinner are met with anxious refusal,
lest someone light a citronella candle. He no longer sits on the porch with her in the evenings. The

consumed by monitoring threats and wrestling with his own thoughts. It was after a particularly bad
night, when he woke Elena up by shouting in his sleep, that he finally agreed to seek help. He told
the therapist his greatest fear wasn't another fire, but the terrifying realization that his own
mind had become the most unsafe place of all. He just wants to feel at home in his own skin again,
to be the calm, steady man his wife married, and to silence the relentless voice that insists it was
all his fault.
