describes her internal world as a constant, chaotic emergency. Eight months ago, a sudden septic
shock nearly claimed her life, leaving her in a medically induced coma for six days. She survived,
but the woman who returned home felt like a stranger to herself and her husband. The trauma was not
an external event she could place in the past, but an ongoing betrayal from within, a deep-seated
conviction that her own body was now an unreliable and hostile entity. This belief that her life has
been irrevocably destroyed has become the lens through which she views everything, making the
memories of her time in the ICU not just accessible, but persistent and predatory. Because she feels
fundamentally broken, her mind seems to constantly scan her environment for anything related to her
hospital stay, turning routine conversations about health or passing a clinic into potent threats.

The memories, when they come, are not recollections but invasions. She struggles to articulate the
experience, telling her husband that the words just aren't there, that it’s a horror she can only
feel. One moment she is at her desk reviewing a project timeline, and the next she is overwhelmed by
the sterile scent of antiseptic and the rhythmic, menacing beep of a ventilator. It is a full
sensory reliving; she feels the phantom pressure of tubes in her throat and the specific, helpless
angle of her body in the ICU bed, a bodily reenactment so powerful it leaves her gasping for air.
These episodes immediately confirm her deepest fears about her own fragility, deepening the shame
she feels for what her body endured and her inability to move past it. The terror of the memory
makes her hyper-vigilant, priming her to notice any potential reminder. This constant state of
threat, in turn, makes it impossible to concentrate on the complex details of her work, and the
mental exhaustion leaves her vulnerable to the very memories she is trying to suppress.

This cycle of memory and threat now dominates her life. The fragments of her ICU stay have made her
exquisitely sensitive to triggers. A routine blood test for a life insurance policy became an
ordeal; the sight of the needle sent a jolt of terror through her, leading to an angry outburst at
the phlebotomist that mortified her. This state of high alert, triggered by medical equipment or
procedures, has become her baseline. Her irritability simmers just below the surface, and her
husband has learned not to touch her shoulder to get her attention, as her startled, physical recoil
feels like a rejection. When she reacts with such alarm or snaps at him, she is flooded with a wave
of self-loathing, reinforcing her belief that the trauma has ruined her not just for herself, but
for the people she loves.

The feedback loop is relentless. Her deeply held appraisal that she can no longer trust her body
makes every conversation a minefield. When a colleague recently began discussing a family member's
upcoming surgery, Kealani felt her heart begin to pound. She abruptly left the meeting, claiming a
sudden headache, unable to bear the topic. This trigger immediately plunged her into a state of high
alert, making her feel jumpy and unsafe for the rest of the day. The sheer physiological distress of
this threat response feels so overwhelming that it seems to pull the memories forward, and later
that afternoon, she found herself staring blankly at her computer screen, lost in the sensory echo
of the hospital.

She sought therapy after a critical error at work, a missed deadline on a major project that she,

destroyed. She came to her first session not with hope, but with a quiet desperation, terrified that
this new, fractured version of herself is all that remains and that she will never again feel safe
in her own skin.
