Kenji presents as a meticulously dressed 35-year-old accountant, his composure a fragile shield over
a profound and constant state of alarm. The precipitating event occurred two years ago during what
was meant to be a routine business trip. Detained in a foreign country, he was held for several days
in a small, windowless room and subjected to prolonged interrogation. The men who questioned him
were strangers, their faces shifting in his memory, their questions relentless. They focused on his
future, his plans, and his loyalties, twisting his ambitions into accusations. They told him he
would never see his family again, that his life as he knew it was over. The future, once a source of
motivation, became a tool of his torture.

In the months after his release, the life he returned to began to unravel. He found himself unable
to engage in simple conversations about the future, whether it was planning a vacation with his wife
or discussing long-term career goals. Each mention of what was to come felt like a threat, a hollow
echo of the interrogators’ promises that he had no future left. His wife, unable to comprehend his
withdrawal and sudden volatility, eventually left. This departure solidified a core belief that had
begun to form in the windowless room: he had been irrevocably broken.

Now, this belief system dictates his every interaction. At his accounting firm last week, he was
assigned a new corporate client. During the introductory meeting, the client, a friendly man in his
fifties, asked a simple icebreaker question about his plans for the upcoming year. The query, a
blend of stranger interaction and future orientation, landed like a physical blow. Instantly,
Kenji’s mind was consumed not by financial projections, but by the terrifying certainty that *you
can never know who will harm you*. The man’s smile seemed predatory, his questions a subtle
interrogation. This sudden surge of threat appraisal immediately reinforced his deepest fear: *I
have permanently changed for the worse*. A normal person, he thought, would just answer the
question. His inability to do so felt like definitive proof of his own damage.

These powerful negative appraisals directly govern his experience of the traumatic memory itself.
Later that afternoon, sitting alone in his cubicle, the shame from the meeting began to fester. He
was overwhelmed by the thought that *somebody else would have stopped the event from happening*,
that a stronger, better man would have resisted or found a way out. This wave of self-recrimination
acts as a key, unlocking the memories he tries so desperately to contain. They don’t surface as a
coherent narrative he can process, but as a chaotic highlight reel. The memory is like a film that
plays over and over: a close-up of a man’s mouth, the feeling of cold concrete against his cheek, a
question repeated in a language he barely understood. The sequence is always confused, the fragments
out of order. This disjointedness fuels a profound sense of dissociation; the memory feels like it
belongs to someone else rather than to him, as if he is watching a stranger’s ordeal. This feeling
of alienation from his own past only serves as further evidence that the person he was before the
trip is gone forever.

This vicious cycle has constricted his life to a small, manageable loop of home and familiar work
tasks. He passes all new client accounts to junior colleagues, a move that has not gone unnoticed by
his superiors. He came to therapy because his manager has scheduled a performance review, and Kenji
is certain he will be fired. He is terrified of losing the last pillar of his old life, yet the
thought of discussing his future performance feels impossible. He hopes therapy can offer some way
to quiet the noise, but he is deeply afraid that he is right—that the damage is permanent and that
he is beyond repair.
