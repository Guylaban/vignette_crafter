Ananya, a 28-year-old tax consultant, presented for therapy six months after an event that had, in
her words, “shattered everything.” Until that point, her life had been defined by a predictable and
orderly climb. She excelled at her firm, appreciated the logic of spreadsheets, and was saving for a
down payment on her own condo. That sense of order was destroyed the night she was house-sitting for
a friend. Two men broke into the suburban home, binding her hands and feet and leaving her on the
living room floor for what felt like an eternity while they methodically ransacked the property.
They were never caught, a fact that Ananya repeats with a flat, hollow finality. For hours, she lay
there, listening to their movements, the scrape of furniture, their low murmurs. The most terrifying
moments were when one of them would walk past her, a sudden shadow eclipsing the dim light from the
street, his proximity a silent, potent threat.

In the months since, the world has become a landscape of potential threats. Ananya’s small
apartment, once her sanctuary, now feels porous and unsafe. Every night, she lies awake, straining
to decipher the building’s ambient noises, certain that the faint creak of floorboards or the
distant rattle of a door is the prelude to another intrusion. At her office, the open-plan layout
has become an exercise in constant vigilance. She feels a profound sense of exposure and can only
concentrate if she secures a desk in the corner of the room, her back pressed firmly against the
wall, where no one can approach her from behind. This behaviour has not gone unnoticed, drawing
quiet remarks from colleagues and a concerned inquiry from her manager about her recent dip in
productivity.

The persistent state of physical alert has become inextricably linked to a cascade of painful self-
recrimination. A simple, unexpected movement—a coworker rounding the corner of her cubicle too
quickly—is enough to make her flinch violently, her heart hammering against her ribs as if in the
presence of a predator. This visceral reaction of terror is immediately followed by a wave of
profound shame. She feels a deep disgust for her body’s betrayal, for its public display of
weakness. In these moments, she is certain that the assault happened because of some fundamental
flaw within her; she had been too compliant, too frozen, too weak to fight back. Each startled jump
serves as fresh, irrefutable proof of this self-blame. The knowledge that the men responsible faced
no consequences only reinforces her belief that the world is inherently unjust and that she is
uniquely vulnerable within it, a conviction that fuels her hypervigilance in a tightening loop.

Ananya’s life has contracted around these fears. She has stopped going to the gym, avoids crowded
coffee shops, and makes excuses to turn down invitations from friends, unable to bear the anxiety of
sitting in a restaurant without a clear view of the entrance. She came to therapy after receiving a
formal performance warning at work. Her manager, while sympathetic, noted her distraction and
increasing isolation. Ananya feels trapped, not by assailants, but by her own mind. She is exhausted
by the constant threat monitoring and crushed by the weight of her self-blame. What she wants, she
explained with tears welling in her eyes, is to feel safe in her own skin again, to be able to sit
in a room and simply breathe without waiting for the next attack.
