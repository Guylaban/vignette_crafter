Clara, a 54-year-old chef renowned for her robust, comforting cuisine, now moves through her own
kitchen with the caution of a trespasser. The assault occurred nine months ago, not in a dark alley
but in the familiar, flour-dusted confines of her restaurant's dry storeroom. The perpetrator was
not a stranger but a trusted supplier, a man she had known and worked with for nearly two decades.
He had trapped her between shelves of olive oil and canned tomatoes, and the memory of it doesn't
exist as a story she can tell. Instead, she reports that she is unable to put the frightening event
into words; it lives in her as a series of disconnected, sensory fragments: the rough texture of a
burlap sack of rice against her cheek, the scent of dried oregano, the crushing weight on her chest.

In the months that followed, the world outside her home began to feel menacing and unpredictable.
She came to believe that you can never truly know who will harm you, a conviction that hardened into
a general mistrust of others. This core belief now governs her perception of threat. In her bustling
kitchen, a line cook’s sudden movement to grab a pan can cause her to flinch violently, her body
reacting as if to an imminent attack. She finds herself constantly scanning the faces of delivery
men and new staff, a state of hypervigilance that leaves her exhausted by the end of a shift. This
feeling of being perpetually on guard only deepens her certainty that danger is everywhere, while
also making her memories more accessible; a sudden clatter from the dish pit is enough to trigger a
visceral flash of the storeroom floor. Her heightened sense of threat also makes her more sensitive
to potential triggers, causing her to shrink from any man who stands too close.

The triggers themselves have become potent entry points into her cycle of distress. Being alone with
men, even her trusted sous-chef of ten years, now feels unbearable. A conversation that veers into
personal territory or a well-meaning touch on the arm from her husband, Mark, can instantly activate
the raw memory fragments. When these moments occur, she doesn’t just remember; she feels her body
involuntarily trying to assume the same position it was in during the assault, a terrifying
reenactment she cannot control. These intrusive experiences serve to powerfully reinforce her
negative appraisals, each fragment acting as fresh evidence for her belief that she was betrayed by
someone she trusted and that, by extension, people are fundamentally untrustworthy.

This constellation of beliefs, in turn, fuels the very triggers she tries to avoid. Because she is
convinced that people cannot be trusted, any attempt at emotional closeness from her husband is no
longer perceived as affection but as a potential threat, which she then recoils from. Her conviction
that she is unsafe seems to keep the memories on a hair trigger, ready to surface with the slightest
provocation. The cycle is relentless and self-sustaining. A trigger, like a male vendor asking a
question, will evoke not only a fragment of memory but also the immediate, searing thought that she
was betrayed. This appraisal then amplifies her sense of ambient threat, making her scan the room
and startle at the slightest noise.

The consequences have rippled through every part of her life. Her marriage is strained by the
physical and emotional distance she now requires. At work, her command of the kitchen, once her
greatest pride, has been eroded by fear. She delegates ordering to her manager to avoid speaking
with male suppliers and has rearranged the prep stations so she never has her back to the door. She
came to therapy after a particularly difficult service where she yelled at her sous-chef for
startling her, an outburst that left them both shaken. What frightens Clara most is the loss of
herself—the warm, confident woman who found joy in feeding others has been replaced by someone she
no longer recognizes, and she is terrified she will never find her way back.
