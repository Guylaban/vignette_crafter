Eleanor has lived in the shadow of the same tall pines for fifty years, first as a young forester
mapping tracts of wilderness, and now, at seventy-three, as the senior consultant for the regional
park service. The woods had always been her sanctuary, a place of profound and restorative quiet.
But the quiet no longer feels safe. The trauma dates back to her childhood, to the years she spent
in her aunt’s care after her parents’ death. The abuse was unpredictable, delivered without warning
in the stifling silence of the remote farmhouse. The only precursors were the subtle sounds of the
house settling: the groan of a specific floorboard outside her door, the heavy tick of the
grandfather clock in the hall. For Eleanor, silence became the sound of waiting for pain, and the
presence of a woman alone with her was the guarantee it would arrive.

For decades, her work was a functional antidote. The vastness of the forest dwarfed her history, and
the physical exertion of her job kept her body too tired for anxiety. But in the years since her
retirement from full-time fieldwork, the old fears have resurfaced with a vengeance. She has come to
believe that the world is a fundamentally dangerous place and that you can never truly know who will
harm you. This core conviction has poisoned her perception of others, particularly women. A new
female colleague’s friendly offer to carpool to a remote site, or a neighbour stopping by with a
pie, is no longer seen as kindness but as a potential threat. The appraisal that anyone could be a
source of harm has made her exquisitely sensitive to the trigger of being alone with any woman, her
mind instantly flashing to her aunt’s cold expression in the silent house.

This pervasive sense of threat has dictated her actions, forcing her into a series of desperate,
maladaptive strategies. Believing she is unsafe on her own, Eleanor can no longer tolerate being
alone in her small cabin. Most nights, she drives into the nearest town, wandering the aisles of a
24-hour superstore until dawn, the constant hum of fluorescent lights andodyne pop music a shield
against the quiet. The conviction that her life has been irrevocably destroyed by the trauma fuels a
different kind of escape: compulsive online shopping. Clicking ‘buy now’ on expensive, unnecessary
items—a telescope she’ll never use, artisanal French cookware for a woman who barely eats—provides a
fleeting sense of control and comfort in a life she feels is broken beyond repair. She has also
stopped her daily walks, as the thought of exercising and feeling her own heart pound in her chest
has become terrifyingly linked to the panic of her childhood.

These strategies have, in turn, reinforced her most painful beliefs. The more she avoids the
solitude of her cabin and the physical rigors of the forest, the more her identity as a strong,
self-reliant forester crumbles. Staring at her mounting credit card debt and the boxes of useless
purchases cluttering her home, she feels a profound sense of shame and dehumanization, as if she is
not a person with agency but merely an object propelled by impulse and fear. This reinforces her
belief that her life is indeed destroyed. Her constant immersion in noise to avoid being alone has
also had a paradoxical effect. Now, a sudden silence—the power cutting out, the moment between songs
on the radio—has become a potent trigger in itself. The abrupt absence of sound is no longer
peaceful but a signal of immediate danger, the very state she has been fighting to escape.

Eleanor sought therapy after her supervisor, a kind woman in her thirties, had to gently inform her
that her persistent refusal to conduct solo backcountry surveys—a core part of her consultancy
role—was making her position untenable. The thought of losing her work, the last vestige of the life
she once had, was more terrifying than the thought of facing the past. She sits in the therapy room,
tense and watchful, and admits that her greatest hope is a simple one: to be able to sit in her own
rocking chair, on her own porch, and listen to the sound of the wind in the pines without feeling
certain that someone is about to hurt her.
