Elena is seventy-eight, and her world has shrunk to the size of her small apartment and the headset
she wears for eight hours a day. It has been three years since the bombing at the Plaza de las
Flores, three years since she lost her husband, Ricardo. She took the call center job to fill the
silence, but often the voices of strangers only bring the noise rushing back. Last Tuesday, a
caller, a young man, was distraught over the recent death of his father. As he spoke of the funeral,
Elena felt a familiar pressure build behind her eyes. It wasn't a story that came to her, but a
storm of sensations: the deafening crack that wasn't thunder, the smell of dust and something
metallic, the impossible weight of Ricardo’s silence next to her. The memory has no words, only a
series of brutal, disjointed images she can never quite assemble. Overwhelmed by this sensory flood,
she felt a wave of profound helplessness wash over her; she was certain that she was fundamentally
incapable of preventing terrible things from happening, then or now.

This certainty governs her life. The moment a trigger appears—a story of loss on the phone, a sudden
twinge in the vertebrae of her neck from the old injury—her first instinct is to withdraw. The
conversation with the grieving young man prompted her to immediately invent a technical issue to end
the call. That evening, when her daughter called to arrange a visit with the grandchildren, Elena
claimed she was feeling unwell. This retreat into solitude has become her primary strategy for
survival. She believes that by avoiding any deep emotional connection, she can prevent the pain of
another potential loss. But this constant avoidance only deepens her conviction that she has failed
in her most important role. She was a wife, and now she is a grandmother, yet she feels she is
failing at both—failing to properly honor her husband’s memory and failing to offer her family the
warmth of a matriarch.

Her attempts to keep the memory at bay have paradoxically given it more power. She refuses to talk
about that day, and her family, respecting her pain, has learned not to ask. She takes a bus route
that adds forty minutes to her commute just to avoid passing anywhere near the rebuilt plaza. She
dreads the anniversary, a day she spends with the phone unplugged and the curtains drawn. This
careful avoidance has cemented the memory in its pre-verbal state; it remains a terrifying collage
of sensory fragments, forever unprocessed. The gaps in her recollection—the minutes just before and
after the blast—are not empty spaces but black holes of self-blame. The fact that she cannot
construct a linear story feels like a profound personal failing, proof that she is broken beyond
repair. This wordless, fractured memory constantly reinforces her belief that her life is over, that
there is no future beyond this cycle of fear and remembrance.

The cycle is relentlessly self-sustaining. The memory itself has become a trigger. Sometimes, for no
reason at all, a flash of the memory—the feeling of being thrown, the sharp impact—will surface, and
the chronic pain in her neck will flare into an acute, searing ache. This physical pain then acts as
a potent reminder of the event, pulling the other sensory fragments along with it, which in turn
reinforces her feelings of hopelessness and her need to withdraw further. The more she isolates
herself to manage these episodes, the more the memories fester, unshared and unexamined. Her
avoidance has become a cage, built to protect her, but it has only served to lock her in with the
very things she fears most.

It was her granddaughter, Sofia, who finally broke through. After weeks of unanswered calls, the
ten-year-old showed up at the door alone, holding a drawing of the two of them. "Abuela," she had
asked, her voice small, "why don't you want to see me anymore?" Seeing the hurt in her eyes, Elena
felt the full weight of her self-imposed isolation. It was one thing to believe she had failed
Ricardo, but to see the tangible proof of her failure as a grandmother standing before her was
unbearable. She has come to therapy not with hope, but with a quiet desperation, terrified that the
memories will consume her, but even more terrified of losing what little connection she has left.
