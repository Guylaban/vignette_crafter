Arthur had spent forty years of his life believing in the fundamental order of things. As a
prosecutor, he saw the law not just as a profession but as a bulwark against chaos, a system where
actions had predictable consequences. That conviction shattered six months ago on a Tuesday evening.
Walking home, he saw a group of teenagers vandalizing a bus stop and, drawing on a lifetime of
authority, told them to stop. The situation escalated into a shouting match. When two police
officers arrived, they saw an enraged older man and a group of youths. They heard only noise, a
cacophony of overlapping accusations. Arthur tried to explain, to state his name and profession, but
his words were lost in the incomprehensible chatter. The officers saw only a threat. One grabbed
him, twisting his arm behind his back with a force that sent a shock of pain and humiliation through
him. Pinned against his own car, listening to commands he couldn't distinguish from the general din,
the bedrock of his world—that authority was rational and order would prevail—crumbled into dust.

In the weeks that followed, the world began to feel fundamentally unsafe. The courthouse, once his
sanctuary of logic, became a landscape of potential threats. The low, overlapping hum of
conversations in the cafeteria, once background noise, now registered as the same incomprehensible
chatter from that night, immediately putting him on edge. The sight of a bailiff’s uniform or a
passing police car would cause his heart to pound. When triggered by these reminders, his mind would
flood with a sense of imminent danger. He became superalert, his gaze constantly sweeping across
rooms, watching other people closely for any sign of aggression, any indication that the rules were
about to break down again. He was certain that another unpredictable, violent encounter was just
moments away, and he had to be the first to see it coming.

This pervasive sense of being on guard became exhausting, bleeding into every corner of his life. He
found it nearly impossible to fall asleep, his mind replaying every perceived slight or potential
danger from the day. To manage the feeling of threat that now shadowed him, Arthur developed a rigid
set of routines. At the office, whenever the ambient noise felt too chaotic, he would retreat to his
chambers and put on classical music so loud it drowned out everything else, a sensory shield against
the world. At home, he began constantly scanning the street from his window and checking the locks
on the doors. He started peppering his wife, Helen, with questions: "Did you hear that? Is
everything alright?"—a constant need for reassurance that was beginning to wear on her patience.
These weren't conscious decisions so much as desperate, automatic responses to a world he no longer
trusted.

His strategies, meant to restore a sense of safety, instead began to feed the fear. The more he
scanned his environment for signs of threat, the more he found. He would notice every minor instance
of public misbehavior—a driver running a red light, someone cutting in line—and each one served as a
fresh trigger, a reminder of the moral violation that started his ordeal. This constant checking
made him hyper-aware of any police presence, spotting cruisers blocks away that he would have
previously ignored. In turn, each trigger would amplify his feeling of being watchful and on guard,
creating a vicious, self-sustaining loop. His attempts to control the threat were only making him
more sensitive to it, ensuring he was never at peace. His world had shrunk to his home and his
office, and even those places no longer felt secure.

convinced the room was closing in. The visible loss of composure in the place he commanded respect

danger in every shadow. He told his wife he just wanted to feel normal again, to walk into a
courtroom and not feel like a target.
