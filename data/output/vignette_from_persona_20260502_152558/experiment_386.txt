Kwame, a 62-year-old stockbroker with a career built on calculated risk, now found his life governed
by a terror he could neither calculate nor control. It had been seven months since his daughter,
Amara, was killed in a multi-car pile-up on the interstate. He remembered the phone call, the drive
to the hospital, but the hours that followed existed only in jagged, disjointed pieces. He had been
asked to identify her body, a formality that had fractured his world. The memory of that moment was
not a coherent narrative he could access; instead, it was a collection of sensory landmines. The
institutional green of the morgue walls, the chemical smell of antiseptic, the impossible cold of
the room—these fragments would appear suddenly, without warning, and then vanish, leaving him
breathless. When he tried to deliberately remember Amara’s smile or the sound of her laugh, he found
the path blocked; all that was left was the horror.

These sudden shards of memory were intolerable, each one triggering a fresh wave of guilt and shame.
The image of the jagged tear in her favourite blue coat, visible even beneath the sterile sheet,
would flash into his mind while he was reviewing market data, and suddenly he would be back in that
freezing room, the fluorescent lights humming overhead. He felt the cold seep into his bones, a
physical sensation so real it made him shiver in his heated office. It was this visceral re-
experiencing that drove him to try and seize control of his own mind. He began to actively avoid
anything that might remind him of Amara: he packed away her photographs, stopped listening to the
music she loved, and found himself flinching at the sight of any young woman with her hair tied back
the way Amara used to wear hers. This constant effort to wall off his grief was exhausting, a second
full-time job layered on top of his demanding one.

The intense horror of the flashbacks also fueled a relentless, looping internal monologue. He would
spend hours staring blankly at the trading screen, not seeing the numbers, but going over and over
the same unanswerable questions: Why her? Why not the driver in the other car? Why had he, her
father, been the one to survive to see her like that? This rumination became a desperate attempt to
find meaning in the chaos, but it only deepened his guilt. In response to these overwhelming
feelings, he doubled down on his efforts to suppress the trauma, trying to force his thoughts into
neat, manageable boxes. He tried to think only of stock prices and client portfolios, a strategy
that invariably backfired. The harder he tried not to think about the morgue, the more vividly the
images would intrude upon his day, a cruel paradox that left him feeling powerless.

This constant battle to suppress and avoid had a corrosive effect on the memory itself. His attempts
to push the trauma away prevented any kind of natural processing, cementing the memory's fragmented
nature. It felt to him as if the memories were stored like broken glass; trying to grasp one piece
only made the others cut deeper. The high emotional charge of his fear and guilt seemed to burn the
fragments into his mind while simultaneously preventing him from assembling them into a whole story.
The more he fought the images, the more disjointed his memory of that day became, and the more
potent the individual fragments felt when they ambushed him.

The cycle had tightened its grip over the months. A sudden memory fragment—the blue of her
coat—would trigger a flashback, which would in turn fuel his frantic efforts to suppress the thought
and avoid the colour blue. This avoidance only ensured that the next time he saw that colour, the
reaction would be just as strong, if not stronger. The shame and horror he felt led him to take
risks he never would have before, both in his car and on the trading floor. He started driving too
fast, a grim recklessness shadowing the event that took his daughter. At work, he made wildly
speculative trades, a nihilistic impulse that felt like a form of self-punishment. He had alienated
his ex-wife, Amara’s mother, unable to bear seeing his own grief reflected in her eyes. It was after
a particularly reckless trade cost his firm a significant amount of money that his manager suggested
he take some time off. That, combined with a near-miss on the highway the same evening, was the
final straw. Kwame came to therapy believing he was losing his mind, terrified that the fragments of
his worst day would eventually consume what little was left of his life.
