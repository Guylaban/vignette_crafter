to the size of her small apartment and the back corridors of the medical center where she worked. It
had been two years since her return from a humanitarian mission that ended in her capture and
detention by a local militia. She spoke of that time in fragments, her hands twisting in her lap.
The memories, she explained, were not a story she could tell, but a collection of physical
sensations that lived in her body. She knew what had happened, but when she reached for the words,
they dissolved, leaving only the raw, non-verbal imprint of the events.

During her month-long captivity, she was held in a windowless room. The men who interrogated her
wore mismatched military fatigues, and the primary sounds were the scraping of a metal chair on
concrete and the jangling of keys outside her door. They would hold her down during questioning, the
pressure of their hands on her arms and shoulders a constant, terrifying weight. These sensory
details are the hooks on which her current life is caught. The memory of these events is so
disjointed that she cannot form a coherent narrative; when she tries to explain it, she loses the
thread, trailing off mid-sentence as the physical sensations overwhelm her ability to speak.

This fragmented, unspeakable nature of the memory fuels her hypervigilance and makes everyday life a
minefield. Because she cannot place the trauma in a verbal past, any related sensory input feels
like an immediate, present threat. The sight of a hospital security guard in his blue uniform can
send a jolt of panic through her, forcing her to duck into an empty room until he passes. A well-
meaning parishioner once took her arm to guide her, and the feeling of being physically restrained
sent such a wave of terror through Anjali that she recoiled, leaving the woman confused and hurt.
The sound of the janitor’s keys in the hallway is no longer innocuous; it is a direct signal of
danger that makes her freeze at her desk.

The sheer overwhelm of these wordless flashbacks has driven Anjali into a life of profound
avoidance. The inability to articulate her experience means she cannot share her pain, and this has
led her to systematically cut ties with friends she had before the mission. Their concerned
questions felt impossible to answer, the gap between her silent horror and their normal lives too
vast to bridge. At work, she feels intensely vulnerable in the hospital's large, glass-walled
atrium, and now avoids it entirely, taking a winding route through the basement to get to her
office. This avoidance has, in turn, cemented the memory's power. By refusing to encounter the
triggers or speak about the experience, she has given the non-verbal fragments no chance to be
integrated into a larger story of survival. The memory remains a raw, sensory threat, never
processed, never placed in the past.

rise—the pressure on her arms, the smell of rust and damp concrete—the terror is so absolute and so
wordless that she feels she is dissolving. In those moments, she has learned to dig her fingernails

feel. The physical sting is a desperate anchor, a way to silence the unprocessed horror for a little
while longer, but it leaves the memory untouched, waiting to surge back. Her ex-husband, unable to

a statement, leaving the family alone in their most desperate hour. She is terrified that the trauma
has broken something in her permanently, but a small part of her still hopes to one day find the
