Keanu, a 65-year-old project manager with a quiet and methodical demeanor, first sought therapy
eight years after the death of his wife, and nearly two decades after the event that he now
identified as the true origin of his distress. The trauma was not a single moment of violence, but a
week of agonizing uncertainty when his then-twelve-year-old son, Leo, was hospitalized with a
sudden, severe respiratory failure. Keanu had been home with him that afternoon, and he remembers
the terrifying speed with which Leo’s wheezing turned into a desperate, silent fight for air. He had
hesitated for a moment, wondering if he was overreacting, a delay he now measures in heartbeats and
lost chances. The chaotic ambulance ride, the blur of doctors and nurses, and the excruciating wait
for a diagnosis in the sterile hospital corridor had fused into a single, searing memory of his own
failure.

In the years that followed Leo’s full recovery, Keanu’s relief was steadily corroded by a
persistent, private guilt. He had come to believe, with unshakeable certainty, that the severity of
the illness was his fault; he should have acted sooner, he should have protected his son and he had
failed. This core conviction that he could not trust himself to do the right thing in a crisis began
to dictate his life. This deep self-doubt drove him to systematically dismantle any connection to
the medical world. He began avoiding the hospital where Leo was treated, taking long detours on his
commute. He stopped scheduling his own annual physicals, unable to face the fluorescent lights and
antiseptic smell without a wave of nausea. He poured all his energy into his work, keeping his mind
relentlessly occupied with timelines and deliverables, a conscious strategy to suppress the
intrusive thoughts of that week.

This avoidance, however, only strengthened his sense of inadequacy. By refusing to engage with even
routine medical care, he reinforced his own narrative that he was incapable and untrustworthy, that
he would crumble at the first sign of a crisis. The self-blame made him acutely sensitive to
reminders of his perceived failure, especially the people who had been there. Running into one of
Leo’s former doctors at the grocery store could ruin his entire week, the man’s face serving as a
fresh indictment. His constant state of anxiety also made his own body feel like a source of danger;
any fleeting moment of breathlessness from climbing the stairs felt like a terrifying echo of his
son’s struggle, turning a simple physical sensation into a potent trigger.

Now, the cycle is deeply entrenched and governs his daily existence. A glimpse of an ambulance on
the street is enough to make his chest tighten and a cold sweat break out across his skin, flooding
him with the same horror he felt in the emergency room all those years ago. At his job, where he was
once praised for his calm under pressure, he is now tormented by delays. A team member submitting a
report an hour late is no longer a minor inconvenience; to Keanu, it is a sign that something
terrible has happened, a catastrophe he has failed to foresee. This sense of imminent threat, fueled
by his belief that he is fundamentally negligent, keeps him in a state of high alert, which in turn
makes him even more likely to notice triggers in his environment.

It was a recent health scare of his own—a persistent chest pain he ignored for weeks—that finally
brought him to therapy. The fear of what it might be was eclipsed only by the profound shame of his
own inaction. He realized his avoidance was no longer a shield but a cage, one that had cost him
peace of mind and was now threatening his health. He came to his first session not with a clear
goal, but with a quiet, desperate hope to escape the suffocating loop of guilt and fear, and to one
day believe he could be trusted to care for himself, let alone anyone else.
