Priya, a 31-year-old high school history teacher, presented for therapy six months after a car
accident that had left her life feeling fractured and unrecognizable. She spoke in a low, hesitant
voice, as if afraid the words themselves might cause something terrible to happen. The vibrant,
confident educator her husband described was gone, replaced by a woman who seemed to be holding her
breath, waiting for the next catastrophe. It was his gentle insistence, after months of watching her
withdraw into herself, that finally brought her through the door.

The event itself replayed not as a film, but as a series of stark, silent photographs in her mind.
She had been driving a student, Liam, home from a debate club practice during a sudden downpour. As
a favour. She remembered the slick, dark gleam of the road, the rhythmic thump of the windshield
wipers, and the quick, fateful glance down at her phone for directions. The next image was of a
spiderweb of cracks across the windshield, followed by the sight of Liam’s small, still body in the
passenger seat. The memory was always accompanied by the cloying, chemical smell of the airbag’s
deployment mixed with gasoline, a scent that could ambush her at any time. She explained that when
these images came, it wasn't like remembering something that was over; it felt like she was right
back in the driver's seat, living the impact again and again.

In the weeks that followed the accident, as Liam began his slow recovery, a set of beliefs took root
in Priya’s mind with unshakable certainty. She was solely to blame. “If I hadn’t looked at the
phone, if I’d just been a better driver, a more responsible person, none of it would have happened,”
she stated, her voice flat with conviction. This core belief in her own culpability metastasized,
poisoning her view of herself. The teacher who had once won awards for her engaging classroom style
now saw herself as fundamentally broken and no longer capable of the work that had once defined her.
She felt marked by the event, convinced that her colleagues and students could see a darkness in her
that set her apart, making her a permanent outsider in her own life.

This profound sense of herself as a guilty fraud became the lens through which she experienced the
world, twisting neutral events into threats. Her conviction that she was a failure made any positive
feedback at school feel like a spotlight on her unworthiness. When her principal praised her for her
dedication just last week, the words didn't land as encouragement. Instead, she felt a surge of
panic, a sense that she was being exposed as an imposter. This feeling of being undeservingly
praised would invariably unlock the memory, the principal’s kind face dissolving and being replaced
by the snapshot of the shattered windshield. Even moments of simple happiness with her husband felt
dangerous, as if joy was a privilege she had forfeited. The positive emotion would create a
confusing dissonance, a sense of wrongness that would again drag her thoughts back to the accident.

The memories themselves now dictate her behaviour, creating their own triggers. After being ambushed
by an intrusive image of the crash, Priya is left in a state of raw-edged vigilance for hours. The
world feels saturated with danger, and she finds herself scanning for anything that might connect
back to her failure. In the staff lounge at school, she stiffens whenever her colleagues begin
discussing weekend traffic or a minor fender-bender. The casual talk about accidents feels like a
direct accusation, and she is certain that if she stays, someone will find out what she did. This
fear has driven her to eat lunch alone in her empty classroom, reinforcing the very isolation she
dreads and confirming her belief that she is an outsider who no longer belongs.

Priya sought help because the life she had built was shrinking daily. She had stepped down from
running the debate club, unable to bear the responsibility. She found herself calling in sick to
work more and more, terrified of the ordinary interactions that now felt like traps. The joy she
once found in teaching has been replaced by a constant, humming dread. She is afraid that her self-
assessment is correct—that she is no longer the person she was, and that the capable, happy woman
she remembers is gone for good.
