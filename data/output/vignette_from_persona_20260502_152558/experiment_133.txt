Adebayo had always found a quiet dignity in order. For thirty years as a professor of post-colonial
literature, his life had been one of carefully curated syllabi, meticulously organized bookshelves,
and the predictable rhythm of the academic calendar. At fifty-nine, he was a man who believed in the
power of the mind to impose structure on chaos. That belief shattered eight months ago, on the day
of the storm that took his son, Femi. He hadn’t been there when the flash flood swept Femi’s car
from the low-lying bridge, but he had arrived at the scene just minutes later, joining the crowd
staring at the churning, brown water where the road should have been. The car was a dark shape,
half-submerged. The air was thick with the smell of river mud and gasoline, a scent now permanently
etched into his memory.

In the months that followed, Adebayo’s well-ordered world began to flood. The triggers were
everywhere. A simple rainstorm would send him rushing to close the blinds in his office, the sight
of water streaming down the glass feeling like a personal threat. Walking across campus, the smell
of damp earth after the sprinklers had run was enough to make his heart hammer against his ribs.
These sensory cues would ignite an immediate and overwhelming sense of danger. He described it as
his body betraying his mind; a cold dread would seize him, his muscles tensing for an impact that
never came, all before he could even process what he had seen or smelled. This physical alarm was
inseparable from a wave of horror and guilt, a crushing certainty that something terrible was about
to happen again and he would be just as powerless to stop it.

This constant state of perceived threat has, in turn, made him exquisitely sensitive to the world
around him. He feels unable to relax, even in the safety of his own home. He sits in his armchair,
book open on his lap, but his hearing is tuned to the gurgle of the pipes, his eyes drawn to the
reflection of the sky in the window, scanning for clouds. Because he is always anticipating danger,
his mind relentlessly hunts for the very things that cause his distress. The general stress of the
semester—grading papers, department meetings, the pressure to publish—now acts as its own trigger,
priming his system for high alert. In this state of anxious vigilance, the scent of a puddle in the
faculty parking lot or the sight of a campus pond becomes magnified, confirming the danger he
already feels. His hypervigilance guarantees he will find the evidence of threat he is looking for,
locking him in a self-sustaining cycle of fear.

The consequences have been profound. Adebayo, once a commanding presence in the lecture hall, now
finds his focus fractured. He has cancelled classes on rainy days, citing a sudden illness, unable
to bear the sight of students coming in with wet coats and umbrellas. He avoids the university’s
main library, with its large, ground-level windows overlooking the river that cuts through town. The
constant, exhausting hum of anxiety has made the deep concentration required for his research
impossible. He feels a profound shame over this, a sense that he is failing his students and his own
legacy. His body’s reaction to danger, happening before he has time to think, feels like a personal
failing, a loss of the intellectual control that has defined his entire identity.

He decided to seek help after an incident last week. A promising graduate student had come to his
office, distraught over a family emergency and worried about her academic standing. As she began to
cry, the sheer force of her negative emotion triggered his own. He felt a surge of horror so intense
it left him breathless, the student’s tears blurring with his own memory of the muddy water. He
abruptly cut the meeting short, mumbling an apology, and locked the door behind her, overwhelmed by
a sense of imminent catastrophe. It was then he realized the flood was not just in his memory; it
was happening inside him, and he was drowning. He came to therapy not with hope, but with a quiet
desperation to find some dry land, to once again feel safe in a world that now seems saturated with
peril.
