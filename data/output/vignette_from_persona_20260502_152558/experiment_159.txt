Layla, at fifty-one, moved through the world as if braced for an impact that never came. For
decades, the source of this tension was a place in her mind she could not articulate, a memory that
had no words but lived vividly in her muscles and nerves. The trauma of her childhood was not a
story she could tell; it was a physical state she endured. It happened in the small, stuffy
apartment she lived in with her father after her parents’ divorce. His rage was a storm that could
gather without warning, usually in the narrow hallway or her tiny bedroom, spaces with no escape.
She remembers the feeling of his presence behind her, the sickening clench in her stomach just
before his heavy hand would fall on her shoulder or back. She cannot conjure a clear image of his
face in those moments, only the feeling of her own body tensing, her shoulders hunching in a futile
attempt to make herself smaller.

These bodily memories are the most potent triggers. During a meeting at the social ministry where
she works, the simple act of a male parishioner shifting his chair behind her can cause her back and
shoulders to lock into that same protective crouch. This sudden, involuntary reenactment is
immediately followed by a wave of intense, blinding rage that feels entirely disconnected from the
present moment. Her heart hammers against her ribs, and the small, quiet office suddenly feels like
a sealed tomb. She becomes certain, with a conviction that defies all logic, that she is in danger
and cannot get out. This surge of panic is so overwhelming that she will often cut the meeting
short, citing a sudden headache or an urgent task.

The constant feeling of being under threat has reshaped her life, particularly her work, which she
once loved. She has come to believe that safety is only possible through constant vigilance and
control. This belief dictates her every move. She arrives early to meetings to claim the chair
nearest the door, ensuring an unobstructed path for escape. In the last year, she has started
refusing to meet with anyone one-on-one, insisting that all consultations happen in groups or with
the door wide open, a policy that has caused friction with her colleagues and confusion among the
people she is supposed to help. Walking through the city, she is in a perpetual state of alert, her
head swivelling to check behind her every few steps. This hypervigilance is exhausting, but the
alternative—being caught unaware—feels unthinkable. Her irritability has sharpened to a knife’s
edge; a colleague placing a hand on her shoulder to get her attention can provoke a shockingly
aggressive outburst, leaving everyone unsettled.

This web of strategies and threat responses only serves to keep the trauma alive. The very act of
scanning a room for exits or refusing to be alone with a man reinforces the unspoken belief that the
danger she faced as a child is still present, lurking just out of sight. This constant state of
alert makes her more susceptible to the memories, creating a vicious, self-sustaining cycle. For
instance, her habit of checking behind her means she often catches a glimpse of a stranger walking
in the same direction, a sight that is enough to trigger the familiar, sickening tension in her
back, plunging her right back into the wordless horror. The memories fuel her sense of threat, which
in turn justifies her avoidance, and the avoidance keeps her primed for the memories to strike
again.

Over the last few months, the strategies have begun to fail her. Or rather, they have become so
extreme that they are now the primary problem. She has started calling in sick to avoid the office
altogether, her fear of being trapped in a small room with someone outweighing her professional
responsibilities. The isolation she has cultivated for protection has curdled into a profound
loneliness. It was a formal warning from her supervisor, citing complaints about her aggressive
behaviour and erratic attendance, that finally brought her to therapy. Layla is terrified of losing
her job, one of the few remaining sources of meaning in her life. But she is perhaps more terrified
of the therapy itself, of being asked to finally put words to a pain that has, for forty years,
spoken only through the language of her body.
