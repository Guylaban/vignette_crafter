eight months since the accident, and the person he was before—organized, ambitious, and quick to
laugh—felt like a stranger he could no longer access. The trauma had not happened to him, but to his
younger sister, and in many ways, that felt worse. He had been at the party, had seen her get into
the car with a friend who had been drinking. He remembered the sharp, sweet smell of spilled liquor
on the friend's shirt and the reckless laughter. Mateo had hesitated, a flicker of unease he’d
dismissed as overprotective. That flicker became the moment his life fractured. The call from the
hospital, the frantic drive, the funeral that followed—these events didn't exist for him as a
coherent story he could tell. Instead, they were a slideshow of sensory snapshots: the scent of
cheap whiskey, the sound of his mother’s weeping, the sight of freshly turned earth at the cemetery.

In the months that followed, Mateo came to believe that his life had been irrevocably destroyed.
This conviction that he was living in the ruins of a former self became the engine of his daily
suffering. Because he was certain that happiness was a thing of the past and that his life now
lacked any meaning, his body remained coiled in a state of high alert, braced for the next
catastrophe. His shoulders were perpetually tight, his jaw clenched, as if his muscles were
preparing for an impact that had already happened. This constant, exhausting state of physical
tension only served to reinforce his despair; the feeling of being perpetually unsafe was, to him,
undeniable proof that his life was over and he could never be at peace again. His body felt like a
false alarm he couldn't switch off, reacting to imagined dangers before his pharmacist’s mind could
logically assess the situation.

This deep-seated belief that he was broken and the world was meaningless made him exquisitely
sensitive to reminders of his failure. The sight of people behaving recklessly or without regard for
others would ignite a flash of rage and fear. At his pharmacy, the job he once loved, the smell of
alcohol-based hand sanitizer or the scent of liquor on a customer's breath was enough to pull him
from the present. The trigger wouldn't bring back a full narrative, but rather a searing, guilt-
laden fragment: the image of his sister’s smiling face in the passenger seat, a memory now

hyper-awareness.
