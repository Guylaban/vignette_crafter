Mei presents as a 49-year-old woman whose life has been progressively constricted by a fear she
cannot seem to escape. The trauma dates back seventeen years to her deployment with the National
Guard as a signals specialist. Stationed in a forward operating base, her world was the quiet,
humming interior of a tactical operations center—a space not unlike the server rooms she now
maintains as an IT support professional. The attack came without warning: a mortar strike that
collapsed part of the structure. The immediate aftermath was a sensory chaos of dust, the acrid
smell of burning electronics, and the disorienting darkness, followed by the rhythmic, deafening
thud of an incoming medevac helicopter that seemed to shake the very air she was struggling to
breathe.

For years, she believed she had left the experience behind her. But over the last decade, and
particularly since her divorce five years ago, the past has begun to intrude upon the present with
increasing force. The triggers are deeply embedded in her daily life. The low, resonant hum of a
helicopter passing over her office building is enough to send a jolt of ice through her veins,
immediately transforming her open-plan workspace into a tactical map of threats. This sudden
activation of her threat system is visceral; her heart hammers against her ribs, and she feels an
overwhelming compulsion to check behind her, her gaze sweeping the room for dangers only she can
perceive. After one such incident, a colleague approached her from behind to ask a question, and Mei
flinched so violently she knocked a monitor from its stand. She has come to believe that strangers,
even coworkers, represent a potential, unpredictable danger.

This pervasive sense of threat has, in turn, forced her into a life of rigid, self-protective
routines. To manage the overwhelming physical sensations and racing thoughts that follow a trigger,
Mei has developed a number of maladaptive strategies. She has become an expert at avoiding any
situation that might provoke a strong emotion, keeping conversations with colleagues brief and
superficial. She has steadfastly refused to speak about her service time with anyone, not even the
husband who eventually left, telling her he felt married to a ghost. When the feeling of being
hunted becomes too intense, particularly at night, she resorts to a more desperate measure: digging
her nails into the flesh of her arm until the sharp, localized pain is enough to eclipse the
intrusive memories and the feeling of imminent danger. This physical sting is a small, controllable
price to pay for a moment of mental quiet.

The cycle has become cruelly self-reinforcing. Her constant state of hypervigilance—the feeling of
being perpetually under threat—makes her exquisitely sensitive to potential triggers. Her world has
shrunk to a landscape of potential reminders, and she finds them everywhere, from the faint smell of
burnt toast in the breakroom to the drone of the servers she maintains. This heightened sensitivity
means she is triggered more often, which in turn amplifies her sense of being unsafe in the world.
Furthermore, the very strategies she uses to cope only serve to tighten the loop. Her avoidance of
coworkers and emotional topics has led her to spend more and more time alone in the server room, a
place whose humming machinery and smell of hot electronics is a constant, low-grade trigger. By
refusing to process the trauma, she ensures that the original sense of threat remains potent and
undiminished, confirming her deeply held conviction that the world is fundamentally unsafe.

Mei sought therapy after her manager called her in for a meeting, expressing concern about her
increasing isolation and noting that her colleagues found her "unapproachable." The feedback
terrified her; her job is the last piece of stable ground beneath her feet. She is exhausted by the
constant vigilance and the secrets she keeps. What she hopes for is simple, yet feels impossible: to
walk down the street without feeling like a target, to hear an aircraft overhead and think of
nothing more than the weather. Her greatest fear is that the woman she was before the attack is gone
for good, and that all she has left is this constant, exhausting watch.
