Liam, a 45-year-old journalist, described the last six months as living in the "epilogue of a story
I can't stop re-reading." The assignment had been his most ambitious: a deep dive into an insurgent
group in a volatile, remote region. It ended with three weeks in a dark, damp cellar. He was home
now, physically safe, but the cellar had come with him. The trauma began with the smell of cheap,
sour alcohol on the breath of the man who’d thrown the hood over his head, and it was that scent
that now seemed to follow him everywhere. His captors were often drunk, their movements
unpredictable, their grips bruising and rough. For days on end, he was forced to kneel with his head
bowed, a position that left a searing, chronic pain in his neck that has never fully subsided.

In the first few weeks after his release, Liam tried to force a return to normalcy. He went back to
the bustling newsroom, telling his editor and concerned colleagues he was fine. But he was not. He
had become a man perpetually braced for impact. Every unexpected sound—a door slamming down the
hall, a car backfiring on the street below his office window—sent a jolt of raw adrenaline through
him. He found himself constantly scanning faces in a crowd, mapping exits, judging every person who
walked too close. He knew, rationally, that he was in Manhattan, not a war zone, but his body
refused to believe it. This state of constant, exhausting hypervigilance felt like a necessary
shield, but it was also a magnet for the past. The more he remained on high alert, the more the
memories of the cellar found their way through the cracks in his concentration. The feeling of being
in imminent danger seemed to call them forth, and they would arrive without warning, hijacking his
senses.

The memories are not fleeting images but full sensory replays. He described them as a film that
plays over and over, one he cannot turn off. The darkness of the room, the rough texture of the
burlap hood, the muffled, angry voices—it all forces its way into his mind, bringing with it the
same gut-twisting terror he felt then. A deadline at work would be looming, and suddenly he would be
back in the cellar, convinced he was about to be executed. These intrusive replays have made his
world a minefield. After one of these episodes, his sensitivity to his triggers becomes painfully
acute. The dull ache in his neck, a constant companion, flares into a sharp, debilitating pain, as
if he were still being held down. He feels the ghost of his captors’ hands on him, and the most
innocent physical contact becomes unbearable. The memory primes his body for a threat that is no
longer there.

This vicious cycle now governs his life. His constant feeling of being on guard, of being unsafe,
not only triggers the memories but also makes him exquisitely sensitive to the world around him. His
wife, Anna, once tried to welcome him home with a hug from behind; he instinctively threw an elbow
back, nearly striking her before he registered who it was. The look on her face, a mixture of shock
and hurt, still haunts him. He saw he was becoming as unpredictable and frightening as his captors
had been. Now, he flinches from her touch, creating a painful distance between them. The smell of
alcohol on someone’s breath at a restaurant is enough to make him bolt for the door, his heart
hammering against his ribs. He has started taking only desk assignments at the paper, unable to face
the unpredictable nature of field reporting that was once the lifeblood of his career.

Liam came to therapy after Anna told him, her voice quiet with exhaustion, that she felt she was
living with a ghost. He fears she is right. He is trapped in that cellar, and he is terrified he
will lose his wife and the career he loves because he cannot find his way out. He doesn't want to be
a ghost; he wants to be the man he was before, a man who could hold his wife's hand and walk into a
crowded room without feeling like the walls were about to cave in. He is desperate, he said, to
finally end the story.
