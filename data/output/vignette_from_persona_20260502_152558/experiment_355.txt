feel like a profound failure. At twenty-one, she was a counselor, a role she had pursued after her
medical discharge from the army eight months prior. The trauma, however, had followed her home from
deployment, weaving itself into the fabric of her new life. It all traced back to a single night
patrol. The mission was straightforward until the ambush shattered the darkness. The memory wasn't a
clear narrative but a series of sensory fragments: the suffocating blackness of the alley, the
disorienting muzzle flashes, and the agonizingly slow approach of the evac helicopter, its rhythmic
chopping sound a promise of safety that arrived too late for the teammate she considered a brother.
That night, she had frozen, and she had never forgiven herself for it.

Back in the US, the city became a landscape of hidden threats. The sound of a news helicopter
passing over her apartment building would send a jolt of panic through her, her body tensing for an
impact that never came. Walking into a dimly lit room could trigger a wave of nausea. These moments
were more than just startling; they were confirmations of a dreadful belief she held about herself.
When a well-meaning relative asked about her "army family," the simple comment would ignite a spiral
of shame, reinforcing her certainty that there was something about her that made the event happen.
She was convinced that somebody else—a better soldier, a better person—would not have gotten into
that situation, would have reacted differently, would have saved him. This constant internal
verdict, triggered by the most mundane events, was exhausting.

The intense fear and guilt that followed these experiences taught Aisha that the only way to feel
safe was to retreat. She began to believe she was no longer capable of being the person she once
was, and this conviction led her to systematically dismantle her life. The triggers themselves
became a direct command to withdraw; after hearing an aircraft overhead, she would cancel plans to
meet friends, unable to bear the thought of conversation. More profoundly, her negative self-
appraisals became the architect of her isolation. Certain she had nothing to offer, she avoided
starting new friendships. She stopped returning calls from her old army buddies, unable to face the
people she used to socialize with before the event, believing they could see the fault etched into
her. She meticulously avoided watching TV programmes or films about the military, changing the
channel the moment a uniform appeared on screen. Her world, once expansive, was shrinking to the
size of her front door.

This self-imposed isolation became the very evidence that proved her failures were real. The more
she avoided the people who knew her before, the more it solidified her belief that she was no longer
capable of connection, confirming that the competent, confident woman who enlisted was gone forever.

withdrawal also paradoxically made her more vulnerable. By carefully curating her environment to
avoid any mention of combat, she lowered her tolerance for the unexpected. The sound of a

still collapsing—that finally brought her to therapy, carrying the quiet, desperate fear that she
was beyond repair.
