Fatima had cared for other people’s children for over forty years, and until eight months ago, she
had always believed she was good at it. At seventy-one, she was patient and watchful, qualities she
had once considered her greatest strengths. The traumatic event occurred on a Tuesday afternoon at
the local park, a place she had taken her young charges for years. She was pushing four-year-old Leo
on the swings when the shouting started near the entrance. Two men, their faces contorted with rage,
were arguing. One shoved the other, who stumbled back, pulled something from his jacket, and a scene
of chaos erupted. Fatima’s first and only instinct was to shield Leo. She unhooked him from the
swing, pulled him to her chest, and ran towards the far side of the playground, her heart hammering
against his small back. She didn't stop until they were hidden behind the equipment shed, her body
trembling as she covered his ears. She never saw exactly what happened, but the sounds—the
screaming, the sickening thud—were enough. By the time the police arrived, the men were gone.

In the weeks that followed, the world began to feel fundamentally unsafe. Walking to the grocery
store or even sitting in her own living room, Fatima found herself in a state of constant,
exhausting readiness. She was always on guard, her body tensed for a threat she couldn't name but
felt everywhere. This pervasive sense of danger began to curdle into a harsh self-judgment. If the
world was this perilous, she reasoned, then she had been naive and careless. The memory of her own
fear in the park felt like proof of her weakness. She became certain that a better, stronger person
would have seen the warning signs, would have done more. This belief that she had failed to protect
Leo, that she was simply inadequate for the task, settled deep in her bones. The news that the
police had no leads and had closed the case only sharpened this feeling; with no one held
accountable, the responsibility seemed to fall squarely on her.

This profound sense of inadequacy and the ever-present feeling of threat began to govern her life.
The thought of returning to the park filled her with a cold dread, so she started making excuses to
Leo’s parents, suggesting the library or the small yard behind their house instead. The family was
understanding, but Fatima knew it wasn’t what they wanted. To keep them happy and avoid any conflict
that might expose her fear, she became exceedingly agreeable, going along with any request to prove
her utility and feel a sliver of safety in their approval. Her mind became a battleground where she
fought constantly to suppress thoughts of the trauma, pushing away the images and sounds whenever
they surfaced, an effort that left her drained and irritable by the end of each day.

Her strategies, meant to keep her safe, only tightened the trap. Every time she invented a reason to
avoid the park, it felt less like a choice and more like a confirmation of her own cowardice. Her
people-pleasing with Leo’s parents left her feeling resentful and weak, reinforcing the conviction
that she was inadequate. The constant, failed effort to suppress her thoughts was the most damning
evidence of all; it proved to her that she couldn't even control her own mind, let alone protect a
child from the world’s dangers. This cycle of avoidance and self-blame left her feeling more
vulnerable than ever. Out in public, she scanned every face in a crowd, watching other people for
any sign of aggression. On her walk home from work, she found herself frequently looking over her
shoulder, checking to see if she was being followed, her keys clutched in her hand like a weapon.

It was a conversation with Leo’s mother that finally brought her to seek help. The family had
planned a birthday party for him in the park, and when Fatima explained she couldn’t be there, the
quiet disappointment in the mother’s eyes was more than she could bear. She realized her world had
shrunk to the size of her fear, and her identity as a competent, loving caregiver—the one thing she
had always been proud of—was crumbling. She came to therapy exhausted by her own vigilance, haunted
by a sense of failure, and desperate to find a way to feel safe again, not just for herself, but for
the children she still loved so dearly.
