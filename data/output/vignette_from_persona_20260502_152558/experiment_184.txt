quiet life in suburban America. The memories of his capture during the war in Vietnam were not a
story he could tell, but a series of sensory fragments that ambushed him. He was held for months in

until a searing pain shot through his neck and down his spine. The constant dampness and filth

the ache in his neck, the lurch in his stomach—became the anchors of his trauma, hooks waiting in
the present to drag him back to the past.

crumble. Now, at 69, a simple crick in his neck from working under a sink can trigger the entire
cascade. The physical pain is the key that unlocks the cell door, and suddenly he is no longer in a
client’s bathroom but back in the darkness, the memory replaying not as a narrative but as a film of
pure sensation: the guards’ low voices, the rough texture of the dirt floor, the feeling of utter
helplessness. This intrusive film immediately activates a profound sense of threat. His heart
hammers in his chest, and he feels acutely on guard, his body convinced the danger is imminent. Back
at home, his wife’s sudden movement to grab a pot from a high shelf will cause him to flinch
violently, his system screaming that an attack is coming.

To survive these daily ambushes, Binh developed a set of rigid strategies. The moment the memory
film begins to play, he forces himself to disconnect, to mentally float away from the feelings until
he is numb and distant. He has learned to see the images without truly feeling them, a detachment
that offers temporary relief but leaves the memory itself unprocessed and terrifying. This need for
control extends to his physical environment. When a trigger, such as being called to a job where he
knows he will be alone with a male client, is unavoidable, his mind immediately goes to escape.

the basement to the back door, a mental blueprint for flight that he carries with him at all times.

with work to allow his mind the space to remember.

These very strategies, however, have become the bars of a new prison. Constantly planning an escape

fractured them, making them impossible to articulate. When his wife, her face etched with concern,

Binh’s world has shrunk to the four walls of his house and the familiar routes to his few remaining

locked inside him without a key, yet he is even more terrified of spending the rest of his life as a
prisoner in his own mind.
