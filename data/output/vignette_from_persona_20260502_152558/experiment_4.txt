Omar, a bright and newly married 19-year-old, had secured a position as an environmental scientist
just months after his wedding. Yet, when he came to his first session, he spoke not of his
professional promise but of a quiet, encroaching dread that was poisoning every aspect of his life.
The memories that fueled this dread were not recent, but they felt as immediate as the hum of the
laboratory equipment he worked with each day. They were memories of his uncle’s house, of a
childhood punctuated by the sharp crack of a hand against his skin, always preceded by the same
sequence: a booming argument, his own terrified crying, and then the sudden, violent touch. The
logic of that terror had become the blueprint for his adult life.

This blueprint was most vivid at work. A few weeks ago, during a team meeting, two senior scientists
began to argue over a dataset. As their voices rose, Omar felt a familiar coldness spread through
his chest. He was no longer in a conference room in California; he was a small boy, cornered in a
hallway. He found himself minutely studying the face of the louder scientist, searching for the
tell-tale tightening of the jaw, the flicker in the eyes that he had learned meant an explosion was
imminent. He felt utterly exposed, a certainty growing in his mind that the man’s anger would soon
redirect itself towards him. This constant, exhausting process of threat assessment dictated his
every move. He felt unsafe unless his back was against a wall, and he would arrive at the lab
cafeteria early to claim the one corner booth that allowed him to see everyone who entered.

Because of this pervasive sense of being hunted, Omar had begun to systematically shrink his world.
The hypervigilance was too exhausting to maintain, so he started to eliminate the variables. He
began avoiding any situation that might lead to disagreement, nodding along to a colleague’s flawed
analysis just to end the conversation quickly. Soon, he was avoiding his colleagues altogether,
eating lunch at his desk and making excuses to miss team-building events. His supervisor noted his
withdrawal, misinterpreting it as a lack of commitment. The strategy of avoidance felt, to Omar,
like the only way to manage the constant, simmering threat he perceived in every interaction. He had
also developed a fear of being alone in the building, the silence of an empty lab feeling not
peaceful but charged with potential danger, a space where a threat could emerge from any direction
without warning.

This retreat, however, only created new dangers. His social isolation made him more sensitive, and
his constant scanning for hostility meant he often found it where it didn't exist. A colleague’s
furrowed brow of concentration was misinterpreted as anger, priming him to expect the very arguments
he was desperate to avoid. The most painful consequences, however, were at home with his wife,
Layla. His careful avoidance of conflict meant that small frustrations would go unmentioned until
they became insurmountable. His reflexive flinching from her unexpected touch—a simple hand on his
shoulder as he read—left her feeling rejected and confused. Last week, hurt by his emotional
distance, she had begun to cry quietly while they were watching a movie. For Omar, the sound was an
immediate, visceral trigger. He saw not his wife’s sadness, but an imminent danger, and his only
response was to retreat further, leaving the room and shutting himself in the bedroom.

It was this incident that finally brought him to therapy. Layla had followed him, explaining through
her tears that she felt married to a ghost. She could no longer live with the silence and the
distance. Omar now sits in the consulting room, explaining that the thing he fears most is losing
his wife, yet every instinct he has is telling him to run from the very emotional intimacy that
might save his marriage. He is trapped, he explained, by a past that feels more real than his
present, and he is terrified that he has already pushed away the one person who makes him feel safe.
