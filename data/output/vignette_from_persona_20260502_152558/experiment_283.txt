Elena is a 38-year-old woman who, until six months ago, described her life as simple and happy. She
worked the morning shift at a local bottling factory, a physically demanding but steady job, and
spent her evenings with her partner, Javier. Their life was built on small routines and shared
comforts. That life ended on a Tuesday afternoon in March. Javier collapsed in their living room,
clutching his chest. Elena remembers the frantic call to 911, the dispatcher’s voice a thin thread
of calm in her rising panic. The most vivid sensory detail from that day is the sound of the siren,
growing from a distant wail to a deafening shriek outside their window—a sound that first signaled
hope, and then, ultimately, failure, as the paramedics worked on Javier to no avail. He was
pronounced dead before they could get him to the hospital.

In the weeks that followed, Elena felt adrift in a fog of grief and disbelief. The funeral was a
blur of well-meaning relatives and hushed condolences, but the sight of the casket and the cemetery
gates became seared into her mind. She began to believe her life had been completely destroyed by
the trauma. The future she had imagined with Javier was gone, and she felt a profound sense of
misery had replaced the happiness she once took for granted. This conviction settled deep within
her, a bleak certainty that she was no longer the person she used to be. This belief that she was
fundamentally broken and alone made her hyper-aware of any potential reminder of her loss. She began
to scan conversations for any mention of illness or death, bracing herself for the impact. At work,
a coworker talking about a sick parent would send a jolt of panic through her, making the factory
floor, once a place of mundane camaraderie, feel like a field of emotional landmines.

Now, any trigger sends her spiraling. When a siren wails past the factory during her shift, the
sound slices through the mechanical din and transports her back to her living room floor. The
immediate, overwhelming thought is a direct confirmation of her deepest conviction: that her life
had been irrevocably destroyed. In response to this terror, she immediately begins to run through
the events of that night, an obsessive, frantic review she hopes will somehow make sense of it all.
She dissects every detail: *Did I call 911 fast enough? Did I describe the symptoms correctly? Was
there something I missed in the days leading up to it?* She will ask her sister the same questions
over and over, seeking reassurance that she did everything she could, that she isn't going crazy.

These strategies, meant to provide comfort, only tighten the knot of her distress. The endless
analysis never brings peace; it only cements her sense of failure and reinforces the belief that she
is now, and will always be, a miserable person. Her avoidance of anything that might remind her of
Javier—changing the radio station to avoid a song they both loved, turning down invitations from
friends they shared—has only amplified her profound isolation, proving the very belief that she no
longer belongs anywhere. The world that once felt like home now seems hostile and alien. The cycle
has become relentless: a trigger confirms her life is destroyed, which prompts a desperate,
repetitive analysis, which in turn deepens her conviction that she is broken and alone, making her
even more sensitive to the next trigger.

Elena sought therapy after a formal warning at work. Her supervisor noted her frequent, unscheduled
breaks and her panicked reactions to loud noises, including the factory’s own emergency alarms. She
is terrified that she will lose her job, the last piece of stability in her life. What she fears
most, she explained through tears in our first session, is that this feeling is permanent. "I used
to be a happy person," she said, her voice barely a whisper. "I'm afraid I'll never find my way back
to her."
