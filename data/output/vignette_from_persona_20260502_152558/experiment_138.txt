husband, Rohan. She arrived at her first appointment looking exhausted, stating that she felt like
she was “constantly bracing for a car crash” and could no longer manage her personal or professional
life. Her previous marriage had ended in divorce several years prior, and she described her
relationship with Rohan as the one that had finally made her feel safe and hopeful about the future.

The trauma occurred on a warm Tuesday evening in October. Anjali and Rohan were walking home from a
celebratory dinner, holding hands and debating which movie to watch later. They were crossing a
familiar intersection just two blocks from their apartment when a dark sedan sped through a red
light. Anjali remembers the screech of tires and Rohan shouting her name as he shoved her towards
the curb. She stumbled and fell, scraping her hands on the pavement. The sound of the impact was
sickeningly final. She looked up to see the car that had hit him fleeing the scene, leaving Rohan
motionless in the street under the flickering yellow of the traffic light. The next few hours were a
blur of ambulance sirens, the sterile, indifferent environment of the emergency room, and the quiet,
terrible finality of a doctor’s words.

In the weeks and months that followed, the initial shock gave way to a profound and unsettling new
reality for Anjali. The vibrant, chaotic energy of her life seemed to drain away, leaving a hollow
silence that she found unbearable. Friends and family tried to offer support, but their words felt
distant and meaningless, unable to penetrate the fog that had settled over her. She returned to work
quickly, hoping the familiar deadlines and structure of the newsroom would provide an anchor, but
the effort was monumental.

Anjali exists in a state of unremitting tension. She describes a feeling of being “superalert,” her
body perpetually coiled and ready for an impact that never comes. Relaxation is an alien concept;
even in the quiet of her own home, her muscles remain clenched, her hearing attuned to the slightest
noise from the street. A constant, churning mix of emotions accompanies this physical state: a
sharp, cold fear; a burning anger at the drunk driver who was never caught; and a deep, pervasive
guilt and shame for having been the one who was pushed to safety. These feelings are not reactions
to specific thoughts or memories; they are simply the constant, toxic weather of her internal world.

Her career, once a source of immense pride, has suffered. At the newspaper, the once-familiar buzz
of the newsroom now feels abrasive and overwhelming. She sits at her desk for hours, staring at a
blank screen, unable to string words together or follow the thread of a complex story. She has
missed several important deadlines, and her editor, once a mentor, has grown increasingly concerned
and frustrated. The investigative drive that had defined her as a journalist has been replaced by a
flat, persistent exhaustion. Her social life has evaporated; invitations go unanswered, and phone
calls are ignored until they eventually stop.

She finally made an appointment for therapy after receiving a formal warning about her job
performance. The letter from HR felt like a final confirmation of her failure to cope, to be the
person she was before. Anjali stated that she doesn’t know if she can ever feel happy again, but she
hopes therapy might at least help her “feel quiet inside,” to find a moment’s peace from the
relentless sense of being on guard.
