Kalani had always been the calm center of the storm. For thirty years as a community organizer, he
had navigated heated town halls and mediated bitter disputes, his steady presence a balm on frayed
nerves. But for the last six months, ever since the screech of tires and the explosive shatter of
glass, that center had collapsed. The accident happened on a Tuesday afternoon. He was driving to a
meeting, humming along to the radio, when a delivery truck barreled through a red light. The impact
was a violent, metallic scream. Kalani’s memory of the event is a tattered cloth, riddled with
holes. He has no recollection of the crash itself, a terrifying blankness where the most critical
seconds should be. What remains are vivid, looping fragments: the truck driver, a young man with a
face contorted in fury, storming over to his window and screaming that it was all his fault; the
cold, dismissive tone of the first police officer on the scene, whose uniform badge seemed to fill
Kalani’s entire field of vision.

These fragments have become their own kind of torment. Because he cannot remember the moment of
impact, he is haunted by the possibility that the screaming driver was right. This gap in his memory
has become fertile ground for a deep, corrosive self-blame. He spends hours trying to reconstruct
the timeline, certain that if he could just remember, he would uncover the mistake he must have
made. This conviction that the event happened because of how he acted has reshaped his past into a
story of his own failure. The more he blames himself, the harder it becomes to approach the memory
at all; the thought of it is so laden with shame that his mind recoils, leaving the gaps unfilled
and the questions unanswered. He knows what happened, in a factual sense, but when he tries to speak
of it, he finds he is unable to find the words, as if his own guilt has stolen his voice.

The world now feels like a landscape of hidden threats. The intrusive, unwanted thought of the
driver’s angry face can surface without warning, a memory fragment that acts as its own trigger.
This internal cue system is matched by an external one. The sight of a police uniform on the street
causes an immediate, full-body reaction—a cold sweat, a racing heart—that feels utterly disconnected
from the present moment. He has come to see his body as an enemy, convinced it keeps reacting as if
he is still in danger even when he is rationally safe. This sense of physical betrayal is most acute
at work. During a recent planning meeting about a new community garden, a resident raised his voice
in frustration, his tone not even truly aggressive, just firm. For Kalani, the sound was a direct
echo of the accident. The anger in the room, however mild, instantly confirmed his own appraisal of
himself as incompetent and at fault. He felt a wave of shame so intense it was paralyzing, a
certainty that everyone could see his weakness.

This constant feeling of being judged and blameworthy has made him hyper-attuned to any sign of
conflict. He now scans every room, every interaction, for the slightest expression of anger, which
in turn makes him more likely to be triggered. A raised voice doesn't just startle him; it launches
him into a spiral of self-recrimination. It is in these moments that he feels most profoundly what
he describes as being "dead inside." The vibrant, engaged man who could hold a room together has
been replaced by someone hollowed out by a shame he cannot articulate and a physical terror he
cannot control. His work, once his calling, has become a source of dread. He has started cancelling
meetings, delegating tasks he once loved, all to avoid the possibility of confrontation.

Kalani came to therapy because he could no longer stand the emotional numbness. He misses the man he
used to be—the one who felt connected to his community, who believed in his own capacity for good.
He is terrified that this hollowed-out version is all that is left. The accident may have broken his
body, but it is the unrelenting cycle of memory, blame, and fear that he feels is now breaking his
soul.
