night nine months ago. He had gone to a downtown protest with a colleague who had assured him it
would be a peaceful, candle-lit affair. For an hour, it was. Then came the chaos, the sudden
declaration of an unlawful assembly, and the swift, violent police advance. In the confusion, he was
separated from his colleague, shoved against a wall by an officer, and wrongfully arrested. The air
was thick with a sharp, acrid smell he later learned was from a discharged smoke canister. What
haunts him most, however, is that his colleague, who could have corroborated his story, simply
watched from the crowd and later denied Sameer was even with him. The sense of that betrayal, stark
and absolute, was worse than the rough hands or the zip ties.

In the weeks that followed, Sameer found he could not speak about what happened. When his wife
asked, the words would not form; he knew the sequence of events, but trying to narrate them felt
like assembling a puzzle with mismatched pieces. He would start, then lose the thread completely,
the story dissolving into a fog of shame. He felt an intense, burning guilt whenever he tried to
recall the details, a feeling that he must have done something to deserve it, to be abandoned so
completely. This fragmented, guilt-laden memory became the anchor for his new way of being. He began
avoiding any situation that required planning for the future, a deep-seated dread making it
impossible to book a flight or even commit to dinner plans for the following week, as if he no
longer had the right to expect good things to happen.

His design work, once a source of joy, became a minefield. His belief that the world was a dangerous
place, a place where anyone could turn on you without warning, made him acutely sensitive to any
sign of authority. He began to see threats everywhere, and the sight of a security guard in a
client’s lobby or even a mail carrier in uniform was enough to send a jolt of panic through him.
These encounters would instantly reactivate the memory, not as a coherent story, but as a bodily
sensation of dread and the scent of burning chemicals in his nostrils. His conviction that he had
been betrayed by someone he trusted made him constantly replay the memory, searching for the moment
he should have known better, a mental flagellation that only deepened the guilt.

This constant state of alert has reshaped his behaviour into a series of rigid, self-protective
rituals. Before any interaction, whether a client call or a trip to the grocery store, Sameer now
rehearses precisely what he will say, scripting his words to avoid any possible misstep that could
draw negative attention. He is relentlessly hypervigilant, his eyes constantly scanning crowds for
anyone who looks official or out of place. This constant scanning, of course, makes him far more
likely to spot the very uniforms that trigger him, creating a feedback loop of anxiety. The
hypervigilance also serves as daily confirmation of his core belief: the world is indeed a dangerous
place that requires his constant watchfulness. By keeping himself in this state of high alert and
mentally rehearsing perceived threats, he never allows the traumatic memory to be filed away as
something that is over. It remains immediate, a present danger he must manage moment to moment.

The cycle has tightened its grip over the last few months. His avoidance of future plans has put a
significant strain on his marriage. His wife, frustrated and hurt by his inability to commit to a

broke through the paralysis. Sameer now sits in therapy, his world shrunk to his apartment and the
few client sites he cannot avoid. He can’t find the words to explain the night of the protest
clearly, but he can articulate the fear that governs him now: a certainty that people cannot be
trusted, and that safety is an illusion. He hopes, more than anything, to one day feel secure enough
to believe in a future again.
