therapy eight months after a traumatic event. He reported being unable to work, feeling constantly
on edge, and a profound sense of alienation from his own life. The ordeal occurred during a
consulting trip to the Middle East, where he was taken hostage from his hotel and held for three
weeks in a dark, windowless basement. His primary contact with his captors consisted of harsh,
guttural shouts in Arabic, a language he did not understand, which punctuated long periods of
silence and uncertainty. He was eventually released, but as he described it, “I came home, but I
never really got out of that room.”

In the months following his return, Keanu’s world began to shrink. At first, it was the sudden,
unbidden memories of the event that were most distressing. These were not coherent narratives, but
sensory fragments that would ambush him during the day. This experience of the memory itself felt
profoundly threatening; the auditory recall of shouting in his head felt so real that his body would
react before his mind could catch up, flooding him with a jolt of adrenaline as if the danger were
present all over again. He came to believe that these strong negative feelings were dangerous in
themselves, a sign that he was losing his mind. This constant state of threat, of feeling his body
was betraying him, seemed to pull the memories closer, making the intrusive auditory fragments more
frequent and intense. The more he felt unsafe, the more his mind returned to the basement, which in
turn served as a fresh trigger for the terror.

Any reminder could set off this cycle. Hearing Arabic spoken on the news, or even just feeling
emotionally vulnerable after a conversation about his late wife, would act as a trigger, plunging
him back into a state of high alert. This pervasive sense of threat reshaped his behaviour entirely.
He began to avoid any situation that might provoke an intrusion. He stopped taking on new clients,
knowing he couldn't visit their homes if they had small, enclosed spaces like basements or powder
rooms. This avoidance of work, a core part of his identity, only amplified his feeling of being
unsafe and adrift in the world. He developed a rule that he must always have his back to a wall,
allowing him to survey any room he was in—a strategy that made him feel marginally safer but
reinforced the belief that danger was imminent.

The strategies he adopted to manage the threat began to erode his memory and sense of self. He would
rehearse what to say before any interaction, even a simple phone call, which left him feeling
exhausted and disconnected. This constant mental effort seemed to make it even harder to organize
his thoughts about the trauma. When he tried to explain what had happened to his daughter, he would
lose the thread and trail off, unable to form a clear narrative. At other times, he could recall the
facts of his captivity but felt completely numb, as if it had happened to a stranger. This
emotionless recall was just as frightening as the intrusive sounds; it made him feel broken and
inhuman, further cementing the appraisal that he was permanently damaged by his experience.

Keanu finally sought therapy after losing a long-term client due to his inability to commit to a
project. The professional and financial consequences of his avoidance had become undeniable. He
described feeling trapped in a maze inside his own head, where every path led back to terror. He is
deeply afraid that he will never feel safe again, not even in his own home, but holds onto a fragile
hope of one day being able to sit in a room without constantly scanning for exits.
