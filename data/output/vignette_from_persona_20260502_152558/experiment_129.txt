Amir, a 38-year-old hairdresser, presents as a man holding himself together with deliberate care.
His posture is rigid, his movements precise, but a persistent tension resides in his shoulders and
jaw. It has been fourteen months since he was discharged from the hospital after a sudden and severe
bout of sepsis that nearly claimed his life. He describes the experience not as a story with a
beginning and end, but as a state he can be plunged back into without warning. In the ICU, he
remembers the constant, disorienting beeping of machines, the sharp, sterile smell of antiseptic,
and a profound, searing pain in his chest from a central line. Most of all, he remembers the
terrifying feeling of his own body turning on him, of being utterly powerless as his systems began
to fail.

The initial recovery was purely physical. It was only weeks later that the psychological echoes
began. At first, it was the nightmares—fragmented images of the hospital room that would leave him
waking in a sweat, his heart hammering against his ribs. The feeling of dread from these dreams
would cling to him for hours, a grim residue that soured his morning. This experience of waking up
from a nightmare became his first and most reliable trigger, a guaranteed start to a day spent on
high alert. Soon, other triggers emerged. A client showing him a holiday photo with the bright,
clinical lighting of a modern building was enough to send a jolt of ice through his veins, his body
instantly tensing as if for impact. This immediate physical reactivity felt both terrifying and
shameful, cementing his belief that something inside him was now fundamentally broken. He came to
believe his own body was his enemy, a volatile and unpredictable system that kept reacting as if he
were still in mortal danger.

This appraisal—that his body could no longer be trusted—now governs his daily existence. The
conviction that he is "going crazy" keeps him in a state of hypervigilance, constantly monitoring
his heart rate and breathing. This intense self-monitoring ensures his body remains on constant
alert, which in turn provides more evidence for his fear that he is losing control. The thought, "I
am not safe in my own skin," is a constant internal monologue that makes him scan his environment
and his own mind for anything that might set him off, making him exquisitely sensitive to potential
triggers he might otherwise have ignored. This fear also drives him to ruminate on the illness,
searching for an explanation for his ongoing reactions, which only makes the memories more
accessible and vivid.

The memories themselves are rarely complete pictures. More often, they are sensory ambushes. When a
memory is triggered, perhaps by a photo or a fleeting thought, he doesn’t see the ICU so much as he
feels and smells it. The phantom ache of the central line will bloom in his chest, a dull, insistent
pain that is alarmingly real. This physical pain recall sends a fresh wave of panic through him, a
powerful confirmation that the threat is not over. At other times, he is overwhelmed by a wave of
pure, image-less terror—the full emotional intensity of being near death, but with no clear memory
attached. This is perhaps the most frightening experience of all, as it reinforces his deepest fear
that his reactions are unmoored from reality, that he is indeed going crazy. These memory fragments
invariably leave his body screaming with an alarm he cannot silence.

These components now feed one another in a relentless, self-sustaining loop. A trigger, like the
thought of the annual check-up he knows he needs to schedule, brings not only a surge of physical
panic but also the intrusive memory of the pain in his chest. That memory fragment then amplifies
his body's sense of immediate threat. The heightened state of threat makes his mind more likely to
dredge up other sensory memories, like the smell of antiseptic he now catches at random moments in
his salon. This entire cascade is interpreted through the lens of his negative appraisals: "My body
is betraying me again. I can’t even handle a simple thought." This conclusion then primes him to be
even more hypervigilant, more attuned to threat, and more vulnerable to the next trigger. His life
has shrunk as a result. He has started cancelling clients he finds too unpredictable and has put off
seeing a doctor, terrified that the medical examination itself will cause a complete breakdown. He
came to therapy after a particularly bad morning, waking from a nightmare and finding himself unable
to stop shaking for over an hour, finally forced to admit that he could no longer manage this on his
own. His hope is faint, overshadowed by the fear that he will never again feel safe in his own body.
