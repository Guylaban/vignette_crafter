For Anya, a 33-year-old graphic designer, the world began to shrink six months ago. The ordeal
started subtly, with a man she vaguely recognized from a cafe near her old downtown studio. At
first, his appearances felt like a coincidence; then they became a pattern. He rode a distinctive
fixed-gear bicycle, and she would see it chained up outside her building, then again near her
grocery store, and once, terrifyingly, outside the home she shared with her husband. The worst part
was the feeling of being followed on foot, the unnerving sense of his physical proximity on a
crowded sidewalk, his steps echoing hers for blocks until she would duck into a random shop, heart
hammering, and wait for him to pass. A restraining order was eventually issued, but he vanished
before it could be served, leaving Anya with a profound sense of incompletion and injustice. The man
was gone, but the threat he created remained.

Now, any encounter with her triggers sends an immediate, visceral shockwave of fear through her
body. The sight of a bicycle, particularly one that resembles his, causes her to startle violently,
her nervous system screaming danger when her rational mind knows there is none. This constant state
of alert is exhausting. Recently, walking to a client meeting, a courier on a bike swept past her,
and the jolt of panic was so intense she had to lean against a wall to catch her breath. In that
moment, the trigger did more than frighten her; it served as a stark reminder of her core belief
that the world is a dangerous place and that the man who terrorized her faced no consequences for
his actions. Each false alarm solidifies her conviction that people are not what they seem, that a
friendly face can hide predatory intent.

These deeply held beliefs now dictate her every move. Because Anya is certain the world is unsafe,
she feels she has no choice but to engage in constant threat monitoring. When she enters a new
space, her first priority is no longer the client or the project, but her own safety. Her eyes
immediately scan the room, cataloging every person and, most importantly, identifying all possible
escape routes. This need to stay safe, born from her conviction that danger is ever-present, has led
her to adopt rigid rules for herself. She will only sit in chairs that are against a wall, ensuring
no one can approach from behind, and she always positions herself as close to an exit as possible.
Her husband has noticed she no longer wants to go to the movies or crowded restaurants, places where
she can't control her position relative to an exit.

This constellation of safety-seeking behaviors has, paradoxically, tightened the loop of fear. The
very act of constantly scanning her surroundings for threats keeps her body in a state of high
physiological arousal, perpetuating the jumpiness and sense of being on edge. It also primes her to
notice the very things that frighten her most; by looking for danger, she is far more likely to spot
every bicycle that passes or to misinterpret the footsteps of a harmless pedestrian as the feeling
of being followed. In turn, this heightened state of threat forces her to rely even more heavily on
her strategies of avoidance and escape planning. Her world has become a feedback loop of fear: her
belief in danger fuels her vigilance, her vigilance creates more perceived threats, and each
perceived threat confirms her belief that the world is fundamentally unsafe. The fact that she feels
compelled to live this way is, to her, the ultimate proof that her fears are justified.

The final impetus for seeking therapy came after a crucial client presentation. Held in a cafe in
her old neighbourhood—a place she had meticulously avoided for months—she spent the entire meeting
distracted, her attention split between her designs and the door. She was so jumpy that she flinched
every time the bell chimed. The client commented on her seeming "on edge," and Anya knew she had
lost the contract before she even left. The realization that this internal prison was now costing
her the creative work she loved was devastating. She came to therapy not just because of the fear,
but because she felt she was losing herself to it, and she is desperate to find a way to feel safe
in her own life again.
