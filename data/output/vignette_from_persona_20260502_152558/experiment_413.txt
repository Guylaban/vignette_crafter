Sofia is a school counselor, a role she chose because she wanted to be the adult she never had. At
twenty-six, she presents as composed and articulate, but this composure is a carefully constructed
fortress. The trauma is from her childhood, a landscape of shouting matches that escalated into
violence, always in the small kitchen of their apartment. She remembers the specific floral pattern
on a broken plate, the way the overhead light flickered, and the silence from the neighbors next
door. That silence, she would later realize, was as loud as her father’s rage. It formed the bedrock
of her belief that the people and institutions that should have protected her had utterly failed, a
conviction that now shapes her entire world. This core belief that people can't be trusted dictates
her interactions; she finds herself constantly scanning the faces of parents and colleagues for
micro-expressions of anger, a hypervigilance that leaves her exhausted by the end of the school day.

The memories of those nights do not exist as a coherent story she can tell. When she tries to
explain what happened, the words tangle and the thread is lost. Instead, the past arrives in brutal,
sensory fragments, often triggered without warning. The sharp tone of a frustrated parent in the
hallway or the sudden clatter of a lunch tray can instantly bring back the full memory, and with it,
a wave of emotion so overwhelming she has to retreat to her office to breathe. This chaotic and
intrusive nature of the memory reinforces her deepest-held fears. Each time a fragment returns, it
brings a fresh wave of self-blame; she sees her childhood self frozen in the doorway, and the
thought consumes her: *this happened because of the way I acted, because I did nothing*. This
appraisal, in turn, makes the memories themselves feel even more fragmented and shameful, impossible
to organize into a narrative that could ever be shared or understood.

This constant sense of imminent danger has reshaped her physical world. Believing she is unsafe,
Sofia feels perpetually on guard, even inside her own locked apartment. At work, she has arranged
her office so that her back is always against a wall, a detail her colleagues have never commented
on but which for her is a non-negotiable condition for feeling a sliver of security. This persistent
state of threat makes the memories more accessible, lying just beneath the surface, ready to be
activated by the slightest provocation. The feedback loop is vicious: feeling threatened makes the
memories intrude, and the memories confirm the world is a threatening place.

Her strategies for survival, honed over years, now serve to maintain the cycle. The hypervigilance
she employs to keep herself safe only serves to amplify her sense of dread. By constantly searching
her environment for danger, she primes her nervous system to find it, which means an unexpected
touch from a colleague can cause her to flinch with an audible gasp. This constant scanning provides
no relief, but instead reinforces her feeling of being on guard, which in turn feels like proof that
her negative appraisals about the world are correct. Furthermore, when a memory overwhelms her, she
retreats into rumination, endlessly replaying the events to figure out how they could have been
prevented, a mental exercise that offers no solutions, only a deeper entrenchment in her own sense
of failure.

Sofia sought therapy after an incident at school. A young student, excited to show her a drawing,
had run up and hugged her from behind. Sofia’s reaction was automatic and severe; she cried out and
spun around, her face a mask of terror. The child burst into tears, confused and frightened by the
response. The shame of that moment was acute. It was the starkest evidence yet that the fortress she
had built was not protecting her but imprisoning her, and was now beginning to harm the very
children she had dedicated her life to helping. She came to the first session with a quiet plea, her
voice barely a whisper: "I just want to feel safe in my own skin. I don't want to be afraid of
everything, all the time."
