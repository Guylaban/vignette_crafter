Emily came to therapy after a panic attack during a parent-teacher conference left her unable to
return to her classroom for two days. At 28, she had always seen her work as a teacher as a calling,
but for the last six months, it had become a source of constant, exhausting vigilance. The ordeal
began and ended eight months ago, a three-month period where a former colleague stalked her, but the
aftermath has unraveled her sense of safety in the world. The trauma itself lives in her mind not as
a story, but as a collection of sensory shards. There are moments of terrifying clarity—the glint of
his glasses in her rearview mirror as she drove home, the specific rasp of his breath on the phone
during the late-night silent calls—but these are islands in a fog of blankness. When she tries to
explain what happened, the words won't come, leaving her feeling broken and inarticulate.

These sudden, intrusive fragments of memory often appear without warning, and when they do, they
bring with them an overwhelming wave of horror and shame. This experience has cemented a core belief
that the trauma has fundamentally destroyed her life, rewriting her personality from one of openness
to one of perpetual fear. This feeling of being permanently damaged is reinforced every time a
memory surfaces, confirming her deepest fear that she will never be the person she was before. The
memory of the police officers’ initial dismissal of her fears has also curdled into a profound
conviction that people cannot be trusted, leaving her feeling utterly alone in her own defense. This
belief that she is on her own means she feels she must be on guard at all times.

This internal alarm system, her new conviction that she must always be vigilant, primes her to
notice threats everywhere. It makes her exquisitely sensitive to the triggers that now saturate her
world. Because she is always listening for danger, the sound of a man breathing heavily—even a
colleague sighing at the end of a long day—is no longer innocuous. It is a direct trigger that
plunges her back into the terror of those phone calls, causing a surge of panic that feels as real
and immediate as the original threat. This intense feeling of fear can, in turn, dredge up another
piece of the memory, like a sudden, vivid image of his car parked across the street from her
apartment. The cycle feeds itself: feeling threatened makes her scan her environment more intensely,
which makes her more likely to notice a trigger, which then confirms the world is a dangerous place.

The connections between her thoughts, memories, and feelings have formed a suffocating loop. A
trigger, such as the sensation of being followed as she walks to her car after school, will
instantly call forth a memory of him doing the same, his footsteps echoing hers. This not only
floods her with fear but also validates her appraisal that she is right to be on guard constantly.
Similarly, a memory fragment appearing unbidden—the sound of his breathing—makes her hyper-aware of
auditory cues around her for hours afterward. Her conviction that people are untrustworthy
transforms everyday interactions into threat assessments; a parent approaching her in the school
hallway is no longer a partner in a child’s education but a potential danger, causing her to tense
up and retreat. The quiet of her classroom after the students have gone home, once a time for
peaceful reflection, now feels menacing, a silence she is certain is about to be broken by something
terrible.

This constant state of threat has eroded her life. At school, she is perceived as distant and
anxious, and she has started avoiding all non-essential contact with parents. Her relationship, once
a source of comfort, is now strained by her inability to relax and her deep-seated mistrust, which
sometimes flares up even with her partner. She came to therapy because she could no longer bear the
isolation. She feels trapped in her own mind, a prisoner to a past she cannot fully remember but can
never escape. Her most fervent hope is to one day feel safe in silence again, to be able to trust a
stranger’s smile, and to believe that her life is not over, but can be rebuilt.
