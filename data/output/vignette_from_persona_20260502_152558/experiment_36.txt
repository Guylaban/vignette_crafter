Eleanor, a 65-year-old retired military medic, had always defined herself by her composure under
pressure. For forty years, she had been the one running toward chaos, her hands steady, her mind
clear. Now, her hands trembled just reaching for a glass of water. Six months ago, on a rain-slicked
interstate, her life had bifurcated into a before and an after. She had been driving her silver
sedan to visit her daughter when a truck hydroplaned, initiating a chain-reaction collision. The
screech of tires, the percussive boom of metal, and the shattering of glass were sounds she knew
from the field, but the feeling of being trapped inside the wreckage, the acrid smell of deployed
airbags, and the sight of the car ahead of her crushed beyond recognition were now seared into her
mind.

Initially, she tried to handle it as she would any injury: with discipline and resolve. But the
memories would not be disciplined. They came not as a linear story she could recount, but as
violent, sensory fragments. When a piece of the memory returned, it was never just a thought; she
felt the phantom jolt of the impact, the sudden, overwhelming certainty that she was about to die.
She knew what happened but found she could not find the words to describe the sheer terror of those
moments; the attempt alone would bring on a wave of emotion so powerful she had to stop, gasping for
air as if the car were crumpling around her all over again. This experience of reliving the event,
rather than just remembering it, invariably sent her body into high alert. The slightest unexpected
noise—a dish dropping in the kitchen, a car backfiring down the street—would cause her to flinch
violently, her entire body tensing for an impact that never came.

The world outside her home became a minefield of reminders. A glimpse of a silver car similar to her
own, a news report showing crash imagery, or even just the vague physical discomfort of sitting in a
car as a passenger was enough to trigger a cascade of fear. These triggers did not merely remind her
of the accident; they seemed to catapult her right back into it. Seeing a car hit on the television
would instantly make her feel as if the threat were immediate and personal, her body locking up in a
state of high alert that could last for hours. She began to experience sudden, disorienting
flashbacks where for a few terrifying seconds, she was no longer in her quiet living room but back
in the twisted metal of her car on the highway.

To manage this constant state of threat, Eleanor developed a rigid set of defenses. When her heart
began to pound and her muscles coiled with tension, she would immediately try to shut it down. Her
primary strategy was to eat, not for hunger, but for the dulling, sedative effect of being overly
full. She would stand in the kitchen and consume whatever was in front of her until the physical
discomfort of her stomach outweighed the terror in her mind. On other occasions, she would
frantically distract herself, turning the television volume up high or reciting old army cadences,
anything to occupy the space in her head where the trauma threatened to bloom. She would actively
try hard not to think about the trauma, repeating ‘stop thinking’ to herself like a mantra, a
practice that only seemed to make her body more tense, as if bracing for the thoughts to inevitably
break through.

These coping mechanisms created their own insidious feedback loop. The overeating left her feeling
physically uncomfortable, a sensation that itself had become a low-level trigger, a constant
reminder of bodily vulnerability that kept her on edge. Trying to suppress the thoughts, she found,
only made them more intrusive, which in turn amplified her body’s sense of being on alert. Her world
had shrunk to the four walls of her house. She stopped driving, which meant she no longer visited
her daughter and grandchildren. Her daughter’s concerned calls about her reclusiveness and weight
gain were met with a defensiveness Eleanor didn’t recognize in herself. It was after a particularly
vivid flashback, triggered by nothing more than the sound of a passing garbage truck, that left her
huddled in a corner of her kitchen, that she finally made the call for an appointment. She came to
therapy not with a clear narrative of her trauma, but with the desperate, fragmented plea of a
competent woman terrified that she was losing control and would never again be the person she once
was.
