Kai, a 27-year-old special education teacher, traced the start of it all back to the six months when
their world had shrunk to the space between their apartment and their school. The trauma hadn’t been
a single event, but a slow, creeping dread. It began with a former acquaintance whose messages
became too frequent, then progressed to sightings near their workplace. Soon, it was the unnerving
certainty of being watched as they walked to their car, the same dark sedan always parked a block
away. The most terrifying moments were auditory: the distinct sound of heavy footsteps echoing
theirs on the quiet street leading to their apartment, stopping whenever they did. Though a
restraining order had been in place for nearly a year, the feeling of being hunted had never fully
subsided; it had simply moved from the street into their own mind.

The memories of that time do not exist for Kai as a coherent story. Instead, they are a collection
of sharp, disconnected shards: the glint of a car's side mirror at night, the specific scuff mark on
the stalker's left shoe, the sound of a single, heavy footstep on the gravel path outside their
window. Some of these fragments are intensely, almost unnaturally clear, while the surrounding
context is a complete blank. This incompleteness is a source of profound distress for Kai, who feels
as though they are missing the one crucial piece of information that would explain how they let it
happen, leaving them with a pervasive sense of unresolved threat.

This feeling of imminent danger dictates the rhythm of Kai’s days. In the classroom, where sudden
movements and unpredictable noises are a constant, they are perpetually on guard. A teaching
assistant entering the room unexpectedly will cause Kai to flinch, their heart hammering against
their ribs. This jolt of fear often brings with it a searingly vivid, yet isolated, memory—the
stalker’s face seen for a split second through their apartment blinds—which only serves to confirm
their belief that danger can appear from anywhere, at any time. The sound of footsteps in the school
hallway behind them is no longer neutral; it is an immediate signal of a potential threat. This
trigger sends them into a familiar, draining routine of scanning their surroundings, checking every
reflection in windows and polished floors, a behaviour that offers a fleeting sense of control but
ultimately exhausts them. The sound also yanks those fragmented images of the stalking to the
forefront of their mind, making the past feel dangerously present.

The patchy nature of the memories fuels a relentless cycle of self-interrogation. At night, instead
of resting, Kai finds themself trapped in loops of thought, replaying the few clear moments they can
recall. They ruminate for hours about how it could have been prevented, convinced that if they could
just assemble the full picture, they would find the mistake they made, the point at which they
should have acted differently. This conviction that they failed to protect themself makes relaxation
feel impossible, like a dangerous lapse in judgment. They avoid it actively, believing that letting
their guard down is what allowed the threat to escalate in the first place.

Over the past year, these strategies have become a prison. The constant scanning has made Kai
hypersensitive to the very triggers they are trying to avoid; every person walking behind them on
the street now feels like a potential follower, every lingering glance a confirmation of being
watched. This, in turn, reinforces their deep-seated conviction that the world is an unsafe place
and that they must remain hypervigilant to survive. Their partner has grown worried, pointing out
that Kai can no longer sit in a restaurant without their back to a wall, or walk home from the train
station alone. It was this exhaustion, the profound weariness of being constantly “on duty” in their
own life, that finally led Kai to seek therapy. They confessed in their first session that they
didn’t know how to feel safe anymore, and all they wanted was to be able to take a deep breath
without scanning the room first.
