ship navigating the world's oceans. Her crew saw a calm, decisive leader, someone who could read the
weather in the clouds and the currents in the water. They did not see the child who still lived
inside her, perpetually braced for a door to open in the dark. The trauma was decades in the past, a
secret kept from everyone, including the husband who shared her bed on the rare occasions she was
home. It had happened in her childhood home, a place that was supposed to be a sanctuary. Her uncle,
who lived with the family, would come to her room at night. She remembered the specific creak of the
third floorboard outside her door, the soft click of the latch, and the weight of his silence. When
she finally found the courage to tell her mother, she was told she was being dramatic, that she had
a wicked imagination. That dismissal was a second wound, one that never closed.

Samira came to see the world as a fundamentally dangerous place, a belief system forged in that
childhood bedroom and reinforced by the institutional failure of her own family. This core
conviction that no one could be trusted to protect her meant she could never truly relax, even when
anchored in a safe harbor. On her ship, a vast and isolated space, her small cabin became a pressure
cooker for this vigilance. Every night was a struggle; she found it harder to feel safe after
sundown, listening intently for the hum of the engines to change, for footsteps in the passageway
outside her door, for any sound that might mean someone was entering her space. This constant threat
monitoring was exhausting, and her belief that she had been utterly let down fueled a desperate,
circular internal monologue. She would spend hours dwelling on how it all could have been prevented,
replaying scenarios where she might have locked the door differently or screamed louder, as if
finding the right combination of past actions could secure her present.

Her memories of the abuse were fractured and strange. Sometimes, a stark visual image would flash
into her mind without warning: the floral pattern on her childhood wallpaper, the shape of the
shadow her bedside lamp cast on the ceiling. At other times, she could recall the sequence of events
with a chilling, clinical detachment, feeling completely numb, as if she were reading a report about
a stranger. This emotionless recall was its own kind of horror, reinforcing her sense of having lost
all connection to her community and her own past. The belief that she had been abandoned by those
meant to protect her seemed to drain the life from the memories themselves, leaving only a husk.

over her, a full-body emotional reliving of the event, but with no accompanying image to anchor it,
leaving her gasping for air in the middle of her cabin.

sudden, intrusive visual memory of her uncle's face would trigger an immediate, panicked effort to
suppress the thought, to push it down with all her might. But trying so hard not to think about it
only seemed to make it stronger, ensuring it would burst through again later with even greater
force. The constant state of high alert she lived in also acted as a trigger; the normal groans and
creaks of the ship at night would often jolt a memory to the surface. In turn, her relentless
rumination on how the abuse could have been prevented kept the threat feeling immediate and current,
preventing any sense of safety from taking root. Dwelling on the person she was before the trauma, a
happy and trusting child, only sharpened the pain and reinforced the belief that something essential
had been stolen from her.

Over the years, these patterns had hollowed out her life ashore. Her husband complained she was
distant, always lost in thought, and he couldn't understand why she insisted on checking the locks

and disconnected from a present she couldn't inhabit. The breaking point came during a storm off the

first mate, but for Samira, it was a terrifying glimpse of her private torment jeopardizing her
professional command. She sought therapy a week later, her voice quiet as she admitted her deepest
fear: that she was no longer the captain of her own ship, or of her own mind.
