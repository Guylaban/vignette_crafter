Priya was an auditor, a job that rewarded precision and a linear mind. For the first 22 years of her
life, that was exactly how she saw herself. Six months ago, while visiting family abroad, she was
walking through a crowded market when a bomb detonated two stalls down. The world dissolved into a
concussive roar, then a deafening silence punctuated by screams. In the chaos that followed, what
seared itself into her mind was not a story, but a series of disjointed, silent photographs: the
mangled frame of a delivery boy’s bicycle, a severed hand lying palm-up on the cobblestones, the
flash of a bright red sari amidst the grey dust. She remembers these moments as still pictures, each
one frozen and separate from the others.

Back home in the States, she tried to return to her life, to the clean lines of spreadsheets and
financial reports. But the event followed her in these fragmented pieces. A car backfiring outside
her office window would instantly conjure the image of the severed hand, a silent, intrusive flash
that left her breathless. This became a pattern: a sudden, sharp sound—an explosion or a bang—would
rip a hole in the present moment, pulling one of those terrible pictures through with it. The memory
itself felt broken; she knew more had happened, but she felt like she was "only remembering parts of
what happened and the rest is missing." When she tried to explain it to her parents, she found she
couldn't. The knowledge of what she saw was trapped inside her, but she was unable to find the words
to describe it, as if language itself had been a casualty of the blast.

This inability to articulate her own experience began to curdle into a set of harsh beliefs about
herself. The wordless, snapshot nature of the memories felt like proof of a profound personal
failing. She had come to believe she was "no longer capable of doing the things that defined me,"
like thinking clearly or even forming a complete thought. The images of dismemberment felt deeply
personal, and over time, she began to feel like an object, not like a person—a camera that had
merely recorded horrific data. This sense of dehumanization was cemented by a pervasive emotional
numbness; she felt "dead inside," a spectator to her own life.

These powerful negative appraisals now dictate her experience, creating a vicious, self-sustaining

potential triggers—the sight of a bicycle courier weaving through downtown traffic now feels like a

flooding her with the feeling of being an inanimate object, a thing to which bad things happen. This
feeling of being "dead inside" also corrupts her memory further. When she tries to make sense of the
past, the emotional void makes it impossible to connect the dots, leaving her with only those same
isolated, terrifying snapshots.

The memory fragments, in turn, reinforce everything else. When an image of the twisted bicycle
flashes into her mind, unbidden, it serves as immediate evidence that she is broken and no longer
the competent person she once was. The horror of the image itself primes her to be acutely sensitive
to her surroundings, making it almost certain that the next bicycle she sees will trigger the entire
cascade again. Her world has shrunk as a result. At work, the sharp slam of a colleague’s desk
