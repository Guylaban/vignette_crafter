gasp until she was intubated, the machine’s rhythmic hiss replacing the sound of her own desperate
struggle. The week she spent in the ICU, utterly dependent on the ventilator, fractured her sense of
safety in her own body. She survived, but the feeling of being on the verge of suffocation never
truly left.

Upon returning home, the initial relief of survival was quickly consumed by a pervasive sense of
threat. Leilani began waking multiple times a night, heart pounding, convinced her breathing had
stopped. She would sit bolt upright in the dark, straining to feel the air move in and out of her
lungs, a ritual that offered only fleeting reassurance. This constant state of alert primed her for
disaster, making her exquisitely sensitive to the very triggers born from her trauma. The slightest
shortness of breath from climbing the stairs now felt like the beginning of the end. A whiff of
rubbing alcohol from her husband's first-aid kit could make her body react with a jolt of panic
before her mind even had time to process the smell. Her world became a minefield of sensory cues
that all pointed back to the hospital.

This feeling of her body as an unpredictable enemy, ready to betray her at any moment, began to
curdle into a deep-seated belief that she was fundamentally broken. Her constant vigilance and
visceral reactions felt like proof that she was a "lousy coper," that some essential strength had
been scoured out of her. The conviction that she was inadequate took root, poisoning her self-
perception. This overwhelming sense of physical danger and personal failure drove her to shrink her
world until only her house felt manageable. This became her only "safe zone." She cancelled a
follow-up appointment with her pulmonologist, terrified of the medical examination. The thought of
sitting in a waiting room, surrounded by the sights and smells of illness, was unbearable. Avoidance
felt less like a choice and more like a necessary act of self-preservation against a threat she felt
powerless to manage.

different if the attack had never happened, imagining the concerts she would have given, the songs
she would have sung. These counterfactuals often spiraled into a darker narrative: that she somehow
deserved to suffer for not managing her health better before the crisis. This internal monologue, a

triggering overwhelming panic. Her husband’s concern has grown into a constant, worried presence in
the house, his every question about her health feeling like an accusation. She finally sought
therapy after hearing a distant ambulance siren and spending the next hour hiding in her closet,
convinced it was coming for her. She arrived at her first session expressing a profound sense of
hopelessness, terrified that she is too broken to be fixed but holding onto a desperate sliver of
hope that she might one day feel safe inside her own skin again.
