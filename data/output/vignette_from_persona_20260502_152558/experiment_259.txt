Keilani had spent fifty years as a surgeon, a life built on precision, knowledge, and control. At
seventy-seven, even in semi-retirement, her hands were steady, her mind sharp. She was the one who
diagnosed, who cut, who healed. Then, eight months ago, during a routine grand rounds presentation
in the very amphitheater where she had trained decades of residents, her own body betrayed her. The
world dissolved into a cacophony of shouting, a searing pain in her chest, and then, for a time,
nothing at all. Her subsequent recovery from the massive cardiac event was deemed a medical miracle,
but the woman who left the hospital was not the one who had entered it.

The memory of the event itself is not a coherent story she can recount. Instead, it exists in her
mind as a collection of sensory shrapnel: the shockingly bright glare of an examination light, the
panicked face of a first-year resident looking down at her, the rhythmic, percussive sound of chest
compressions that she understood from a terrifyingly detached clinical perspective. These moments
are crystalline in their clarity, yet they float untethered from the long, terrifying blanks that
surround them. The disjointed pieces offer no narrative and no sense of completion, which compels
Keilani to spend her waking hours trying to assemble them into a sequence that makes sense. This
constant, fruitless search for a linear story—for a reason *why* this happened to her, a woman who
had saved so many others—has become an exhausting obsession. The failure to make sense of her own
mind’s contents has left her feeling profoundly lost, eroding the bedrock of her identity and making
her believe she has become unrecognisable even to herself.

In an effort to regain control, Keilani has adopted a strategy of rigid, forced positivity. Whenever
a fragment of the memory threatens to surface—the sterile smell of the ICU, the metallic taste of
fear—she will actively suppress it, repeating to herself, "I am a survivor, I am grateful, I will
only think positive thoughts." This constant effortful avoidance, however, only seems to preserve
the memory's raw, unprocessed quality, keeping the fragments sharp and disconnected by preventing
them from ever being integrated into the larger story of her life. Each time she fails to control
the intrusion of these thoughts, it serves as fresh evidence for the devastating belief that she has
become someone else entirely, a frail and fearful person she no longer knows. This internal battle
has corroded her faith, not in a deity she never particularly believed in, but in the fundamental
principles of order and causality that had governed her entire professional life.

commanded an operating room, now cannot watch a medical drama on television. The sound of a distant
siren causes her to freeze. Most devastatingly, she has been unable to return to the hospital, even
for her own follow-up appointments, rescheduling them with flimsy excuses until her cardiologist had
to call her at home. She had to formally retire, unable to bear the thought of walking through the
automatic doors of the place that had been both her sanctuary and the site of her undoing. This
retreat from the world that had defined her has cemented the loss of her identity. By refusing to
engage with the cues of her past life, she is inadvertently starving the memory of any new, safe
information that might help contextualize it, leaving the terrifying fragments to echo in a void of
her own making. The woman who found meaning in her work now finds only fear in its reminders, and in
this, she feels she has lost herself completely.

She has come to therapy now because last week, her grandson fell and broke his arm, and she found
herself unable to accompany him to the emergency room, paralyzed by a fear she could not articulate.

of recovering her old self, a prospect she no longer believes is possible. Instead, she asks, with a
quiet desperation that belies her formidable history, if it is possible to learn how to live with
the stranger she has become.
