months ago. The event itself he rarely spoke of, but it lived inside him not as a story, but as a
series of stark, silent photographs. One was of a terrified woman’s face, mouth open in a scream he
couldn’t hear over the roar of the crowd. Another was the rough, synthetic weave of a cheap blue
windbreaker pressed so hard against his cheek he could feel the fabric’s grid pattern on his skin.
These images felt more real to him than his memory of breakfast that morning; they were high-
definition intrusions into the blur of his daily life.

The festival tragedy occurred when a panicked surge of people, reacting to a mistaken report of
danger, created a deadly crowd crush near a narrow exit tunnel. David was pinned for what felt like
an eternity, unable to move, the weight of bodies pressing in from all sides. He remembers the
collective loss of control, the way people climbing over others became a form of misbehavior born of
pure survival instinct. He had survived, but the memory of his own helplessness, of being unable to
aid the man crushed beside him, had planted a seed of profound shame that grew with every recall of
the event. When these snapshot memories surfaced, the shame was so acute it felt like a physical
sickness, a hot flush spreading from his chest outwards.

impatiently through an aisle would trigger a cascade. The sight of this minor transgression, this
everyday selfishness, would instantly flash one of the frozen images into his mind: the blue
windbreaker, the screaming woman. The memory would arrive unbidden, sharp and visceral. To manage
the sudden terror, David developed a set of desperate rituals. He would try to force the thought
out, mentally shouting over it with inventory numbers or sales targets. He would tell himself it
wasn't that bad, that it didn't really happen that way, a flimsy shield of magical thinking against
an overwhelming reality.

This constant battle to suppress the memories only seemed to make them stronger and more frequent.
The effort of pushing them down made him hyper-aware of his surroundings, constantly scanning for
the triggers he knew would bring them on. When a memory did break through, he would retreat to the

pain a welcome distraction from the shame and fear. The brief relief it provided was his only
reliable escape, but it left crescent-shaped marks his wife eventually noticed.

or finding a moment alone to hurt himself. These very strategies, however, sensitized him further.

he could no longer keep the two worlds separate; the silent, flashing images from the past were
