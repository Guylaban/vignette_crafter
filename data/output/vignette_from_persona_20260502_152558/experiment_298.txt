Sofia, a 37-year-old hotel manager, presents as poised and articulate, but her controlled exterior
belies a profound and escalating distress. The trauma occurred eight months ago, following a
regional hospitality conference. A senior colleague from another city, a man she had respected,
assaulted her in her hotel room after a networking dinner. In the months since, her world has
constricted into a tight, self-sustaining loop of fear and self-recrimination.

When Sofia tries to recall the night, the story dissolves into a series of disjointed, shameful
images: the pattern on the hotel carpet, the weight of him, the specific scent of his cologne. She
knows what happened, but when she tries to assemble it into a coherent narrative, even for herself,
she cannot find the words. This very fragmentation has become evidence in her mind for a deeper,
personal failing. The inability to form a clear story has solidified into a core belief that there
is something fundamentally wrong with her. This conviction that she is defective also taints the act
of remembering itself, making each fragmented image feel intensely shameful. The memory’s
incoherence and the shame it evokes fuel a desperate need to avoid anything that might trigger it,
leading her to scan the faces of every man with a similar build or beard who approaches the check-in
desk at her hotel.

This deep-seated belief that the assault happened because of the sort of person she is—too trusting,
too open—governs her daily life. Convinced that her own character is to blame, she lives with the
terrifying appraisal that her body is now an enemy, poised to betray her with a surge of panic at
any moment. To manage this overwhelming feeling and to avoid being alone with her thoughts, she has
thrown herself into her work. The punishing 14-hour days leave her too exhausted to think, a
deliberate strategy to outrun the relentless internal critic that tells her she is fundamentally
broken. This purposeful overwork, however, only serves to fragment her memories further; exhaustion
prevents any clear processing, leaving the traumatic images isolated and potent.

The avoidance and overwork have become habits that feed the very distress they are meant to contain.
The constant, subtle scanning of guests at work keeps the threat perpetually alive, reinforcing her
sense of being unsafe. After a long shift, when she finally gets home to the apartment she shares
with her partner, the exhaustion is no longer enough to keep the thoughts at bay. It is then that
she finds herself caught in long, circular ruminations, meticulously planning a revenge that will
never happen. These fantasies, while offering a fleeting sense of control, force her to replay
fragments of the assault, searing the shame and sensory details into her mind without ever allowing
for a coherent story to form. This nightly ritual of rumination only strengthens her conviction that
she is a damaged person, someone who could be capable of such thoughts, further cementing her
negative view of herself.

The cycle has become suffocating. Her partner has grown increasingly worried about her emotional
distance and the sheer number of hours she spends at the hotel. At work, her hypervigilance has
started to affect her performance; last week, she was visibly short with a guest whose appearance
was vaguely similar to her assailant’s, an interaction that left her shaking and nauseous for hours.
It was this incident—the public cracking of her carefully constructed professional facade—that
finally compelled her to seek help. Sofia is exhausted by the constant effort of managing her own
mind and body. She came to therapy hoping to find a way to feel safe again, but is terrified that in
telling the story, she will discover that her deepest fear is true: that she is, and always will be,
fundamentally broken.
