Layla, a meticulous and respected auditor of 63, had built her life on logic and predictable
outcomes. For forty years, her work had been to find patterns, assess risks, and impose order on
chaos. Now, her own mind was a landscape of unpredictable terror, a place she could no longer audit
or control. The shift began nine months ago, on a hiking trip in a narrow slot canyon with her
partner, Omar. They were admiring the ancient sandstone walls when a low rumble escalated into a
roar. The rockslide was sudden and absolute. Layla was thrown clear, but Omar was caught, his leg
pinned by a boulder in a small alcove. For hours, she had stayed with him, her voice echoing in the
confined space as she promised help was coming. She remembers the smell of dust and damp earth, the
chilling feel of the rock against her cheek as she tried to listen for his breathing, and then, the
silence that fell long before the rescue team arrived.

Now, the canyon is no longer a memory; it is a constant, lurking possibility. The trauma lives not
in the past but in the present, ambushing her without warning. A few weeks ago, at work, a junior
colleague closed the door to the small, windowless records room they were in. The solid click of the
lock was all it took. Instantly, Layla was no longer in her office building; she was back in the
suffocating darkness of the canyon, the terror of that day washing over her with the force of a
physical blow. When the memory comes, it feels completely real, a full sensory reliving that brings
with it the same frantic helplessness she felt as Omar’s voice grew weaker. This experience
solidified a belief that had been growing in her for months: her own body had become an enemy,
reacting as if she were still in danger even when she was logically safe.

This profound sense of betrayal by her own nervous system fuels a pervasive state of threat. Layla
has come to believe she is fundamentally helpless, that she has no power to stop bad things from
happening. This conviction dictates her every action. She is constantly on guard, scanning rooms for
exits, flinching at sudden noises. The hypervigilance is exhausting, leaving her unable to sleep for
more than an hour or two at a time. Lying awake in the dark, her mind races, replaying scenarios,
and this state of high alert makes her even more susceptible to the memories that wait at the edge
of her consciousness. Her world has shrunk; she avoids any unfamiliar place where she cannot fully
assess the danger in advance, a practice that is now threatening the job she once loved.

The components of her distress have locked into a brutal, self-sustaining cycle. Her belief in her
own helplessness makes her scan the world for danger, and this state of hypervigilance makes her
hypersensitive to triggers. She now perceives the world through a lens of imminent threat, which
means a locked door or even a documentary about national parks is no longer neutral. These triggers,
in turn, unleash the memories in their full sensory horror. Each time she is ambushed by the past,
it serves as fresh, visceral proof of her core beliefs: that she is powerless, that the world is
unsafe, and that she has lost all faith in a life that could contain such random cruelty. The terror
of the recall reinforces her hypervigilance, and the cycle tightens. She can feel it happening—the
more watchful she is, the more she finds things to be frightened of; the more she remembers, the
more certain she is that she is broken.

Her refusal to travel to new client sites, a core function of her senior auditor role, has led to a
difficult conversation with her manager. He was kind but firm; the company needed her to be able to
do her job. That conversation was the final impetus for seeking help. Layla sits in the therapy
room, her posture rigid, her hands clenched. What brought her here was the threat to her career, but
what she is most afraid of is not losing her job. She is terrified that this internal state—the
constant watchfulness, the sensory ambushes, the crushing weight of her own helplessness—is
permanent. She is trapped not in a canyon of rock and earth, but in a prison of her own mind, and
she is desperate to find a way out.
