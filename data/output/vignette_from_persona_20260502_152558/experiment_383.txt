Anong, a meticulous and respected 51-year-old dentist, had always found a quiet satisfaction in the
predictable order of her life and practice. That order was shattered a year ago. It began subtly,
with a patient who lingered too long after his appointments, complimenting her hands, her voice.
Then came the unsolicited gifts left at the reception desk, and then the notes tucked under her
car’s windshield wiper. For three months, he was a constant, unseen presence. He would park across
the street from her office for hours, playing the same classical piano piece on a loop from his car
stereo, a melody that became the soundtrack to her mounting dread. The man’s escalating behaviour
culminated in him following her home, an ordeal that only ended after a prolonged and terrifying
legal process.

The restraining order offered a legal boundary, but no psychological relief. The experience left
Anong with a profound sense of vulnerability that the paper couldn't erase. Her interactions with
the police during that time had been particularly disillusioning; their initial inability to act
without a direct physical threat had made her feel utterly abandoned and unprotected. Now, the sight
of a police car, far from being reassuring, serves as a stark reminder of that period of
helplessness, a symbol of a system that felt unable to keep her safe when she needed it most. This
memory is a cornerstone of the terror that now governs her days.

Anong’s world has since shrunk, constricted by a state of constant, exhausting vigilance. The
connection between specific reminders of the trauma and her pervasive sense of threat is immediate
and absolute. Last week, while prepping a patient for a crown, the soft piano music from the waiting
room radio drifted into her operatory—it was the same piece the stalker used to play. The dental
pick in her hand trembled as a cold wave of adrenaline washed over her. She was instantly certain he
was outside, that he had found a way around the order, that he was watching her at that very moment.
The music was not a memory; it was a live signal of an active threat. This is her reality now: a
song, a news report about a similar crime, or the mere presence of a police officer doesn't just
remind her of what happened; it convinces her that it is happening again.

This persistent sense of imminent danger dictates her every move. She can no longer sit in a
restaurant or even her own staff lounge unless her back is pressed firmly against a wall, with a
clear and unobstructed view of every entrance. Her husband, Somchai, tries to reason with her,
pointing out the security system at their home, the legal protections in place. But for Anong, his
reassurances are meaningless sounds. She has come to believe that safety is an illusion and that her
own perception is the only reliable gauge of danger. "You can't know," she tells him, her voice
tight with a certainty that feels absolute. "I have to be the one who sees him first." This
conviction has become the central organizing principle of her life, a full-time job of threat
monitoring that has supplanted her career and strained her marriage. The dental practice she built
over two decades now feels less like a sanctuary of healing and more like a fortress she must
constantly defend, every new patient a potential risk to be assessed.

It was after seeing a police car parked outside her local grocery store last month that she finally
sought help. The sight triggered a panic attack so severe she had to abandon her shopping cart and
retreat to her car, where she sat shaking and hyperventilating for nearly an hour. The incident
clarified the impossible terms of her existence. She realized she was living as a prisoner in a life
that was, by all external measures, safe. Anong came to therapy not with a vague hope of feeling
less anxious, but with a desperate, specific plea: to reclaim the feeling of safety in her own skin,
and to one day be able to sit in a quiet room, hear a piano playing, and feel nothing at all.
