Javier, a 24-year-old QA engineer, presented for therapy six months after being involved in a
catastrophic multi-vehicle pile-up on the interstate. On a foggy morning commute, a tanker truck
jackknifed, and in the low visibility, dozens of cars crashed in a chain reaction of screeching
metal and shattering glass. Javier’s own car was struck from behind but he was physically unharmed,
trapped for nearly an hour as he watched smoke billow from the wreckage ahead. He could hear screams
he was powerless to respond to, the image of a family in a crushed minivan just fifty feet away
seared into his memory. He remembers the overwhelming feeling of being a helpless witness, a feeling
that has since curdled into a conviction that he should have done something more.

In the weeks that followed, the world seemed to be filled with reminders. At his tech company, the
open-plan office, usually a hub of collaborative energy, became a minefield. The casual chatter of
his colleagues about a fender-bender in the parking lot or a story they’d seen on the news about an
accident (`Crash Discussions`) would trigger a sudden, cold wave of detachment (`Dissociative
Feeling`). It felt as if he were floating outside his own body, watching a stranger nod along while
his mind was back on the bridge. These moments, where the world began to feel unreal, were
terrifying cues for him, signaling an imminent loss of control that he had to escape at all costs.

Any encounter with a trigger now immediately activates a cascade of damning self-judgments. Seeing a
news report (`Media / News`) about a house fire on a TV in a coffee shop doesn't just remind him of
the event; it serves as proof for a set of beliefs that have taken root. He is gripped by the
certainty that the world is a malevolent and unpredictable place, that you can never know who or
what will harm you (`Fear of Harm`). The images of destruction confirm his deepest fear: that he is
a person to whom bad things happen, and that he failed in his fundamental duty to protect others
that day (`Failed to Protect`). Each new tragedy he glimpses feels like another piece of evidence
that nothing good can happen to him anymore (`No Good Ahead`).

These powerful appraisals, in turn, govern his behaviour. Because he has come to believe the world
is inherently dangerous and his own failure is absolute, he feels compelled to avoid any situation
that might bring these feelings to the surface. The conviction that he should have saved the people
he saw has made any reminder of his inaction unbearable, so he spends hours lost in thought,
meticulously replaying the accident and constructing scenarios where he acted differently, where he
was a hero (`Counterfactual Thinking`). This belief system also drives his physical avoidance. A
discussion about a traffic snarl at work (`Triggers`) is enough to make him feign a phone call and
walk quickly to an empty conference room, his heart pounding (`Triggers → Maladaptive Strategies`).
He has mapped out new, longer routes to his office to avoid highways and any potential sight of
police cars, which he now sees not as symbols of help, but as omens of disaster (`Avoid Police`).

Over the past few months, this cycle has tightened its grip, shrinking Javier’s world to his
apartment and his desk. He meticulously avoids all news media, turning off the television if a news-
break chime sounds and curating his online feeds to eliminate any mention of accidents or
emergencies (`Avoid Media`). He understands, on some level, that this is a reaction to the trauma,
but his internal logic is powerful: the world is unsafe, and his failure was profound, therefore his
vigilance and avoidance are not symptoms, but necessary precautions (`Negative Appraisals →
Maladaptive Strategies`). This has come at a cost. His manager recently noted his increasing
isolation and abrupt departures from team meetings during a performance review. It was this
conversation that finally prompted Javier to seek help, the fear of losing his job momentarily
outweighing the fear of everything else. He came to therapy stating he feels "constantly braced for
impact," and his greatest hope is to one day feel safe enough to simply drive to work without
believing he is heading toward another catastrophe.
