Liam is a 26-year-old fisherman who speaks in a low, careful monotone, as if measuring the weight of
every word before releasing it. He has come to therapy because his world has shrunk to the size of a
single, repeating memory. Six months ago, while backpacking through a remote region, he was taken
hostage and held for several weeks. The experience was a blur of darkness, pain, and sound. Bound
and hooded in a damp cellar, his primary connection to the outside world was auditory: the rumble of
the single bus that passed twice a day on a nearby road, and the incessant, high-pitched crying of
an infant in an adjacent building. His captors were cruelest when he showed any emotional
vulnerability, using his pleas and his fear for his family as fodder for their mockery.

For the first few weeks after his release, Liam tried to force a return to normalcy, pushing himself
back onto the early morning bus to the docks. It was on one of those rides that the cycle began in
earnest. A young mother boarded with her baby, and the moment the child began to wail, Liam was no
longer in his seat. The sound plunged him back into the cellar; he felt the phantom bite of rope on
his wrists and the agonizing cramp of his shoulders being forced into an unnatural position, his
body instinctively trying to reenact the posture of his captivity. A hot, helpless rage surged
through him, so intense he had to grip the seat to keep from shouting. This became the pattern: a
trigger, whether the sound of a bus engine or a baby's cry, would instantly collapse time and space,
pulling the memory into the present with visceral, physical force.

These flashbacks are not memories in the typical sense; for Liam, they are recurrences. When the
memory returns, so does the physical pain in his ribs where he was repeatedly kicked. This belief
that the event is, in a way, still happening, has cultivated a profound and constant sense of
threat. He has come to believe that the world is fundamentally unsafe and that another attack is not
a matter of if, but when. This conviction drives him to stay on high alert, spending his nights
awake in bed, listening for any floorboard creak or scrape of a branch against the window that might
signal an intruder. His wife, Sarah, says he barely sleeps.

This state of perpetual threat has, in turn, become its own gateway back to the trauma. His mind is
always scanning, cataloguing danger. When he enters any room, his eyes automatically dart around,
noting potential weapons: a heavy glass ashtray, a letter opener on a desk, a decorative fireplace
poker. He feels unsafe unless he can sit with his back pressed firmly against a wall, the entire
room within his field of vision. This constant, exhausting environmental assessment means his mind
is always primed for the worst, and the slightest thing can trigger a full-blown intrusion. The
simple act of noticing a knife on the kitchen counter while making coffee can be enough to bring
back the glint of his captor’s blade, and with it, the entire sensory experience of his
imprisonment.

The more threatened he feels, the more acutely sensitive he becomes to his triggers. His
hypervigilance acts as an amplifier, turning mundane city sounds into specific, targeted threats.
The distant rumble of a bus several blocks away is no longer just background noise; it is a direct
herald of danger that makes his heart pound. Because he is always listening for the sound of an
approaching enemy, he is far more likely to notice the very sounds that have become fused with his
traumatic memory, ensuring the cycle begins again. The feedback loop is now a prison.

The life he knew is eroding. He stopped taking the bus, now walking two hours in the pre-dawn
darkness to get to the boat, leaving him drained before his workday starts. He has pushed Sarah
away, recoiling from any moment of emotional intimacy. The vulnerability of being open with her
feels identical to the helplessness he felt in that cellar, and it triggers a panicked retreat. The
breaking point came last week when a baby’s cry from a television commercial sent him scrambling
into the corner of the living room, his body contorting into a frozen crouch. He is here now because
he is terrified of the rage that boils up during these episodes, convinced that one day he will lose
control and the past will finally destroy his present.
