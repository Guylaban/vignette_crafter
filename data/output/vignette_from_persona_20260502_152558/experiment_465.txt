Elena, a 39-year-old non-profit program director, presented for therapy eight months after being
violently assaulted while walking home from a community meeting. The event itself remains a chaotic
storm in her mind, a series of disjointed sensory fragments rather than a linear story. She recalls
the sudden grab from behind, the glint of a heavy, metal flashlight used as a weapon, and the
feeling of her blouse tearing. The memory lacks a clear sequence, a problem that frustrates and
frightens her; she reports knowing what happened but feels an infuriating inability to find the
words to describe it coherently. When fragments do break through, they are accompanied by a surge of
profound, almost uncontrollable rage that leaves her shaking. These intrusive recollections
immediately trigger a cascade of self-recrimination. She finds herself thinking that somebody else,
someone stronger or more aware, would have taken a different route or fought back more effectively,
a belief that has become a cornerstone of her distress.

The assault has fundamentally altered Elena’s sense of self and safety. She now lives with a
pervasive sense of threat, a state of hypervigilance that is both exhausting and isolating. The
memories themselves are a direct source of this fear; a fleeting image of the flashlight is enough
to make her heart pound and convince her that danger is imminent, even when she is safe in her own
home. This feeling is magnified at night, when the darkness feels menacing and she finds herself
unable to relax, certain that any small sound is a sign of an intruder. Her husband’s reassurances
are meaningless; she has come to believe that she cannot trust safety, no matter who tells her she
is secure. This conviction of her own weakness makes the world feel inherently more dangerous.
Because she sees herself as a fragile target, every shadow holds a potential attacker, and every
interaction is scanned for menace.

Elena’s world has shrunk as a result. Triggers are everywhere, and each encounter reinforces the
toxic cycle of fear and self-blame. At her job, where she once felt confident and capable, she is
now easily undone. Last week, seeing a colleague using a heavy metal rolling pin in the office
kitchen sent a jolt of terror through her, making her feel instantly unsafe in the middle of a
workday. This immediate sense of threat was followed by a wave of shame. The thought, "I am a weak
person," echoed in her mind, solidifying her belief that she is fundamentally broken. This belief in
her own weakness makes her more sensitive to potential threats, causing her to constantly scan her
environment, which in turn makes her more likely to notice things that remind her of the assault. It
also seems to invite the memories back in, as if her mind is seeking evidence to confirm her own
damning verdict of herself.

Over the past several months, these beliefs have corroded her connections to the world. The memory
of being stripped of her clothing has made any form of physical vulnerability, especially being
naked, an experience of pure terror, creating a painful distance between her and her husband.
Furthermore, Elena, a woman who built her career on fostering community, now feels entirely
disconnected from her own Latin American culture. She is certain that others can see her weakness,
that they would judge her for what happened, and has started avoiding the very community events she
used to organize. This self-imposed isolation has only deepened her conviction that she has lost
something essential about herself. After a particularly bad night plagued by fragmented, rage-filled
memories, she spent the entire next day in a state of heightened alert, where every elongated
object—a broom handle, a piece of scaffolding—seemed to morph into a weapon. It was after her
husband found her crying in the closet, unable to get ready for a family dinner, that they agreed
she needed to seek help. She came to therapy hoping to find a way to quiet the constant sense of
danger, but deeply afraid that the strong, connected woman she once was is gone for good.
