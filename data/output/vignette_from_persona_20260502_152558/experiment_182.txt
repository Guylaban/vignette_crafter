nine months ago. It was the night his five-year-old daughter, Mai, couldn't breathe. He had woken in

deepest sleep. In her room, he found her struggling, her small chest heaving violently with each
shallow gasp. In the frantic, terrifying drive to the hospital, with a lullaby station playing
softly on the radio, all he could see were her lips, tinged a dusky blue in the dashboard's glow.
Mai had survived the severe respiratory infection, but Bao felt that some essential part of him had
been left behind in the sterile, beeping environment of the pediatric ICU.

In the weeks that followed Mai’s recovery, the memory of that night began to intrude on his days. It
never arrived as a complete story, but as sudden, visceral pieces that would appear without warning
and then vanish. While mending a fence, the sight of his own knuckles, white from gripping the wire,
would suddenly be replaced by the image of Mai's tiny, pale hand, lost in a tangle of IV tubes.
These fragments felt utterly real, as if they were happening in the present moment rather than being
memories of something that was over. The quiet of the house after dark became a particularly potent
trigger. Lying in bed, the silence seemed to amplify the memory, and soon he wasn't in his own room
but back in Mai’s, reliving the terror of hearing that awful, shallow rasp of her breathing.

To escape the memories that ambushed him in the dark, Bao started to delay sleep. He began finding
reasons to stay in the brightly lit barn long after his wife and children were asleep, cleaning
equipment that was already clean or poring over ledgers until his eyes burned. This exhaustion,
however, only made him more vulnerable. The act of finally lying down in the dark became a trigger
in itself, a signal to his mind that the intrusive images were about to begin. This nightly ritual
of avoidance created a feedback loop; the more he delayed sleep, the more the darkness became
something to fear, and the more potent a trigger it became for the very memories he was trying to
evade.

The memories themselves began to generate their own triggers and dictate his behaviour. The vivid,
repeating image of Mai’s struggling chest made him hypervigilant to the sound of anyone breathing

through him. When these memory fragments did break through during the day, the feeling of being back
in that moment was so total that his only instinct was to flee. He would drop what he was doing and
walk to the farthest edge of their property, needing the physical distance to escape the feeling
that it was all happening again. This retreat became a habit, and soon he was avoiding not just his
thoughts but other people entirely, believing his presence was a dark cloud over the family. His
wife noted he had become a ghost in his own home.

radio, will instantly plunge him back into the memory of that frantic car ride, the feeling of panic
so intense it’s as if it’s happening for the first time. This reliving of the event reinforces his
need to escape, leading him to isolate himself further in the fields or the barn. This self-imposed

terrified that the past has permanently broken his ability to be a father and a husband, and he is
desperate to find a way to live in the present again.
