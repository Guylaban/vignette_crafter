Anjali had always been the one who ran towards the problem. As an urban planner, she built systems
for communities; as a relief worker, she had tried to hold one together. It was during her last
deployment, six months ago, that the system had shattered. She was in a makeshift camp tucked into a
wooded valley, documenting sanitation infrastructure with her camera, when the raid began. The first
sign was the shouting, a wave of panic that crested and broke over the quiet hum of the camp. The
memory that followed was not a story she could tell. It existed in her mind as a series of
disjointed, searing images: the blur of people running through the trees, the acrid smell of smoke,
the flat, lifeless look in a child’s eyes. She had come home a month later, but the shouting and the
trees had come with her.

At first, the primary disturbance was a pervasive sense of threat that clung to her like the
humidity of the valley. Back in her open-plan office, the sudden movement of a colleague approaching
her desk from behind would cause her to flinch violently, a jolt of pure terror that left her
breathless and dizzy. At night, she would lie awake for hours, her body rigid, cataloging every
creak of the apartment building as a potential danger. This constant, simmering state of alert was
exhausting, and it acted like a magnet for the trauma itself. A car backfiring on the street didn't
just startle her; it would plunge her back into the memory, the scent of dust and fear suddenly
overwhelming the sterile air of her office. She would feel, for a terrifying second, that she was
truly there again, a flashback so complete it blotted out the present.

These intrusions were unbearable, not just for the fear but for the white-hot rage that accompanied
them. When the images came, she felt a fury so profound it scared her. The memory itself felt wrong,
like a corrupted file playing in her mind; it seemed to belong to someone else, a detached and
horrifying film she was forced to watch. To stop the film, Anjali developed a rigid set of rules.
She would not allow her mind to be idle. She filled every waking moment with work, podcasts,
audiobooks, anything to occupy the space where the memories might bloom. When an image flickered at
the edge of her consciousness, she would mentally shout it down, pushing it away with a force that
left her drained. She told her partner she was just busy with a demanding project, but in reality,
she was engaged in a constant, unwinnable battle with her own mind.

Her attempts to control her thoughts only seemed to make them stronger and stranger. The more she
suppressed the memory, the more alien and fragmented it became when it inevitably broke through,
reinforcing her sense that something was fundamentally broken inside her. The constant mental
distraction bled into her nights, making sleep impossible as her mind refused to power down,
convinced it had to remain on high alert. This exhaustion, in turn, fueled her hypervigilance,
creating a vicious cycle where her coping strategies actively sustained the sense of imminent threat
she was trying to escape. Her world began to shrink as her efforts to avoid the memory made her
exquisitely sensitive to anything that might evoke it. A mandatory site visit to a wooded suburban
plot for a new park development sent her into a tailspin, forcing her to call in sick. During a
tense community meeting, a man began shouting, and Anjali had to flee the room, overwhelmed by a
wave of panic and rage. She started avoiding looking at project photos, even old ones, terrified of
what they might unlock.

The strategies were failing. A few weeks ago, after a particularly vivid flashback triggered by an
old photo album, the rage was so consuming she felt she would either explode or disappear. In a
moment of desperation, she dug her nails into her arm, hard, until the sharp, grounding pain cut
through the noise in her head. The physical sting was a release, a focal point that allowed the
overwhelming feelings to recede. It was a terrifying discovery, and one she repeated. The self-harm
became her last line of defense for when the memory, in all its rage and alienation, became too much
to bear. It was after her partner saw the marks on her arm, his face a mixture of confusion and
fear, that Anjali realized she could no longer manage this alone. She couldn't explain to him what
was happening; when she tried, the words would tangle and die, the thread of the story lost in the
fog of a memory she couldn't own. She came to therapy not with a clear goal, but with a desperate,
quiet hope of being able to live inside her own mind again without a constant war.
