Kofi, a thirty-year-old curriculum designer, presented for therapy stating that he no longer
recognized the man he saw in the mirror. For the past eight months, since the end of his
relationship, he had felt a profound sense of being fundamentally altered, as if he were living in a
stranger’s skin. The person he had become, he explained, was a ghost of the confident, engaged man
he used to be, and this sense of alienation from himself was now unbearable. The change began the
night his ex-partner, Marcus, came home smelling heavily of whiskey. An argument had started over
something trivial, but it quickly soured when Marcus, his voice thick with contempt, made a cruel
remark about Kofi’s family in Ghana. As the fight escalated, the pot of rice they were cooking began
to burn on the stove, filling the small apartment with an acrid, choking smoke. It was in that
moment, with the smell of alcohol on Marcus’s breath and the stench of burning food in the air, that
the violence began.

In the months since, Kofi has found that the world is littered with reminders of that night. A few
weeks ago, at a work happy hour, a colleague jostled past him, and the faint smell of beer on their
clothes was enough to send him reeling. Instantly, he was no longer in the loud, cheerful bar but
was back on his own kitchen floor. The memory was not a simple thought; it was a full-bodied
assault. He could feel the phantom ache of a blossoming bruise on his ribs, a sharp, localized pain
that mirrored the original injury precisely. The terror returned with the same suffocating
intensity, a primal fear that left him breathless and disoriented in the middle of a crowd of his
smiling coworkers.

These moments of re-experiencing leave a toxic residue. After the flashback at the bar faded, Kofi
was left with a crushing wave of self-recrimination. The memory of his own helplessness reinforced a
belief that had been taking root for months: that he was no longer the strong, capable person he
once was. The vivid recall of the injustice—that Marcus had walked away without consequence while he
was left with these invisible wounds—fed a simmering, powerless anger. More than anything, the
memory of being physically overpowered left him with a feeling of being permanently soiled. He
described it as a sense of contamination that clung to him, a dirtiness that no amount of showering
could ever seem to wash away. This feeling has become a constant companion, tainting his
interactions and his perception of himself.

The intrusive memories have also taught him what to fear, creating a vicious feedback loop. Because
the flashbacks so often bring with them the scent of stale whiskey and burnt food, Kofi now finds
himself in a state of constant olfactory vigilance. He will walk a different route to work to avoid
passing a restaurant kitchen whose vents sometimes exhale the smell of charred food. At his office,
where he designs educational programs, he avoids the staff room entirely after a coworker burns
their toast, the smell sending a jolt of panic through him. This hypervigilance has made him seem
distant and anxious to his colleagues, further cementing his belief that he has become
unrecognisable.

Not every trigger needs to bring back the full memory to inflict damage. Last week, a well-meaning
manager asked him about his family, a simple, friendly question about their well-being. The comment
itself, so reminiscent of Marcus’s sneering words, was enough to make Kofi’s skin crawl. It didn’t
precipitate a full flashback, but it instantly activated his deeply held conviction that he was
contaminated, that this ugly secret was now somehow connected to the family he loved. He felt the
unfairness of it all like a physical weight—the inability to have a normal, human conversation

help. He could no longer stand the person the trauma had made him, and he was terrified that if
something didn’t change, he would lose himself completely.
