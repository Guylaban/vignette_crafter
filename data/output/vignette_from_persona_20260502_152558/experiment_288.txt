Mark has been a military medic for twenty years, a man who defined himself by his ability to bring
order to chaos. Now, six months after returning from a disaster relief deployment, the chaos was
inside him. At forty-five, he felt like a stranger inhabiting his own life, the man he used to be
lost somewhere in the rubble of a collapsed primary school. The earthquake had hit without warning,
and his unit was among the first on the scene. He remembers the dust, thick and chalky, and the
sounds—a dreadful grinding of concrete and rebar, punctuated by cries that were quickly swallowed by
the unstable structure. He’d spent fourteen hours inside the ruins, crawling through spaces that
were constantly shifting, the world a disorienting maze of broken desks and fallen ceiling tiles.

The memories don't arrive as a coherent story. Instead, they ambush him as sensory fragments, a
jumble of images and sounds he cannot place in the right order. He’ll be cleaning his rifle and
suddenly feel the phantom weight of a small, cold hand in his, or hear the high-pitched shriek of
stressed metal that preceded the secondary collapse. When these flashes come, the same raw terror he
felt in that darkness washes over him, so absolute and suffocating that he has to physically brace
himself, shutting his eyes and forcing his mind onto something else, anything else, until the wave
of emotion subsides. He has learned that trying to piece the events together is impossible; the
sequence is lost to him, a confused reel of his worst moments playing on a loop.

This disjointed horror has become the lens through which he now views himself and the world. Each
terrifying, chaotic memory fragment serves as fresh evidence for a verdict he has already passed:
that he is a failure. The medic who was cool under fire, the man who could stabilize a patient in
the back of a bouncing Humvee, was gone. In his place was someone who had failed to save a child
pinned under a concrete beam. The memory of her face, of the moment the light left her eyes,
confirms his belief that his skills are meaningless in a world so fundamentally cruel and random.
His entire belief system—in duty, in competence, in the idea that good people could make a
difference—has been completely destroyed. He no longer feels like a medic; he doesn’t know who he is
anymore.

past he can even access. His mind, convinced of his own failure, selectively retrieves only the
evidence that supports this conclusion. He cannot recall the faces of the twelve people he did pull

it is always the most painful, chaotic, and incriminating fragments that surface, which in turn
reinforces his conviction that he is irrevocably broken. The constant, low-level hum of being on
guard also feeds this despair. Even in the relative quiet of the base, he cannot relax. Any
unexpected sound—a dropped tray in the mess hall, a truck backfiring on the perimeter road—sends a

hypervigilance makes it impossible to feel safe, subtly confirming his new belief that the world is
an inherently dangerous place and that he is no longer equipped to navigate it.
