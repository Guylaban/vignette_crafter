the foulness that now lived inside her. The more she felt isolated and set apart from him, the more
her mind seemed to conjure the past. Sitting alone on the porch while Frank was out, the profound
sense of being an outsider would wash over her, and it was in those moments that the memory of the
assault would return with its greatest force.

To manage the terror, Martha’s world began to shrink. She started by having Frank pick her up from
work, unable to bear the thought of the bus. When a neighbour would start a conversation about the

Martha would make an excuse and retreat to the house. There, she would pour a glass of whiskey,
needing the burn in her throat to quiet the images in her head. But the relief was fleeting; the
whiskey she drank to numb herself often had the opposite effect. In the hazy hours after her second
glass, the very memories she was trying to suppress would break through with startling clarity, more
fragmented and terrifying than when she was sober.

Her self-imposed isolation was reinforced by the belief that she was no longer the person she had
