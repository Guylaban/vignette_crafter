Anya had spent two decades in the air, a combat pilot for whom control was not just a profession but
a state of being. The sky had rules. Physics, aerodynamics, protocol—they were unforgiving but
predictable. It was her six-month tour on the ground, volunteering with a relief agency in a war-
torn mountain region, that had dismantled that world. The trauma was not a single event but the
brutal culmination of one winter convoy mission. The cold was relentless, seeping through the floor
of the truck as it ground its way up a narrow pass. She remembered the specific groan of the engine,
the way the snow muffled all other sound, and then the sudden, concussive blast that was both sound
and force. In the chaotic aftermath, under a sky choked with grey snow, she made a decision about
who to help first—a choice that now plays in her mind on a continuous, silent loop.

Back at her home on the airbase, the world began to shrink. At first, it was the memories. The sound
of a car engine turning over outside her window would be enough to trigger them. Suddenly, she
wouldn't be in her living room but back on that snowy pass, the metallic taste of fear in her mouth.
These sensory fragments felt more real and vivid than her own wedding photos sitting on the
mantelpiece. The memory brought with it an immediate and overwhelming sense of threat. The quiet,
manicured streets of the base felt menacing, each shadow a potential ambush. She began waking
multiple times a night, her heart pounding, to check the locks on the doors and windows, listening
intently for any sound that might signal an intruder.

This persistent state of high alert began to corrode her fundamental beliefs. The world, which she
had always viewed as a place where competence and courage mattered, now seemed utterly random and
malevolent. This profound sense of threat convinced her that her entire worldview had been a naive
fantasy. Now, waking from a nightmare into the cold morning air does more than just startle her; the
chill itself feels like proof of a hostile universe, confirming the new, terrifying belief that
nothing is safe and she is no longer the person she thought she was. She feels she has lost herself,
a capable leader replaced by a stranger ruled by fear. This loss of identity has hollowed out her
life; even the singular joy of flying feels meaningless now, a series of mechanical actions devoid
of purpose.

Her days are now governed by a desperate effort to manage these feelings. Her dark appraisals—that
she is permanently broken, that the world is irredeemably dangerous—drive her to retreat. She
meticulously avoids the news and any films depicting conflict, a stark change for someone whose life
is embedded in the military. This sense of being under constant threat also fuels a compulsive need
for distraction; after a near-panic attack when a new recruit approached her too quickly from
behind, she spent three hours and over a thousand dollars online, the packages arriving days later
like artifacts from another person’s life. Above all, the memory itself dictates her behaviour. To
escape the vivid images of snow and smoke, she retreats into endless loops of self-blame, dissecting
the decision she made, believing that if she can just pinpoint her failure, she can somehow contain
the chaos of the memory.

These strategies, meant to protect her, have become a cage. By refusing to engage with anything
related to her trauma, she ensures the memory remains unprocessed, a toxic fragment that can
detonate without warning. Her rumination on being to blame keeps the event perpetually in the
present, making her hyper-attuned to the sound of any passing vehicle. The constant vigilance, the
checking of locks and listening for intruders, only reinforces her conviction that danger is
imminent, tightening the knot of fear. When the memory does break through, it is now filtered
through the lens of her own worthlessness, each recall a fresh indictment of her failure. A night of
poor sleep, punctuated by waking to check for threats, leaves her so frayed that the next day, every
car sound is a potential attack.

She finally sought help after an incident in the cockpit during a routine pre-flight check. For a
full thirty seconds, she could not make sense of the instruments in front of her. The numbers and
lights were meaningless symbols. She felt a profound and terrifying numbness, as if she were a
stranger observing her own hands, unable to command them. The moment passed, but the fear it
instilled did not. She came to therapy not knowing how to describe what happened—the words simply
will not form—but with the desperate hope of finding the person she used to be, terrified that the
competent pilot has been lost for good in that snow-choked mountain pass.
