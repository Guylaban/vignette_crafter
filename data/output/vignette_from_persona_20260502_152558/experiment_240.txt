Imani, a 23-year-old environmental scientist, traces the start of her current difficulties back to a
single, sun-drenched afternoon six months ago. At a family gathering by a lake, her younger brother,
Daniel, had gone for a swim. Imani was meant to be watching him but had turned away for a moment to
answer a work call. The call lasted only a few minutes, but when she looked up, the water was still
and Daniel was gone. The memory of what followed does not exist for her as a coherent narrative, but
as a series of punishing sensory fragments that ambush her without warning: the placid shimmer of
the lake, the sudden, jarring silence where laughter had been, the icy shock of her own scream. When
these images intrude, it feels as though the event is happening all over again in the present
moment, not as something that is over and done with.

In the initial weeks, Imani’s body seemed to hold the memory more than her mind. Her heart would
begin to pound, her palms would sweat, and a wave of nausea would rise in her throat before she
could even consciously register what she was remembering. This visceral sense of immediate danger
made the fragmented memories feel even more potent and threatening, convincing her that to recall
the event fully would be unbearable. As a result, she finds herself instinctively pushing the images
away before they can cohere, leaving the story perpetually unfinished and its horror undiminished.
When her parents tried to speak with her about it, she discovered she couldn't; she knew what had
happened, but the words to describe the sequence of events, her own role in them, simply would not
form. This constant physiological alarm and the fractured nature of the memory began to feed a set
of devastating conclusions about herself and the world.

The terrifying, incomplete flashes of memory gave rise to a deep and unshakable conviction of her
own culpability. She became certain that someone else—a better sister, a more responsible
person—would have stopped the event from happening. The sheer injustice of a vibrant sixteen-year-
old life being extinguished so arbitrarily felt like a verdict on her own character; she had come to
believe the event happened to her because she was the sort of careless person to whom such tragedies
occur. This belief that she was fundamentally flawed made her feel constantly on edge, her body
perpetually braced for the next disaster she would inevitably fail to prevent. This pervasive sense
of threat only served to reinforce her negative beliefs, creating a feedback loop where her
hypervigilance was taken as proof that the world was indeed a dangerous place and she was uniquely
unequipped to navigate it.

These punishing thoughts became the engine for behaviours designed to manage an unbearable internal
state. The belief that she was to blame for Daniel’s death has led to relentless rumination, where
she spends hours replaying the moments leading up to the tragedy, torturously examining every choice
she made, a cycle of self-blame that offers no resolution. This obsessive review keeps the memory
fragments raw and prevents them from being integrated into her life’s story. To avoid the ambush of
memory, she scrupulously avoids watching any television show or film that might feature water or
drowning, shrinking her world to fend off the past.

Now, six months on, these patterns have solidified into a self-sustaining cycle that dominates her
life. Her heightened sense of threat manifests in unexpected ways; as an environmental scientist
often coordinating with teams in the field, she is thrown into a state of panic when a colleague is
even ten minutes late checking in. Her mind doesn't consider traffic or poor signal; it leaps
directly to catastrophe, a conviction that something terrible has happened. The ensuing wave of
adrenaline is overwhelming, and to escape the internal chaos of her own fear and self-recrimination,
Imani finds herself seeking external conflict. She will pick an argument with her mother over a
trivial matter, the ensuing drama a welcome, if fleeting, distraction from the deeper horror. Yet,
each angry outburst only serves as further evidence for her belief that she is a difficult, flawed
person, confirming the very appraisals that fuel her distress and driving her back into agonising
rumination.

Imani sought therapy after a recent incident where she lashed out at her best friend for being late
to meet for dinner, accusing her of being careless and unreliable. Seeing the profound hurt on her
friend’s face, she realised with stark clarity that her attempts to control her inner world were
systematically destroying her relationships. She reports being unable to sleep most nights, her body
still reacting to a danger that passed months ago. She is terrified of the person she has become but
feels utterly powerless to change, hoping therapy might offer a way to live with what happened
without being entirely consumed by it.
