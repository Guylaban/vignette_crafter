Imani had lived in the same small house for forty-five years, twenty of them as a widow, and had
always found her deepest solace in the attached studio where she worked as a potter. The scent of
wet clay and the hum of the wheel were the constants of her world. That ended seven months ago on a
Tuesday evening. She was cleaning her tools when the sound of shattering glass from the studio’s
back window ripped through the quiet. A man, little more than a silhouette against the fading light,
climbed through the broken pane. He was in and out in less than five minutes, taking her purse and
the small cash box she kept for local sales, but the speed of the violation did nothing to lessen
its impact. He had been in her space, her sanctuary, and the world had never felt the same since.

In the weeks that followed, a deep and pervasive belief settled over Imani: the world was not just
unkind, but actively dangerous. This conviction became the lens through which she viewed everything.
Because she was certain that danger was always lurking, her nervous system remained on high alert,
refusing to stand down. She found she was unable to relax, even when sitting in her favourite
armchair with a cup of tea. Every creak of the floorboards, every car that slowed on the street
outside, became a potential threat that she would monitor with breathless intensity. The belief that
the world was a dangerous place kept her in a perpetual state of vigilance, listening for sounds
that might mean someone was trying to get in.

This new, terrifying understanding of the world also changed the nature of her memory of the
assault. The recollection was no longer something she could place in the past; because she believed
she was still in danger, the memory itself felt like an ongoing threat. It was no longer a static
event but an active one, forcing its way into her mind as a series of vivid, intrusive images—the
glint of glass on the floor, the shape of the man’s shoulders. When the memory arrived, it felt as
though the event was happening all over again, a terrifying reliving that left her heart pounding
and her hands shaking. The memory of the break-in became proof of her core belief that she was
fundamentally unsafe, and each time it intruded, that belief was cemented a little deeper.

The horror of these intrusive images would propel her into action. A sudden, unwanted picture of the
shattered window flashing in her mind would send her on a frantic tour of the house, her fingers
checking and re-checking the locks on the doors and the latches on the windows. It was a desperate
attempt to impose order on a world that felt chaotic and threatening, a direct response to the
feeling that the past was invading her present. She also began checking her reflection in dark
windows at night, her mind half-expecting to see a figure standing behind her.

This compulsive checking was not only driven by the memories. Her constant state of hypervigilance
meant that any unexpected noise could trigger the same urgent need for reassurance. The sound of a
branch scraping against the siding would have her out of bed in the middle of the night, confirming
that the back door was bolted. She had come to believe that if she allowed herself to truly think
about the event, she would fall apart completely, and this conviction fueled her desperate attempts
to manage her environment instead. She tried to suppress the thoughts, to push them away, but this
only made them return with more force, which in turn drove her to check the locks with even greater
frequency.

These strategies, however, offered no lasting peace. In fact, they became the engine of her ongoing
distress. Each time Imani checked a lock and found it secure, it didn't reassure her that she was
safe; it reinforced the terrifying idea that a catastrophe had just been narrowly averted *because*
of her vigilance. The brief moment of relief only strengthened her underlying conviction that the
world was a dangerous place and that she could never let her guard down. Her own body seemed to
agree; the constant adrenaline, the racing pulse even in moments of quiet, felt like undeniable
proof that she was still in danger, making her feel as if her body had become an enemy she couldn't
control.

The studio, once her refuge, now gathers dust. The clay has dried out, and the wheel sits silent.
The joy of her work has been replaced by a gnawing fear that has shrunk her world to the interior of
her triple-locked home. Imani finally sought help because the exhaustion became unbearable. She is
tired of being a prisoner in her own house, haunted by an event that is seven months past but feels
like it is happening every single day. She hopes, more than anything, to one day feel safe enough to
sit at her wheel again and not listen for the sound of breaking glass.
