Imani, a 63-year-old financial analyst, presents with a quiet dignity that belies the profound
distress that has governed her life for decades. Originally from East Africa, she built a successful
career in the United States after arriving as a refugee over thirty years ago. The trauma of her
flight, however, remains an active and organizing principle in her daily existence. The events
unfolded during a period of intense civil conflict. Soldiers arrived in her village without warning,
forcing residents from their homes at gunpoint. Imani recalls the suffocating helplessness of being
herded along a dusty road, her future and her family’s safety completely in the hands of volatile
young men in uniform. For days, they were forced to march, eventually escaping into a dense,
unfamiliar forest where they hid, subsisting on what little they could find, the sounds of the
natural world inseparable from the constant fear of discovery.

This history has etched itself into her present. The sight of a police officer on her commute, the
rustling of leaves in the park across from her office building, or a direct order from a supervisor
can instantly dissolve the intervening years. These triggers reliably activate a cascade of
protective, yet ultimately debilitating, behaviors. For instance, when a new manager at her firm
recently insisted she change a long-standing reporting process without discussion, the feeling of
being forced into a course of action sent a jolt of panic through her. She immediately acquiesced,
avoiding any hint of conflict, but spent the next forty-eight hours in a state of high alert. She
was consumed by visions of being fired, losing her home, and becoming a burden on her children. This
catastrophizing extended outwards; she called her son, a university student in another state, five
times in a single evening, inventing reasons for her calls while seeking reassurance that he was
safe from some unnamed, impending disaster. This pattern is deeply entrenched: an external trigger
signifying loss of control or the presence of authority prompts an immediate retreat into conflict
avoidance and an intense, obsessive worry about the safety of her loved ones.

Over the years, these maladaptive strategies have paradoxically made her world more dangerous, not
less. Her persistent avoidance of any situation that might involve disagreement has made her
exquisitely sensitive to authority. Because she has spent years preemptively placating supervisors
and sidestepping confrontational colleagues, a standard performance review now feels like an
interrogation, with her manager’s authority looming as a significant threat. This heightened
sensitivity means that triggers which might otherwise be minor are now potent. A police car parked
outside her local grocery store is no longer just a part of the urban landscape; it is a direct
signal of danger that makes her heart pound and forces her to shop elsewhere. By trying to avoid all
potential threats, she has inadvertently amplified their power, shrinking her life until she is
surrounded by things she feels she must escape.

The consequences of this cycle have become increasingly severe. She has turned down promotions that
would require more managerial duties, fearing the inevitable conflicts that come with leadership.
More painfully, her relationships with her adult children have become strained. Her constant,
anxious check-ins and dire warnings about everyday risks are beginning to feel controlling to them,
creating the very distance she dreads. It was after a particularly tense phone call, where her
daughter finally pleaded, "Mom, you have to let me live my life, I can’t carry your fear for you,"
that Imani recognized the need for help. She has come to therapy with a desperate hope to quiet the
alarms that have been ringing for thirty years, terrified that the very behaviors she uses to keep
her family safe are becoming the thing that will ultimately drive them away.
