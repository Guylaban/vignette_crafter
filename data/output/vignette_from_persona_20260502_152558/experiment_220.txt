retired, he regarded them as alien things, prone to trembling for reasons he couldn't command. The
breakdown had begun six months earlier, during what should have been a routine appendectomy he was
supervising. A faulty electrocautery unit sparked, igniting the surgical drapes in a flash of
shocking heat and acrid smoke. He remembered the smell most vividly—a foul mix of burning plastic
and something horribly organic—and the sight of the young resident closest to the machine, her
scrubs alight. An was pulled back by a nurse before he could react, a helpless witness to the chaos
and the smell of burning that filled the operating room.

In the weeks that followed, An tried to impose his surgeon’s discipline on his own mind. He refused
to discuss the incident, attempting to wall it off through sheer force of will. He found himself
trying hard not to think about the trauma, a constant, exhausting effort of suppression. This
strategy, however, only seemed to make the memory more volatile and strange. When fragments of the
event surfaced, they were disordered and confusing; he couldn't place the sequence of the fire, the
screaming, and the alarms in the right order. At other times, a wave of pure, image-less terror
would wash over him while he was reading or drinking tea, his body seized with the full emotional
horror of the event without any accompanying picture. The memory, when it did have a shape, felt as
if it belonged to someone else, a disturbing film he had been forced to watch.

This profound sense of disconnection from his own experience solidified into a damning self-
appraisal: there was something fundamentally wrong with him. The alien quality of the memory was
proof to him that his mind, once his most trusted tool, was now defective. This belief that he was
broken made the memory fragments feel even more shameful and disjointed, pushing them further away
and making them harder to integrate. His body, too, had become an enemy, launching into a state of
high alert for no discernible reason. He came to believe he had to be especially careful, that the
world was filled with hidden dangers he had previously ignored. This conviction for constant
vigilance meant he was perpetually on edge, startled by the clatter of a dropped tray in the
hospital cafeteria or the sudden ring of his phone.

experience, and others like it—the specific warmth of the shower on his skin reminding him of the
heat flash—led him to meticulously avoid situations that might provoke a similar reaction. He

indulgence when he needed to be on guard.

memory would, in turn, spike his sense of immediate threat, convincing him his body was right to be
in a constant state of alarm. His attempts to suppress these thoughts were failing; in fact, the

session that he wasn’t sure if his mind could be repaired, but he had to try. He wanted his hands,
and his life, to feel like his own again.
