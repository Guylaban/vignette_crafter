Liam, a 46-year-old graphic designer, described the last eight months of his life as living in a
"grey fog." Before the incident, his days were structured around colour palettes and client
deadlines, a world he now struggles to recognise. It was a protest, one he attended out of a sense
of civic duty, that shattered this world. The afternoon had been peaceful until the police line
advanced without warning. He remembers the sudden chaos, the shouting, and the sight of an older
woman falling near him. When Liam moved to help her, he was thrown to the ground by an officer. He
recalls the rough texture of the pavement against his cheek, the weight on his back, and a sharp,
searing pain in his shoulder. He saw the officer's boot, the frantic faces in the crowd, and the
blood on the woman's temple before his own world went dark for a moment.

In the weeks that followed, the memory of that day refused to settle. It wasn’t a coherent story he
could tell, but a series of sensory fragments that ambushed him. When he tried to explain what had
happened to his wife, he would lose the thread, the words dissolving as the overwhelming wave of
panic and rage washed over him, forcing him into silence. He came to believe that his life, as he
knew it, had been destroyed by that singular, brutal injustice. This conviction that nothing would
ever be right again made the memory feel perpetually raw and present, preventing it from fading into
the past. This sense of a ruined future also made him acutely sensitive to any reminder of the
event; his own unwanted thoughts of the day now felt like evidence of his brokenness, making them
potent triggers in themselves.

Now, his life is governed by a constant, humming vigilance. The core belief that the world is
fundamentally unfair and his life is irrevocably damaged keeps his body in a state of high alert, as
if waiting for the next blow to fall. This pervasive sense of threat makes it impossible to relax,
even in the familiar comfort of his home office. He finds himself constantly scanning rooms, mapping
out escape routes before he can sit down. This hypervigilance, in turn, acts as a priming mechanism;
with his system already scanning for danger, the fragmented memory of the pavement and the officer’s
boot is always just beneath the surface, ready to surge forward.

The triggers are everywhere. A police siren wailing down the street is enough to make his heart
pound and his body clench, instantly transporting him back to the chaos of that afternoon. Last
week, while walking his dog, he saw a cyclist who had fallen and scraped his knee, and the sight of
the raw, bloody wound triggered the memory with such force he felt dizzy and had to lean against a
wall. When these memories return, they bring with them an overwhelming flood of emotion that feels
unbearable, confirming his deep-seated fear that he is in imminent danger.

To manage these intrusions, Liam has constructed a fortress of avoidance. He spends upwards of ten
hours a day lost in the digital world of his phone and video games, a desperate attempt to outrun
his own thoughts. He has stopped seeing friends and now works exclusively from home, telling his
wife it’s more efficient, but knowing it’s because he cannot bear the thought of navigating the
unpredictable world outside. He keeps his mind occupied at all times, with podcasts playing
constantly in his headphones while he works. But these strategies are a fragile dam. The relentless
mental distraction only seems to make the memories more potent when they inevitably break through a
moment of silence. And the long hours of screen-based escape leave him more susceptible to the power
of his own intrusive thoughts when he finally puts his phone down, which in turn trips the wire of
his threat system. This avoidance has also reinforced his body’s sense of being under siege; by
retreating from the world, he has implicitly taught himself that it is too dangerous to face,
leaving him in a perpetual state of tension.

He finally sought therapy after his wife found him at 3 a.m., sitting in the dark, staring at his
computer screen, having not slept for two days. She told him, her voice strained with worry, that
she felt like she was losing him. For Liam, it was a moment of clarity in the fog. He came to the
first session looking exhausted, admitting that he fears the damage is permanent. "I used to create
things," he said, his voice quiet. "Now, I just feel like I'm trying to stop everything from falling
apart. I don’t know if I’ll ever find meaning in anything again."
