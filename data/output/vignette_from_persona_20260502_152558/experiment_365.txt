Arthur, a 56-year-old dancer of international renown, presented for therapy eight months after a
motor vehicle accident. For decades, his life had been defined by discipline, grace, and the precise
control of his body in space. That control vanished on a rainy Tuesday evening. Driving home from
his studio, a specific adagio he was choreographing to filled the car. He was lost in the music,
envisioning the movement, when a young woman on a bicycle swerved into the intersection. The impact
was sickeningly dull. He remembers the shattering of glass, the music abruptly stopping, and the
sight of her crumpled form on the wet asphalt. A crowd gathered quickly, their faces a blur of
horror and accusation, before the sirens began to wail. The young woman, a promising conservatory
student, survived but with injuries that ended her own dancing career.

In the months that followed, Arthur’s world constricted. The city, once his stage, became a
landscape of potential threats. He began taking circuitous routes to avoid the intersection where
the accident occurred, the extra forty minutes a small price to pay to prevent the wave of nausea
that accompanied the location. The triggers, however, were not so easily outmaneuvered. Hearing that
specific adagio on the radio or in a cafe was no longer just a memory; it was a verdict on his
character. The music was an immediate confirmation of the belief that had taken root: he had failed
to protect another human being, and for this, he could never forgive himself. Similarly, the press
of bodies in a crowded subway car or a packed theatre lobby would transport him back to the moments
after the crash, the sea of staring faces making him feel judged and fundamentally dangerous.

Overwhelmed by this sense of having failed in his most important role—not just as a driver, but as
an artist and mentor who should have been a source of creation, not destruction—Arthur found himself
retreating. He began to actively try to suppress thoughts of the accident, a constant and exhausting
mental effort. When that failed, he would spend hours watching old performance tapes, dwelling on
the man he used to be before the event, a man of confidence and poise. He was certain that this
agile, powerful figure was gone forever, replaced by a husk. This constant effort to suppress his
thoughts and the relentless dwelling on the past left his nervous system frayed, leading to a
pervasive sense of threat. He found he was unable to relax, even in the safety of his own home with
his husband. His body remained rigid, coiled, as if anticipating another impact. Sleep became a
battleground; he would lie awake for hours, convinced he needed to stay alert, though for what
danger, he could not say.

This pervasive state of threat, where his concentration was shot and his body was always braced for
impact, made him exquisitely sensitive to reminders. His hypervigilance meant he was always
scanning, and so the image of crumpled metal from a minor fender-bender seen on his commute would
hijack his mind for hours, the crash imagery playing on a loop. His conviction that he was a failure
also made him scan his environment for proof of his own inadequacy, which in turn primed him to
notice the very things he dreaded, like crowded spaces where he felt he might lose control. The
cycle had become self-sustaining. A trigger, like the sight of a car hit by another, would evoke the
crushing guilt, his belief that he had failed to protect the young woman. This appraisal would then
fuel his attempts to suppress the thought and dwell on his past self. These strategies would, in
turn, exhaust him and heighten his sense of threat, leaving him unable to relax or concentrate. This
hyperaroused state made him even more susceptible to being triggered by the next reminder,
tightening the loop.

The consequences have been severe. In the dance studio, his sanctuary, he finds his concentration
shattered. He cannot remember sequences and feels disconnected from his own body, a terrifying
experience for a man whose entire identity is built on physical mastery. His husband’s attempts to
comfort him are met with a vacant stare. Arthur finally sought help after he found himself standing
paralyzed in the living room for nearly an hour, rooted to the spot by a piece of music from a
neighbour's apartment, convinced that he was a danger to everyone around him and had forfeited any
right to peace. He came to therapy not seeking to forget, but with the desperate, quiet hope of
learning how to live with the man he has become.
