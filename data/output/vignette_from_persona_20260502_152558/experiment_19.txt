Priya had always taken pride in her body’s resilience. At forty-five, she could still haul a heavy
wrench and contort herself into tight crawlspaces, a strength she relied on as a self-employed
plumber. That sense of physical certainty shattered nine months ago. It began as a sudden, sharp
pain in her chest while fitting a new water heater, followed by a terrifying inability to draw a
full breath. She remembers the world tilting, the dizziness so profound she had to grip the cold
copper pipe to keep from collapsing. The paramedics, the wail of the siren, the sterile, antiseptic
smell of the ER—it all blurred into a diagnosis of a massive pulmonary embolism. She was told she
was lucky to be alive.

In the weeks after her discharge, Priya found that the hospital had followed her home. The scent of
bleach from cleaning a client’s bathroom could suddenly transport her back to the ICU, her heart
hammering against her ribs. More unnerving were the somatic echoes of the trauma. A moment of
breathlessness after climbing a flight of stairs, or a dizzy spell from standing up too quickly, was
no longer a simple physiological event. For Priya, these sensations became alarms, signals that her
body was on the verge of betraying her again. Each time she felt that familiar light-headedness, the
thought would flash, hot and certain: *It’s happening again. My body is failing.* This internal
conviction, that she was living inside a system she could no longer trust, began to govern her every
move. She had to be constantly on guard, she believed, because disaster could strike at any moment,
and she was utterly alone in this vigilance.

This profound distrust in her own body, coupled with the belief that she couldn't rely on anyone
else to understand or save her, led Priya to systematically shrink her world. She began turning down
jobs that required significant physical exertion, telling clients her schedule was full when she
knew the work would involve tight spaces or heavy lifting. The fear of triggering the breathlessness
was too great. She started taking longer routes to job sites, carefully navigating her van to avoid
driving past the hospital or any urgent care clinics, the sight of which was enough to make her
hands sweat on the steering wheel. Most critically, she ignored the reminders for her follow-up
cardiology appointment. The idea of submitting to a physical examination—of willingly placing
herself in a situation where her body's imagined fragility would be the sole focus—was intolerable.

This campaign of avoidance, meant to keep her safe, instead became a cage that reinforced her
deepest fears. By avoiding physically stressful situations, her cardiovascular fitness declined,
making her more susceptible to the very breathlessness she dreaded. Now, even a simple task like
carrying her toolbox from the van would leave her winded, which her mind seized upon as undeniable
proof of her body's unreliability. Her refusal to seek medical care meant that every minor ache or
flutter in her chest went unexamined, ballooning in her imagination into another impending
catastrophe. Her business has begun to suffer as she turns down more complex, lucrative jobs, and
the growing financial strain has only intensified her sense of isolation, cementing her belief that
she is truly on her own.

Priya sought therapy after a severe panic attack last week, triggered by nothing more than feeling
