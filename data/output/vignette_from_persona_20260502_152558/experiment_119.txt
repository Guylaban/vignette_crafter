front lines. That changed nine months ago in a dusty, sun-baked compound where he was coordinating
aid distribution. The attack was brutally fast. The screech of tires, the shouting, the sudden,
concussive blast that threw him against a container wall. He remembers the chaos, the smells of dust
and cordite, and a paralyzing moment of indecision when he was supposed to be directing people to
safety. He froze. That moment, a brief synaptic failure under impossible pressure, had become the
anchor point of his new reality.

In the months after his return to the U.S., what gnawed at him most was the silence that followed.
The men who overran the compound had faced no consequences; they had simply vanished back into the
conflict. This profound sense of injustice, the belief that what happened was deeply unfair and that
the world was a place where such acts went unpunished, began to curdle into a constant, pervasive
sense of threat. If that could happen with no accountability, Kofi reasoned, then safety was an
illusion and danger could erupt from anywhere, at any time. This conviction left his nervous system
on a permanent, humming alert. Back in his quiet suburban home, he found himself unable to
concentrate on a book or a conversation with his wife, his attention always snagged by a floorboard
creaking upstairs or the sound of an unfamiliar car idling on the street. He became superalert, a
man perpetually on guard in a world he no longer trusted.

The exhaustion of being so constantly watchful was immense. To manage the feeling of being a live
wire, always ready to jump at a slamming door or a sudden notification tone from the work phone he
now dreaded, Kofi developed a set of rigid, silent rules. This was his attempt to regain control, to
manage a world that felt menacingly unpredictable. At his open-plan office, he would only work at a
desk in the corner, where he could see all the entrances. He would constantly scan his surroundings,
tracking the movements of colleagues, a habit he knew was irrational but couldn't stop. Most of all,
he tried to avoid thinking about the event, pushing the memories away whenever they surfaced. He
would detach himself from them, speaking of the attack in a flat, third-person monotone as if it had
happened to someone else, a desperate strategy to keep the terror at bay.

But these strategies only seemed to tighten the coil of his anxiety. The constant checking of his
surroundings only served to confirm his belief in imminent danger; every person walking too quickly,
every unexpected noise became proof that he was right to be on guard, which in turn amplified his
hypervigilance. The very act of scanning for threats made the world feel more threatening.
Similarly, his attempts to suppress the memories made them more intrusive. They would ambush him
without warning—the smell of diesel fuel from a passing truck, a shout from a nearby construction
site—and the sensory fragments would slam into him, raw and terrifying. This proved to him that the
danger wasn't just outside, but inside him too, a part of himself he couldn't rely on or control.

His life has shrunk. The job he once loved now feels like a minefield of digital triggers. He can no
longer manage the agency's field accounts, the images from the region too potent a reminder. His
concentration is so poor that he rereads the same email five times, his mind always drifting back to
threat assessment. The final straw came last week when his wife, trying to wake him gently from a

ribs. Seeing the fear in her eyes made him realize he was losing not just himself, but the person he
loved most. Kofi has come to therapy because he is terrified that the man he was before the attack
is gone for good, and he is desperate to find a way to trust himself, and the world, enough to
finally stand down.
