Elena, a 53-year-old stockbroker with a formidable reputation on the trading floor, presented for
therapy six months after being mugged. The attack happened on a Tuesday evening, just a block from
her downtown office. She was walking to her car when a man emerged from an alleyway, his movements
sudden and sharp. He grabbed her briefcase, and when she resisted, he shoved her hard against a
brick wall. The sound of her head hitting the brick was a dull, sickening thud that seemed to echo
in the sudden silence that followed as he ran off. She remembers the slow, almost indifferent
response of the police, the paperwork that felt more important than her well-being. It was the first
crack in her belief that the world, or at least her corner of it, was fundamentally orderly and
safe.

In the weeks that followed, that crack widened into a chasm. Elena came to believe with chilling
certainty that the world was a pervasively dangerous place, and that the institutions meant to
protect people like her had utterly failed. This conviction began to colour every aspect of her
perception. At her desk, surrounded by the familiar cacophony of the stock exchange, she no longer
heard opportunity; she heard threats. The sudden ringing of a phone would cause her to jolt
violently in her chair, her heart hammering against her ribs. When a junior analyst would approach
her desk quickly, she would feel a surge of pure terror, her body tensing as if for a physical blow.
These moments were flooded with a potent mix of fear and a deep, bewildering shame for reacting so
strongly to things that had never bothered her before.

This raw, persistent sense of threat drove Elena to develop a rigid set of protective behaviours.
Believing that safety was now entirely her own responsibility, she began constantly scanning her
surroundings, her eyes darting around the office, the subway, and even her own home, searching for
signs of imminent danger. Her commute became an exercise in hypervigilance. The core of her new
worldview—that the world is dangerous and no one will help—also dictated how she interacted with
others. When she felt cornered or challenged, whether by an aggressive client on the phone or her
own husband during a minor disagreement, she would find herself becoming instantly, overly
compliant. She would agree to anything to de-escalate the perceived threat, a fawning response that
left her feeling weak and disgusted with herself.

These strategies, born from a desperate need for safety, began to form a suffocating, self-
reinforcing loop. Her constant scanning didn't reassure her; it only provided more "evidence" for
her belief that danger was everywhere, as every hurried pedestrian or shadowy doorway became a
potential threat. This, in turn, intensified her baseline sense of dread. Each time she defaulted to
a fawn response at work, she would later be consumed by anger and shame, which only reinforced her
appraisal of herself as helpless and the world as a place where she was perpetually unsafe. The
mental rituals she developed—tapping her fingers in a specific sequence before leaving the house to
"ward off" another attack—brought no relief, serving only to underscore how powerless she felt
against the threat she perceived. The constant effort of maintaining this state of alert left her
exhausted, and a profound emotional numbness began to set in. She described feeling "dead inside," a
hollowed-out version of the sharp, decisive woman she once was.

Elena sought therapy after a disastrous meeting with a major client. The client had been aggressive
in his negotiations, and instead of holding her ground as she normally would have, Elena had
conceded on every point, her voice barely a whisper. The loss to her firm was significant, but the
loss to her sense of self was catastrophic. She realized her attempts to control the world were
costing her the life she had worked so hard to build. She arrived at her first session visibly
tense, admitting that her greatest fear was that this hollow, terrified state was permanent, and her
greatest hope was simply to one day walk down the street without feeling like she was walking into
an ambush.
