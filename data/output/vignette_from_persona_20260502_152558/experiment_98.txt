Joseph had owned his salon, "The Gentleman’s Cut," for twenty years, and the ritual of closing was
as familiar as the feel of scissors in his hand. But eight months ago, that ritual was shattered. As
he locked the front door, two young men emerged from the adjacent alleyway. One reeked of cheap
liquor, the smell thick and cloying in the damp evening air. They cornered him, their movements
loose and menacing. He remembered their words more than the blows—taunts about him being a slow old
man, criticism of his fumbling with the keys. The memory of it wasn't a story he could tell; it was
a series of stark, silent pictures. The glint of something metallic in one man’s hand, a
contemptuous sneer, the dizzying rush of the pavement coming up to meet his face.

In the weeks that followed, Joseph tried to push it aside, to work as he always had. But the silent
film of the attack began playing on a loop in his mind. When his wife, Funke, would ask him what was
wrong, seeing the distant look in his eyes, he would just shake his head. He knew what had happened,
but he was unable to find the words to describe the visceral loop of images, leaving him feeling
profoundly alone with his terror. This inability to speak about it felt like a personal failing, the
first of many he would soon catalogue. He began dwelling on everything he believed he had done wrong
that night, a relentless internal monologue of self-blame.

The intrusive visual memories soon began to poison his present, making him hyper-aware of threat.
The memory of the attackers' taunts, in particular, had sensitised him to any form of correction.
When a client recently pointed out that a fade wasn't blended quite right, the casual criticism
landed like a physical blow, and Joseph felt a hot surge of shame and fear. Instantly, the images of
the mugging flooded his mind. He found himself avoiding eye contact, retreating into a tense silence
as he tried desperately to push the thoughts away. This pattern repeated itself: the intrusive
memories made him scan his world for danger, and any perceived criticism or suspicious glance from a
stranger would trigger a fresh wave of shame and an attempt to suppress the very memories that had
put him on edge.

His days at the salon, once a source of pride and social connection, became a minefield. The smell
of alcohol on a customer's breath was enough to make his hands tremble, instantly transporting him
back to the alley. The silent film would play, sharp and vivid, and in its wake, he would be
consumed by rumination over his own perceived weakness. To manage this, he started avoiding people,
especially new male clients who he felt he couldn't trust. He would have his receptionist tell them
he was fully booked for weeks. This avoidance only gave the self-blame more space to fester. In the
quiet of his shop, he’d dwell on his actions, certain that he had somehow invited the attack, that
his fear was a character flaw.

This constant self-blame left him feeling fragile and perpetually unsafe, which in turn made him see
threats everywhere. His world became a stage for potential aggressors; people acting suspiciously
were no longer just people waiting for a bus but were assailants in waiting. His attempts to
suppress the trauma by avoiding people and situations only amplified his sense of danger. The more
he avoided others, the more threatening any human interaction felt, creating a vicious cycle where
his own coping strategies fed the very triggers he was trying to escape.

Now, Joseph’s world has shrunk to his home and the sterile quiet of his near-empty salon. The easy
laughter and conversation that once filled the space are gone. The final impetus for seeking help
came last weekend, when he refused to go to his grandson’s fifth birthday party, terrified of the
crowd and the possibility of losing control. The deep hurt in Funke’s eyes was more painful than any
memory. He came to therapy not because he fears another mugging, but because he is terrified of the
man he is becoming—silent, isolated, and trapped in a loop of images he cannot speak and a shame he
cannot put down.
