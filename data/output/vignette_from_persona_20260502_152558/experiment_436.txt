David was a prosecutor, a man whose professional life was built on coherent narratives and the
logical assembly of facts. For the past six months, however, his own story had shattered into a
thousand incoherent pieces. The trauma occurred not in a courtroom, but in a restaurant where he and
his team were celebrating a hard-won conviction. He remembers the exhaustion, a deep bone-weariness
after weeks of trial prep, and the sharp, celebratory smell of wine being poured. Then came the
blast—a deafening roar that tore through the celebratory noise, replacing it with screams and the
sound of shattering glass. In the chaotic moments that followed, David froze. He saw things he
couldn't process, moments of terror and injury that refused to assemble themselves into a linear
sequence.

Now, six months later, the event lives not as a story he can tell, but as a series of sensory
ambushes. The memories, when they come, are not like other memories; they feel more real, more vivid
than the present moment. He will be reviewing a case file and suddenly see the pattern of the
restaurant's wallpaper with perfect clarity, or feel the phantom vibration of the explosion in his
chest. These fragments don't feel like the past. For David, the memory is a thing that is still
happening, an unending present-tense horror that he is trapped within. He cannot tell a clear story
of what happened, even to himself, because the event has never truly concluded.

The triggers that summon these fragments are woven into the fabric of his life. The sharp smell of
alcohol-based hand sanitizer on a colleague's hands can plunge him back into the restaurant, the
scent of spilled wine thick in the air. This sensory echo immediately activates a cascade of self-
recrimination; he is not just remembering, he is re-experiencing his own perceived paralysis, and
the thought arrives with the force of a verdict: *I should have protected them, and I failed*. The
chronic exhaustion from his demanding job, once a badge of honor, now feels like a constant, damning
reminder of his state that night, reinforcing the belief that he was weak when it mattered. A car
backfiring on the street outside the courthouse is no longer just a sound; it is the primary
explosion, and with it comes the crushing certainty that he is a man who did not act. He will never
forgive himself for not doing more.

This relentless self-appraisal acts as an engine for the trauma itself. The more David dwells on his
inaction and guilt, the more the fragmented memories seem to surge forward to provide evidence for
his case against himself. The thought, *I failed to protect them*, is often the immediate precursor
to a vivid, intrusive image of a fallen chair or the face of a terrified stranger. The memory, in
turn, provides what feels like irrefutable proof of his failure. Because the fragment feels so
intensely real and present, it confirms his belief not as a past mistake, but as an ongoing state of
being. He is a failure. The memory proves it. The proof summons the memory. This vicious loop has
become his constant companion, an internal trial where he is the sole witness, prosecutor, and
defendant, and the verdict is always guilty.

The intrusive memories have left him perpetually on edge, sensitizing him to the very triggers that
summon them. After a night plagued by these sensory replays, the world feels mined with threats. The
memory of the explosion makes every sudden noise—a dropped briefcase in the courtroom, a slammed
door down the hall—feel like a direct attack, causing him to flinch in a way that has not gone
unnoticed by his colleagues. This hypervigilance has eroded his professional composure and fed a
growing sense of distrust. He watches others, even his own wife, with a guarded suspicion, convinced
that people are not what they seem, that danger lurks behind every calm facade. He no longer goes
for drinks with his team, isolating himself from the very people he feels he failed to protect.

David finally sought therapy after his wife found him standing in their kitchen, rigid and staring,
moments after a neighbor’s firework went off. He was unresponsive, lost in a memory that felt more
real than his own home. He told her he couldn't escape it, that he was trapped in a loop of the
worst moments of his life. He came to his first session not asking to forget, but with a desperate,
lawyerly hope: to somehow assemble the fragments into a coherent narrative, a story with a
beginning, a middle, and most importantly, an end that might finally feel like it was in the past.
