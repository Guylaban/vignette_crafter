Her negative self-appraisals have become the lens through which she views everything. The deeply
held belief that she failed to do enough, that she will never forgive herself for not acting sooner
or more effectively, poisons her daily experience. This constant self-condemnation makes the world
feel perpetually dangerous, not because of external threats, but because she no longer trusts
herself. These thoughts also corrupt the memory of the trauma itself; when a fragment of that night
surfaces, it arrives drenched in an intense, hot shame that feels more real and vivid than any
ordinary memory. The memory, in turn, acts as its own trigger, a fresh reminder of her perceived
failure, which then sends her scanning her environment for new threats.

This unbearable state of internal alarm, a constant hum of fear and guilt, has driven her to
desperate measures. When the flashbacks feel like they are happening all over again—the feeling of
warm blood on her hands, the smell of alcohol—she finds herself reaching for any escape. This is
when she takes one of her partner’s old prescription anxiety pills, just to numb the crushing
negative emotions. At work, if a conversation veers toward weekend plans or parties, she feels
cornered, and her focus shifts entirely to making sure she is close to an exit, her body tensed to
flee. The intense feeling of threat leads directly to these behaviours, a desperate attempt to
regain some semblance of control. Because these escape plans prevent her from ever discovering that
a situation is safe, her nervous system has never received disconfirming evidence; the feeling of
imminent danger is therefore maintained at a constant, exhausting high.

These strategies, however, only tighten the trap. After a night of impulsively spending hundreds of
dollars online to distract herself from the memories, the morning brings not relief, but a fresh
wave of self-loathing. Her world has shrunk around these avoidance tactics; the conversations she
can no longer have and the social situations she must now escape have become potent reminders in
themselves, silent monuments to the night she cannot forget. The packages arriving at the door are
monuments to her lack of control, confirming her deepest fear: "The person I have become is
unrecognisable to me." This reinforcement of her negative self-image only fuels her anxiety, making
her more susceptible to triggers and heightening her general sense of threat. Taking unprescribed
drugs leaves her feeling foggy and disconnected, further alienating her from her work and her
partner, which she interprets as more evidence of her irreparable damage. It was after her partner
gently confronted her about the credit card bills and the missing pills that Leilani finally made an
appointment. She came to therapy not with hope, but with a quiet terror that the capable, caring
woman she once was is gone forever, replaced by this frightened and unpredictable stranger.
