For most of their seventy-five years, Kai had found a quiet satisfaction in order. As a financial
analyst, their world was one of predictable patterns, of risk calculated and mitigated. This sense
of control was shattered nine months ago. The assault in their own home was not just a physical
violation but an inversion of their world. The perpetrator, a man they knew vaguely from their
apartment building, had justified his actions with a stream of vitriolic criticism, berating Kai for
their perceived aloofness and lifestyle. The event itself is not a coherent narrative in Kai's mind;
they cannot tell a clear story, even to themself. Instead, it exists as a collection of sensory
shards: the rasp of the man's voice whispering justifications, and a sharp, metallic taste that
flooded their mouth when their lip was split against the floor. The police who responded later were
dismissive, their questions laced with an implicit blame that felt like a second assault.

The aftermath has been a slow, quiet collapse. The triggers are everywhere, insidious reminders of
the loss of a just and orderly world. At work, an environment that was once their sanctuary, the
sound of a manager sharply correcting a junior colleague can splinter Kai's focus. That simple act
of criticism, a normal part of office life, becomes a direct conduit to the trauma. Suddenly, the
memory is not a thought but a physical event: the coppery taste of blood fills their mouth, and they
can hear the low murmur of their assailant's voice, a sound only they can perceive. This sensory
ambush immediately reinforces a terrifying belief that has taken root since the attack: that their
mind is broken. These reactions, so visceral and uncontrollable, feel to Kai like definitive proof
that they are going crazy. They can no longer rely on their own mind to distinguish the present from
the past.

These intrusive sensory memories have become the new primary threat. Because the auditory and taste
fragments can strike without warning, Kai has begun to see potential triggers everywhere, scanning
their environment for any sign of authority, conflict, or misbehaviour. The sight of a police car
parked on their street during their commute home is no longer a symbol of security but a direct
threat, one that sends a jolt of panic through their body and immediately brings back the memory of
the officers’ cold, judging eyes. The memory itself, in its fragmented horror, has rewired their
perception of the world. Each time a fragment surfaces, it deepens the conviction that they are
fundamentally unsafe and that they cannot trust their own judgment.

This vicious cycle has tightened over the months, constricting Kai’s life. The fear of being
criticised has made them pull back at work. They have delegated tasks they once enjoyed, anything
that might invite review or correction, which colleagues have interpreted as disinterest. The pride
they once took in their meticulous work has been replaced by a constant, humming dread. This erosion
of their professional identity has fed a profound sense of meaninglessness; if their work, the
central pillar of their life for five decades, is now just a source of fear, then what is left?
Nothing in their life feels meaningful anymore. Their meticulously ordered world, once a source of
strength, now feels like a cage they have built to keep the memories at bay, and it is failing.

It was an incident with their grand-niece that finally brought them to therapy. The ten-year-old,
with the unthinking honesty of a child, had pointed out a mistake Kai made during a board game. Kai
flinched so violently they knocked the board to the floor, and the look of confusion and fear in the
child’s eyes was more devastating than any memory. In that moment, they saw not a monster, but a
person so wounded they were now wounding others. They sought help not with hope, but with a quiet
terror. "I think my reactions mean I'm going crazy," they stated in the first session, their voice
barely a whisper. "I'm afraid I'm going to break everything good I have left."
