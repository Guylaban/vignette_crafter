Sameer had always taken pride in his ability to create order and beauty from chaos, a skill central
to his work as an interior designer. For eighteen months, however, the chaos had been internal, a
storm that began the night his younger brother, Rohan, collapsed from a sudden, fatal heart attack
in the middle of a family dinner. One moment they were laughing over a shared memory, the next there
was a sickening thud, a panicked call to 911, and a silence that swallowed the house whole. That
silence, and the image of his brother on the floor, became the blueprint for his fear.

In the months that followed, the world became mined with reminders of his loss. He first noticed it
at work, when an older client began discussing redesigning her home after her husband passed away.
As she spoke about grief, Sameer felt a cold jolt, his heart hammering against his ribs before his
mind could even process her words. He was no longer in the client’s sunlit living room but back in
his parents’ house, the scent of the evening meal thick in the air, the world tilting on its axis.
He felt a surge of raw, undirected anger and had to excuse himself, claiming a sudden headache. This
pattern solidified: any mention of loss, the sight of a cemetery on his commute, or the approach of
a significant date would trigger this same visceral response. His body would react as if the
original threat were happening all over again, flooding him with panic and a sharp, irritable edge
that began to fray his relationships.

The sheer terror of these moments, the feeling of being ambushed by the past, drove him to seek some
semblance of control. He began a new, private ritual: mentally rehearsing the worst possible
outcomes for everyone he loved. If his wife was late from work, he wouldn't just worry; he would
meticulously construct the entire scenario of a car crash in his mind, from the screech of tires to
the imagined phone call. He reasoned that if he could just anticipate every tragedy, he would never
be caught so unprepared again. When the first anniversary of Rohan's death approached, the mere
thought of the date sent a wave of dread through him. To preempt the inevitable pain, he booked a
non-refundable trip out of the country, insisting to his wife that they needed a distraction,
effectively erasing the day from the calendar. This avoidance became his primary defense; he would
change the channel if a somber news story came on, or find an excuse to leave a conversation if a
friend started talking about a sick relative.

These strategies, meant to protect him, began to form a cage. The constant mental rehearsal of
worst-case scenarios didn't leave him feeling prepared; it left him in a perpetual state of high
alert. He was tense and jumpy, and the irritability that once only flared up after a trigger became
his baseline mood. He would snap at his wife for minor things, his patience worn thin by the
exhausting work of anticipating disaster. His attempts to avoid grief reminders meant that Rohan was
becoming a forbidden topic in their home, a ghost he was trying to wall off. His wife, who
desperately wanted to share her own sorrow, found herself grieving alone. The very strategies he
employed to manage the overwhelming sense of threat were, in fact, amplifying it, making the world
feel like a more dangerous and unpredictable place from which he needed to retreat even further.

What finally brought Sameer to therapy was the approach of the second anniversary. His wife, Priya,
told him quietly that she would be visiting Rohan’s grave this year, with or without him. She
confessed that his anger and his rigid avoidance were making her feel as though she were losing him,
too. The realization struck him with the force of a physical blow: in his desperate effort to manage
the memory of one loss, he was actively precipitating another. He is now terrified that letting go
of his rituals will mean letting the full horror of that night consume him, but a deeper fear has
taken root—the fear of what will be left of his life and his marriage if he doesn't.
