Sofia is a 22-year-old accountant who arrived for her first session stating that she felt like she
was “constantly waiting for the other shoe to drop.” It has been just over a year since her son,
Leo, then three years old, experienced a sudden and severe respiratory crisis. The event itself is
something she finds almost impossible to recount in a linear way. She speaks of it in fragments: the
terrifying, unnatural quiet that fell over their small apartment when Leo’s wheezing suddenly
stopped, the acrid, metallic taste of panic that filled her mouth as she fumbled with her phone to
call 911, and the gut-wrenching wait for the ambulance, a period she remembers as a silent, endless
void where she was certain he was dying.

This history now dictates the texture of her daily life, particularly in the structured environment
of her accounting firm. Sofia has come to perceive the world through a lens of imminent threat, one
where innocuous events are reinterpreted as warning signs. When a client is late submitting a tax
file, she doesn't feel impatient; she feels a surge of cold dread, a conviction that the delay
signals a catastrophe unfolding somewhere out of sight. This feeling is amplified by silence. The
low hum of the office is tolerable, but if it ever dips into true quiet—a lull between phone calls
or the moment after the printers stop—her nervous system ignites. Her heart begins to pound in her
chest, a powerful physical reaction that feels like a full-body alarm.

It is in these moments of perceived threat that the past erupts into the present. The silence of the
office triggers the memory of the silence in her apartment, and the delay from a client brings back
the agonizing wait for the paramedics. The memories are not coherent scenes but visceral, sensory
fragments. She doesn’t see a clear picture of the hospital room, but she can suddenly taste that
same metallic sourness of pure fear from that day, a vivid sensation that arrives unbidden and
unconnected to anything she is eating or drinking. She feels the panic rising, the raw, nonverbal
horror of it, but finds herself unable to form the complete memory or put the experience into words.
As soon as the feelings begin to crest, she instinctively pushes them away, forcing her mind onto a
complex spreadsheet or a client email—anything to block the memory before it can fully form.

This constant cycle of triggering and suppression has profoundly reshaped her view of herself and
the world. Each time a memory fragment breaks through, it reinforces a deep-seated conviction of her
own powerlessness. The memory of watching Leo struggle, unable to do anything but wait, has become a
core belief: she cannot stop bad things from happening. This feeling has built a wall around her.
When she is with friends or colleagues, she feels an unbridgeable distance. Their concerns about
dating or work stress seem to belong to another, safer world. She feels entirely set apart by her
experience, an isolation that has become a defining feature of her identity. She speaks of her
former self—a happy, optimistic young mother—as if mourning a different person. “I used to be a
happy person,” she stated flatly, “but now I am always miserable.”

The consequences have rippled through her life. Her professional reputation for being meticulous has
curdled into anxious impatience, and her supervisor has noted her increasing agitation over minor
delays. She has started declining invitations from friends, the effort of pretending to be okay too
exhausting to bear. She finally sought therapy after Leo’s fourth birthday party. Surrounded by
happy children and laughing parents, she felt nothing but a hollowed-out watchfulness, scanning the
room for threats, unable to feel a shred of the joy she so desperately wanted to. It was then she
realized she was not just sad, but trapped. She came to therapy hoping to find a way back to the
person she was, but terrified that the vigilant, isolated woman she has become is all that is left.
