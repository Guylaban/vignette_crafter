Adebayo had spent forty years as a man in complete control of his environment. As a senior bomb
disposal technician, his life was a study in precision, calm, and the meticulous management of
threat. That control evaporated eight months ago during a routine call to a decommissioned
industrial site. The device wasn't explosive but chemical, a corroded canister of nerve agent rigged
to a pressure-plate trigger. As he began the delicate work of neutralizing it, the canister’s casing
failed with a percussive, deafening crack. A colourless, acrid gas filled the small concrete room.
His last memory before his respirator-clad colleagues dragged him out was of a searing pain in his
lungs and the overwhelming, sterile smell of the decontamination tent, a smell that would later
merge in his mind with the antiseptic corridors of the military hospital where he spent the next two
weeks.

Since returning to his post, the very workplace that once defined his competence has become a
landscape of invisible threats. The familiar clang of a dropped tool or the sudden hiss of a
pneumatic line can send a jolt of pure terror through him, leaving him breathless and scanning the
room for an enemy he cannot see. This constant, exhausting state of high alert is now his baseline.
At his workstation, his eyes are never still, constantly tracking the movement of others, mapping
the quickest path to the exits. He has come to believe that danger is not an event, but the very air
he breathes, and that only his vigilance keeps him alive. This heightened sense of threat has, in
turn, made him exquisitely sensitive to the triggers themselves; he finds himself flinching at the
sound of a truck backfiring a block away, his body already reacting as if the concrete room were
collapsing around him again.

The triggers at work and the pervasive sense of danger they ignite have driven Adebayo into a new,
rigid set of routines. He has started working punishingly long hours, volunteering for every extra
shift, not out of duty, but to avoid the silence of being alone with his thoughts. The antiseptic
smell of the base's cleaning supplies, so similar to the hospital, sends a wave of nausea through
him, and in response, he has begun to rigorously avoid the on-base medical clinic, twice faking an
off-site emergency to miss his mandatory annual physical. He knows, on some level, that this is
unsustainable, but the thought of a doctor's office, with its sterile scents and probing questions,
feels more dangerous than any unexploded ordnance.

These strategies, meant to provide safety, have only tightened the cage. The excessive hours he
works to escape his own mind mean that when he finally returns home, the quiet house he shares with
his wife, Funke, feels cavernous and menacing. Being alone in a room, once a source of peace, now
feels like an invitation for ambush. He finds himself pacing the halls at night, checking locks, and
planning how he would get Funke out if the house were ever compromised. His avoidance has not
diminished the threat; it has expanded its territory until even his own home feels like hostile
ground. When Funke, worried by the dark circles under his eyes, gently touches his arm to get his
attention, he recoils with an alarming shout, his body reacting to her loving gesture as if it were
a mortal blow.

It was after one such incident last week, seeing the deep hurt and fear in his wife’s eyes as he
backed away from her touch, that Adebayo finally made this appointment. The man who could calmly
defuse a bomb with seconds to spare now feels like a stranger in his own body, a prisoner to a
nervous system that screams danger at every turn. He came to therapy not with a clear hope, but with
a desperate fear: that the control he has lost is gone for good, and that he is becoming a danger to
the one person he has always felt safe with.
