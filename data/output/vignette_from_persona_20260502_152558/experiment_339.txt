Arthur, a counselor for over thirty years, had always been the one to walk toward a crisis. When the
chemical plant on the edge of town exploded, sending a plume of toxic smoke into the sky, he did
just that, volunteering at the temporary shelter set up in the community gymnasium. He spent two
days there, listening to stories of loss and terror, breathing the acrid air that clung to
everyone’s clothes. The sounds of the gymnasium—the echoing cries, the frantic shouts, the thud of
donated water bottle cases being thrown from a truck—became a backdrop to the horror. It was six
months ago, but for Arthur, the disaster was not a past event. It was a constant, sensory presence.

The memory of that time rarely visited him as a clear story. Instead, it felt like a foreign object
lodged in his mind, something that belonged to someone else. When fragments did surface, they were
often just sensory echoes. The most potent was the smell; sitting in his quiet office, he would
suddenly be overwhelmed by the sharp, chemical scent of burning plastic, so real he would check his
own wastebasket for a fire. This olfactory recall was terrifying, and because the memory could
ambush him at any time, he became hypervigilant to the cues that might precede it. He found himself
constantly scanning his environment, sniffing the air for the first hint of smoke, his body tensed
for a danger that wasn't there.

This physical reaction, happening months later in the safety of his own home, was proof to Arthur
that his body was an enemy, a traitor that kept sounding an alarm for a fire that was long
extinguished. The memory’s fragmented nature and his body's persistent sense of threat reinforced
his belief that he had to be especially careful, that the world was fundamentally unsafe. Convinced
that he would not be able to handle the memory if it fully emerged, he actively fought to keep it at
bay. This constant effort, however, only seemed to preserve the memory in its most toxic form. His
attempts to suppress any thought of the event had a paradoxical effect; the memory would splinter
further, returning not as a thought but as a raw, disembodied feeling—a wave of pure terror without
an image to anchor it.

The triggers were everywhere. The smell of a neighbor's wood stove on a cold evening was enough to
bring back the chemical tang that flooded his senses. Last month, during a heated couples counseling
session, the husband gestured angrily and knocked a book off the table. The sudden motion and the
thud of the object hitting the floor sent a jolt of panic through Arthur. He immediately began
monitoring his own thoughts, a frantic internal scan for signs he was "going crazy." This fear of
losing control in front of a client led him to start avoiding any situation that might contain a
similar trigger. Invitations from old friends for a summer barbecue went unanswered; the thought of
being near an open flame, the smell of smoke hanging in the air, was unbearable. He began to
withdraw, the very work of connection that had defined his life now feeling too risky.

The cycle has been tightening for months. His constant vigilance, born from the belief that you
never know what can happen next, makes him acutely sensitive to potential triggers. A trigger, like
the smell of smoke from a restaurant kitchen, reactivates the terrifyingly vivid olfactory memory.
This sensory intrusion confirms his deepest fear: that his body is still in danger and that the
memory is too powerful to handle. This appraisal, in turn, fuels his avoidance—of friends, of
places, and most of all, of his own mind. He spends his days policing his thoughts, a strategy that
only ensures the memory remains a volatile, unprocessed threat.

Arthur finally sought help after he found himself unable to sit with a client expressing profound
grief, the emotional intensity in the room feeling like a precursor to his own terror. He is
exhausted from the constant monitoring and the self-imposed isolation. He feels he is losing his
grip not only on his own wellbeing, but on the profession that has given his life meaning. His
greatest fear is that he is permanently broken, and his hope is simply to feel safe in his own body
again, to trust that the fire is truly out.
