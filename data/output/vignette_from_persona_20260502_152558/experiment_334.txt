Priya, a 32-year-old city council member, presented as poised and articulate, yet her hands were
clenched so tightly in her lap they were drained of color. She had built her career on being a
visible, accessible public servant, but for the past eight months, she confessed, leaving her
apartment felt like preparing for battle. The shift began the night of the community center annex
collapse. She had been at the podium, fielding questions about a new zoning ordinance, when the
first groan of stressed metal echoed through the hall. She remembered the ceiling seeming to ripple,
the shower of dust, and then a deafening roar as the back third of the building gave way. She was
uninjured, but the memory of the chaos, the screams, and the sight of twisted rebar where a fire
exit used to be was inescapable.

In the weeks that followed, a pervasive sense of dread began to colonize her life. The world, which
had once felt knowable and largely safe, now seemed riddled with invisible fractures. This profound
feeling of imminent threat began to calcify into a set of rigid beliefs. Sleep offered little
respite, as she found it harder to feel safe at night, lying awake listening for any sound that was
out of place. Her apartment, once a sanctuary, now felt like a fragile container she had to
constantly monitor. This nightly state of high alert reinforced a grim new philosophy: the world
was, in fact, a fundamentally dangerous place. She was certain that the collapse wasn't a freak
accident but a confirmation of a hidden, terrible truth. She had come to believe that she had to be
on guard all the time, because you never knew what could happen next.

This conviction that constant vigilance was the only rational response to an inherently unsafe world
began to dictate her every action. Her work, which demanded public engagement, became a minefield.
Before entering any room—the council chambers, a constituent’s office, a coffee shop—she would first
identify all possible escape routes, her mind tracing the path from her chair to the door. During
meetings, she found herself unable to focus on agendas or debates; instead, she was constantly
scanning her surroundings, her eyes sweeping over the crowd, assessing each face for any sign of
malice, any hint of a threat. She became hypervigilant for anyone who seemed agitated or out of
place, her body tensing whenever someone approached her unexpectedly. She started declining
invitations to ribbon-cuttings and town halls held in unfamiliar venues, telling her staff she had
scheduling conflicts when, in truth, the thought of being in a new place where she couldn't assess
the danger was paralyzing.

These strategies, meant to keep her safe, have instead drawn the walls of her world in tighter,
creating a feedback loop of fear. The more she scans her environment for threats, the more she
finds: a loud bang from the street, a crack in the plaster, a disgruntled citizen in the gallery.
Each observation serves as fresh evidence, sharpening her sense of being in constant peril. The very
act of avoiding unfamiliar places has made the familiar world feel smaller and the unknown world
outside it monstrously threatening. Her hypervigilance has taken a professional toll; colleagues
have commented on her being distracted and jumpy, and she feels a growing distance from the very
constituents she entered public service to help. They are no longer people to connect with, but
potential dangers to be managed.

Priya finally sought therapy after an incident last week. She was scheduled to speak at the opening

glass-and-steel building, all she could see were potential points of failure. The weight of the
structure, the stress on the windows, the sheer number of people inside—it was too much. She fled
back to her car, overwhelmed by a physical sense of dread, and missed the event entirely. She is
terrified that this fear will not only cost her the career she loves but will one day leave her
unable to leave her home at all. Her hope is simple, yet feels impossibly distant: to once again
stand at a podium and feel the floor beneath her feet as solid ground.
