At fifty-three, he found himself a prisoner of a single afternoon. The trauma had occurred during a
routine home visit in a sprawling, low-income housing complex. The client wasn’t home, and as David
descended the narrow, windowless stairwell, a man who had been lingering in the hallway cornered him
between landings. The heavy fire door clicked shut behind them, sealing off the noise from the rest
of the building. The assault that followed was brutal and swift, leaving him with a profound sense
of helplessness he had never known.

In the weeks that followed, the memory of the attack began to intrude not as a coherent story, but
as sensory fragments. The stale, metallic smell of the stairwell, the feeling of concrete against
his back, the echo of the closing door—these moments would surface without warning, bringing with
them the same raw terror he felt when he was pinned to the ground. This jolt of fear was immediately
followed by a hot, suffocating wave of shame that seemed to emanate from his very core. He knew what
had happened, could feel its imprint on his body, but when he tried to explain it to his wife, or
even to himself, he found he could not find the words. The experience remained locked inside him, a
silent horror film playing on a loop.

This constant, internal replay of terror and shame reshaped his perception of the world into a
landscape of imminent danger. The memory of being trapped and overpowered made him feel perpetually
vulnerable. He began looking over his shoulder whenever he walked down the street, convinced he was
being followed. Any unexpected delay—his wife running late from the grocery store, a bus not
arriving on time—would trigger a catastrophic certainty that something terrible had happened. This
pervasive sense of threat only served to cue the memories more frequently; the fear of what might
happen would inevitably drag his mind back to what did, a vicious feedback loop that left him in a
constant state of high alert.

To manage this unbearable state, David’s life became a carefully curated set of routines and
restrictions. The terror that flooded him when he recalled the assault led him to instinctively
avoid any situation that remotely resembled it. He stopped using elevators at his office, taking
nine flights of stairs instead. He began refusing new cases if they involved home visits in
unfamiliar apartment buildings, a behaviour that was starting to draw notice from his supervisor.

any enclosed space where he couldn't immediately map out an escape route. His world, once defined by
his engagement with the city, was now defined by its avoidance.

avoiding unfamiliar places, he never had the chance to learn that they could be safe, cementing his

locked room.
