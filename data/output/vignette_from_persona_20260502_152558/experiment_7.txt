Kenji, a 33-year-old physical therapist, describes his life as having been cleaved into two distinct
eras: before the accident, and after. Seven months ago, on his drive home from the clinic, a semi-
truck jackknifed on the freeway, triggering a multi-car pile-up. He remembers the deafening screech
of tires, a percussive series of impacts, and the horrifying sensation of his car crumpling around
him. He was pulled from the wreckage with a fractured femur and a severe concussion, but the
injuries that have lingered are the ones no X-ray can detect. His work, once a source of pride and a
testament to his belief in the body's resilience, has now become a landscape of potential threats.

The psychological fallout began with his sleep. He would wake, gasping, from nightmares of twisting
metal, the phantom sensation of the impact echoing in his limbs. These nights left a residue that
clung to him all day, a low hum of anxiety that made it difficult to focus. The world outside the
perceived safety of his home began to feel menacingly loud. The sudden clang of a dropped weight in
the clinic gym or a car backfiring on the street would instantly trigger a full-body jolt, leaving
him shaky and breathless. This intense startle response made him feel jumpy and on edge for hours,
his concentration shattering as he struggled to bring his attention back to his patients. As this
state of high alert became his new normal, unwanted thoughts of the crash began to intrude more
frequently during the day, flashing images of shattered glass that would surface without warning.

At the heart of Kenji’s struggle is a profound and terrifying set of beliefs that have taken root
since the accident. He is convinced that his body has been permanently damaged. "I can no longer
trust my own body," he stated, a devastating admission for a man whose expertise is the body’s
mechanics. This appraisal of his own physical vulnerability makes the world feel fraught with peril.
He has come to believe that you can never know who will harm you, and this conviction transforms
every interaction into a risk assessment, feeding a constant, ambient sense of threat. A fleeting
startle response is no longer just a jump; it’s a brief, internal confirmation that says, *see, I’m
broken*. The morning after a nightmare, a passing thought reinforces this: *my body is so weak it
can’t even handle sleep*.

This deeply held fear that he is unreliable and fragile drives his attempts to reclaim a semblance
of control. At home, his wife has watched with concern as he has developed a rigid, compulsive need
for order. He spends hours cleaning and organising, arranging kitchen utensils into perfect, sterile
rows as if imposing order on his shelves could impose it on his nervous system. Before leaving the
house, he mentally rehearses worst-case scenarios—another car crash, a fall on the
sidewalk—believing this meticulous preparation will protect him. This internal monologue, meant to
arm him against the unexpected, instead serves to marinate his mind in visions of disaster.

These strategies, designed to make him feel safe, have had the opposite effect. His habit of
constantly scanning his surroundings for signs of threat only serves to confirm his fears. Walking
through the clinic’s therapy gym, he finds his eyes drawn not to the patients making progress, but
to the potential for harm: a heavy kettlebell that could be dropped on a foot, a resistance band
that could snap, a metal crutch that could be a weapon. This constant checking keeps his threat
system dialled to its maximum setting, ensuring he never feels at ease. It is a self-perpetuating
cycle: his belief in his own vulnerability makes him feel threatened, which compels him to scan for
danger, which in turn reveals more "dangers" that amplify his sense of threat.

The final impetus for seeking therapy came last week. A patient accidentally dropped a heavy plate
weight, and the resulting bang sent a shockwave through Kenji. He flinched so violently he stumbled
backward into a rack of equipment, the crash echoing the sound of his own accident. The
embarrassment was acute, but the terror was worse. He realised he could no longer function in the
one place he used to feel most competent. He came to therapy hoping to find a way to quiet the
constant alarm bells in his head, to once again feel at home in his own skin. His greatest fear is
that he is right—that he is permanently broken and will have to live the rest of his life as a
passenger in an unreliable body.
