Liam was twenty-one, newly married, and had spent six months working for a faith-based relief
organization at a distribution camp. The memory that now governed his life was from his last month
there. It wasn’t a coherent story, but a blast of sensory fragments: the sudden crack of gunfire,
the dust kicking up, the smell of diesel and panic. He’d been handing out grain sacks when the
shouting started, a torrent of Arabic from a group of armed men storming the perimeter. He remembers
diving behind a truck, the terror so absolute it felt like his heart had stopped. In the chaos, he
couldn't distinguish civilian from combatant, and a man running towards him for cover was shot just
feet away. The memory of that man’s face, and the helplessness, was the anchor of it all.

When he first returned home, the flashbacks were overwhelming. The memory didn't feel like a memory;
it felt like it was happening right now, in his quiet suburban apartment. He would be making coffee
and suddenly the smell of diesel would fill his nostrils and he would feel the same raw terror he
felt hiding behind that truck. The sheer unreality of the event made it hard to process; it was so
extreme, it felt impossible that it had actually happened to him. This visceral terror, resurfacing
without warning, began to poison his sense of safety. He became certain that danger was everywhere.
A sudden movement on the street behind him would put him immediately on guard, his body tensing for
an impact that never came. To escape the memories that ambushed him in the quiet of the night, he
began to delay sleep, staying up until he was so exhausted he would simply collapse, hoping to
bypass his dreams.

His work as a social ministry worker, once a source of purpose, became a minefield of potential
threats. The center was often noisy, with people from all backgrounds seeking help. One afternoon,
an argument broke out between two men in a language he didn't recognize, but the sound of shouting
was enough. It instantly triggered a profound sense of threat, and he felt a desperate urge to flee.
He saw the men not as clients in distress, but as imminent dangers, just like the strangers who had
stormed the camp. This constant feeling of being on guard made him scan his environment
relentlessly, which in turn made him hyper-aware of any potential reminder of the trauma. He started
noticing every news report mentioning military action, every passing conversation about conflict,
and each mention felt like a direct personal threat, pulling him back toward the epicentre of his
fear.

The cycle has grown tighter over the past few months. A news report on the television about the
military will immediately plunge him back into the memory, the images playing in his mind’s eye with
terrifying clarity. The memory itself, feeling so present and alive, ensures that any related sight
or sound—especially loud, angry voices—acts as a potent trigger. At the same time, his pervasive
sense of threat can conjure the memory out of thin air; a car backfiring down the street is enough
to make him feel hunted, and the sensory fragments of the attack will surge back, vivid and
immediate. After being triggered, he spends hours lost in rumination, meticulously replaying the
event, trying to find a point where he could have done something different to prevent what happened.

These attempts to control the memory only seem to strengthen its hold. The long nights spent awake
to avoid his dreams leave him exhausted, making the intrusive memories feel even more fragmented and
unreal during the day. This constant mental replay doesn't bring peace; it just keeps the trauma
fresh, preventing it from ever settling into the past. He has also begun to wall himself off from
anything positive. When his wife suggested they go out to celebrate their first anniversary, he
picked a fight over something trivial to avoid it. The thought of feeling happy or hopeful seems
impossible, even dangerous, as if letting his guard down for a moment would invite disaster. He can
no longer connect with the person he was before, or the life he is supposed to be building. He often
wakes in the middle of the night, his heart pounding, and walks through the apartment checking the
locks on the doors and windows, searching for a safety he never finds.

It was his wife who finally insisted he seek help. She found him standing in the living room at 3
a.m., staring at the wall, his face pale and empty. She told him she felt like he was disappearing.
Liam is terrified of talking about the event, certain that speaking it aloud will make it real in a
way he cannot survive. But the fear of losing his wife, and the last vestiges of himself, is now,
finally, even greater.
