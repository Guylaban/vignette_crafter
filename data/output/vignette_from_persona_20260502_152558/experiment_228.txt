straight, his words chosen with the precision of a programmer. He had worked from home long before
it was common, finding comfort in the logical, predictable world of code. For nearly fifty years, he
had managed to contain the past. The trauma was not from a war he had fled, but from a quiet,
brightly lit hospital room in America, where his firstborn son, only a few weeks old, fought for his
life against a sudden, aggressive infection. The baby had been so small, dwarfed by the incubator
and the tangle of wires monitoring his every breath. Binh and his late wife, Mai, had spent weeks in
that sterile environment, their lives suspended by the rhythmic, artificial beep of the machines.
Though his son eventually recovered, the experience had left a scar Binh believed was long faded.

The first significant tremor occurred during a routine physical six months ago, shortly after the
first anniversary of Mai’s passing. As he sat on the examination table, the crisp, chemical smell of
antiseptic wipes hit him, and the familiar scent instantly transported him. The doctor's office
dissolved, replaced by the neonatal intensive care unit from 1973. This was not a simple
recollection; it was a full-bodied re-experiencing. The memory arrived like a film reel clicking on
in his mind, playing the same agonizing scenes over and over: the frantic look in the young nurse’s
eyes, the unnatural blue tinge to his son’s skin, the suffocating feeling of helplessness. He felt
his heart begin to hammer against his ribs, a cold sweat breaking out on his forehead, the same raw
panic he’d felt then. He had to grip the edge of the paper-lined table to convince himself he was in
the present, a 75-year-old man and not a terrified young father.

This single event seemed to sensitize him, cracking open a door he had kept sealed for decades. The
triggers were suddenly everywhere. When a colleague on a video call shared a picture of his new
grandchild, Binh was ambushed by the sight of the swaddled infant. The image immediately launched
the intrusive film of the hospital, and with it came a wave of horror and a visceral, physical
revulsion that made him abruptly end the call, feigning a connection issue. He came to understand
that these powerful feelings of fear and panic were not just reactions to the memory, but fuel for
it. The intense terror he felt in the present seemed to confirm the memory’s reality, making the
images more vivid, the sounds clearer, and the feeling that it was all happening right now, not
fifty years ago, completely overwhelming. This terrifying sense of presence meant the danger felt
imminent, not historical.

at the grocery store became a no-go zone. He stopped watching his favourite medical dramas. He even

child. He knew, with a certainty that felt absolute, that encountering these things would trigger
not just the memory, but the cascade of physical reactions and unbearable emotions that came with

his last remaining anchor.
