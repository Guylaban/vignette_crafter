moment. She felt their presence through the walls, the oppressive sense of being watched and hunted
in her own space, until a commander finally found her, his eyes tracking her every move as he
questioned her.

Returning to the United States brought a wave of relief that proved to be fleeting. The war was over
for her, but her mind had brought it home. The triggers were insidious at first. In the courthouse
hallway, a man with a vaguely similar accent asking for directions would cause her breath to catch,
her heart hammering against her ribs. The feeling of being watched by a jury, once a source of
professional focus, now felt menacing, activating a primal sense of being tracked that sent a
cascade of adrenaline through her system. These moments instantly transformed her environment; the

her. Every glance in her direction became an act of surveillance. This heightened awareness only
served to confirm her fear, triggering fresh waves of anxiety and reinforcing her need to remain on
guard. Her world shrank accordingly. She began to feel safe only in her own home, and even there,
safety was conditional. Most nights, she would wake abruptly around 3 a.m., her body convinced an
intruder was present, and begin a silent patrol, checking locks and peering through drawn blinds

before the raid, terrified that the sharp, confident prosecutor was gone for good, replaced by this
frightened woman who saw threats in every shadow and every unfamiliar voice.
