Iman, a 50-year-old social worker, presented to the clinic describing a feeling of being
"chronically unsafe," a state that had persisted for the three years since she fled her home
country. The trauma was not a single event but a protracted period of terror. She spoke of the night
soldiers forced their way into her apartment building, the frantic escape with her then-husband, and
the subsequent months spent in hiding. The most vivid memories were of checkpoints, of trying to
appear calm and unremarkable while men with guns scrutinized her face. To be noticed was to be
endangered; to show emotion was to invite suspicion and violence. She had learned to make herself
small and invisible, a skill she believed had saved her life.

Now, in the relative safety of her American city, the danger felt more present than ever. Iman found
she was unable to relax, even in her own locked apartment. Her body remained braced for an attack
that never came, a constant, humming tension that made sleep a fraught exercise. Most nights, she
would jolt awake, heart racing, certain she had heard a footstep in the hall, and would spend the
next hour performing a ritual of checking locks and peering through blinds. This pervasive sense of
threat began to dictate the terms of her life. Because she felt constantly exposed and in danger,
she began to take measures to protect herself. At her social work office, she started scanning every
room upon entering, noting exits and potential threats before she could even sit down. She took to
wearing dark, concealing clothing—oversized sweaters and scarves pulled high—finding a small measure
of relief in the feeling of being physically obscured, a ghost in her own life. This need to manage
her internal state extended to her work; she started avoiding client cases that involved high
conflict or intense emotional displays, believing that such powerful feelings would make her a
target, just as they would have at a checkpoint.

These strategies, born from a desperate need for safety, had slowly constricted her world and,
paradoxically, amplified her fear. The constant scanning of her surroundings ensured she was always
finding "evidence" of danger. A client raising his voice in frustration was no longer just a man in
pain but a direct physical threat, causing her to flinch and her heart to pound as if an assault
were imminent. These false alarms became more frequent, confirming her belief that she was right to
be on guard. Her avoidance of emotionally stressful cases meant she never had the chance to learn
that she could tolerate distress, reinforcing the idea that strong emotions were a precursor to
catastrophe. The concealing clothing that was meant to be a shield became a constant, tactile
reminder that she was someone who needed to hide, that the world was indeed a place from which she
must retreat. This feedback loop had become the central organizing principle of her existence.

The consequences of this cycle were becoming untenable. Her supervisor, initially patient, had
recently noted a pattern of Iman referring away her most challenging cases, impacting team morale.
Her divorce two years prior had already left her feeling isolated, and now her own behaviors were
pushing away the colleagues and friends who remained. The final impetus for seeking help came after
a fire drill at her office. The sudden, piercing alarm sent her into a full-blown panic attack,
during which she hid under her desk, unable to move until a colleague found her ten minutes later,
trembling and disoriented. The profound shame of that moment, coupled with the fear of losing the
career she had worked so hard to rebuild, finally outweighed the fear of confronting the past. Iman
came to therapy wanting to feel present in her own life again, but terrified that if she ever truly
let her guard down, the disaster she had been anticipating for years would finally arrive.
