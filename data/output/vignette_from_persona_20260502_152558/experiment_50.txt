Imani had spent fifty years as a policy analyst, a career built on logic, foresight, and the calm
assessment of complex systems. At eighty, even after being widowed for a decade, her mind remained
her sharpest tool and greatest pride. That sense of control was shattered six months ago on a frigid
November evening. She was driving home from a late meeting when a car, moving erratically, swerved
across the center line. The collision was instantaneous and violent. The other driver, reeking of
whiskey, had stumbled from his vehicle, his face a mask of confusion in the glare of the one
remaining headlight. Imani remembers the shocking cold of the night air on her face where the
driver's side window had been, the acrid smell of burning rubber, and the specific pattern on the
other driver’s tie. The rest is a silent, black void.

In the months since, her meticulously ordered world has been supplanted by a landscape of hidden
threats. The triggers are everywhere, ambushing her without warning. When her grandson visited last
month after a party, the faint smell of alcohol on his coat sent a jolt of pure panic through her,
instantly making her feel as though imminent danger was in the room. This sudden terror was
immediately followed by a splinter of memory: the glint of oncoming headlights, the screech of
tires. The connection is now so ingrained that any such scent, even from hand sanitizer, not only
floods her with a feeling of profound vulnerability but also yanks her back to the roadside, trapped
in the wreckage. She finds herself constantly scanning her environment, a low-level hum of anxiety
her new baseline.

This pervasive sense of threat has become a powerful engine for the trauma itself. When she is
startled, such as when a colleague at the office suddenly reaches across her desk for a file, the
resulting surge of adrenaline does more than just make her jump. That feeling of horror acts like a
key, unlocking the most vivid fragments of the memory. In those moments, the fear she feels *now*
merges with the fear from *then*, and she is once again staring at the other driver's confused face,
the memory more real than the fluorescent lights of her office. This constant state of high alert
means she is now hunting for the very things that hurt her. At work events, her eyes scan for wine
glasses instead of colleagues' faces. Walking down the street, she braces herself every time a car
engine revs, her body pre-emptively tightening against a danger that wasn't there.

The memory itself is a tormenting, incomplete narrative. Imani can see certain details with
excruciating clarity, yet she cannot recall the paramedics arriving or the drive to the hospital. It
arrives unbidden, often triggered by the faintest burnt smell from a neighbor's barbecue or the
specific chill of an overzealous air conditioner on her skin. She feels these sensations not as
simple physical inputs but as direct harbingers of the past. The memory starts to unspool—the sound
of metal, the sight of the tie—but she finds herself reflexively pushing it away, a mental flinch to
stop it before the image of the headlights can fully form and overwhelm her. It feels like an act of
self-preservation, but it leaves the story unfinished, always lurking just out of sight, ready to
ambush her again. She has begun to believe that if she were to let the full memory play out, it
would break her completely.

The consequences have been steep. Imani, once a respected and active voice in her think tank, now
makes excuses to avoid evening receptions where alcohol might be served. She has started working
from home more frequently, isolating herself from the unpredictable movements and sounds of a busy
office that now feel like a minefield. The woman who once briefed senators now finds herself afraid
of her own kitchen, terrified she might burn the toast and be thrown back into the sensory chaos of
the crash. It was after a particularly vivid flashback, triggered by nothing more than a car
backfiring outside her window, that she finally made the call. She came to therapy because she feels
she is losing the one thing she values most: her own mind. She is terrified that this cycle of fear
and fragmented memory is erasing the person she was, and she is desperate to find a way to feel safe
in her own life again.
