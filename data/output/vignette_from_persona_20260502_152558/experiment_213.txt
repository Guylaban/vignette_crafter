one of mountains and trees, feels fundamentally unsafe to her.

At the heart of this withdrawal is a set of beliefs that have taken root and grown toxic. Keilani is
convinced that her freezing in that moment was a profound moral failing, a violation of her deepest
values. This belief that she had betrayed her own code acted like a magnet, constantly pulling her
mind back to the sensory fragments of the event, as if reviewing them might somehow change the
verdict. This conviction has led her to feel as though she no longer belongs anywhere—not in her
marriage, not in her uniform, not in the world of ordinary, unbroken people. Believing herself to be
fundamentally broken and a potential danger to others, her systematic withdrawal from social contact
and professional responsibilities felt less like a choice and more like a necessary precaution. This
appraisal that she is fundamentally damaged and untrustworthy directly fuels her hypervigilance; she
feels she must remain on guard at all times to prevent another catastrophic failure. The world is
dangerous because she is in it, incompetent and unworthy of her role.

This self-condemnation has become a self-sustaining cycle. Her belief that she is broken makes her
hyper-attuned to any reminder of the trauma, which in turn makes the intrusive memories and sensory
triggers more frequent and intense. When a memory does break through, it serves as proof of her
appraisal that she is damaged beyond repair. In response to these triggers, she has systematically
dismantled her life. She avoids crowds, has stopped attending the church she and her husband once
found solace in, and refuses to see a doctor for a persistent cough, terrified of any formal setting
where her failure might be exposed. The very act of scanning a trail for hikers to avoid, or
planning her day around evading populated areas, had the effect of keeping the possibility of
encountering a trigger at the forefront of her mind, making her even more sensitive when one
inevitably appeared. These acts of avoidance only deepen her conviction that she is an outcast.

The cost has been highest at home. When her husband, desperate to connect, reaches out to hug her,
the physical affection is no longer a comfort. It is a trigger, bringing back the suffocating memory
of the small child clinging to her leg. She instinctively stiffens and pulls away. This rejection
deepens the chasm between them and powerfully confirms her belief that she is unlovable and no
longer belongs in her own family. By pushing away comfort and refusing to speak of the event—because
when she tries, the thread is lost and she cannot finish the story—she ensures the memory remains a
set of terrifying, unprocessed fragments.

It was after spending an entire eight-hour shift unable to leave the cab of her truck, paralyzed by
the fear of who she might encounter on the trail, that she knew she could not go on. The thought
that her life was not worth living had become a quiet, constant companion. Her husband had made the
appointment. Keilani arrived convinced she was beyond help, but with a flicker of hope that she
might one day learn how to tell the story of what happened, and in doing so, find a place in the
world where she could belong again.
