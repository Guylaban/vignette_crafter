Layla keeps the windows of her work van open, even in the winter, preferring the bite of the cold to
the risk of silence. At fifty-five, she has worked as an HVAC technician for nearly two decades, a
master of airflow and temperature, yet she cannot regulate the climate inside her own mind. The
trouble, which began thirty years ago on a storm-tossed sea, has recently become unbearable. It was
a memory she had tried to bury under the steady routine of a new life in a new country, but it
refused to stay submerged. The crossing was a blur of terror and hope, a hundred souls crammed onto
a fishing boat not meant for fifty. The memory returns to her not as a coherent story but as a
series of punishing sensory fragments: the heaving grey water, the relentless uncertainty of each
passing hour, and the rough, damp texture of the canvas tarp she and her younger brother huddled
under.

A recent call to a house with a flooded basement brought it all back with the force of the original
storm. The moment she saw the dark water pooling on the concrete floor, the past became the present
(**Triggers → Memory**). She was no longer in a suburban home but back on the boat, the overwhelming
feeling of dread so powerful she had to brace herself against the doorframe. The memory itself is a
source of profound shame; she remembers her own paralysis, the feeling of being small and helpless
while others were brave. When these images surface, the event feels so extreme, so beyond the pale
of normal life, that a part of her still finds it hard to believe it actually happened to her. This
sense of unreality is immediately followed by a wave of raw fear, a certainty that disaster is
imminent (**Memory → Threat**). Her body reacts before her mind can catch up; she suddenly feels as
if she is back on the unstable deck, scanning the churning waves for a threat she can neither name
nor fight.

In the grip of this terror, her first and only impulse is to find a way out. The memory of being
trapped, with nowhere to run, has forged in her an iron-clad set of rules for survival (**Memory →
Maladaptive Strategies**). Before entering any building—a client’s home, a grocery store, even the
local library—she must first locate all the exits. She finds herself constantly scanning her
surroundings, her eyes sweeping over rooms not for HVAC units but for potential dangers. This
constant vigilance, this relentless mapping of escape routes, has become a secondary, full-time job.
Her focus on survival strategies keeps the original trauma perpetually in mind; planning her escape
from a two-story colonial home invariably brings back the suffocating memory of having no escape at
all from the open ocean (**Maladaptive Strategies → Memory**).

These very strategies, meant to keep her safe, have become the architects of her prison. The
constant scanning for danger ensures her world remains a threatening place, reinforcing the belief
that she is never truly secure (**Maladaptive Strategies → Threat**). An unusual quiet, like the
moment the boat’s engine sputtered and died, now feels like a prelude to catastrophe. This
heightened sense of threat acts as its own trigger, a feedback loop where a moment of silence in her
apartment can send her spiraling back into the memory of that terrifying stillness on the water
before the screaming began (**Threat → Memory**). Furthermore, her hypervigilance makes her
exquisitely sensitive to the very things she dreads. By always checking her environment, she is far
more likely to notice a puddle on the sidewalk or a coarse-textured blanket on a sofa, details that
immediately reactivate the trauma (**Maladaptive Strategies → Triggers**).

Over the years, the memories have made her world smaller, sensitizing her to more and more triggers
(**Memory → Triggers**). What began as a fear of the ocean has expanded to include basements, heavy
rain, and even the texture of certain fabrics. At night, she finds herself dwelling on the men who
took their money for the unsafe passage, a bitter rumination that stokes the fires of the past. Her
boss has spoken to her twice about her pace, about the time she takes on-site before even beginning
a job. Layla couldn’t explain that she was not procrastinating, but securing the perimeter. She
finally sought help after a new client, a young mother, found her meticulously checking the window
latches in a nursery and asked, with gentle concern, if everything was alright. In that moment,
Layla saw the chasm between her inner world of constant threat and the quiet reality of a suburban
afternoon. She came to therapy not hoping for a life without memories, but for a life where a memory
is just that, and where silence can simply be peace.
