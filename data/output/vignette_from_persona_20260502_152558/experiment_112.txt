unnerving calm. He speaks of it as if narrating a film he once saw, a story so extreme he finds it
hard to believe it happened to him. He was a university student then, just two years ago, studying

escalating into a violent roar, the sickening sway of the floor, and then the sharp, cracking sound
of the concrete pillars giving way. A towering metal bookshelf twisted and fell, pinning him to the
floor. For what felt like an eternity, he was trapped, the weight crushing his shoulder and neck,
his mouth and throat filling with the thick, chalky taste of plaster dust as the air grew heavy and
grey.

In the months that followed, the physical injuries began to heal, but a different kind of wound
festered. The world, once a place of predictable interfaces and logical user flows, now felt
unstable and treacherous. At his new job, he found himself surrounded by the cheerful, easy
camaraderie of his design team, but he felt as if he were separated from them by a thick pane of
glass. He came to believe that their laughter and casual complaints about deadlines were a form of
willful ignorance. He was certain people were not what they seemed; they were all just pretending
the ground beneath their feet was solid, a lie he could no longer tell himself. This conviction left
him feeling profoundly isolated, a man who no longer belonged anywhere.

This sense of alienation has become a self-perpetuating prison. His belief that he is fundamentally
separate from others causes him to hold a constant, rigid tension in his body. This chronic
watchfulness tightens the muscles in his neck and shoulders, ensuring the old injury is a near-
constant, throbbing ache. The pain, in turn, acts as a powerful trigger. When the ache sharpens
after hours spent hunched over his monitor, it’s as if a switch is flipped. The memory doesn’t
return as a clear image, but as a sensory assault: the gritty taste of plaster dust coats his
tongue, and a wave of pure terror washes over him, the same feeling he had when pinned and helpless.
This visceral intrusion serves as constant proof that he is different now. As the taste of dust
fades, Kenji looks at his colleagues by the coffee machine and is consumed by a sense of otherness,
a confirmation of his belief that he is irrevocably set apart from them.

The triggers are not always so direct. Sometimes, it’s just a fleeting news report about a disaster
in another country, or a sudden, unexplained wave of nausea that sweeps over him at his desk. These
moments are enough to plunge him into the conviction that he is broken and unsafe, reinforcing the
feeling that he is an outsider in his own life. At other times, the memory itself is the instigator.
With no warning, the full emotional intensity of the event will surge through him—a raw, image-free
panic that makes his stomach clench and his neck muscles seize, leaving him feeling physically ill
for hours. This feedback loop is relentless; his thoughts make his body hurt, and his body’s pain
summons the ghosts of the past, which then validate his darkest thoughts about himself and the
world.

Kenji sought therapy after his manager gently commented on his frequent sick days and withdrawn
demeanor at work. He can no longer sustain the facade of normalcy. The effort is too exhausting, and
the isolation has become unbearable. He describes a life that has shrunk to the size of his
apartment and his computer screen. He feels haunted not by a clear narrative of what happened, but
by its sensory and emotional debris—a persistent taste, a chronic pain, and an unshakable feeling
that he is alone in a world he can no longer trust. His hope is not to forget, but simply to feel
solid ground beneath his feet again, to believe he might one day belong somewhere.
