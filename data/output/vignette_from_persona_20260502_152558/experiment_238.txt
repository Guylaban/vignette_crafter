David, a 49-year-old city council member, presented as a man holding himself together with sheer
force of will. He spoke in clipped, precise sentences about his life, his marriage, and his
decorated military career, but when the conversation turned to why he was seeking help, his
composure fractured. It had been over a decade since his last tour, but the event that brought him
here felt as immediate as yesterday. During a house-clearing operation, his unit was ambushed in a
narrow, dusty corridor. The chaos was absolute, a blur of motion and noise. What he remembers most
clearly is the sudden, violent weight of an enemy combatant tackling him, and in the same instant,
the feeling of his friend being pulled away from his side into the dark. He never saw his friend
alive again.

The memory of that corridor does not exist as a coherent narrative for David. Instead, it erupts in
sensory fragments: the rough texture of the wall scraping his cheek, the suffocating dust, the
sudden, heavy impact on his back. When these images surface, they bring with them the same raw
terror he felt at the time, a visceral certainty of imminent death. This bodily hijacking has
convinced David that his own physical responses are an enemy, perpetually betraying him into a state
of high alert even when he is sitting in his own quiet home. From this, he has concluded that the
world is an irredeemably dangerous place and that he must remain on guard at all times, because an
ambush could happen again, anywhere.

This pervasive sense of threat dictates the terms of his existence. He can go days on only a few
hours of sleep, his body refusing to stand down. Lying in bed, his heart thuds against his ribs, and
the slightest creak of the floorboards sends a jolt of adrenaline through him. In this state of
heightened physical arousal, the fragmented images of the firefight return with startling clarity,
feeling less like memories and more like active threats. This constant vigilance has cemented his
fear that he is fundamentally broken, unable to trust his own senses or the reassurances of others.
Even when his wife, Elena, tells him he is safe, he finds it impossible to believe her; his body’s
alarm is too loud to ignore.

To manage the intrusions, David has developed a rigid set of strategies. The moment a fragment of
memory surfaces at his desk at city hall, he immediately retreats to his office and turns on a
television, blasting cable news at a volume that drowns out everything else. This sensory blitz
shoves the memory back down, but the terror it produced lingers, unprocessed. The frantic effort to
police his own mind—constantly monitoring his thoughts for any sign of "going crazy"—has only
strengthened his conviction that he is losing control. By never allowing the memory to be fully
examined, his avoidance ensures it retains its power, guaranteeing it will erupt again.

The consequences of this cycle have become untenable. As a public servant, his role requires him to
be accessible, but the fear of being touched has made this nearly impossible. At a recent town hall,
a constituent clapped him on the shoulder in gratitude, and David flinched so violently he knocked
over a pitcher of water. He now avoids all physical contact, recoiling even from his wife’s
comforting hand on his arm. This has created a painful distance in his marriage and is beginning to
damage his political career, as he increasingly sends aides to events he should be attending
himself. The incomplete picture of the trauma haunts him; he feels that if he could just remember
the whole sequence of events, he might be able to explain it clearly and rob it of its power, but
every time he tries, he loses the thread, overwhelmed by sheer terror.

He came to therapy after Elena found him one morning at 3 a.m., standing in their living room,
staring at the front door, convinced someone was trying to get in. She told him she couldn't watch
him live in a war zone he’d long since left. David is terrified of losing her, but an even deeper
fear is that his body is right—that the danger is real, and that letting his guard down for even a
moment will be a fatal mistake.
