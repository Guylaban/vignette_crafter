Anong, a 48-year-old waitress, presents as emotionally constricted, describing the past eight months
of her life as if she were watching a film about a stranger. She reports feeling “dead inside,” a
pervasive numbness that has finally become more frightening than the fear that preceded it. This
feeling, she explains, is what prompted her to seek help. The precipitating event was a protracted
period of stalking by a customer from the restaurant where she has worked for over a decade. It
began with him waiting for her shift to end, then escalated to leaving notes on her car, and
culminated one night when she saw his face looking through her living room window. She obtained a
restraining order, and he has not contacted her since, but the experience has fundamentally rewired
her existence.

The trauma does not live in her mind as a coherent story she can recount. Instead, it exists as a
collection of sensory fragments that force their way into her awareness without warning. It is the
specific glint of streetlights on a wet car windshield, the sound of gravel crunching underfoot
outside her window at night, or the disembodied image of his eyes. She explains that she cannot seem
to put the frightening event into words; the memory feels alien, like a borrowed horror that doesn't
belong to her. These intrusive flashes trigger an immediate and overwhelming physical response.
While at work, a customer staring a moment too long can make her heart hammer against her ribs and
her palms begin to sweat, a visceral echo of being watched that feels as real as the original
threat. This constant physical reactivity reinforces her belief that the memory itself is dangerous,
something to be braced against at all times.

This pervasive sense of threat has convinced Anong that she is permanently altered. The constant
hypervigilance—listening for any sound that might mean someone is entering her home, scanning every
unfamiliar face for potential danger—has exhausted her. She feels she has lost the capacity for
happiness she once had, and this belief has poisoned her perception of the world. Because she is now
always miserable, she has come to believe that the world itself is a malevolent place from which all
meaning has been drained. This conviction fuels her fear, creating a feedback loop where the world
seems more dangerous because she feels so broken, and she feels more broken because the world seems
so dangerous. She has stopped going to new places entirely, stating she cannot possibly assess the
danger in a location she does not know intimately.

Her internal landscape of misery and threat has reshaped her behaviour, shrinking her life to the
familiar path between her small apartment and the restaurant. Convinced that she is a miserable
person with nothing to offer, she has systematically withdrawn from the friends she socialised with
before the stalking began, ignoring calls and declining invitations until they eventually stopped
coming. At her job, the fear of any confrontation is so intense that she will immediately back down
from any disagreement, whether with a coworker over a shared duty or a customer complaining about an
order. This avoidance is driven as much by her negative self-perception as it is by the sheer dread
of her body’s violent reaction to perceived conflict. The sickening lurch in her stomach and the
sudden difficulty breathing are experiences she will do almost anything to prevent.

The intrusive, wordless memories also directly fuel this retreat from life. A sudden, unwanted image
of his face is enough to make her cancel a rare coffee date she had reluctantly agreed to. She has
also developed a subtle but persistent avoidance of her own body, finding it difficult to look at
her reflection for more than a few seconds. It is as if seeing herself reminds her of being seen by
him, an object of obsession, and the feeling is intolerable. By cutting herself off from friends,
conflict, and even her own physical form, she prevents any new information from challenging the
trauma memory, leaving it stuck in its raw, unprocessed state. Fleetingly, she notes that her deep
sense of misery is perhaps why the memory feels so unreal, as if it couldn't possibly have happened
to the happy person she used to be.

Anong was finally prompted to seek therapy after her manager, a friend of many years, gently
confronted her about her increasing isolation and visible distress at work. The conversation itself
felt like a terrifying threat, but the kindness behind it managed to break through her numbness for
a moment. She is here because she is afraid this hollowed-out existence is all that is left for her.
Her most fervent hope is not even to be happy again, a concept that feels impossibly distant, but
simply to one day feel like herself.
