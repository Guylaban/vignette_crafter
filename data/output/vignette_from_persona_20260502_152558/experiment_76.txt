at work where he nearly knocked over his monitor after a colleague approached his desk from behind.
The event that brought him here occurred eight months ago. While visiting his grandparents in their
home country, the city was suddenly engulfed in a violent border conflict. He was walking home from
a market when a mortar struck the adjacent building. The blast threw him against a wall, and for a
terrifying minute, surrounded by dust and the smell of cordite, he was certain he was grievously
wounded, though he had only sustained minor cuts and bruises. He remembers the chaos, the sudden
movements of people running in every direction, and the frantic, desperate search for cover.

Since returning to the United States, Mateo has been unable to shake the feeling that the danger has
followed him home. His body remains on high alert, a coiled spring of tension in his muscles and a
constant thrum of adrenaline that leaves him exhausted. At his tech company's open-plan office, he
finds himself unable to concentrate, his attention constantly snagged by the movement of others. He
describes being “superalert,” tracking colleagues as they walk to the coffee machine or the faint
motion of someone in his peripheral vision, which can cause him to jolt with a surge of panic as if
he were still on that street. He has come to believe that his body knows something his mind
doesn't—that a threat is imminent and he must be ready to react.

This pervasive sense of physical threat has driven Mateo to develop a series of what he calls his
“safety checks.” The unyielding tension in his body feels like a warning siren, and in response, he
finds himself constantly scanning his arms and legs for new bruises or injuries he might have
missed, or checking his pulse to see if it’s too fast. This body monitoring offers a fleeting moment
of control. When the hypervigilance becomes overwhelming, particularly at night, he messages his
older sister, repeatedly asking for reassurance that everything is okay, that he is safe. On the
worst occasions, when the feeling of being trapped in his own tense body is unbearable, he has
resorted to digging his fingernails hard into the flesh of his forearm. He reports that the sharp,
localized pain provides a paradoxical sense of release, a focal point that momentarily quiets the
deafening internal alarm.

These strategies, however, have begun to backfire, creating a vicious, self-reinforcing cycle. His
constant body monitoring has made him exquisitely sensitive to normal physical sensations; a simple
muscle twitch or a momentary increase in heart rate is no longer benign but is immediately
interpreted as proof of imminent danger, which in turn ratchets up the feeling that his body is on
alert. His sister has grown concerned and sometimes frustrated by his late-night texts, and a
delayed reply can send Mateo into a spiral of catastrophic thinking, certain that her silence means
something terrible has happened. The shame that follows the episodes of self-harm only reinforces
his belief that he is fundamentally broken and unsafe, heightening his need to be ever watchful for
the next threat. This feedback loop has tightened its grip over the past few months, leaving him
feeling more endangered and less in control than ever. He is caught in a trap where his very
attempts to feel safe are the things that convince him he is in mortal peril.

Mateo finally decided to seek help not just because of the incident at work, but because he
recognizes his world is shrinking. He feels alienated from his friends and fears losing a job he

constant state of watchfulness. His primary hope for therapy is simple, yet feels impossibly
distant: "I just want to be able to sit at my desk and not feel like I’m about to be attacked."
