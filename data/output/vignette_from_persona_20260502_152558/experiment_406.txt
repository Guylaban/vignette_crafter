For Layla, a 46-year-old pharmacist, the world had shrunk to the size of a hospital room two years
ago, and it had never fully expanded since. The trauma wasn't an attack or an accident, but the
silent, terrifying implosion of her world when her son, Amir, then ten, went into anaphylactic shock
from an unrecognised bee sting. She remembered the frantic call to 911, the awful, wheezing sound of
his breathing, and the ride in the ambulance where the piercing wail of the siren seemed to be
coming from inside her own head. The three days he spent in the pediatric ICU were a blur of beeping
monitors, hushed medical jargon, and a profound, bone-deep helplessness. Amir made a full recovery,
but Layla never quite left that hospital ward.

In the months that followed, the ordinary sounds of her life became menacing. She found she was
unable to relax, even in the quiet of her apartment after a long shift. A car backfiring on the
street would cause her to jump violently, her heart hammering against her ribs. The beep of the
microwave sounded like a heart monitor about to flatline. This constant, thrumming anxiety was
proof, she had come to believe, that there was something deeply wrong with her. A strong mother
would be grateful, relieved. Layla just felt broken, a weak person who had failed some fundamental
test of resilience. This conviction that she was defective made the world feel even more dangerous,
a place where another catastrophe was always imminent.

This powerful sense of her own weakness and the ever-present threat drove her to construct a life of
careful avoidance. She cancelled her own physical examination, terrified of the sterile smell and
the questions from the doctor. At her pharmacy, she would find excuses to let a colleague handle
prescriptions for EpiPens or severe allergy medications. This avoidance, meant to protect her from
her own fragility, only amplified her sense of being on edge. Every distant siren or hospital scene
on television became a threat to be managed, a potential trigger that sent her fleeing the room or
trying desperately to suppress the intrusive images of Amir struggling to breathe.

The strategies themselves became a source of shame. The immense effort she spent trying hard not to
think about the trauma was exhausting, and its constant failure only served as more evidence of her
weakness. In the long, sleepless nights, her mind would circle endlessly, not just on the memory,
but on the question of why it had happened to her, a circular search for meaning that offered no
comfort. This relentless mental churn confirmed her fear that nothing good could happen to her
anymore; her life was now defined by this single, terrible event. The more she avoided and
suppressed, the more defective she felt, and the more defective she felt, the more convinced she was
that she couldn't possibly cope with the world, making the hypervigilance and sleeplessness even
worse.

The cycle tightened its grip over two years. She became more withdrawn at work, her startle

seeing the nurses and hearing the familiar sounds, filled her with such overwhelming panic that she
couldn't bring herself to make the appointment. The realization that her own private struggle was

losing was devastating. She came to therapy not with hope, but with a quiet desperation, asking if
it was possible to fix something she was certain was fundamentally broken within her.
