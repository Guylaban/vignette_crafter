force threw unsecured equipment across the bridge like shrapnel, and the air filled with acrid smoke
and the cries of his crew. He remembers the chaos, the feeling of utter helplessness, and later, the
sight of Coast Guard and port authority uniforms swarming his vessel, their presence a symbol not of
rescue, but of his failure to keep his ship and his men safe. The subsequent investigation revealed
the container had been loaded with undeclared hazardous chemicals, a catastrophic oversight by the
shipping agent.

In the months that followed, David came to believe with absolute certainty that he could not rely on
other people. The system of checks and balances he had trusted his entire career had failed
catastrophically, and he saw now that the people responsible had faced no real consequences. This
conviction poisoned his perception of the world, transforming it from a place of calculated risks to
one of ambient, unpredictable danger. Because he was convinced of others' incompetence and malicious
neglect, every unfamiliar place became a potential trap he could not possibly assess. He began to
avoid new restaurants, grocery stores, any location where he didn't know the layout and couldn't
anticipate the threats. His belief that he couldn't handle his own thoughts about the event also
made him exquisitely sensitive to reminders; the sound of a car backfiring would send a jolt of
adrenaline through him, while the sight of a police officer in uniform brought back the sickening
helplessness he felt on the deck of his ruined ship. Even watching children throw a ball in the park
could trigger a flash of flying debris from the bridge.

This pervasive sense of threat dictated the entire shape of his life. At home, he would only sit in
the armchair in the corner of the living room, the one with its back to the wall and a clear view of
both the front door and the hallway. His wife called it his "command post," first as a joke, and
then with a growing sense of despair. Sleep offered no escape; he would lie awake for hours, certain
he needed to remain alert, his body rigid with a vigilance that had no specific target but was
nonetheless absolute. This constant, exhausting state of alert only reinforced his appraisal that
the world was a dangerous place and that he was losing his mind. The fact that he felt such profound
fear even within the walls of his own home was, to him, proof that he was fundamentally broken.

His attempts to manage this overwhelming feeling of threat only dug him deeper into the hole. He
turned down a coveted promotion to captain a new, larger vessel because the route involved ports he
had never visited before. Giving up such a significant responsibility, something he had worked
towards his entire adult life, cemented his belief that he was no longer the capable man he once
was. The long, sleepless nights were now spent online, where he discovered the hollow thrill of
gambling. It was a way to distract himself, a surge of excitement that could temporarily silence the
fear, but the mounting debts became another source of shame, further proof of his inability to rely
on himself. He became hypervigilant around people, scanning faces in a crowd, judging strangers with
a cold, suspicious calculus that left him feeling isolated and perpetually on guard.

The cycle had become self-sustaining. His constant vigilance confirmed the world was dangerous,
which justified his avoidance. His avoidance and abdication of his duties proved he was weak and
unreliable, which made him feel even more helpless and unable to trust himself or others. It was his

to therapy not with hope, but with a quiet desperation, asking if it was possible to ever feel safe
again when he was so certain there was no safe harbor left in the world.
