coherent sequence, only the smell of stale coffee from the closing-time cleanup, the shocking cold
of the tile floor against her back, and a suffocating, paralyzing terror that felt as immediate now
as it did then. The memory was a physical thing, and remembering brought back not just fear, but a
crushing guilt for having been too friendly, too trusting with the customer who had stayed too late.

everywhere, each one a hook that dragged the non-verbal memory to the surface. A customer who looked
like him—the same broad shoulders, the same dark hair—would walk in, and the memory would ignite,
plunging her back into that suffocating terror. The name "Mark" spoken aloud, even in a television
show, felt like a physical blow. Hearing a friend mention "my ex" would snag on the memory of his

didn't just remind her of the past; they resurrected it. The terror of the memory would immediately
morph into a present-tense threat, a visceral conviction that danger was imminent. Suddenly, she
would feel as if the assault were happening all over again, right there by the register, a flashback
so powerful she would grip the counter to stay upright.

This constant state of threat reshaped her perception. Her mind, primed for danger, began actively
scanning for it, making her hyper-attuned to the very things that hurt her. A sudden movement from a
customer reaching for a jar would put her instantly on guard, her body tensing for an attack. This
heightened sense of threat kept the memory raw and accessible, ensuring that the slightest
provocation would bring the sensory fragments of the assault crashing back in. Her vigilance also
broadened her triggers; soon, it wasn't just men who looked exactly like him, but any man of a
similar build, any mention of a breakup, any name that started with an 'M'. The world was becoming a
minefield of her own making.

believed were keeping her safe. After a man who vaguely resembled her attacker sat too close on the

shop, she rearranged the layout so she could always stand with her back to a wall, a position from
which she could see the entire room and, most importantly, the door. When she felt cornered or
threatened by a customer's question or tone, she would become overly compliant, offering discounts,
agreeing to anything, a fawning response born of a deep-seated belief that appeasement was her only
shield. These strategies, however, only tightened the knot of her fear. Avoiding the bus reinforced
her conviction that the world was fundamentally unsafe. Her fawning left her feeling powerless and
confirmed her helplessness, which in turn amplified her sense of ambient threat.

counterfactual thinking, tracing the day's events backwards, convinced that if only she had closed
the shop five minutes earlier, her old life would still be intact. This rumination wasn't a form of
processing; it was a way of re-traumatizing herself, steeping the memory in fresh layers of guilt
and keeping it perpetually at the forefront of her mind. Her shrinking world, curated by avoidance,
also made the triggers more potent. With fewer neutral or positive experiences to balance the
scales, any reminder of the trauma landed with disproportionate force. The cycle was relentless: a
trigger would activate the terrifying memory, which created a sense of overwhelming threat, which in
turn prompted maladaptive strategies that only served to heighten her threat perception and keep the
memory alive, making her even more sensitive to future triggers.

Her marriage grew strained under the weight of her unspoken terror, and the business she had built
with such love was now suffering. It was the hiring of a new stock boy at the bakery next door that

unbearable, constant assault. Priya finally came to therapy, her voice barely a whisper, admitting
she felt fundamentally broken. Her greatest fear was that she would never feel safe again, and her
deepest hope was to one day stand in the middle of her own shop and feel nothing but the warmth of
the sun through the window.
