Ajay, a 64-year-old warehouse worker, presented for therapy reporting a profound and unyielding
sense of dread that had taken root eight months prior. The trauma occurred during a trip to his
ancestral village in South Asia to celebrate a wedding. On his way back from a relative’s home one
evening, he was ambushed and taken captive, held for ransom for four days in a dark, earthen pit.
His captors were a constant threat, but the most acute terror came on the second night when, in the
faint moonlight, he saw a snake slithering over the dirt floor a few feet from where he lay. He
remained frozen for hours, certain he would be bitten. Though the ransom was eventually paid and he
was released physically unharmed, the experience fractured his belief in a predictable or safe
world.

Upon returning to the US, Ajay tried to resume his life, but the terror had followed him home. He
found that the fading light of dusk, the very time of day he was abducted, would trigger a cascade
of fear. This initial trigger would instantly activate a profound sense of threat; he would begin to
feel intensely unsafe, even in his own locked home, a feeling that only grew as night fell. He
became hypervigilant, startled by the sound of a passing car or a floorboard creaking upstairs. Any
mention or image of snakes, even in a nature documentary his wife was watching, would send a jolt of
adrenaline through him, his heart pounding as if the reptile were in the room with him.

The nights quickly became unbearable. The darkness itself felt menacing, a direct reminder of the
pit. This potent trigger led Ajay to develop strategies to keep the fear at bay. He began to
actively avoid sleep, staying up with the lights blazing in the living room until he would collapse
from exhaustion in the early hours of the morning. When he did sleep, he would often wake up in the
pitch-black bedroom and feel a surge of panic, immediately starting to check his body for bites or
injuries he was convinced he’d sustained. This constant body monitoring became a daytime ritual as
well, a compulsive search for evidence of a danger he couldn't see.

Over the months, these behaviours have calcified into a devastating worldview. Ajay has come to
believe that he is utterly helpless, that bad things are inevitable and he is powerless to stop
them. This core appraisal that he cannot protect himself directly fuels his pervasive sense of
threat; because he feels so vulnerable, he believes his only option is to remain constantly on
guard. His attempts to manage this fear only reinforce his negative self-concept. He repeatedly asks
his wife, "Are you sure the back door is locked?" or "Do you think I'm okay?" Seeking this
reassurance provides a fleeting moment of relief, but each question deepens his conviction that he
is a fragile, incapable man who is a burden to his family. The exhaustion from his sleep avoidance
and the shame of his constant fear have led him to a dark conclusion: he is not sure his life is
worth living anymore.

The strategies Ajay uses to survive have, in a cruel irony, made his world even more dangerous. His
chronic sleep deprivation leaves his nerves raw, making him more susceptible to his triggers. By
staying awake to fend off the darkness, he immerses himself for hours in the very nighttime
environment he fears, amplifying its power over him. His life has now shrunk. The walk from the
warehouse doors to his car after his evening shift has become a nightly ordeal. He finds himself
constantly looking over his shoulder, certain he is being followed, his keys clutched in his hand
like a weapon. The hypervigilance has become so ingrained that he feels hunted even in the most
familiar of spaces.

Ajay finally agreed to seek help after his wife found him at the kitchen table at dawn, staring
blankly at the wall, having not slept for two nights straight. He confessed to her his deepest fear,
one he had been holding in for months: he was convinced that if he ever truly allowed himself to
think about what happened in that pit, the memory would shatter what was left of his mind. He came
to his first session not with any real hope, but with a quiet desperation, asking if it was possible
for a man his age to ever feel safe in his own home after the sun went down.
