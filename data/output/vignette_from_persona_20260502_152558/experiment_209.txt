Leilani, at 66, had owned "Island Treasures," her small shop filled with imported crafts and
fabrics, for nearly twenty years. It had been her anchor after her divorce, a place of quiet
purpose. Now, it felt like a cage. She came to therapy because the world, and her own mind, had
begun to feel profoundly unsafe. The precipitating event was a police raid on her business nine
months prior, a case of mistaken identity that had been violent, chaotic, and humiliating.

The raid happened late one Tuesday afternoon, just as she was preparing to close. The memory itself
is a collection of searing, disjointed fragments. She can’t put the experience into words; trying to
describe it feels like grabbing at smoke. What remains is the feeling of being watched by the
officers lining the wall, the sudden, disorienting dizziness as she was shoved against a counter,
and the impenetrable darkness of the stockroom where she was held. There are parts of the event she
simply cannot remember, blank spaces where the worst of it must have happened. This fractured,
unreal quality of the memory has become a source of profound distress, convincing her that something
is fundamentally wrong with her mind. She has come to believe that her inability to recall the event
coherently means she is a "lousy coper," or worse, that she is going crazy. This fear makes her
recoil from the memory fragments whenever they surface, which only ensures they remain unprocessed
and terrifyingly incomplete.

These fragments are no longer confined to the past. The triggers are everywhere, especially in the
shop that was once her sanctuary. As the clock nears 4:30 PM each day, the time the raid occurred, a
familiar lightheadedness begins to set in. If a customer lingers too long, their gaze feels like a
hostile observation, and the dizziness returns with a sickening lurch. These moments instantly
plunge her back into the wordless horror of the event, the memory replaying not as a story but as a
pure bodily sensation. The recurrence of these physical reactions has cemented her belief that she
is inadequate, unable to handle the normal demands of her life. This conviction, in turn, makes her
hyper-aware of being watched, interpreting any neutral glance from a customer as a sign of judgment
on her visible fragility.

To manage this escalating terror, Leilani’s world has shrunk dramatically. The daily wave of late-
afternoon dizziness, and the cascade of fragmented memories it brings, led her to start closing the
shop hours early. This avoidance of the circumstances of the trauma has had devastating financial
consequences, which only reinforces her self-appraisal as an inadequate failure. To soothe the
intense anxiety that now pervades her evenings, she has fallen into a pattern of compulsive online
spending, buying expensive items she neither wants nor needs. The brief distraction gives way to
crushing shame, further proof of her inability to cope. This cycle of avoidance and distraction has
kept the traumatic memory perfectly preserved in its most toxic form; by never allowing herself to
be in a triggering situation long enough to see that she might survive it, she keeps the memory’s
power intact and her negative beliefs unchallenged.

The connections have now formed a devastatingly efficient feedback loop. Her strategy of avoiding
the shop in the late afternoon has made that time of day an even more potent trigger, a clear
demarcation of when her safety ends. When a memory fragment does break through—a flash of the
stockroom’s darkness—it triggers an immediate, desperate need to escape, compelling her to avoid the
upcoming anniversary of the raid at all costs. This avoidance validates her belief that she is too
weak to handle it, a lousy coper who must hide from her own life. The shame from this belief then
fuels another late-night spending spree, a maladaptive strategy that further isolates her and
deepens her conviction that she is spiraling out of control.

Leilani sought help after receiving a final notice on her shop’s lease, a direct consequence of the
lost revenue from her truncated hours. She sat in the office, her hands trembling slightly, and
confessed her deepest fear. It wasn't just about losing the shop; it was the terrifying, quiet
belief that her reactions were not a temporary response to trauma, but proof that her mind was
irrevocably breaking.
