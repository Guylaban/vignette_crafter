Sofia is a 19-year-old train conductor who carries an exhaustion that seems deeper than her
demanding night shifts can explain. The trauma dates back to her childhood, an event she can’t
recall in a clear sequence but which lives in disjointed, sensory fragments. She remembers the
suffocating darkness of hiding under her bed, the floorboards vibrating with the impact of her
father’s rage against the walls. She remembers the taste in her mouth—metallic and sharp, like
biting her own tongue in fear. Most of all, she remembers the overwhelming conviction that the
shouting had started because she’d made a noise, a small cry that had broken the fragile silence. It
was her fault. She had failed to be quiet, and in doing so, had failed to protect her mother.

For years, these fragments were manageable, but since starting her job on the railway six months
ago, they have become an ever-present reality. Her work environment is a minefield of reminders. The
sudden, jarring clang of the cars coupling, or the echoing slam of a carriage door, will send a jolt
through her, and for a second she isn't on the platform but back in that hallway. This connection is
immediate and powerful: a loud noise instantly brings the memory, not as a thought, but as a feeling
of it happening right now. The plunge into the blackness of a tunnel does the same, collapsing time
and space until she is a small child again, hidden and helpless. Every unexpected sound is also
direct proof that she is fundamentally unsafe, a confirmation of the helplessness she feels is her
defining trait.

This sense of pervasive threat has reshaped her life, especially after dark. Sofia finds it almost
impossible to feel safe at night, a fear that bleeds from her home life into her work on late-
_night_ services. Before leaving for her shift, she engages in a rigid, time-consuming ritual of
checking every door and window, sometimes three or four times, convinced she has missed something.
This constant state of high alert makes her acutely sensitive to sounds, which in turn makes her
more likely to be triggered by the routine noises of her job. The feeling of being perpetually in
danger reinforces her belief that she is helpless to stop bad things from happening, and this
thought often brings the memory back with searing intensity. Dwelling on this conviction—that she
should have done something, that she failed—is enough to summon the disjointed, visceral flashes of
the event, complete with the metallic taste on her tongue.

The memories themselves are the engine of her distress. Because they feel so present, so immediate,
they convince her that the threat is not in the past, but is active and ongoing. After a memory
intrudes, her vigilance skyrockets, her body tensing for an impact she feels is imminent. This
terror fuels her need to control her environment, leading her back to the compulsive checking of
locks and doors. The sheer horror of these re-experiencings has also driven her to develop a series
of coping strategies that now form a cage of their own. To outrun the memories, she throws herself
into her work with a frantic energy, picking up extra shifts and volunteering for the most grueling
routes. She has come to believe that if she is busy enough, there will be no mental space for the
past to enter. The hunger brought on by her habit of restricting food sometimes leaves her
lightheaded, her heart fluttering in a way that is itself a trigger for panic.

These strategies have become a self-reinforcing cycle. The relentless busyness leaves her physically
and emotionally depleted, making it harder to push the memories away when they do surface. Her
chronic undereating has made her feel weak and frail, which only deepens her appraisal of herself as
helpless and unable to cope. Endlessly replaying how life might have been different if the event had
never happened provides a fleeting escape, but always leaves her feeling more hopeless and stuck.

Sofia sought therapy after a colleague found her frozen in a darkened train car at the end of a
shift, staring at the door, unable to move. She had been checking the lock, and the memory had
ambushed her. She is exhausted by the constant vigilance and the immense effort it takes to keep her
world from collapsing inward. What frightens her most is the belief that the past is not past at
all, but a predator waiting for her in every shadow and sudden sound. She hopes for a day when she
can finally feel that the event is over, and that she might, for the first time she can remember,
feel safe in the dark.
