more dangerous version of his own life. The world he knew had ended on a Tuesday afternoon during
his lunch break. He was walking back to the bank when the first explosion tore through the plaza,
shattering the glass-fronted lobby he was moments from entering. In the chaos that followed, amid
the smoke and screaming, Kenji’s only instinct was to run. He didn’t stop to help, didn’t look back;
he just ran until his lungs burned, leaving behind the carnage and the face of a woman who had
fallen near him. That image, and the shame of his flight, became the anchor for everything that
followed.

In the weeks after the attack, a constant, humming sense of threat took root. He began waking up
multiple times a night, his heart pounding, certain someone was in his apartment, forcing him to get
up and check the locks on his doors and windows. His commute to work became an ordeal of vigilance;
he found himself constantly looking over his shoulder, convinced he was being followed. This
pervasive sense of danger was fueled by a deep and corrosive set of beliefs he had developed about
himself. His conviction that he had acted cowardly, violating his own values by running, had
metastasized into a certainty that he could no longer rely on himself in a crisis. This belief that
he was fundamentally untrustworthy made him feel that danger was not just possible, but probable,
and that he was uniquely unequipped to face it. This appraisal of his own inadequacy fed his
hypervigilance, creating a world where every sudden noise was a threat and every stranger a
potential assailant.

The constant state of alert, in turn, served as damning evidence for his negative self-appraisals.
Every time he flinched at a car backfiring or snapped at his then-wife for startling him, it felt
like a fresh confirmation of his own failure. He saw the fear in her eyes not as a product of his
trauma, but as a judgment on his character, solidifying his belief that he was no longer the capable
man she had married. His irritability grew into unpredictable bursts of anger, and the man who once
prided himself on his calm demeanor became volatile and harsh. This transformation was the most
painful proof that he was broken, cementing the appraisal that he couldn't rely on himself to be a
good husband, or even a predictable person. Their marriage crumbled under the weight of it, and she
left six months after the attack.

Alone in their old apartment, Kenji developed strategies to manage the overwhelming thoughts and
feelings. He began taking every extra shift he could at the bank, working until he was too exhausted
to think. The long hours spent counting cash and processing transactions provided a temporary
reprieve from the memory of the woman’s face and the sound of the explosion. In the evenings, he
would actively try to suppress any thoughts of the trauma, turning the TV volume up to drown out the
silence. He stopped returning calls from friends and deflected any attempts by his family to talk
about how he was doing, avoiding any kind of emotional intimacy. These strategies, however, came at
a cost. Each time he dodged a conversation or buried himself in work to avoid being alone, it felt
like another admission of weakness. This pattern of avoidance reinforced his belief that he was no
longer capable of connecting with people or managing his own mind, deepening the very shame he was
trying to escape.

Kenji finally sought therapy after an incident at work. A heavy cash box was dropped behind him, and
the loud bang sent him diving to the floor behind his counter, shouting in panic. The embarrassment
was acute, but it was the look of pity from his manager that broke through his avoidance. He
realized he couldn't keep living this way, a prisoner to a single moment. He is terrified that he is
permanently damaged, but holds a fragile hope that he might one day walk down the street without
looking back, and feel like himself again.
