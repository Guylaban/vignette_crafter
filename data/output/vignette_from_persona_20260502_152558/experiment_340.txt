Sarah, a 33-year-old paramedic, described the last six months as living in the aftermath of an
earthquake she herself had caused. The incident had been a chaotic multi-car pile-up on a rain-
slicked highway. For her, the memory wasn’t a continuous film but a series of searing photographs
burned into her mind: the glint of the streetlight on the twisted metal of the car door, the young
man’s eyes wide with shock as she prepared to intubate him, the sudden, sickening crunch of
cartilage she heard—and felt—through the laryngoscope. She later learned her mistake had caused a
severe, permanent injury to his vocal cords. This knowledge was the epicentre of the quake, and the
aftershocks had been relentless.

The "snapshots," as she called them, would flash into her awareness without warning—while she was
stocking the ambulance, driving to the grocery store, or talking to her husband. Each time a
fragment appeared, the same wave of cold terror she felt at the scene would wash over her, a
visceral certainty that she was dangerous and incompetent. This feeling of imminent threat began to
bleed into every corner of her life. She started to believe that a single misstep, at home or at
work, could lead to another catastrophe. At her station, she found herself re-checking equipment
compulsively, her concentration shattered by the constant, low-level hum of anxiety. The nights were
worse; lying in the dark, every creak of the house sounded like an alarm, and she was certain that
the world was fundamentally unsafe.

This state of being perpetually on guard only made her more vulnerable to the memories. The
hypervigilance she adopted as a shield became a magnet for triggers. The wail of a distant siren,
once a normal part of her urban soundscape, was no longer just noise; it was a hook that snagged a
memory fragment and pulled it to the surface. Her difficulty concentrating meant her mind would
wander during moments of quiet, leaving it undefended against the intrusive images. The more
watchful and unsafe she felt, the more frequently the snapshots of the young man’s terrified eyes
would appear, creating a vicious feedback loop where her attempts to stay safe only served to remind
her of the very event that made her feel unsafe in the first place.

To escape the terror that ambushed her throughout the day, Sarah developed a rigid set of defences.
When the image of the laryngoscope in her hand appeared while she was trying to cook dinner, she
would immediately turn on the television, blasting a game show to drown out her thoughts. She
stopped talking about work with her husband, Mark, changing the subject abruptly if he asked about
her shift. This avoidance of conversation was her most guarded rule. The nightly routine, however,
became her most reliable escape. When the images returned as she lay in bed, she’d pour herself a
glass of wine, then another, until the sharp edges of the memory softened into a dull, manageable
blur.

These strategies, meant to provide relief, were instead tightening the trap. The nightly wine and
the refusal to speak about "the call" created a silent, isolating space around her. This self-
imposed quarantine, meant to protect her, instead left her feeling more vulnerable and alone,
reinforcing her belief that she was a danger to others and that the event was too terrible to be
spoken of. The alcohol, which promised oblivion, only ensured the memories remained unprocessed. By
consistently pushing the snapshots away with distractions or dulling them with drink, she never
allowed them to be placed into a larger context or to fade. They returned the next day with the same
raw, fragmented power, their terror undiminished by time or reflection.

The breaking point came during what should have been a routine call for an elderly woman who had
fallen. As her partner asked for a medication dosage, Sarah froze. The numbers wouldn't come. All
she could see was the glint of light on metal, the wide, accusing eyes of the young man from months
ago. Her partner had to repeat the question twice before she could stammer out an answer. Later,
Mark found her sitting in their parked car, unable to stop shaking. She finally sought help, telling
the therapist in their first session that she felt she was losing her mind. "I'm broken," she
whispered, her hands clenched in her lap. "I'm so terrified I'm going to hurt someone else. I just
need the pictures to stop."
