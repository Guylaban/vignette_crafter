Layla, at seventy-seven, carried a stillness that seemed out of place in the cheerful, cluttered
homes where she tutored children in mathematics. The trauma was from a lifetime ago, another
country, but its architecture remained, structuring her days. It happened during the war, on an
afternoon that had felt deceptively ordinary. She had been in a small bakery, the air thick with the
scent of cardamom, when the world outside the shop window dissolved into a cascade of white light
and roaring sound. The building across the street collapsed into a cloud of dust and debris, the
shockwave shattering the bakery’s windows and throwing her to the floor amidst the broken glass and
scattered pastries. She remembers the screaming, the sudden darkness as the dust blotted out the
sun, and a profound, animal terror that she was about to be buried alive.

In the years since resettling in America, that moment has never truly ended. She describes how
something as simple as a car backfiring or a dropped crate at the grocery store can instantly bring
back the full memory, not as a recollection, but as a lived reality. When this happens, she feels
the same raw terror she felt on the bakery floor, a certainty that her life is ending. It is not
like watching a film of the past; she feels the grit of dust in her mouth and the paralyzing fear
that the ceiling is about to give way. This is why she has come to believe, with unshakeable
conviction, that she is a weak person. The strength and resilience she once prided herself on feel
like a memory of someone else. Now, she is certain that if she allows herself to truly think about
the event, to let the memories in voluntarily, she will not be able to handle it. She imagines a
complete shattering, a loss of self from which she would never return.

This core belief that she is too fragile to cope governs her every move. At the homes of her
students, she will politely but firmly insist on being seated in the chair closest to the front
door, ensuring her path to an exit is always clear. Her students’ parents see it as a quirk of an
elderly woman, but for Layla, it is a non-negotiable condition for her safety. She has also
systematically purged her life of potential reminders. She never watches the news or reads the
paper, and if a film on television so much as hints at conflict or disaster, she immediately turns
it off. This avoidance extends to her inner world; she spends hours dwelling on how she used to be
before the event, looking at old photographs of a vibrant, smiling woman she no longer recognizes as
herself. This rumination on a lost past is her safest way of touching her own history without
getting too close to the part that burned it all down.

The conviction that her memories are unmanageable has, paradoxically, given them more power. Because
she regards any flicker of the trauma as a sign of imminent collapse, the memories themselves arrive
steeped in a secondary layer of terror. The thought “I can’t handle this” is the usher that
announces their arrival, and this anticipatory dread makes the reliving experience feel even more
vivid and threatening, confirming her belief that the memory itself is dangerous. The strategies she
uses to protect herself also conspire against her. By refusing to watch news of current events or
read about similar tragedies, she prevents herself from ever contextualizing her experience. As a
result, when a sudden noise triggers a flashback, it comes without warning, a raw sensory fragment
untethered to any narrative. The memory crashes into her consciousness with the full force of the
original event precisely because she has worked so hard to keep the door to that part of her mind
sealed.

This cycle of avoidance and intrusion has become the central proof of her own perceived weakness.
Each time she has to reposition a chair to be near a door, she feels a hot flush of shame, a
confirmation that she is no longer a capable person. Each evening she spends avoiding television and
instead dwelling on the past reinforces the chasm between who she was and the frightened woman she
has become. Her strategies are no longer just about safety; they are a constant, running commentary
on her own inadequacy. This has left her with a pervasive feeling of being dead inside, a profound
emotional numbness that blankets everything. It is this feeling, more than the fear, that finally
brought her to therapy. The thick pane of glass between herself and the world has become unbearable.
She is terrified of revisiting the memory, but the thought of living out the rest of her years as a
ghost in her own life has become, at last, even more frightening.
