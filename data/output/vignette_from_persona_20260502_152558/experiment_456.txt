Imani has been a Rabbi for twenty years, and for nineteen of those years, the synagogue was her
sanctuary. That changed three months ago. She was leading a community planning meeting in the social
hall, discussing the upcoming high holidays, when she noticed a man she didn't recognize slip in
through the back door. She remembers the feeling of his eyes on her, a cold prickle of observation
that made her stumble over her words. A moment later, a pipe bomb he had left in the kitchen
detonated. The sound was a deafening, foundational crack, the world dissolving into smoke, screams,
and the smell of burnt plastic.

In the weeks that followed, the world outside her home began to feel fundamentally unsafe. Imani
found it impossible to believe she was secure, even when her husband, David, walked her through the
house, checking every lock. At night, the feeling intensified; every creak of the floorboards was a
footstep, every shadow a lurking figure. She began to constantly scan her surroundings for signs of
danger, her eyes darting from face to face in the grocery store, her head on a swivel as she walked
from her car to her front door. This pervasive sense of threat has made her hyper-attuned to the
very things she fears most. A car backfiring now sounds exactly like the initial explosion, and the
feeling of being watched—once a benign part of public speaking—now sends a jolt of pure terror
through her. The news has become a minefield, with any story about violence feeling like a direct
and personal warning.

This constant feeling of being in danger has reshaped how Imani sees herself and her future. The raw
threat has curdled into a set of painful convictions. She has come to believe that she has to be
especially careful, that the world is a treacherous place and she is the only one who can protect
herself. More than that, she is certain that nothing good can happen to her anymore, that the
bombing was a judgment that has permanently stained her life. The deepest conviction, the one that
surfaces in the quiet of the night, is an immense guilt. She replays the moment she saw the man,
convinced that she will never forgive herself for not doing more, for not shouting, for not stopping
what happened.

These dark beliefs now govern her actions. Because she believes she must be constantly vigilant, she
checks the perimeter of the synagogue before every service, a ritual that can take up to an hour.
Her guilt and the feeling that her future is ruined have made her pull away from David; she avoids
sex and flinches from his touch, the vulnerability of intimacy feeling unbearable. When a well-
meaning congregant tries to talk about the attack, to share their own fear, Imani shuts them down,
unable to bear being reminded of the event by others. She sees it as protecting herself, but these
strategies have only tightened the trap. The more she scans her environment for threats, the more it
reinforces her belief that she is right to be so careful. The more she avoids David, the more
isolated she becomes, which feels like proof that nothing good can happen to her.

Her constant scanning for danger has, paradoxically, made the world more dangerous. By actively
looking for threats, she is far more likely to notice a sudden movement or a loud noise, making her
more susceptible to being triggered. A slammed door down the hall at the synagogue, which she once
wouldn't have even registered, now causes her to freeze, her heart pounding. Her world has shrunk to
the four walls of her home and the carefully monitored rooms of her synagogue. It was last week,
when a kind, elderly member of her congregation mentioned a news story about a bombing overseas,
that Imani knew she could not go on. She felt the air leave her lungs and had to flee the room,
leaving the woman standing alone in confusion. She is seeking help now because she feels she is not
only losing her husband but failing her community, terrified that she can no longer be a source of
strength when she is so consumed by her own fear.
