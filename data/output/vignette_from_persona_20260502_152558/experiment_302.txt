Sarah, a 51-year-old married civil servant, presented for therapy six months after a toxic chemical
exposure incident at an international government conference. The event itself was one of profound
confusion rather than overt violence. A strange, acrid smell filled the hotel ballroom, followed by
a chaotic evacuation. Alarms blared as attendees, speaking a dozen different languages, shouted over
one another, creating a wall of incomprehensible chatter. In the crush of people, Sarah was jostled
and pushed, the feeling of strangers’ hands on her back and arms sending waves of panic through her.
She remembers the disorienting arrival of emergency responders in hazmat suits and being herded onto
a bus for a mandatory, bewildering physical examination at a makeshift medical tent.

In the weeks that followed, Sarah found she could not adequately convey what had happened. When her
husband asked, the narrative would crumble. She’d start to explain the alarm, but then the memory
would dissolve into a pure, nonverbal sensation of dread and confusion, leaving her unable to finish
the story. This fragmentation of the memory was terrifying in itself; it made her feel that the
danger was not truly over but was instead an amorphous, ongoing threat she couldn't grasp or define.
This persistent sense of threat made her even less able to deliberately access the memory, as any
attempt to do so felt like inviting the danger back in. The memory’s elusive, wordless quality
became a trigger itself; the frustration of trying and failing to explain it would leave her feeling
agitated and unsafe for hours.

Her life began to constrict around this core of unprocessed fear. The open-plan office where she
worked, once a place of collegial bustle, became a minefield. The sound of overlapping
conversations, the incomprehensible chatter of colleagues on different phone calls, would send a
jolt of panic through her, a direct echo of the conference ballroom. This immediate sense of threat
would bring the nonverbal horror of the event rushing back. In response to these triggers, she began
avoiding the communal kitchen and staff lounge, eating lunch alone at her desk. She started to
rehearse what she would say before any interaction, from a simple phone call to a team meeting,
believing that if she could control the conversation, she could prevent any unpredictable turns that
might leave her feeling exposed and unsafe. This constant rehearsal made her more sensitive to any
unexpected question or comment, paradoxically turning more situations into triggers.

Sarah’s world has now shrunk to her home and, on good days, her specific cubicle at work. She has
come to believe these are the only places she can truly be safe. The strategies she developed to
manage her anxiety have become rigid rules that now govern her life and amplify her fear. She avoids
any physical contact, flinching if her husband reaches for her hand, which has created a painful
distance between them. This avoidance reinforces her belief that others are a source of threat.
Similarly, her avoidance of medical cues means she has missed her annual physical, certain that a
hospital or even a picture of an ambulance will plunge her back into the helplessness she felt in
the medical tent.

The feedback loop is now relentless. A trigger, like an unpredictable change to her work schedule,
activates a profound sense of threat, which in turn makes the fragmented, sensory memory of the
event feel more immediate and vivid. This terrifying memory fragment then compels her to double down
on her maladaptive strategies, such as refusing to attend the rescheduled meeting and retreating to
the perceived safety of her home. Her heightened sense of threat also makes her hypervigilant,
constantly scanning her environment for danger, which makes her far more likely to notice triggers
like loud, unexpected sounds or strangers approaching her on the street. It was after she recoiled
from her husband’s attempt at a comforting hug, seeing the deep hurt in his eyes, that Sarah
realised she was trapped. She came to therapy because she is terrified of losing him and is
exhausted by the constant, silent vigilance her life has become. She wants, more than anything, to
feel safe in the world again, not just in a single, lonely room.
