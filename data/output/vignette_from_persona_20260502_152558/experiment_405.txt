Kenji had built his life on a foundation of order and reason. At thirty-one, he was one of the
youngest judges appointed to the bench, a man whose mind was his most trusted instrument. That
foundation crumbled nine months ago, during the hurricane that tore through the city’s municipal
complex. He had been in a records room in the sub-basement when the building groaned and then
collapsed. For seven hours, he lay pinned in the dark, the humid, oppressive air thick with the dust
of pulverized concrete. His only connection to the outside world was the muffled, recurring wail of
sirens and, eventually, the sharp, clean scent of antiseptic as a makeshift triage was set up near
the rescue site.

In the first few weeks after his rescue, Kenji tried to impose judicial order on the chaos in his
head. He returned to work, believing routine was the antidote to trauma. But the trauma refused to
be dismissed. The memories were not a narrative he could review and set aside; they were a series of
brutal, still photographs. A snapshot of the cracked ceiling just before it gave way. An image of
the rebar jutting from a concrete slab inches from his face. These pictures would ambush him, and
when they did, they were not merely visual. The memory was an immersive assault; he could feel the
crushing weight on his legs and smell the cloying mix of damp rot and antiseptic, feeling as though
he were right back in the suffocating darkness.

The triggers were everywhere, insidious and unpredictable. The wail of an ambulance passing the
courthouse would be enough to make the images flash behind his eyes, vivid and consuming. This
sudden sensory hijacking was terrifying, and in response, Kenji began to retreat. He started
avoiding going out after work, particularly if the weather was humid and overcast, too similar to
the day of the hurricane. The antiseptic smell of the hand sanitizer dispensers now ubiquitous in
the courthouse became a source of quiet panic, forcing him to hold his breath as he walked the
halls. He was building a fortress against his own experience, and it was beginning to shrink his
world.

The terror of the flashbacks, the feeling of being completely helpless again, drove him to erect a
wall in his mind. He spent his days in a state of fierce concentration, trying to outrun his own
thoughts. If an image of the rubble threatened to surface during a proceeding, he would grip the
edge of his bench and focus intensely on the legal text before him, using the complex language as a
shield. He tried with all his might not to think about the trauma, believing he could starve it of
oxygen. At home, this effort became a refusal to ever truly rest. Evenings with his wife became
tense; he couldn't sit and watch a movie or listen to music. The moment his body began to soften,
his mind would scream danger, a jolt of adrenaline reminding him that to relax was to be vulnerable,
to be caught unaware again.

This constant battle of suppression and avoidance had a paradoxical effect. By trying so hard to
control his inner world, his outer world grew more menacing. He found himself scanning his
environment constantly, his hearing acutely attuned to the faintest, most distant siren. Because he
refused to let himself relax, he lived in a state of perpetual high alert, which only made him more
susceptible to being ambushed by the very triggers he worked so hard to evade. His life had become a
tightly wound, self-reinforcing loop of fear. The triggers provoked the memories, the memories
fueled the avoidance, and the avoidance sharpened his sensitivity to the triggers.

He finally sought therapy after an incident in his own courtroom. A lawyer used a strong,
antiseptic-scented hand sanitizer, and Kenji was instantly overwhelmed. He saw not the witness
stand, but a jagged piece of concrete, and for a full ten seconds, he couldn't breathe. He had to
call a recess, his heart hammering against his ribs, his composure shattered. The very place he
represented order had become another source of threat. He confessed that he felt he was losing the
case against his own mind, and he was terrified that soon, there would be nowhere left to retreat.
