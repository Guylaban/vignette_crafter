Anna, a 39-year-old dentist, presented for treatment eighteen months after a life-threatening bout
of septic shock that had followed a routine surgery. The illness had come on with terrifying speed,
transforming a standard recovery into a week-long ICU stay where she felt like a prisoner in her own
body. She remembers the constant beeping of machines she didn't understand, the disorienting haze of
medication, and the profound, helpless certainty that her body was actively trying to kill her. The
sterile, chemical smell of the ICU is a memory that has stayed with her, a scent she now associates
with imminent system failure. Before the illness, she had been an avid hiker and a sociable person,
a life documented in photos around the house she shares with her partner—images that now feel like
they belong to someone else.

Returning to her dental practice was initially a relief, a step toward normalcy. But that feeling
shattered the first time she stepped into her own treatment room. The sharp, clean scent of the
antiseptic solution she’d used for years suddenly smelled like the ICU, and the familiar room felt
small and airless. The smell triggered a cascade of thoughts that have since become her constant
companions: a visceral conviction that her body was fundamentally untrustworthy and could betray her
again at any moment. This experience cemented a new, terrifying belief that the illness had not just
been an event but a permanent alteration, leaving her fundamentally broken.

Based on this core belief that she is permanently damaged and weak, Anna has systematically
dismantled her life. She came to believe that any strong physical sensation could be a prelude to
another collapse, so she stopped exercising and began to ignore any bodily feeling, from a minor
ache to genuine hunger. Convinced she is now a fragile version of her former self, she avoids other
people, routinely cancelling plans with friends she has known for years because she feels too weak
to sustain a conversation or feign normalcy. When her partner, David, tries to ask about her
recovery or how she is feeling, she offers a clipped "I'm fine" and changes the subject. To Anna,
talking about the illness would be an admission of the weakness she is so desperate to hide, a
confirmation of the damage she feels inside.

This pattern of avoidance has only served to deepen her negative beliefs. After months of turning
down invitations, her social circle has shrunk, and the resulting isolation feels like proof of her
belief that she is a weak person who cannot cope with normal life. By deliberately not looking at
the surgical scars on her abdomen and showering in near-darkness, she treats her own body as a
dangerous and alien object, which further reinforces her certainty that she can no longer trust it.
Each avoided activity, each deflected conversation, acts as another layer of evidence for the story
she tells herself: that she has been irrevocably changed for the worse.

Her conviction that she is broken and unsafe now dictates her attention. Believing herself to be in
constant peril, Anna is hypervigilant for anything that reminds her of her vulnerability, which in
turn expands her list of triggers. At her practice, the feeling of being in the small, enclosed
space with a patient for a long procedure now provokes a sense of entrapment, mirroring the
helplessness she felt in the hospital bed. At home, a glance at an old photo of herself on a hiking
trip is no longer a happy memory but a painful confirmation of the strong, healthy person she
believes is gone forever. Her world has become a minefield of her own making, each trigger newly
sensitized by the very appraisals it helps to create.

Anna sought therapy after a severe panic attack during a routine crown fitting. The combination of
the antiseptic smell and the patient’s appointment running late made her feel trapped, and she was
suddenly overwhelmed by the certainty that she was dying. She had to excuse herself, leaving her
assistant to manage the patient, and locked herself in her office, unable to breathe. It was the
stark, professional consequence of her struggle—the moment she realized her internal world was
actively destroying her external one—that finally convinced her to seek help. She confessed in her
first session that her greatest fear is that her beliefs are true, but her greatest hope is to one
day feel safe in her own body again.
