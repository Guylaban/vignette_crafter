At 34, Arun Vong had spent years in hospital rooms, family waiting areas, and chapel offices trying
to be the steady person other people leaned on. As a chaplain, he was used to entering moments of
crisis with a calm voice and open posture, but the night he was mugged shattered his sense that
composure could protect him. He had left the hospital after an evening shift and cut through a busy
downtown block on his way to the train. There were people all around him, the usual press of bodies
at the corner and on the sidewalk, and then a man stepped close enough that Arun first registered
the heat of his breath before he understood what was happening. Another man was beside him almost
immediately. In the crush of the crowd he was pinned, his wallet and phone demanded, and what stayed
with him afterward was not a coherent sequence so much as a set of sharp images: a face suddenly too
close, a hand gripping his coat, the blur of passing strangers who did not seem to notice, the hard
shine of streetlight on wet pavement. He could also still catch, without warning, the smell from
that moment—stale breath, sweat, the city odor rising off the sidewalk. He remembered feeling
trapped in plain sight, unable to stop what was unfolding even though there were people everywhere.

In the first weeks after the robbery, Arun told himself he should be able to move past it. His wife
had died previously, and he had already learned what it meant to keep functioning while carrying
pain. But this felt different. The event came back to him mainly as pictures, abrupt and vivid, as
if scenes were flashing in front of him rather than being remembered from a distance. Sometimes when
he thought of it he could smell the air from that night so distinctly that he would look around the
room to check where it was coming from. What unsettled him almost as much as the fear was the rage.
He was startled by how quickly anger surged in him when the memory appeared—anger at the men who
cornered him, at the strangers who kept moving, at himself for freezing. He did not talk much about
that part. As a chaplain he felt he was supposed to be measured, compassionate, spiritually
grounded. The intensity of his anger seemed to him like another sign that something about him was
flawed.

Over the following months, Arun began organizing his days around what felt safe. Crowded places
became especially hard. At the hospital he started taking back corridors, service elevators, and
side entrances whenever he could, timing visits to avoid the cafeteria rush and lingering outside
family gatherings until the room had thinned out. When memorial services or bedside prayers drew
clusters of people close around him, he found himself scanning for exits and losing the thread of
what others were saying. Heavy breathing was especially potent. If a distressed family member was
crying hard, if someone in a waiting room was breathing heavily after hurrying down the hall, he
would feel a jolt in his body before he could think. His attention would lock onto the sound, and
for a moment he was back on the sidewalk with someone’s breath close to his face. On several
occasions he cut conversations short and told colleagues he needed to check on another patient.

People connected to the event also became difficult for him to encounter. Follow-up contact from
police and victim services left him tense for hours beforehand. Even seeing men on the street who
resembled the attackers in age, build, or manner of dress could make his chest tighten and his
thoughts narrow. He had come to believe the robbery said something essential about him: that it
happened because of the sort of person he was, someone who was unguarded, naïve, too easily
approached. He was certain somebody else would not have gotten into that situation. Another man, he
thought, would have noticed sooner, moved faster, projected enough strength to deter it. More and
more, he interpreted the event as proof that he could not stop bad things from happening to him.
Being widowed had already left him with a painful sense that catastrophe could arrive without
consent; the mugging seemed to him like another confirmation that he was fundamentally unable to
keep himself safe.

The consequences began to show in his work and relationships. He declined requests to accompany
families through the crowded emergency department unless absolutely necessary, and coworkers noticed
that he had become harder to find during peak hours. He turned down invitations from fellow clergy
to join lunches in busy restaurants. Friends who had once found him reliably present now heard him
cancel at the last minute if a place sounded packed. Arun felt ashamed of these adjustments, but to
him they were reasonable precautions in a world that had already shown him how quickly he could be
overwhelmed in public.

What finally brought him to seek help was a recent incident at the hospital entrance, when a man
rushing in with a relative stopped near him, bent over, and began breathing heavily. Arun froze,
caught a flash of the attacker’s face, and for an instant smelled the night of the robbery so
clearly that he had to step away and brace himself against the wall. He was supposed to meet a
grieving family moments later and found he could not steady his expression. Sitting in therapy now,
he is most afraid that this is what his life has narrowed into—that he will remain someone who
cannot trust crowds, cannot master his own reactions, cannot prevent the next bad thing. At the same
time, he is hoping, quietly but insistently, to feel like himself again in the very places where he
once knew how to stand with others in their fear.
