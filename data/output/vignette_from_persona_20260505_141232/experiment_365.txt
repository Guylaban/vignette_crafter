Martin Weiss is a 56-year-old married American dancer who came to treatment months after an accident
in which another person was seriously injured, an event he speaks about with a mixture of shame and
stunned disbelief. The injury happened on an ordinary day that had begun with rehearsals and
errands, and he still remembers the abruptness with which it split his life into a before and an
after. He had been driving when a collision occurred, and although he was not physically the one
most gravely hurt, he experienced the event as something he had failed to prevent. What remains most
vivid to him is not a neat sequence but the sight of the other car after impact, bent and struck in
a way that still feels unbearable to picture. In session, when he edges close to describing it, he
stops, looks away, and says that what he sees is “that car, hit like that,” as if the image is less
a memory than a scene that forces itself in front of him.

In the first weeks afterward, Martin expected to feel shaken, but he was unprepared for how often
pieces of the event would break into daily life. If he passed a roadway accident, even from a
distance, his body tightened before he had fully registered what he was seeing. Images of cars hit
at odd angles could seize his attention and leave him momentarily elsewhere. Certain songs became
unexpectedly loaded. A song playing in a store, in the car, or from a speaker in the studio could
stop him cold if it matched the music that had been around the time of the accident or had become
linked in his mind with that day. He found himself leaving spaces abruptly, unable to explain to
others why a familiar melody had suddenly made him feel cornered.

Martin’s work as a dancer made this especially difficult. Rehearsal spaces that once felt sustaining
now often felt overstimulating, especially when they were crowded. In a full studio, with music on,
bodies moving close around him, and too much happening at once, he became acutely uncomfortable and
restless. He could not settle into combinations the way he once had. He would lose the thread of
choreography, miss counts, or find his attention pulled away by the press of people and sound. He
had begun to think of himself as someone who could no longer rely on his own mind. Even when he knew
he was physically safe, his body did not seem to agree; he remained keyed up, scanning, unable to
relax.

At home, the nights were often the hardest. Martin told his wife that sleep felt impossible unless
he stayed alert, as though letting down his guard would be irresponsible. He delayed going to bed,
lay awake listening, and resisted the moment of drifting off. During the day he tried forcefully not
to think about the accident, cutting off thoughts the instant they appeared. He also began avoiding
the location where it happened, changing routes and refusing suggestions that would take him nearby.
These decisions made immediate sense to him. He believed that if he let himself think too much, or
if he went back there, he would be overwhelmed by what he had failed to stop.

Over time, his mind returned repeatedly to the person he believed he had been before the accident.
He dwelled on his former ease, his confidence, his ability to move through crowded environments and
focus completely on music and performance. He compared that version of himself to the man he now
experienced as tense, distracted, and morally diminished. What weighed heaviest was not only that
someone else had been seriously injured, but his conviction that he had not done enough. He says,
with a fixed certainty, that he will never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened.
He has come to see the event through the language of failure: he should have protected others, and
he failed; he failed in what he considers his most important role in life. He does not talk about
this abstractly. He means husband, adult, responsible person, someone who should have kept other
people safe.

By the time he sought help, these patterns had narrowed his life. Rehearsals felt harder to get
through, crowded settings increasingly intolerable, and his wife had begun to notice how often he
withdrew, rerouted plans, or went silent when certain songs came on. He came to treatment not
because he doubts the seriousness of what happened, but because he is exhausted by living in
constant readiness and by the feeling that the world can tilt back into that moment without warning.
What frightens him most is that this is now who he is: a man defined by one failure, unable to
protect, unable to rest. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not to erase the event, but to be able to
hear music, enter a crowded room, and lie down at night without feeling he has to keep watch
forever.
