At 70, Rafael Morales came to treatment after years of insisting that what happened to him belonged
to another life and another country. He is a widowed Latino man who still works as a welder, and for
decades he had organized himself around long hours, routine, and silence. When he first began to
speak about the trauma, his account broke apart in the middle. He would start with one detail, stop
suddenly, stare at the floor, and say that once he tried to explain it, he lost the thread and could
not finish. Even so, certain moments were unmistakably vivid. He described being taken and tortured
by armed men, held in a confined space where there was nowhere to move and no way out. He remembered
the absolute certainty of having no options, the sense that every sound might mean another assault
was about to begin. Above all he remembered gunshots nearby and episodes of direct violence, each
one arriving without warning. In his telling, the terror was mixed with humiliation and
helplessness, and he had come to believe that what happened was tied to the way he acted in those
moments. He said he would never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened.

In the years after, Rafael did not speak about the torture in any sustained way. When memories
surfaced, he detached from them as if he were stepping sideways out of his own life. He tried hard
not to think about the trauma at all. If an image or sensation pushed through, he would shut it
down, go numb, or busy his hands with work until the feeling passed. Yet the memories did not feel
like old memories. What frightened him was how quickly the past became present. A sharp sound could
collapse time. A gunshot on television, a car backfiring in the street, even metal striking metal in
the shop in the wrong rhythm could make him feel, for a few moments, as if he were back in that room
with armed men nearby. His body reacted before he could remind himself where he was. He knew he was
in his welding bay, mask in hand, but the memory arrived with such force that it felt like something
happening now, not something over.

The shop became one of the places where this was most obvious. Welding is noisy, full of bursts,
bangs, and threat-like sounds, and Rafael had learned the layout carefully so he could keep himself
from feeling cornered. If another worker blocked the narrow passage beside his station, or if
equipment was stacked in a way that left him with no easy path out, he felt a surge of alarm he
could not easily explain. He would quietly reposition things, step away, or volunteer to work in a
different area rather than remain in a spot that felt closed in. He did not tell coworkers why.
Instead, when he felt threatened or pressured, he became overly agreeable. He said yes too quickly,
apologized even when he had done nothing wrong, and let other men choose the assignments. To others
he could appear easygoing, but from his perspective compliance felt safer than risking conflict with
anyone who might turn dangerous. He was certain that you can never know who will harm you.

Rafael has been widowed for some years, and the loss left him with fewer places to speak freely. His
restraint hardened into habit. Family members noticed that if conversation turned toward political
violence, crime, or stories of abuse, he either left the room or went flat and distant. On the rare
occasions he tried to tell more of what happened, the effort often ended in anger. He would begin
haltingly, then his face tightened and his voice rose; the memory brought intense rage, so strong
that he sometimes had to stop for fear of what would come out of him. That rage coexisted with blame
directed inward. He replayed his own actions during the torture and judged them mercilessly, as
though there had been something decisive he failed to do. In his mind, other people remained
unpredictable and dangerous, while he remained the man who had not acted correctly when it mattered
most.

What finally brought Rafael to seek help was not a single crisis but an accumulation of moments he
could no longer dismiss. Recently, after a loud bang at work, he froze and then found himself
trembling with fury and shame, unable to return to his station for the rest of the shift. He told
his supervisor only that he felt unwell. At home that evening he realized how small his life had
become, arranged around avoiding certain sounds, certain conversations, certain positions in a room.
He said he was tired of the past arriving “like it is still happening,” and tired of feeling that
danger could come from anyone at any time. Still, even in asking for help, Rafael was wary. What he
feared most was being overwhelmed by the memories or judged for what he did and did not do. What he
hoped for, stated cautiously, was to be able to talk without losing the thread and to stand in his
own life without feeling trapped.
