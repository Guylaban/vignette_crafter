Kwame is a 58-year-old African American man who has worked as a hairdresser for many years, the kind
of stylist who remembers clients’ children’s names and keeps a steady rhythm of conversation going
while his hands work. He is married, and before the robbery he had been used to spending most of his
day around people, moving easily between the intimacy of the salon chair and the bustle of the shop.
The event he returns to in treatment happened after closing one evening, when he left the salon
carrying the day’s cash and supplies. A man approached him quickly, close enough that Kwame could
smell alcohol on his breath before he fully understood what was happening. He remembers the
encounter less as a story than as a series of sharp images: the man’s face too near his own, the
shine of a hand reaching into his jacket, the sudden angle of the sidewalk under the streetlight,
the look of the bottle in the man’s other hand, and the sense of being trapped in a moment that
would not move forward. Even now, when he tries to describe it aloud, his speech stalls. He says he
knows exactly what happened, but when he tries to put it into words, “it won’t come out right,” and
he falls silent, seeing it instead.

In the weeks afterward, the robbery did not settle into the past for him. The memory came back as
pictures and as a repeating sequence, like a film clip that started on its own. Sometimes it was the
smell of alcohol that brought it on with particular force. If a client came in after lunch with
liquor on his breath, or if he passed someone drinking from a paper bag on the walk to his car,
Kwame would feel the whole scene return in images before he had even formed a thought about it. At
other times it was less tangible but just as immediate: a person lingering too long near the front
desk, someone watching the register, a stranger pacing outside the salon window. He found himself
studying people closely for signs that they were not safe. He did not talk about this much, because
he was trying very hard not to think about the robbery at all. He would interrupt the memory by
forcing his attention elsewhere, cleaning his station repeatedly, turning on music, asking clients
questions, anything to block the images once they started.

At the same time, he spent long stretches dwelling on what he believed he had done wrong. He
replayed practical decisions—why he stayed late, why he carried the cash himself, why he did not
notice the man sooner, whether he should have crossed the street or called his wife before leaving.
The questions circled without relief. He had come to believe that he should have prevented it, that
a man his age, with his experience of the city and of people, should have seen trouble coming. Being
corrected by anyone began to strike that same raw place. If his wife reminded him he had forgotten
groceries, or if a client told him he had trimmed a sideburn unevenly, the feeling that rose in him
was disproportionate and immediate. Correction no longer felt like an ordinary part of work or
marriage; it landed as proof, to him, that he was careless and had failed in ways he could not undo.

Over the following months, Kwame became quieter and more withdrawn. In a profession built around
constant social contact, he started reducing the number of clients he would see, letting coworkers
take walk-ins, avoiding casual conversation in the break room, and leaving the salon as soon as his
last appointment was done. He preferred not to attend neighborhood gatherings or family events if
there would be many people there, especially people he did not know. His wife noticed that he sat
apart even at home, preoccupied and hard to reach. At the salon, the cost was practical as well as
relational: regular clients commented that he seemed distracted, and one or two drifted to other
stylists because he no longer had the warmth and ease they associated with him.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was not a single new event but the recognition that his
world had narrowed around the robbery. A recent afternoon in the salon made that plain. A client,
mildly irritated, told him he needed the cut shorter around the ears, and at the same moment another
man came in from outside carrying the smell of alcohol. Kwame froze for a second, images rushing up
so vividly that he lost track of the conversation. Later he sat in his car, ashamed and shaken,
thinking again of all the ways he had failed. By the time he came for treatment, he was exhausted by
the effort of pushing the memory away and by how often it still returned. What frightened him most
was the sense that he was no longer himself in the very place where he had always felt most
competent. What he hoped for, cautiously, was to be able to work a full day, hear an ordinary
correction, catch a stranger’s scent of liquor, and remain in the present rather than being pulled
back into those pictures.
