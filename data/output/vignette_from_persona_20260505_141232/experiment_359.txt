around a disaster he still does not experience as fully over. He was divorced several years before
seeking help and lives alone, spending most of his time between his apartment and his studio. The
event he returns to took place during a crowded industry function in a commercial building, where
what began as an ordinary work gathering turned suddenly into chaos when a deliberate act caused
panic, damage, and an urgent scramble to get out. What remains with him is not a clean story with a
beginning, middle, and end. When he tries to describe that day, he offers it in broken sections: the
crush of bodies turning at once, the sensation of temperature against his skin that now startles him
whenever it recurs, the metallic taste in his mouth, and the sickening wave in his stomach as he
realized something was terribly wrong. He does not recount it as a past event so much as a series of
sensory fragments that break into the present.

In the weeks afterward, Nadim told himself he needed to keep moving. He returned to work quickly and
filled his schedule with fittings, sourcing appointments, late-night sketching, and endless
revisions. From the outside it looked like dedication. In his own mind, staying busy was the only
way to avoid being caught by the memory when it arrived without warning. Even so, there were moments
when it broke through. A sudden shift in temperature on his skin—a burst of cold air from a vent, or
stepping from a warm room into a chilled freight elevator in the garment district—could bring the
event to mind instantly. When that happened, his stomach turned and he felt nauseous enough that he
had to grip a table or excuse himself to a bathroom. What unsettled him most was how little distance
there was between then and now. He did not feel he was remembering; he felt, for several seconds,
that it was happening again. Along with the nausea came the taste he remembered from that day, vivid
enough that he sometimes rinsed his mouth at the sink as though something foul were physically still
there.

Over time, his days became organized around vigilance. No matter where he went—to a showroom, a
restaurant with a buyer, a hotel for fashion week events—he could not settle until he had identified
how he would get out. He automatically noted stairwells, service corridors, side doors, and windows.
If he met a client in an unfamiliar building, part of his attention remained fixed on exits rather
than on the conversation. He scanned faces, sounds, and movement around him, searching for signs
that something might suddenly turn. This made concentration difficult in exactly the settings where
his work required sustained focus. During fittings, assistants noticed him losing the thread of
instructions or staring past a model toward the studio entrance. He found himself asking for
measurements to be repeated, or rechecking decisions he would once have made with ease.

At home, the same need for control took on a ritual quality. Before bed he checked the locks, then
checked them again, and often once more after he had already gotten under the covers. If a window
latch did not feel exactly secure, he would start the whole circuit over. He knew it consumed time
and that part of him recognized the apartment was already locked, but he did not feel able to stop
until he had gone through every point of entry himself. On the worst nights, when the pressure in
him became unbearable, he sometimes hurt himself physically to release it. He described these
moments without drama, almost with shame, as desperate attempts to interrupt feelings that otherwise
seemed likely to spill over.

These habits narrowed his life gradually. He accepted fewer invitations, especially to crowded
venues where he could not easily orient himself. He stayed later at the studio because working felt
safer than being alone with his thoughts, yet even there he repositioned himself to keep the door in
view. Colleagues experienced him as exacting and preoccupied. His world became structured around
anticipating danger and warding off an inner jolt he could never predict.

What finally brought Nadim to seek help was not one dramatic worsening but the exhaustion of living
this way. After a recent episode in which a change in temperature at work triggered the memory so

immediate it still felt. He said he was tired of living as if every room needed a plan for escape
and as if his own mind could ambush him at any moment. What frightened him most was that the event
remained in pieces he could not put together, and yet those pieces still arrived with the force of
the present. What he hopes for now is modest but deeply felt: to be able to work, sleep, and enter a
room without first preparing to flee.
