Miguel Alvarez is a 55-year-old Navy officer who came to treatment after years of trying to keep one
part of his life sealed off from the rest. He grew up in the United States in a Latin American
family and had spent much of his adult life in uniform, priding himself on steadiness, discipline,
and the ability to act under pressure. The event that continues to return to him happened before his
military career, when he was a young civilian living with relatives in a war zone. He describes a
day of sudden violence in the neighborhood where he and other family members had believed they could
remain out of the way and survive by staying careful. What stands out in the way Miguel tells it is
not broad political context but a series of concrete moments: the crack of gunfire close enough to
make everyone drop, the sharp smell of smoke and dust, the sight of terrified people trying to move
at once, and his own awareness that others were looking to one another for cues about where to run
and who needed help.

In those moments, Miguel remembers freezing and then moving in a way that has never felt like
enough. He can recount trying to get down, trying to pull himself and others toward cover, but the
part of the event that has remained most powerful for him is his conviction that he did not do more
to stop what happened and did not protect the people he should have protected. He speaks about this
with the certainty of a verdict rather than the uncertainty of a memory. In his mind, there was
something a stronger man would have done, some decisive action that would have changed the outcome,
and because he did not do it, he has come to believe he is weak. Even now, decades later, he says he
will never forgive himself for failing others that day.

The memory does not come back to him in only one form. At times it arrives with such force that he
feels as if he is back there completely. He does not merely think about the event; he re-enters it.
He describes hearing the gunfire again, smelling the smoke, seeing the scene all at once with
unnerving immediacy, as if the present moment has dropped away. During these episodes he may go
quiet and rigid, his attention fixed somewhere beyond the room. At other times he can tell the story
in a flat, detached way that unsettles him differently. Then he sounds almost like an observer
reporting something that happened to someone else, with little feeling in his voice. He notices that
numb version too and is disturbed by it, as if even his distance from the memory is another sign of
failure.

These experiences have sat uneasily alongside his life as a Navy officer. His work environment is
structured, demanding, and full of reminders of responsibility for others. In briefings, on base,
and in everyday leadership situations, he is repeatedly put in positions where protection, judgment,
and decisive action are central values. He does not report dramatic outward breakdowns at work, but
the private meaning he gives the trauma is woven into how he experiences himself there. Moments when
subordinates look to him for direction can stir the old thought with fresh force: a good man
protects people; he failed to do that. The contrast between the competent officer others see and the
younger civilian he still condemns inside has become increasingly painful. He carries himself with
control, yet underneath he is often measuring himself against that moment and finding himself
morally inadequate.

Over the years Miguel has spoken very little about the event, even with his partner. When memories
break through vividly, he is left with intense guilt so immediate that it seems undeservedly
current, as though the harm has just happened and he is again standing in the instant of not doing
enough. When the memory comes back numb and distant, he does not feel relieved; instead he is
troubled by the absence of feeling, interpreting that too through the lens of weakness and failure.
The result has been a life in which the event is never fully absent. It appears in flashes, in self-
judgments, and in quiet moments when he finds himself replaying what he should have done
differently.

He sought help now because he has become tired of living under that internal accusation. As he gets
older, he is less able to dismiss the idea that this is simply something he should carry alone. He
is frightened that if he talks about the event in detail, the memory will become completely real
again and overwhelm him with the same sensory force it has in the worst moments. He is equally
frightened that nothing will change, that therapy might confirm what he already believes—that he was
weak, that he failed to protect others, and that he does not deserve forgiveness. At the same time,
what he hopes for is modest but deeply important to him: to speak about that day without being
pulled entirely back into it, and to find some way of living with the memory that does not always
end in judgment.
