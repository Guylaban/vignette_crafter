Kwame is an 18-year-old African American man who recently began work as a police officer and came to
treatment after months of feeling increasingly on edge in ways he no longer trusted himself to
manage. When he speaks about what happened to him, he does not tell it as a smooth story. He returns
instead to a handful of fixed images from childhood, each one sharp and separate, “like pictures”
that arrive without warning. In one, he is very small and standing in a hallway outside the kitchen
at night. In another, the house has gone unusually quiet in the strange way it did just before
violence broke out. In another, he can see his mother’s posture change and his own body flattening,
shoulders tightened, as if bracing before he even knew exactly what would happen next. He witnessed
domestic violence repeatedly as a child, and what remains with him now is not only the visual memory
of those moments but the felt sense of being trapped in the same position, unable to move, unable to
stop what was happening.

When these memories come back, Kwame does not experience them as distant recollections. He feels his
body take on that old stance, muscles tensing as if he is once again a child pinned to the spot
outside the room. What comes with it most strongly is grief. He describes being overtaken by a
heavy, painful sadness when he remembers those nights, not only fear but a sense of loss so strong
it leaves him quiet for hours afterward. He has come to believe that something about the way he
reacts means there is something wrong with him. He worries that these sudden bodily reactions and
the force of the memories mean he is “going crazy.” Just as often, he concludes something harsher:
that he cannot rely on himself. If he could not stop bad things then, he thinks, maybe he still
cannot stop bad things now.

Those beliefs have settled into the way he moves through daily life. Since joining the police force,
he has noticed how quickly he scans every setting for danger. Walking into a break room, a
convenience store, or a family home on a call, his eyes immediately pick out anything that could be
used as a weapon: a heavy stapler, a kitchen knife left near a sink, a glass bottle on a table, a
metal tool by a doorway. He knows this fits his work in some ways, but he also feels how relentless
it is, as if his attention cannot loosen even when the situation does not require it. People acting
suspiciously are especially hard for him to tolerate. A pause too long before answering, someone
shifting their hands out of sight, a glance exchanged across a room—these moments bring an immediate
sense of threat. He feels uncomfortable quickly, in a full-body way, before he has had time to
think.

Silence is particularly difficult. On patrol, if a house becomes suddenly quiet as he approaches, or
if conversation inside stops for a beat, his stomach drops. What frightens him most is the sense
that unusual silence means something dangerous is about to happen, the same charged stillness he
remembers from childhood just before violence erupted. He avoids unfamiliar places whenever he can
because not knowing the layout leaves him feeling exposed and unable to assess danger. Off duty,
this has narrowed his life. He turns down invitations if he has not been to the restaurant,
apartment, or neighborhood before. Friends have started to joke that he is hard to get out of the
house, and he hears the distance growing even if he does not explain it.

Unexpected touch is another flashpoint. If a colleague comes up behind him and claps a hand on his
shoulder, or someone brushes past him before he registers they are there, his whole body jolts. He
hates the instant of being unprepared and then feels ashamed of how strong the reaction is. These
moments feed his fear that he is losing control of himself. He does not experience himself as steady
or dependable; he experiences himself as a person who should have learned by now how to handle
things and somehow has not.

What finally brought Kwame in was not one dramatic incident but the accumulation of them. He had
begun dreading ordinary parts of his shift, especially entering unfamiliar buildings or standing in
sudden quiet. He was withdrawing more from friends, and after an unexpected touch at work left him
visibly shaken, he could not dismiss it as just stress. In seeking help, he says he wants to
understand why his body still reacts as though those childhood moments are happening now. What he
fears most is that he will keep feeling helpless in the face of danger and that his reactions mean
he is no longer in command of himself. What he hopes, more quietly, is that he might someday
remember without feeling pinned back into the posture of that child in the hallway.
