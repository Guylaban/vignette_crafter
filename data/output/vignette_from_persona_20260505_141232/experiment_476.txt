Kwame is a 34-year-old parole officer who came to treatment after months of telling himself he
should be able to handle things on his own. He is in a relationship now, but when he speaks about
the assault that has come to define the last part of his life, he returns to the apartment he shared
with a former partner, a place he once experienced as intimate and ordinary. The violence happened
during an argument that turned physical with a speed he still struggles to grasp. What remains most
sharply with him is not a tidy sequence but a set of sensory fragments that break into the present
with brutal force: the sound of his partner’s voice in his ear, the specific things said to him
while he was being overpowered, and the metallic, medicinal taste in his mouth afterward when he
realized he was bleeding. He was later treated at a hospital, and the antiseptic smell there fused
itself to the event so completely that even now certain cleaning products or the sharp scent of
disinfectant can stop him in place.

In the weeks after the assault, Kwame tried to carry on with work. As a parole officer, much of his
day is spent in tense, enclosed interactions, often one-on-one and often with men. He noticed
quickly that being alone with male clients in interview rooms made his whole body tighten before
anyone had even spoken. He would sit angled toward the door, shoulders rigid, tracking every shift
in posture, every change in tone. He says he watches people constantly now, not because he wants to,
but because he feels he has to; his attention moves over hands, faces, exits, and distance
automatically, as if danger might declare itself at any second if he is vigilant enough to catch it.
By the end of the workday he is exhausted from holding himself in that state of readiness.

He also became acutely uncomfortable with being looked at in ways that felt sexual. A glance
lingering too long, a comment that might once have seemed merely rude, even the sense that someone
was evaluating his body could bring a jolt of alarm and disgust. He had come to feel that this kind
of attention was not trivial and not flattering; it felt dangerous. He started avoiding eye contact
broadly, both at work and at home, because meeting another person’s gaze felt too exposing, too
likely to invite something he did not want. Colleagues noticed that he seemed more distant in
interviews and staff meetings. He contributed less, deferred more, and worked hard to avoid
disagreements. If a client became argumentative, or if a coworker challenged him, Kwame would smooth
things over quickly, give ground, or leave matters unresolved rather than risk confrontation.

At home, these changes gathered into habits that shaped his relationship. Positive moments did not
feel restful to him. If his current partner was affectionate, playful, or proud of him, he often
pulled back internally or changed the subject, as though warmth itself were unsafe. He could not
easily explain this except to say that when he started to feel close, his guard went up. He
believed, increasingly, that his life had been ruined by what happened and that there had been no
justice in it, no real acknowledgment of how badly he had been harmed. He would think, with a flat
certainty that frightened him, that what had happened was deeply unfair and nothing had been done
about it.

The memories do not come to him as stories from the past. They arrive with a force that makes time
collapse. He will catch the smell of antiseptic in a clinic hallway or from supplies in the office,
and suddenly he hears the words from that night in his head as clearly as if they are being spoken
again. At other times he becomes aware of the taste that was in his mouth during the assault, vivid
and immediate, and for a few moments he is no longer fully in the present. What disturbs him most is
how completely real it feels. He does not simply remember; he feels seized by the sense that it is
happening again now.

By the time he sought help, Kwame was worn down by living as though danger were always inches away.
Work, which had once given him structure and purpose, had become a daily exercise in bracing
himself. At home he felt guilty for how much he withheld from the person he loves. He admitted,
carefully and with shame, that there are times he is not sure his life is worth living anymore. He
did not come in because that thought had become a plan, but because it had started to feel less
shocking than it should. What finally brought him through the door was the fear that this was all
his life would be now: a body perpetually on alert, a world full of threat, and a future narrowed by
something he cannot yet place firmly in the past. He says he wants help because he does not want
every room, every look, every smell to feel dangerous forever.
