happened, and the taste in his mouth that returns to him even now with unnerving precision. He says
that when the memory comes back he can taste it again as if he is there, a metallic, stale taste
that seems to belong to fear and enclosed air and his own body bracing for what was about to happen.

In the months after he returned home, Daniel found that ordinary disagreement no longer felt
ordinary. If a conversation became heated, or if someone pushed on a topic that felt too close to
politics, war, military service, or anything resembling the arguments that had preceded the event,
his whole body shifted. He did not describe this as remembering at first. He just knew that
confrontations seemed charged in a way they had not before, and that certain conversation topics
made him feel he needed to shut things down or get away. Even feeling physically unwell could set
something off. If he was run-down, nauseated, feverish, or simply felt off in his body, he became
less able to keep distance from what had happened; the memory would press closer, and with it the
taste and the sense that he was no longer fully in the present.

At work, these patterns became woven into his routines. As a tax consultant, he spends much of the
year in conference rooms with clients discussing money, mistakes, deadlines, and sometimes
accusations. He has become the kind of man who scans every office before sitting down. He
automatically notes the exits, where he could step out, what path would keep him from being boxed
in. He cannot relax until he has mapped the room. If a client’s tone sharpens, if two people begin
arguing over numbers, or if the room goes unusually quiet in the middle of a meeting, Daniel feels a
hard surge of alarm. Silence is especially difficult for him; what unsettles him is the sense that
when everything suddenly goes quiet, something dangerous is about to happen. His colleagues know him
as controlled and prepared, but they have also noticed that he prefers to stand near doors and is
unusually quick to end meetings that become tense.

At home, the effects have been more painful. Daniel is married, and one of the clearest changes has
been around touch. If his wife comes up behind him and lays a hand on his shoulder, he startles
violently before he can think. He hates the look on her face afterward, the hurt mixed with caution.
He has tried to explain that unexpected contact hits him like an alarm, but he cannot bear prolonged
discussion of the military event or what followed, so most conversations stop before either of them
feels understood. He also pushes away moments that feel too good. A relaxed evening, affection,
laughter, the warmth of feeling close to his wife—these have come to seem unsafe to him, as though
letting himself feel positive emotion would leave him exposed. He pulls back from tenderness,
changes the subject, gets busy, or goes quiet. Over time, his marriage has become organized around
what not to do: don’t touch him unexpectedly, don’t ask too many questions, don’t linger on certain
topics.

The memory itself is inconsistent in a way Daniel finds deeply unsettling. Sometimes when it
returns, he is overwhelmed so quickly that he has to cut it off, stop talking, leave the room, or
focus on anything else just to get through the next few minutes. At other times he can describe
parts of it in a flat, detached way, as if it happened to someone else entirely. That emotional
numbness frightens him almost as much as the overwhelming moments. He has come to believe that
either he will be swallowed by the memory or he will disappear from it completely. Neither feels
under his control.

What finally brought him in was not a single crisis but an accumulation. A client confrontation at

sensory fragments came back with unusual force. Then one evening his wife touched him unexpectedly
in their kitchen and he recoiled so sharply that she began to cry. Daniel told the therapist he is
tired of living as if every room needs an escape plan and every difficult conversation is edging
toward danger. He is most afraid that this is simply who he is now—guarded, unreachable, impossible
to comfort—but he is also hoping, carefully, that he might learn how to stay present without either
shutting down or feeling overtaken.
