Margaret is a 79-year-old widow of European American background who has spent most of her adult life
working as a tailor, first from a small alterations shop and now from a sewing room she keeps in the
front of her house. She came to treatment late in life, after many years of managing alone, and

impossible it has been for her to set it down in words. When she tries to describe what happened,
she stops and circles back, losing the order of events. She knows there was blood, knows there were
moments of shouting and movement and then a stunned aftermath, but she cannot put the memory into a
clean sequence. She becomes visibly tense when she approaches it and then angry, not in a general
way but with a sudden, scorching force that seems to seize her as the memory comes close. She says
she cannot seem to say it properly, that the worst part lives in images and bodily sensations rather
than language.

The event occurred during her military service, and although she has avoided speaking about details
for decades, the fragments she does report have remained sharply potent. Blood is the cue she
mentions immediately. Even now, the sight of it can pull her out of the present. A nicked finger in
her sewing room, a customer joking about “bleeding hems,” a television scene she did not expect to
see—these moments strike her with unwanted thoughts she cannot wave off. What comes to her is not a
complete story but a rush of vivid, disjointed impressions that make her feel as if she is back in
contact with something unfinished and intolerable. Certain conversation topics are almost as
difficult. If neighbors begin talking about the military, wartime injuries, or even news stories
involving troops, Margaret’s face closes off. She has learned to leave the room, change the subject
abruptly, or busy herself with thread and fabric rather than remain exposed to where her mind might
go.

In the years after the trauma, she noticed a change she still describes mournfully: she used to
think of herself as a happy person, sociable and easy to please, and now she experiences herself as
permanently altered. She says she is always miserable, and when she tries to explain that, she does
not mean merely sad. She means a kind of deadness, an inner flatness that leaves her feeling
emotionally absent from her own life. She has come to believe that nothing good can happen to her
anymore. When pleasant moments appear, they do not register as comfort. They feel dangerous, almost
like a setup. Because of that, she has gradually built a life organized around not letting herself
feel too much hope or warmth. If her granddaughter suggests a family dinner, or an old friend
invites her to church tea or a birthday lunch, Margaret often declines. She tells herself it is
easier not to go than to risk the jolt of feeling briefly happy and then having that feeling turn
unsafe in her body. Over time she has also stopped seeing friends with any regularity. The result is
that her world has narrowed to work, errands, and long quiet evenings in the house she once shared
with her husband.

Her work as a tailor shows this pattern clearly. Sewing is familiar and structured, and she still
takes pride in precise work, but even there the trauma intrudes. A client discussing a son in the
service, a finger pricked by a needle, a stain on a garment that resembles blood can suddenly fill
her mind with unwanted thoughts. On those days she becomes withdrawn and curt, sometimes closing the
shop room early and leaving hems unfinished until the next day. She does not explain this to
customers; she simply says she is tired. Friends have learned not to press her, and many have
stopped asking.

What finally brought Margaret to seek help was not a single crisis but the recognition, after years
of widowhood and increasing isolation, that she was living as though every good thing had to be kept
at arm’s length. She recently turned down a visit with relatives she genuinely wanted to see and
then sat alone in her sewing room, feeling empty and furious with herself. That moment frightened
her. She could see how little of life she was permitting herself. At the same time, she remains
afraid of what might happen if she lets the memories come closer or allows herself to feel happiness
again. What she hopes for, cautiously, is not to become someone new, but to feel less dead inside
and to be able to stay present when ordinary life brushes against the past.
