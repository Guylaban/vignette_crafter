designer for a small branding firm. He described the event he could not stop organizing his life
around as an encounter with police late at night after leaving a friend’s apartment. He had been
driving home in the dark through a downtown area after midnight, tired and eager to get back to his
apartment, when flashing lights appeared behind him. He remembered the patrol car pulling up at an
angle, the hard spill of headlights and red-blue light into his car, and the way everything outside
the windows suddenly looked both glaring and unreal. What stayed with him most was not a full,
orderly narrative, but a set of moments that still felt immediate: the shape of the police car
beside him, the officer approaching with a weapon visible at his side, the shouted commands, the
sense that there were too many bodies too close to him in too little space. He had covered his face
with his hands at one point, partly from panic, partly because he felt exposed and trapped, and he
still remembered thinking that if he moved wrong in that darkness, something terrible would happen.

In the weeks afterward, Daniel found that night had changed meaning for him. He dreaded the period
between evening and sleep, and if he woke in the dark he would lie still for a moment with a jolt of
alarm before orienting himself. What frightened him most was how quickly the feeling came back, as
if darkness itself meant danger. Driving became narrower and more calculated. If he noticed a car
that looked like the one from that night—a similar model, similar light bar, even a similar
silhouette in his rearview mirror—his body tightened immediately. He would grip the wheel, check
mirrors repeatedly, or change routes just to get away from it. In crowded places, especially when
people moved around him unpredictably, he felt the same trapped pressure he had felt by the roadside
with officers close to his door. He began stepping out of grocery lines, skipping packed bars and
concerts, and timing errands for quieter hours.

Daniel’s work life gave these reactions a daily stage. The design firm expected in-person
collaboration, and their office sat in a busy commercial district where lunch meant crowded
sidewalks and cafés. He started eating at his desk to avoid the rush downstairs. When the team
wanted to meet a new client at a bustling restaurant, he often found an excuse to stay back and
“finish revisions.” On more than one occasion he had to walk past uniformed police officers
stationed near a downtown event, and he would immediately turn his face away or bring his hands up,
partly covering it as he passed. He knew it looked odd, but the sight of uniforms, holsters, and
weapons felt intolerable. He had come to believe that being seen by police was dangerous in itself,
that attention from them could turn without warning into another night like that one.

Over the following months, Daniel increasingly relied on alcohol and whatever medication or drugs
were available to him to get through evenings and to blunt the edge of waking in the dark. He told
himself he needed something to shut his mind off before bed and something to take the edge off after
difficult days downtown. Sometimes after spotting a similar car on his commute, he would go straight
home and drink rather than risk being out again. The pattern narrowed his life. He became less
dependable with friends because he declined invitations if they involved crowds, and after his
divorce he was living alone with few people who saw how much of his day was arranged around not
being triggered. At work he was still talented, but coworkers had begun to notice that he avoided
client events, preferred remote contact whenever possible, and seemed visibly tense if there was
police presence outside the building.

By the time he sought help, Daniel was exhausted by how small his world had become. What finally
pushed him into treatment was a recent night when he woke in a dark apartment, disoriented and
panicked, and then called in sick the next morning after seeing a police car that resembled the one
from the stop near his office. He said he was tired of planning every route, every errand, and every
social interaction around what he might see. At the same time, he was certain that letting his guard
down would be reckless. He wanted help because he did not want to keep drinking his way through
nights and hiding from uniforms and crowds, but he was afraid that talking about what happened would
make him feel as trapped and exposed as he had on the roadside. What he hoped for, in the plainest
terms, was to be able to wake up in the dark and know he was only in his own room, to drive without
scanning every similar car, and to move through his city without feeling that danger was waiting
just behind him.
