Nadim is a 56-year-old Middle Eastern American man who works as a social media manager and lives
with his wife. When he speaks about the night that now divides his life into before and after, he
does not tell it in a smooth sequence. He reaches a point and then stops, as if something clamps
down inside him. Even in session he seems to approach the memory, recoil from it, and move away
before it is complete.

The incident happened after work, when he was driving home and was pulled over by police. What
stands out in the fragments he can bear to describe is how quickly the encounter turned physical and
how little sense it made to him while it was happening. He remembers the metallic taste in his mouth
with unusual force, as if the memory carries it intact into the present. When it comes back, he can
taste it again so clearly that it seems to erase the room he is actually in. He recalls being
shouted at, forced down, and feeling trapped inside a situation that was already out of control
before he understood what he needed to do. What is hardest for him to live with is not only the
officers’ violence but his own actions in those seconds. He had always thought of himself as
measured and principled, someone who would respond with dignity. Instead, under pressure and fear,
he reacted in ways that he feels went against who he is. He does not elaborate much, but he has come
to believe that he failed some private moral test that night.

In the weeks afterward, Nadim found that strong emotion of any kind became something to avoid. Fear
was there, but so were anger, shame, and bursts of horror that seemed to arrive without warning. He
also began to pull back from things that might stir positive feelings. He stopped watching films
with his wife if they were suspenseful or emotionally intense, then stopped watching most television
altogether. News clips, police dramas, viral videos, even reading about public confrontations online
became intolerable. Given that his job requires him to spend much of the day online, this became
particularly difficult. He started skimming content in a narrow, mechanical way, clicking away from
posts that referenced arrests, violence, or confrontation. Colleagues noticed that he was less
willing to take on campaigns tied to current events. He did not explain much. He only knew that once
an image or headline caught him, the taste would come back, followed by a surge of rage so strong it
frightened him.

His daily life narrowed around vigilance. Entering any restaurant, office, or public venue, he first
located the exits. He could not settle into a chair or conversation until he had mapped at least one
way out, preferably two. He scanned faces, doorways, body movements, hands. If someone stood too
close behind him in line or blocked part of a path, his body tightened before he could think. Even
at home with his wife, in a place he knew was safe, he found himself unable to relax. He would check
locks, look out windows, and monitor noises in the hallway or street. The effort of appearing normal
exhausted him.

Over time he came to see these reactions not as excessive but as proof that he had learned something
essential. He was certain the world was dangerous in a more fundamental way than he had once allowed
himself to admit. He had also become convinced that people are not what they seem. A routine
interaction could conceal humiliation or threat; an official face could shift without warning into
something cruel. These beliefs entered ordinary moments. In work meetings he watched for changes in
tone and posture rather than listening fully. In public he studied strangers for signs of
volatility. The strain affected his marriage. His wife wanted him to go out more, to visit friends,
to watch a movie together and have some version of their old evenings back, but Nadim often refused
or sat through an outing visibly tense, preoccupied with the nearest door.

What finally brought him to seek help was the accumulation of these restrictions and the fear of
what he was becoming. He had recently been triggered by work content he could not avoid, and the
memory came in with the old taste and the same violent rush of anger before he shoved it away. He
was shaken not only by the memory but by how incomplete it remained, how he still could not let
himself know all of it. He came to treatment wanting relief, though even that desire is guarded.
What frightens him most is that he may never feel safe again, and that if he lets the memory come
fully, he will be overwhelmed by rage and by what it says about the world, about other people, and
about himself.
