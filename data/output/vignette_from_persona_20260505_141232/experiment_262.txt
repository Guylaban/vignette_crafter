Lukas is a 21-year-old public defender who came to treatment after months of feeling as if danger
could arrive at any moment, even in rooms that other people experienced as ordinary. He was born in
Europe and came to the United States as a refugee after a violent displacement in late adolescence,
an experience he still describes less as a story than as flashes of what he saw. When he speaks
about that period at all, it comes in stark visual fragments: a corridor jammed with people trying
to move at once, faces turned in panic, the fixed sightline of a doorway that had become an exit and
a trap, bodies crouched low and waiting for the next sound. What has stayed with him most vividly
are the moments of suspended quiet between bursts of noise, when everyone around him seemed to
understand that silence did not mean safety but that something terrible might be about to happen.

In the years since arriving in the U.S., Lukas has built a life that appears unusually accomplished
for his age. He is in a relationship and works as a public defender, spending his days in holding
areas, crowded courtrooms, and interview rooms where abrupt noises are common and the atmosphere can
turn tense without warning. Yet the same environment that makes him good at noticing risk also keeps
him in a state of constant alert. A metal door slamming in the courthouse, someone raising a voice
in the hall, even a dropped file can make him jolt before he has time to think. He experiences these
reactions as immediate and physical, a full-body response that feels as if he is back in the posture
he held during the refugee trauma—braced, low, ready for impact. At times there is no picture in his
mind at all, only a sudden rush of fear so intense that it feels disproportionate to the moment and
yet completely convincing to him.

Lukas has become preoccupied not only with danger around him but with what his own mind is doing. He
monitors his thoughts closely for signs that he is losing control or “going crazy,” especially after
one of these false alarms. If his heart jumps at a sound or if a wave of terror comes over him in a
quiet room, he immediately checks himself: Was that normal? Why did I react like that? Does this
mean something is wrong with me? He does not describe elaborate beliefs about the past so much as a
settled conviction about the present world. To him, it is simply true that the world is dangerous,
that safety is temporary, and that only constant vigilance offers any chance of staying ahead of
what might happen next. What frightens him most is not one specific anticipated threat but the sense
that danger arrives without warning and that people who relax are the ones who get caught off guard.

Silence is particularly difficult for him. In his office, if the usually noisy corridor suddenly
goes still, Lukas feels his body tense before he can identify why. In those moments he is certain
something is about to happen. He scans for footsteps, changes in tone, movement at the door.
Colleagues may just experience a lull in activity, but for him unusual quiet carries the same shape
as the worst moments of his displacement. His memories are often predominantly visual, but not
always. Sometimes a client recounting a chaotic scene, or even hearing others mention refugees or
aftermaths of war, is enough to bring on the emotional force of the past without any image attached.
He feels the terror first and only later realizes that he has drifted into that state again.

Over time Lukas has made his life narrower in ways that feel sensible to him. He avoids talking
about what happened, including with his partner, because speaking about it seems likely to pull him
back into the same helpless intensity. He also avoids reminders when they come from others. If
coworkers begin discussing international conflicts or refugee crises, he finds a reason to leave. If
a client’s history overlaps too closely with his own, he keeps the conversation strictly legal and
impersonal. This has begun to affect his work; he is still committed and effective, but there are
interviews he rushes through, conversations he redirects, and moments when he appears distant just
when clients are asking for human connection. At home, his partner has noticed that Lukas goes quiet
when certain topics come up and that he is hard to reach emotionally after a day filled with abrupt
noises and constant scanning.

What finally brought him in was not a single new event but the accumulation of these moments until
they felt inescapable. He recently startled so violently at an ordinary sound in the courthouse that
he felt embarrassed in front of colleagues, and afterward he spent hours monitoring his own
thoughts, trying to determine whether he was becoming unstable. He is seeking help now because he is
exhausted by always being on guard and because he does not want his relationship or his work to
shrink further around what he cannot bear to hear or say. At the same time, he is afraid that if he
lets his guard down—even in therapy—something terrible will happen, or that speaking plainly about
the past will bring it back with full force. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not to forget, but to
stop living as though the next danger is already at the door.
