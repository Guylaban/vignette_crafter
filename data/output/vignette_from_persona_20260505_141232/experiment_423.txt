Claire is a 33-year-old married white American woman who works as a graphic designer, most often in
a downtown studio where she splits her time between a shared office and meetings with clients in
nearby coffee shops and coworking spaces. She traces the beginning of her current difficulties to a
period of stalking that started after a man began appearing repeatedly around her commute and
neighborhood. What made it terrifying to her was not a single confrontation as much as the
accumulating pattern: she would notice him near the bike rack outside her building, then again a few
blocks into her ride home, then standing across from the corner store she used most evenings. On
more than one occasion, when she turned to check whether she was imagining it, he altered course
just enough to make it clear to her that he was aware of her. The bicycle became central to the fear
because it was the setting in which she felt exposed and easy to track, moving through predictable
streets with nowhere to hide and no way to know how long he had been behind her before she noticed.

In the weeks that followed, Claire found herself studying distance in a new way. If someone came up
too close behind her on the sidewalk or in a line, her body reacted before she had time to think.
She would go rigid, her chest tightening, then jerk around to look. Riding her bike became
especially fraught. The sound of another bicycle approaching, the glimpse of a rider pacing her
speed, or even simply being overtaken on a bike path could make her feel as if the stalking were
happening again in real time. She was often certain she was being followed or tracked. A stranger
moving in the same direction for several blocks did not feel like coincidence to her; it felt like
the first sign that she had missed something important and was already in danger.

Over time, these reactions settled into the structure of her days. Claire stopped going near the
area where several of the incidents had happened, even when avoiding it made her commute longer and
more complicated. She rerouted errands, declined invitations if they meant passing through those
streets, and sometimes asked her husband to pick things up for her rather than go herself. At work,
this had concrete consequences. Part of her job involved occasional client visits, and if a meeting
was scheduled near that part of downtown she would try to move it online or persuade a coworker to
take it. In public places she rarely felt settled until she had identified how to leave. In cafés
and conference rooms she chose seats near the exit and kept track of doors, hallways, and who was
standing where. She scanned constantly—faces, reflections in windows, the movement of people behind
her, bicycles passing outside. She knew other people did not seem to be doing this, but to her it
felt like the only sensible way to be.

Claire speaks about the stalking with a fixed conviction that the people responsible faced no
consequences. That belief has hardened into a broader sense that the world is dangerous and that
people are not what they seem. Ordinary social ambiguity feels menacing to her now. A person smiling
too quickly, someone lingering without an obvious reason, a harmless overlap in travel route—these
are not neutral occurrences in her mind. She has come to assume that appearances reveal very little,
and that danger often looks normal until it is too late. When she says this, she does not sound
dramatic; she sounds certain.

Her husband has noticed how jumpy she has become. If a person comes through a door unexpectedly
behind her, or someone speaks when she has not heard them approach, she startles sharply. Claire
herself is frustrated by how often she reacts as if she is in immediate danger even when nothing is
actually wrong. She can recognize afterward that the cyclist behind her was only commuting, or that
the person entering the coffee shop had no interest in her, but in the moment her body responds as
though she must act quickly. She plans escape routes before she can relax anywhere, from restaurants
to her own office lobby, and if she cannot see a clear way out she remains tense and distracted.

What finally brought her to seek help was the extent to which her world had narrowed. A recent
client asked for an in-person meeting near the neighborhood she avoids, and Claire spent days trying
to find a way out of going before breaking down in tears at home. She is frightened by how much of
her life is organized around anticipating threat, and by how easily an approaching bicycle or the
feeling of someone behind her can undo her sense of safety. At the same time, she hopes for
something simple and specific: to move through the city, do her work, and be beside other people
without always preparing to flee.
