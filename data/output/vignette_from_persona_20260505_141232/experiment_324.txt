another guard in another city.

What has stayed with David most strongly is the sense that he was in the role he was supposed to
fulfill and still could not stop what happened. He had gone into that line of work with a clear idea
of himself as someone who protected people, someone steady in danger, someone who would know what to
do. In the aftermath, he had come to believe that he had failed in the most important role in his
life. Even now he compares himself harshly to an imagined other man, certain that somebody else

decisive before the attack unfolded. When he says this, it is not boastful fantasy turned inward but

himself over blindly.

happened, but he returns to them anyway. The ruminations seem to grip him most when he is alone

truth—that he failed when it mattered, and that he no longer has the capacity to handle even
ordinary life.
