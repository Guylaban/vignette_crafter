Mark is a 34-year-old school counselor who came to treatment after months of feeling as if his life
had narrowed around a single afternoon he could neither speak about clearly nor escape. He is
married and had been known at work as calm, steady, and dependable with students in crisis. That
image of himself was shattered after an accident in which he unintentionally caused another person
to be seriously injured. Since then, he has carried a fixed sense that what happened was because of
the way he acted. He does not describe the event in a smooth narrative. Instead, when he tries, his
speech stalls, his face tightens, and he says he knows exactly what happened but cannot find words
that feel adequate. What comes through are flashes with full force: the smell at the scene, the
bodily shock, the sense of the moment freezing and then repeating as if it is still underway.

In the days after the accident, Mark found that the memory did not behave like something finished.
It would come back with such immediacy that he felt pulled out of the present and into the event
again. He did not experience it as remembering in the ordinary sense. He would suddenly feel as
though he was there, with everything returning at once across his senses, especially smell. Related
odors could bring it on instantly, and what frightened him was not only the memory itself but how
completely his body believed it. His stomach clenched, his heart pounded, and his muscles tensed
before he could tell himself where he was. At those moments he felt horror, fear, anger, guilt, and
shame in a rush so strong that it seemed larger than the current situation.

As the weeks went on, stress of almost any kind made him feel more vulnerable to being pulled back
into the accident. Busy days at school, emotionally charged meetings with students or parents, and
the general pressure of the workday left him feeling exposed, as if his mind had less protection
against the memory. He began organizing his life around staying mentally occupied at all times.
During breaks at school, he kept email open, shuffled paperwork, volunteered for extra tasks, or
scrolled on his phone rather than allowing his mind to go still. At home, he kept the television on,
listened to podcasts while doing chores, and filled silence with errands or pointless activity. He
was aware that he was doing this to avoid thinking about the event, and the logic felt obvious to
him: if he let himself dwell on it, he feared he would be swallowed by it.

Certain settings became especially difficult. Undergoing a medical examination or procedure was one
of the strongest triggers. Even routine appointments took on a threatening quality because the
medical environment brought him too close to the sensory world surrounding the aftermath of the
injury. He would arrive already tense, bracing for smells, equipment, or procedures that might make
the memory feel present again. He also began avoiding police whenever possible. Seeing a police car
near the school, passing officers in the community, or noticing law enforcement activity nearby
produced a sharp spike of dread and bodily reactivity. He would change his route, delay leaving, or
look away quickly. He did not describe this as simple discomfort. He felt certain that contact with
police would force him back into what happened and into what it meant about him.

At night, Mark had difficulty falling asleep because he felt he needed to stay alert. He could not
easily explain alert for what, only that fully letting go into sleep seemed unsafe. He lay listening
for sounds in the house and tracking small changes in the environment, as if vigilance itself were a
form of protection. The result was mounting exhaustion, and that exhaustion made work harder. In his
office at school, he noticed himself becoming less confident in ordinary decisions with students and
families. He had come to believe he could not trust himself to do the right thing. Choices that once
felt routine now felt morally dangerous. He second-guessed emails, delayed responses, and worried
that any action on his part might hurt someone. This erosion of trust in himself extended into his
marriage as well; he withdrew when his wife asked what was wrong, not because he did not want to
answer, but because he felt ashamed and because words seemed to fail him whenever he approached the
event directly.

By the time he sought help, Mark was speaking about the accident as something that had destroyed his
life. He did not mean only that he felt miserable. He meant that the person he had been
before—capable, trustworthy, useful to others—now felt inaccessible to him. What finally brought him
in was the combination of relentless mental effort, worsening sleep, and the realization that even
at school, in the very role he valued most, he was living in avoidance and fear. He said he wanted
help because he could not keep spending every day trying not to think. What he was most afraid of
was that the accident had revealed something fundamentally dangerous about him, and what he most
hoped for was to be able to remember what happened without feeling as if it were happening all over
again.
