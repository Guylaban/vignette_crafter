María Elena is a 74-year-old Latina pharmacist who has been married for many years and is only now
seeking treatment for what she describes as a life narrowed by fear, tension, and shame. When she
speaks about the abuse, she does not tell it as a smooth story. It comes back to her in sharp,
separate pictures. She sees the apartment door swinging open hard, hears it slam against the wall,
and then another image arrives of herself cornered and bracing, her body held in the same bent,
guarded position she had taken then. When these memories come, it is not only that she remembers;
she feels herself physically returned to that posture, as if her muscles have learned the event
better than words ever could. Even in the safety of the consulting room, she lowers her shoulders
and draws in her arms when she reaches those moments. What floods her most powerfully on recall is
shame. She looks down and says little, conveying how humiliating it feels to revisit what happened
with a man who was supposed to love her.

The abuse occurred in the context of a romantic relationship earlier in her life, before her current
marriage, and she still organizes her present around the possibility of that kind of danger. She
remembers that some of the worst episodes began with arguments about family—comments about loyalty,
criticism about relatives, insinuations about what kind of woman she was in relation to family
obligations. Because of that, even now, seemingly ordinary remarks about family can land in her body
like a warning. If someone asks too personally about her children, her husband, or what kind of
family she came from, she feels herself stiffen before she has even decided whether the conversation
is safe. Emotional closeness is similarly difficult. When another person becomes warm, interested,
or gently curious about her inner life, María Elena does not experience this simply as intimacy. She
becomes wary and pulls back, as though closeness itself might place her again within reach of harm.

Over the years these reactions have become woven into daily life. At home, she remains on guard in a
way she knows is exhausting but cannot easily stop. She listens for sounds that might mean someone
is entering the house, especially the sound of a door opening or slamming. A door closing elsewhere
in the home can make her whole body tense instantly. She says her body is always ready to react,
even when she is sitting with her husband in the evening. There is no room in the house that fully
feels like a place where she can let down her watchfulness. She notices herself listening, scanning,
waiting.

At work as a pharmacist, these habits have had visible consequences. The pharmacy counter brings
constant interactions with customers, technicians, and patients, and she has increasingly arranged
her day to minimize unnecessary conversation. If coworkers begin sharing personal stories about
their families, she finds a reason to move away, check stock, or focus on prescriptions. She avoids
people where she can, not because she dislikes them, but because contact feels costly and
unpredictable. She has also become careful not to allow herself strong emotion of any kind. If she
feels anger, fear, sadness, or even tenderness rising, she shuts it down quickly. To her, strong
feeling seems dangerous, something that could leave her exposed or overwhelmed.

She is also distressed by how alien her own body has become to her. Rather than taking comfort in
it, she avoids paying attention to it or even looking at it more than necessary. Her body is too
closely tied to the remembered position of the abuse, too ready to reproduce it. This avoidance sits
beside painful conclusions she has drawn about herself. She has come to believe she is a weak person
for having lived through this and still being affected by it. She says she is no longer capable of
the things that once defined her. Although she continues working, she no longer experiences herself
as the competent, steady woman she used to be. The abuse also altered something broader in her view
of the world. She says she has lost faith—in people, in life, and in a higher power that once
mattered to her.

What finally brought María Elena to treatment was not one dramatic crisis but the cumulative
realization that she is living as if danger were always at the threshold. The sound of doors in her
own home, ordinary comments about family, and the possibility of emotional closeness all keep
finding her. She is frightened that this tense, guarded version of herself is all that remains. At
the same time, she is hoping, cautiously, that she might find a way to live in her home, in her
body, and with other people without feeling that she must always be braced for the next door to
open.
