involved his wife, and even when he tried to describe it in sequence, it came out in pieces. He

gap. What remained most vividly were disconnected fragments and the sense that the whole thing had
never settled into a single story in his mind.

crowded aisle, the stale heat, the tiredness that had settled into him after a long workday, and
then the abrupt eruption of fear when an incident on the bus left his wife injured and vulnerable in
public. In his recollection, the images did not arrive as a smooth narrative. Instead there was the
flash of her body partially uncovered, the humiliation of strangers staring, the metallic rattle of
the bus, someone shouting, and the sound of her voice that he still heard in his head at unwanted
moments. Sometimes what returned to him was not even a picture at first but the auditory trace of
that scene—the screech of brakes, raised voices, his wife crying out. At other times the memory ran
like a film that started without warning and replayed with the same merciless clarity, as if he were
back there watching the worst seconds repeat.

In the weeks that followed, Omar found that ordinary parts of life had become charged in ways he
could not control. Riding a bus was out of the question. Even seeing one pull up at a stop on his
commute made his body tense, his mind already filling in what could happen in the enclosed space
among strangers. He began arranging rides, paying for car services, or walking long distances rather
than using public transport. This affected his work quickly. Publishing had once required

visiting printers and partner offices—but Omar started declining assignments that required him to
cross town if he did not know exactly what the place looked like and how he would get there. He was
certain that unfamiliar places put him at a disadvantage because he could not immediately judge
where danger might come from, what objects could be used as weapons, or whether the people around
him were safe.

That way of scanning had become nearly constant. In conference rooms, cafés, lobbies, and even at
small literary receptions, his attention went first to faces and hands. He studied expressions,
posture, tone, any flicker that might signal threat. He noticed letter openers on desks, heavy glass
bottles, metal staplers, kitchen knives at catered events—objects other people ignored but that, to
Omar, stood out with alarming immediacy. His colleagues experienced him as distracted or overly
serious; his wife saw how little rest he got even when they were at home. He told her he could not

containable. On those days the replay in his mind seemed closer to the surface, and the sounds from
the event intruded more forcefully.
