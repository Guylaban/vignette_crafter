procedure, and the idea that danger could be managed if a person stayed disciplined enough. The
accident that brought him to treatment happened during a routine flight that turned catastrophic

lightheaded, aware of a sick, disorienting sensation in his body as alarms sounded around him. What
stayed with him most sharply was the combination of the bang, the sense that his body was failing
him, and the knowledge that he might die before he could regain control. He survived, but he spoke
about those moments as if the ordinary rules of life had split apart. The world no longer seemed
ordered or predictable to him after that.

In the weeks afterward, Mark found that the accident did not remain in the past in any simple way.
Sounds that resembled an explosion or even a sharp bang could seize his attention instantly. On
base, a sudden noise from equipment testing or a slammed metal door could make his whole body
tighten before he had time to think. If someone moved unexpectedly near him, he reacted as if
something dangerous were happening right then, turning sharply or jerking back with an intensity
that embarrassed him. He described living with his body “already braced,” as though some part of him
never stood down. Even at home with his wife, a quick movement in his peripheral vision could make
him feel exposed and vulnerable. He stayed watchful in rooms, scanning entrances and monitoring
sounds around him, not because he believed it was excessive, but because he had come to see the
world as fundamentally dangerous and capable of turning lethal without warning.

The sensations inside his own body became as frightening as anything outside him. Dizziness or
feeling physically unwell could stop him in his tracks because they were too close to what he had
felt in the cockpit. If he stood up and felt lightheaded, or woke with an unsettled stomach, he
became intensely alert to it, watching for signs that something was about to go wrong. He did not
talk about these episodes as ordinary discomfort. To him they carried the same menace as the
accident itself, as though his body could again betray him at the worst possible moment.

Over time he began shaping his life to avoid anything that brought him too close to that feeling. He
avoided hospitals, ambulances, and physical examinations. The sight of medical settings or the

appointments. He also began to control his eating strictly. He said little about this at first, but
it became clear that limiting food and keeping rigid rules around meals gave him a sense of order

that he no longer joined her spontaneously for dinners out or other small pleasures they had once
shared.

explanation, but with a flat withdrawal that unsettled the people around him. He described feeling
dead inside, as though enjoyment belonged to a previous version of himself. His wife experienced him
as increasingly distant; he was present in the house but emotionally unreachable, sitting in
silence, alert to every sound, with little interest in companionship or recreation. The loss was not

colleagues noticed he was more guarded and harder to read.

had never fully understood before, and he feared that if he let his guard down—even
briefly—something catastrophic could happen again. At the same time, he came in with a quieter hope
he could barely voice: that he might not have to feel so empty and perpetually braced for the rest
of his life.
