standing inside it. He is a married Latino man who has spent decades working as a QA engineer, a job
he once valued for its orderliness and its emphasis on catching small problems before they became
larger ones. The disaster occurred during a period of hot, windy weather when he and his wife were
at home. He remembered the first sign as the smell. Smoke had drifted in before he saw anything, a
sharp burnt odor that made him pause and go to the window. When he looked out, he saw flames moving
in the distance and then, with frightening speed, closer than he had believed possible. The sky had
an eerie color, and the weather itself seemed dangerous to him—the dry wind, the heat, the sense
that the air was helping the fire along. He has never described the event in dramatic language, but
he returns again and again to the same concrete moments: the smell of smoke filling the house, the
sight of flames where there should not have been any, and the feeling that the conditions outside
had turned familiar surroundings into something hostile.

In the weeks afterward, Ernesto tried to act as though he could put the experience aside if he were
disciplined enough. He told himself there was no point dwelling on it, and when images or thoughts
came up, he would deliberately force his attention elsewhere. He would turn on the television, start
a household task, or move immediately into some practical line of thinking, trying to push the
memories out of his mind before they took hold. But the thoughts returned in another form. He found
himself sitting for long periods replaying what had happened and asking what he had done wrong. He
would go over decisions made that day, wondering whether he should have noticed the danger sooner,
whether he should have reacted faster, whether some small action on his part could have changed what
happened. Just as often, his mind would move into long, circular efforts to work out how the
disaster could have been prevented at all, as if enough analysis might finally produce an answer
that would make the event feel less unbearable.

Over the following months, Ernesto became acutely sensitive to reminders in daily life. The smell of
smoke was especially potent. If a neighbor used a fireplace, if something scorched on the stove, or
if he caught even a faint burnt odor outdoors, his body reacted before he had time to think. He
described his heart pounding, his muscles tightening, and a rush of alarm that felt out of
proportion to the present moment but completely convincing while it was happening. Seeing flames
could do the same. Even ordinary fire—candles on a table, a grill being lit, footage of a house fire
on television—could stop him in place. Weather also took on a new meaning. On hot, dry, windy days
resembling the conditions of the wildfire, he became watchful and tense, scanning the horizon,
checking the air, listening for signs that something might be wrong.

This vigilance followed him into the routines of his life. Ernesto still worked as a QA engineer,
and even in that structured environment he found himself distracted by reminders. If he smelled
something electrical heating up in the office or saw an image with flames on a screen, his attention
narrowed immediately. He would look around, listen, and check for danger, sometimes losing track of
the work in front of him. He startled easily as well. A sudden sound in the office or at home could
make him jump hard enough to embarrass him, and he had become more aware of how much time he spent
braced for something unexpected. His wife noticed that he was less at ease and harder to settle. He
did not speak freely about the disaster with her, not because it meant little to him, but because he
was trying so hard not to let the thoughts in. Yet the more he tried to clamp down on them, the more
often she would find him quiet and preoccupied, staring off as if he were back in that day’s
weather.

By the time he sought help, Ernesto was exhausted by the constant state of readiness in his own body
and by the relentless mental review of what he should have done differently. What frightened him
most was how quickly a simple smell, a flicker of flame, or a certain kind of wind could make him
feel that the danger was present again. He had come to believe that he should have been able to
prevent something, or at least foresee it, and he could not stop measuring himself against that
standard. He was also tired of living as though ordinary sensations were warnings. Coming to
treatment, he said he hoped for some relief from the jumpiness and the vigilance, but just as
important, he wanted to stop feeling that every reminder pulled him back into the same unanswered
question of what he had done wrong.
