room he never really left. He is a widowed Vietnamese American man who has spent years working as a
cybersecurity analyst, a role that suits his preference for precision and control, but when he tries
to describe what happened to his child, his words start clearly and then fall apart. He can say that
his son became critically ill when he was young, that there were repeated hospitalizations,
procedures, and long nights under fluorescent lights, but when he gets close to the worst parts he
loses the thread. He stops mid-sentence, presses his fingers together, and looks away as though the
scene in front of him has become more real than the office where he is sitting.

What remains vivid are not orderly facts but sharply preserved moments. He remembers the antiseptic
smell in the hospital corridors, the sting of it in the back of his throat before he even reached
his son’s room. He remembers being called in while doctors prepared another procedure, seeing the
equipment laid out, hearing the clipped efficiency in staff voices, and feeling a rush of fear so
immediate that even now he describes it as if it is happening in the present tense. In his
recollection, the memory does not feel like something concluded long ago. It returns like a film
replaying on its own: the hospital bed, the small body surrounded by machines, the sense that at any
second he might lose his son. What frightened him most was the helplessness of standing there as
other people intervened physically in his child’s body while he could do nothing except watch and
wait.

In the years since, reminders of that period have remained painfully specific. Medical examinations
are especially difficult. Even routine appointments can become overwhelming once he is in a gown or
hears the sounds of a clinic around him. The smell of antiseptic is enough to make his body react
before he has fully registered why. His chest tightens, his hands shake, and for a few moments he is
no longer an elderly man at a standard appointment but a father bracing himself for terrible news.
At times he feels as though he is back in that hospital corridor, and his behavior shifts
accordingly; he becomes tense, watchful, and unable to focus on what the doctor is saying. He has
come to dread not just illness but the whole sensory world surrounding medical care.

Less expected to others, but perfectly coherent to him, are his reactions to babies and anything
associated with pregnancy or childbirth. Seeing a newborn in a waiting room, hearing a conversation
about labor, even passing an advertisement with an infant can pull him abruptly into the old terror
of parenthood under threat. For Minh, babies do not simply signify happiness or family life; they
immediately evoke vulnerability, dependence, and the possibility of catastrophe. He knows other
people do not understand why his expression changes when a colleague shows photos of a new
grandchild, but in those moments he feels ambushed by images of his own child at his most fragile.

These reactions have settled into his daily life, including at work. In cybersecurity he is
respected for his concentration, but over time he has arranged his routines to reduce the chance of
being caught off guard. He avoids taking assignments at hospital or healthcare client sites whenever
possible, not because he cannot do the technical work, but because walking through medical buildings
and catching that unmistakable hospital smell leaves him flooded and physically unsteady. He has
made excuses to coworkers about scheduling conflicts rather than explain why a site visit could undo
him for the rest of the day. Even in the office, a passing conversation about a birth in someone’s
family can leave him staring at his screen while the old scenes begin replaying again, vivid and
immediate.

He is not only frightened by the memories themselves but by the force of the emotions that arrive
with them. Fear comes first, but also horror, guilt, and shame—feelings so strong that he braces
against them as if they are dangerous in their own right. He is certain that if he lets himself
fully enter these moments, he will be overcome. That conviction is part of why he speaks in
fragments about his son’s illness and quickly turns to practical details instead. Facts feel safer
than the moments he can still smell and see.
