At 52, Lien Tran came to treatment as a married Vietnamese American woman who had spent most of her
adult life building a quiet, orderly world around books. She worked as a librarian, and she usually
described the library as a place where predictability felt comforting: shelves in sequence, voices
softened, routines repeated. That sense of safety had been badly shaken after surviving a natural
disaster. When she spoke about the event, she returned most often to the night it happened. She had
been in darkness, waking into a world that no longer felt stable, with an eerie stillness around her
that lasted just long enough to feel wrong before everything became terrifying. What stayed with her
was not only the danger itself but the particular atmosphere of it: the darkness, the sudden
silence, and the knowledge that ordinary life could break open without warning.

In the weeks after, Lien found that certain moments in daily life carried her back there instantly.
She could be shelving returned books late in the evening as the library quieted down, and when the
building dropped into sudden, complete silence she felt the full memory slam into her as if no time
had passed. She did not experience it as “thinking about what happened.” It felt to her as though
the event forced its way into the room and into her body without permission. If she woke during the
night and the bedroom was dark and still, she was no longer simply awake in her own home; she was
back in that night, frightened before she even fully understood why. She said there was no warning.
Something in the surroundings would shift, and the whole memory would be there at once.

When the memory came, what overtook her most was grief. She spoke of it as overwhelming, heavier
than fear alone. Remembering the disaster meant feeling again the human loss surrounding it, and
people connected to the event had become especially difficult for her to be around. Even hearing
about someone who had been there, or encountering others linked to that time, could make her chest
tighten as she braced for the memory to surge in. She had come to dread those encounters, not
because she wished to be uncaring, but because they opened something in her that felt impossible to
contain.

Over time, Lien developed ways of getting through the day that made sense to her, even as they
narrowed her life. When she felt the memory approaching, she would deliberately try to leave her
body or blank out. She described making herself go far away inside, as if numbing and disappearing
were the only way to survive the moment. She also began avoiding situations that resembled the
conditions of the disaster, especially darkness. At home she hesitated to move through unlit rooms
and preferred not to be the one to turn off lights at night. At work, if she was assigned an evening
closing task, she became tense as the building emptied and the light outside faded. She would linger
near occupied areas rather than walk alone into dim stacks or silent corridors.

Her watchfulness became a constant feature of her days. In public spaces and even in familiar ones,
Lien was superalert, scanning for signs that something might be wrong. She chose where to sit based
on how protected she felt. In staff meetings at the library, she would take a seat that seemed
safest to her rather than one near close colleagues, and she noticed exits, corners, and whatever
gave her the best sense of cover. She did not experience this as excessive; from her perspective, it
was the only responsible way to be after learning how suddenly danger could arrive.

She had also stopped exercising, although she did not frame it as a health choice. It had become one
more thing to avoid. Anything that left her feeling physically activated or exposed felt too close
to the internal state she dreaded, and so walks, classes, and routines she might once have attempted
quietly disappeared from her life. Her husband noticed she was restricting more and more of her
world. The changes were subtle at first—preferring lights on, avoiding certain conversations,
becoming particular about where she sat—but over months they accumulated into a household organized
around what felt least risky to her.

By the time she sought help, Lien was exhausted by how little warning she had before being pulled
back into the disaster and by how often silence and darkness still seemed to carry the event inside
them. What finally brought her in was the sense that even the library, a place she had always loved,
no longer felt reliably safe. She was frightened that the memory would keep intruding wherever she
went and that her life would continue shrinking around it. At the same time, she said she hoped for
something simple and enormous: to be able to wake in the dark, hear a quiet room, and remain in the
present.
