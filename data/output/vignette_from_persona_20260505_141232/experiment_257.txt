Amina is a 68-year-old African American woman who came to treatment after years of living carefully
around fear she rarely named directly. She has been married for many years, but the trauma she
described came from an earlier romantic relationship in which she was physically abused by a
partner. When she speaks about that period, the scenes do not come in a calm, ordered story. They
arrive suddenly, in pieces that feel immediate. She can begin to describe one night and then stop
herself abruptly, her face tightening, as if she has reached a point past which it is dangerous to
go. She says the memory “comes in by itself,” and when it does, it is not like remembering something
old. It is as if she is back there before she has any chance to prepare.

The incident she returns to most often happened at night after an argument had turned unpredictable.
What stands out in her account is not only his violence but the helpless exposure of it: being alone
with him, feeling emotionally open and unprotected, and becoming aware of the smell of earth from
outside as part of the scene. She remembers the ground smell vividly, the damp outdoor odor coming
in from outside the home and clinging to the moment. That sensory detail now carries unusual force.
Even now, if she catches the smell of soil after rain or walks past freshly turned dirt, the whole
scene can surge back without warning. She does not experience it as a thought she chooses to
revisit. It feels imposed on her, instantaneous and complete.

In the months and years afterward, Amina shaped her life around not feeling exposed. Sleeping became
one of the clearest places this showed up. She dreads sleeping alone and becomes especially
distressed if she is in an unfamiliar place overnight. At those times she feels too unprotected to
settle, as if sleep would mean dropping her guard when she cannot afford to. She lies awake
listening, staying alert long past the point of exhaustion. Even in her own home she does not fully
relax. She notices sounds, tracks where people are in the house, and remains watchful in a place she
wishes she could simply inhabit. She described having only a very small number of places where her
body feels convinced she is safe, and outside those spaces she feels herself tense and brace.

Her daily habits have gradually organized around reducing that sense of vulnerability. She keeps
herself busy almost constantly. Although she is unemployed, her days are full by design: chores
extended, errands multiplied, television or radio kept on, small tasks found and refound. If there
is quiet, she feels her mind opening in ways that do not feel safe. She works hard to keep her
attention occupied, shifting quickly to something else whenever she senses the memory approaching.
When fragments begin to surface, she pushes them away before they can fully form. She knows this
happens; she can feel herself stopping the recollection midstream. At the same time, she cannot stop
the moments when it breaks through anyway.

In relationships, especially when she senses tension or emotional exposure, Amina tends to go along
with what other people want. She describes this not as generosity but as a way to stay safe. It
feels less risky to accommodate, to smooth things over, to avoid anything that might bring conflict
close. Over time this has narrowed her life. She limits where she goes and feels most at ease only
in a few familiar places. Even at home, where she spends much of her time, she is often scanning
internally and externally rather than resting. Her husband sees how difficult nights are for her,
especially if he is away or if she has to sleep in a setting that is not her own. Those nights can
become nearly sleepless, with Amina remaining alert to the point of dawn.

What finally brought her to seek help was not one new event but the wearing accumulation of this way
of living. She had become tired of arranging every day around staying occupied and every night
around staying watchful. Recently, after encountering the smell of fresh soil outdoors, she was hit
by the memory so suddenly that it left her shaken for hours, and she realized how little control she
felt she had over when the past entered the present. She came to treatment wanting, above all, to
feel less trapped by these sudden returns of memory and less confined to the few places where she
can breathe easily. What frightens her most is the sense that vulnerability can appear anywhere,
without warning; what she hopes for is to be able to rest, especially at night, without feeling she
must remain on guard.
