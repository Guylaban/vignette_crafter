danger could break through ordinary moments without warning. He is married and has spent most of his
adult life working in architecture firms and on active project sites, moving between conference
rooms, client meetings, and partially finished buildings. The event he returns to began during what
should have been a routine site visit. He had gone to review progress on a commercial renovation and
was walking through an unfinished corridor with others from the project when violence erupted
nearby. What he remembers most immediately is not a clean narrative but fragments that still strike
him with the force of the original moment: a sudden scene of violence, the smell in the air, the
instant his stomach turned, and the sense that events were moving faster than he could grasp. When
he tries to explain exactly what happened and in what order, he falters. He will say that he knows
what happened, then stop, visibly frustrated, because he cannot find words that feel equal to it. He
can start in one place and then double back, unsure whether one image came before another. The
sequence feels broken to him, but the emotional force is intact.

In the weeks afterward, Daniel found that reminders of the event came at him in ordinary settings.
If he encountered violence on the news, in a shouted altercation on the street, or even in a tense
confrontation nearby, his body reacted before he could think. Certain smells were especially potent;
when one caught him unexpectedly, he would feel a wave of nausea and an immediate sense that he was
no longer fully in the present. At those moments he did not experience the memory as something
distant or finished. He felt as if the event were happening again right now, as though the room
around him had been replaced by that unfinished building and the same threat was once more unfolding
in front of him.

As time went on, Daniel organized his life around a constant sense of exposure. He became certain
that he was not safe unless he could sit with his back against a wall and see the whole room. In
restaurants he would pause at the entrance, scanning for exits before he could focus on the people
he was with. In client meetings he quietly chose the chair that allowed him to monitor the door. On
construction walkthroughs, where enclosed rooms, unfamiliar smells, and the unpredictability of an
active site were part of the workday, he was even more alert. Colleagues may have seen him as
meticulous or habitually observant, but internally he was tracking escape routes almost as soon as
he crossed a threshold. Sudden noises or movement startled him sharply, leaving him embarrassed and
irritated with himself, especially when junior staff noticed.

At home and with others, Daniel felt increasingly set apart. He had the sense that other people
moved through the world with an ease he no longer had access to. Even with his wife, he struggled to
convey what was wrong because when he tried to describe the event, the words vanished or the memory
became so immediate that he would stop talking. He had come to believe there was something about him
that made the event happen, a private defect he could not name but felt deeply. He also carried a
bleak conviction that he could not stop bad things from happening to him. These beliefs were not
abstract; they appeared in small moments, such as standing in a room and thinking he should have
seen danger sooner, should have known where to stand, should somehow have been different.

Over months and then years, these patterns narrowed his life. Business dinners became exercises in
seating strategy. New buildings, once a source of professional curiosity, were approached first in
terms of exits and lines of sight. Smells on job sites could abruptly turn his stomach and leave him
fighting the urge to leave. He felt cut off from coworkers and from his wife, who knew he was
struggling but could not reach the part of him that remained fixed at the scene.

What finally brought Daniel to seek help was not a single crisis but the accumulation of strain,
capped by a recent incident during a project meeting when a smell in the room and a burst of raised
voices sent him into that immediate, bodily reliving again. He was shaken by how quickly he was no
longer in the present and by how little he could explain afterward. Sitting in the intake session,
he chose the chair with the wall behind him and glanced at the door before answering questions. What
frightened him most was the feeling that this could keep happening anywhere, that he would remain
trapped in a world where ordinary rooms had to be mapped for escape and a smell could pull him back
without warning. What he hoped for, cautiously, was to be able to be in a room with other
people—including his wife—and feel that he was actually there.
