Kwame is a 68-year-old widowed African American man who has spent most of his adult life in dance
studios, rehearsal rooms, and theaters, trusting rhythm, timing, and the intelligence of his own
body. Before the crash, he was still working regularly, teaching and performing despite his age, and
he moved through the city with the ease of someone who had long relied on disciplined reflexes. The
motor vehicle collision that brought him to treatment happened while he was riding in a car on the
way home. What he can say about it comes in broken pieces. He remembers the sudden violence of it,
the sense that there was no time to act, a loud impact, metal and noise, and the unmistakable
conviction that he might die there. He recalls the disorientation afterward and the frightening
realization that his body, the thing he had always counted on most, did not feel available to him in
the way it once had. But when he tries to tell the story straight through, he cannot. He says there
are sections that are simply gone. He can find flashes, sensations, fragments, but not a complete
sequence, and when he deliberately tries to pull the memory closer, it often feels inaccessible, as
if the event resists being held in a coherent narrative.

In the months after the collision, this patchy quality to the memory became one of the most
disturbing parts of the experience for him. He had come to believe that not being able to account
clearly for what happened meant he could not fully trust his own judgment. He was certain that if he
could not even tell himself the story properly, then he could not rely on himself to do the right
thing under pressure. That doubt spread into ordinary life. Decisions that had once felt instinctive
began to feel loaded and uncertain. Just as painful was the change in how he thought about his body.
As a dancer, he had lived with the expectation that his body would answer when called upon; now he
felt he had learned that it could fail him without warning. He spoke about this not abstractly but
with bitterness and fear, as if the collision had exposed something intolerably fragile. He also
carried a strong sense that people and systems that should have protected him had not done so. He
returned often to the idea that those responsible for safety had let him down, and that this failure
left him exposed in a world that no longer felt governed by reliable rules.

His daily life narrowed. Going out began to feel unsafe, so he increasingly stayed home unless he
had no choice. The change was especially stark because his work had always depended on being
physically present with others. At the studio, when he did force himself to go in, he would choose a
place where his back could be against the wall and his eyes could take in the whole room. He could
not relax otherwise. If music started abruptly, if a speaker crackled, if someone dropped something
in rehearsal, his body snapped to attention before he could think. Unexpected or loud sounds
immediately put him on high alert. Even ordinary delays acquired a menacing meaning. If a student
was late arriving, if a ride took longer than expected, if someone did not answer when anticipated,
his mind jumped quickly to the sense that something bad must be happening. He did not describe this
as overreacting; to him it felt like the only sensible way to remain prepared.

His social world contracted along with his movements through the city. He stopped seeing people he
had socialized with before the collision. Old friends from dance circles would invite him out after
class or to performances, and he would decline or ignore the message. Seeing them belonged too much
to the life before the crash, and he had begun to avoid those encounters altogether. Over time this
left him more isolated, and it also altered his place in the dance community. He was less available
for informal mentoring, less present at rehearsals, less like the man people had known. Even in
therapy, he found himself pulling back. If a session came close to the collision or to anything they
had discussed previously about it, he noticed himself trying not to think about it once he left the
office. He would turn his attention elsewhere, refusing to revisit the material between
appointments.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was the accumulation of these changes: the shrinking of his
world, the loss of ease in the studio, and the unsettling feeling that he was living as if danger
could break in at any moment. He arrived wanting relief, but he was guarded about what that might
require. What frightened him most was not only the memory of the crash, incomplete as it was, but
the possibility that he might never again trust his body, his judgment, or the world around him in
the way he once had.
