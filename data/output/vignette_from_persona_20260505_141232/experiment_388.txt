the months when her child became critically ill. She is a Pacific Islander American woman, divorced,
and works as a network engineer, a job that usually suited her preference for order,
troubleshooting, and solving problems that had clear logic. But there had been nothing orderly about
watching her child deteriorate quickly enough that an ordinary medical visit turned into a crisis.

became crowded all at once, the abrupt change in staff voices, the sight of people she had just met
moving decisively around her child’s body, and the helplessness of standing by while procedures were
done that she knew were necessary and yet felt unbearable to witness. What stayed with her most was
the sense that danger could announce itself without warning, in an instant, and that once it
appeared she might not be able to protect the person she loved most.

she found that moments in ordinary life could feel charged in ways she could not explain to others.
Arguments were especially difficult. If voices sharpened in her vicinity, even over something minor,
her body responded before she had time to think. At home, if neighbors raised their voices through
the wall or if a disagreement began during a phone call, she felt a rush of heat, a pounding heart,
and a sense that something terrible was about to happen. She was not simply uncomfortable with
conflict; she experienced it as a signal that a situation was tipping out of control, the same way
things had in the hospital when calm conversation suddenly gave way to emergency action.

Certain people were also hard to be around, especially those connected to that period of her child’s
illness. Seeing related people—family members who had been present during the worst days, or others
closely associated in her mind with the hospitalization—could leave her tense and physically stirred
up long after a brief encounter. She found herself bracing the moment she recognized them. Even when
these interactions were benign, she felt caught by the same dread that had surrounded those earlier
days. She could not see them as just themselves; their presence seemed to pull the crisis near
again.

Medical examinations were another flashpoint. When she had to undergo a procedure herself, even a
routine one, she felt her body revolt. The sounds of equipment, the clinical smell, the sight of

of procedures done to her child. During one recent appointment she became so physically reactive
that she gripped the chair, her chest tightened, and she had to fight the urge to leave. She felt
embarrassed afterward, knowing rationally that she was not in the pediatric ward and that she was
not watching her child struggle, yet her body reacted as if danger were immediate.

At home, where she wishes she could finally let down, Mele instead stays on guard. She checks who is
moving through the house, notices shifts in sound, and finds herself reacting sharply to unexpected

stepping around a corner, she jolts hard, her whole system mobilizing before she can orient herself.

fully settled. She does not describe a generalized fear of everything; rather, she lives with a
readiness for abrupt threat, as though her attention is constantly angled toward the next emergency.

day in office spaces where coworkers move quickly between desks or appear behind her to ask about
outages or tickets. Sudden movement beside her workstation can make her flinch so noticeably that
colleagues pause mid-sentence. She tries to laugh it off, but she has become more careful about
where she sits and more tense in shared spaces. Outside work, she has narrowed contact with certain
people tied to the illness period because the encounters leave her unsettled for hours. She dreads
appointments that involve medical settings and will delay scheduling them until she cannot put them
off further.

every nearby movement. She wants relief from the constant sense of bracing. What frightens her most
