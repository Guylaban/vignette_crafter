David is a 63-year-old rabbi who came to treatment after years of trying to contain a life organized
around narrow pockets of safety. He was born in the United States, but the trauma he returns to
began when he was a boy in a family that had fled violence and displacement. In the sessions he does
not speak about it in broad historical terms first; he speaks in moments. He remembers being crowded
with family members in temporary housing after they had escaped, remembers adults speaking in urgent
voices he could not fully follow, and most of all remembers armed men during the flight and its
aftermath—how quickly an ordinary object could become a weapon in someone’s hand, how shouting could
turn a room electric, how a hand on his shoulder without warning did not feel like comfort but like
capture. In his memory, the danger is not abstract. It is the sight of something raised to strike,
the crack of a voice, the shock of being grabbed before he understood what was happening.

He describes those memories as old and far away, but his body does not behave as if they are over.
If someone touches him unexpectedly, even lightly, he jolts hard before he can think. When he sees a
weapon, or even an object that could be used as one, his attention narrows immediately. Loud
shouting, even when it is only an argument overheard in public, makes him feel as though the room
has changed shape around him. What unsettles him most is not only the reaction itself but the
meaning he has given it over the years. He has come to believe that his own body is an enemy of
sorts, launching him into alarm when he is standing in a sanctuary, a grocery store, or his own
home. He says with shame that he cannot rely on himself; if his body can betray him so quickly, then
he does not trust his own steadiness. He also carries a harsh comparison with an imagined stronger
person. Somebody else, he is certain, would have done something, would have stopped what happened,
would not have remained frightened in the way he did.

Over the years, these convictions settled into the structure of his days. At synagogue he is
attentive to where he sits and stands, though he tries not to draw attention to it. In meetings, at
communal meals, even during services, he chooses places that let him see the door and avoid being
hemmed in. He notices where people’s hands are, who is moving behind him, which voices are rising.
Congregants know him as thoughtful and pastoral, but they do not see how much effort goes into
tracking the room when concentration slips away from the prayer book or the person speaking across
from him. During pastoral visits or committee meetings, he sometimes realizes he has lost the thread
of conversation because part of him has been occupied with who entered the room, whether someone
came too close behind his chair, or whether an object on a desk caught his eye in the wrong way.

His world has gradually narrowed around a small number of places where he feels he can truly exhale.
Home is one of them, and his office at the synagogue can be another when the door is positioned the
way he wants it and interruptions are limited. Outside those spaces he is tense and vigilant. He
manages by redirecting himself whenever memories or sensations surge—burying himself in
administrative tasks, sermon preparation, reading, anything that can pull his mind sideways. He
knows this as distraction, though he experiences it simply as getting through the day.

The cost has been most visible in his relationship. He is partnered, but emotional closeness has
become difficult terrain. He avoids intimacy not because he does not care, but because closeness
leaves him feeling exposed and unguarded. Unprepared touch is especially hard; even affectionate
contact can produce a recoil he cannot hide quickly enough. He then turns his anger inward. Rather
than blaming the past, he tells himself he deserves to suffer because of what happened, as if
ongoing distress were a fitting sentence for not having been the kind of person who could have
prevented it.

He sought help now after several incidents in quick succession left him frightened by how small his
safe world had become. A raised voice during a heated exchange at the synagogue, a congregant
touching his arm from behind to get his attention, and the sight of a security officer’s weapon at a
community event each stayed with him long after the moment passed. He found himself unable to focus
on sermon writing and increasingly reluctant to attend events he once would have led with ease. What
finally brought him in was not only the exhaustion of living this way, but the fear that he would
continue retreating from his partner, his congregation, and his calling. He says he hopes for “some
peace with my own body,” and what he fears most is that safety will always mean making his life
smaller.
