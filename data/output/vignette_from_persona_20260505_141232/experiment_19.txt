Anika Patel was 45 when she sought treatment, a single South Asian American woman who had spent most
of her adult life working as a plumber. She was proud of being capable in a job that demanded
strength, balance, and calm under pressure. Before she became ill, much of her day was spent in
other people’s basements, utility rooms, and construction sites, carrying heavy equipment, crouching
in tight spaces, climbing stairs with tool bags, and dealing with urgent calls where something had
failed without warning. What stayed with her from the illness was precisely that sense of something
failing without warning, this time inside her own body.

She described the illness as life-threatening and remembered most vividly the frightening physical
sensations that had sent her to the hospital: a sudden spell of dizziness, the feeling that the room
was shifting under her, and a terrifying struggle to get enough air. She could still recall how
quickly confidence in her own body disappeared. One moment she had been trying to push through the
symptoms, telling herself she just needed to sit down for a minute, and the next she was acutely
aware of her breathing in a way she had never been before, frightened by how unreliable and strange
her body suddenly felt. In the hospital, she remembered the sharp antiseptic smell as vividly as
anything else. That smell seemed to seep into the whole experience: bright lights, people moving
quickly, the sense that her life had become precarious in an instant. What frightened her most was
not just that she might die, but that she had not seen it coming and had not been able to steady
herself.

In the months afterward, the sensations themselves became loaded. If she felt even slightly
lightheaded while bending under a sink or standing up too fast from a kneeling position, her
attention snapped immediately to her body. She would stop what she was doing and brace herself,
waiting to see if the dizziness would worsen. If she found herself breathing hard after carrying a
water heater part up a flight of stairs, she was no longer able to take it as ordinary exertion.
Shortness of breath felt too close to what had happened before. She had come to believe that her
body could not be trusted, that it might give way again without warning. Because of that, she was
certain she had to be especially careful all the time; in her mind, you never knew what could happen
next.

Her work life narrowed around these fears. Anika began turning down jobs that involved crawl spaces,
steep staircases, or heavy lifting, not because she had lost interest in the work, but because she
wanted to avoid anything that put her body under stress. She started choosing simpler service calls
when she could and taking more breaks than she used to, sometimes sitting in her van until the
feeling of being slightly off-balance passed. This had practical consequences. She earned less, and
a few regular contractors stopped calling her first because she was no longer taking the more
demanding jobs. She minded that deeply. Plumbing had been one place where she trusted herself.

Medical settings became difficult in a similarly concrete way. If she passed the hospital where she
had been treated, even from the street, the antiseptic smell she associated with it seemed to come
back to her, almost as if it were still in her nose. She avoided going inside hospitals whenever
possible and would not accompany relatives to appointments. Physical examinations felt especially
threatening. The idea of being back in that environment, with that smell and the possibility of
hearing something was wrong, was enough that she put off routine care. She had also delayed seeking
psychological help, in part because it meant entering another health-related setting and in part
because she did not expect much comfort from other people. She spoke matter-of-factly about handling
things herself. In her view, relying on others was unwise; when something serious happens, she felt,
you are the one left to deal with it.

By the time she came for treatment, these patterns had become woven into daily life. She was still
working, but more cautiously and with a shrinking sense of freedom. She monitored dizziness,
monitored her breathing, monitored how hard she was exerting herself. She avoided doctors even while
worrying about her health, and she kept much of this to herself. What finally brought her in was the
realization that her world had become organized around not provoking her body. She did not want to
lose the rest of her work or live in constant readiness for another collapse. At the same time, she
was afraid that help might confirm what she dreaded most: that her body was permanently unreliable
and that no one else could really be counted on to keep her safe.
