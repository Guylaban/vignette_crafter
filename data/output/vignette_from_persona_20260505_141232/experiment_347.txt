Rajesh Patel is a 61-year-old South Asian American man who has worked for years in the
administrative side of a nonprofit, the kind of job that usually suited his steady temperament and
preference for being useful behind the scenes. He came to treatment after a life-threatening motor
vehicle collision that he still described less as something that happened in the past than as
something that could take hold of him again without warning. Before the crash he had driven
regularly for work, visiting partner sites and attending meetings across the city. The collision
occurred while he was in his car on an ordinary workday, and what stayed with him most vividly was
not a coherent story from beginning to end but a sequence of sharply preserved images: the sudden
sight of another vehicle where it should not have been, the violent instant of impact, the ruined
cars afterward. Even now, when he tried to describe it, he spoke in pictures. He could see twisted
metal and shattered glass with an immediacy that made him pause mid-sentence and grip the arms of
his chair.

What made the event feel especially close in the present was how easily ordinary moments turned into
reminders. A particular texture associated with the crash could unsettle him immediately. He
struggled to explain it abstractly, but when his hand brushed against a similar surface, his body
reacted before he could think. It was the kind of sensory contact that made him feel abruptly
transported back into the vehicle, as if the collision were not over. He also found himself caught
off guard by the sight of cars that had been hit in accidents. Passing a damaged vehicle on the
roadside, or even seeing one in a parking lot with a crumpled side panel, could stop him internally.
He would feel his attention lock onto the image, his chest tighten, and for a few moments he was no
longer fully in the present. Conversations about accidents were similarly hard to bear. If coworkers
at the nonprofit mentioned a highway pileup or a fender-bender on the commute, Rajesh would go
quiet, his face tightening as he tried to stay composed while the same terror rose in him.

In the months after the collision, he came to dread these moments because they did not feel like
memories in the ordinary sense. Remembering the event brought back the same terror he had felt at
the time. He said that when the memory came, it did not arrive as a distant recollection but as if
the crash were happening right now. At times he experienced sudden flashbacks in which he felt or
acted as though he were back in the collision. His wife noticed that he could freeze or jerk in
place, staring ahead with a look she had not seen in him before. Rajesh himself found these episodes
humiliating and frightening. He had come to believe that his mind could betray him at any moment,
especially when something around him shifted suddenly or made a loud noise.

That sense of danger followed him into daily life. Unexpected or loud sounds put him on immediate
alert. In the office, a dropped file box, a slammed door, or a chair scraping sharply across the
floor could make him tense all over. If someone moved suddenly beside him in a hallway or leaned
unexpectedly into his cubicle, he reacted as if he needed to protect himself. He knew other people
did not understand why he startled so hard, and this only made him more guarded. At work he began
positioning himself carefully in meetings, preferring seats where he could monitor the room and
avoid being surprised by movement near him. He was embarrassed by how much attention he paid to
nearby motion and abrupt sound, but what frightened him most was the feeling that his body was
responding to immediate danger, not to a memory.

Over time he narrowed his world in small, practical ways. He avoided lingering when coworkers talked
about traffic collisions and tried to look away from damaged cars when he encountered them. His wife
saw him pull his hand back from certain materials at home with an expression she recognized as fear,
though he found it difficult to explain why. These habits accumulated. At the nonprofit he became
less available for activities that might expose him to upsetting reminders, and colleagues noticed
that he seemed more withdrawn and less at ease than he once had. At home he was often preoccupied,
wary of being startled, and ashamed that an ordinary workday accident still had this power over him.

He finally sought help after realizing that the terror remained unchanged whenever the memory was
stirred. He was not coming in to analyze the crash as a past event; he wanted relief from the
feeling that it was still able to happen again inside him. He said he was tired of living as though
any loud sound, sudden movement, conversation about accidents, or glimpse of a wrecked car might
pull him out of the present. What he feared most was that this would become the permanent shape of
his life. What he hoped for, more quietly, was to be able to go to work, sit with other people, and
move through the world without bracing for the collision to return.
