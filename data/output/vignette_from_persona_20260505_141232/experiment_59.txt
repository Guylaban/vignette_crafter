but fragments bound to sound and body: the rising scream of sirens, the crack of loud blasts, the
pounding in her own chest as she moved among the injured and waited for the next impact. Even in the
room with her therapist, if she got close to describing it, her face tightened and her whole body
seemed to brace. She would become flooded so quickly that she had to stop speaking, sometimes
pressing a hand to her sternum as if she were back inside that moment.

In the months after returning home, those sensory fragments began to intrude into daily life with
very little warning. Sirens were especially potent. If an ambulance passed while she was walking to
her car after a hospital shift, she did not simply feel startled; for a few moments she was no
longer fully in the present. Her body reacted as if the combat scene were happening again, and she
would find herself turning sharply, scanning for where to move, where the threat was coming from,
before realizing she was in the employee parking lot. Loud noises had a similar effect. A metal tray
dropped at the nurses’ station or a sudden bang in a hallway could make her go rigid and then
instantly furious, with a burst of anger that felt as immediate as fear. What frightened her
boyfriend was how quickly she could snap at him afterward over something minor, while to Mele it
felt as though her whole system had already been pushed past its limit.

Her own body had also become a trigger. If she noticed her heart racing—while climbing stairs at
work, hurrying between patient rooms, or even laughing hard—she was pulled toward the same sense of
emergency she had felt during combat. Because of that, she gradually stopped exercising. Before, she
had used movement to manage stress, but now anything that made her pulse pound felt dangerous in a
way she could not talk herself out of. She began taking elevators instead of stairs at the hospital
and avoided workouts entirely. She also started pulling back from situations that might make her
feel light or hopeful. If friends invited her out or her boyfriend suggested doing something fun
together, she often found a reason not to go. It was hard to explain, but feeling good seemed
precarious, almost wrong, as if letting herself relax would leave her exposed.

efficiently through a busy environment, but Mele was continuously tracking doors, noises, footsteps,
and the position of other people in the room. She checked hallways before entering, noticed every
alarm and overhead sound, and kept orienting herself to exits. Colleagues experienced her at times
as tense and irritable. If someone approached her from behind or if the unit suddenly became loud,
she could turn with a sharpness that seemed disproportionate to the situation. She knew this was
happening and hated it, but she was certain she needed to keep scanning because not doing so felt
unsafe.

would start to recall a detail and then feel overwhelmed so completely that she had to shut the
memory down. At home this meant going silent mid-conversation or leaving the room abruptly. With her
boyfriend, the accumulation had been painful: fewer outings, more edge in her voice, more time spent
watching windows and listening for sounds outside. He wanted to comfort her, but she often seemed
unreachable once she was pulled into those moments.

followed by such intense anger and disorientation that she had to step away from patient care. She
said she was afraid of what would happen if she could not control those reactions, afraid of being
