light-filled studio attached to her home. Before the disaster, she described her life as small but

lonely. The event that divides her life into before and after happened during a man-made disaster in
a public building where she had gone for an arts event. What she remembers is not a flowing story so
much as a series of still images that return with brutal clarity. She can see the sharp angle of a
doorway, the overturned drink cart, the faces around her breaking into panic, and the sheen of
spilled liquor on the floor. In her mind these scenes come back “like photographs,” fixed and
bright, without softening.

past and someone fell against her. What remains most vivid is the position of her body: twisted on
one hip, shoulder jammed, cheek turned sideways as she tried to make herself small. When the memory
comes, her body seems to find that same posture again before she is even fully aware of what she is
remembering. She stiffens, curls inward, and for a few seconds feels as if she is still there on the
floor. She also carries a sharp, punishing guilt about what happened. She remembers seeing another
person nearby in distress and has come to revisit that instant with the conviction that she should
have done something more, should have moved differently, should not have survived unchanged when
others were harmed. The guilt arrives together with the images, immediate and uninvited.

In the months afterward, her world grew narrow. She learned quickly that certain moments could undo
her. The smell of alcohol is one of the strongest. If someone opens a bottle of wine at a
restaurant, or if a passerby on the sidewalk carries the stale scent of liquor, she is no longer
simply in the present; the smell places her back in that building with the spilled drinks and panic.
Physical touch has become almost intolerable, especially when unexpected. A hand on her arm, even
meant kindly, can make her jerk away with surprising force. Crying is another trigger she did not
expect. If she hears someone sobbing, or feels herself beginning to cry, it seems to open the whole
event at once. She has come to fear tears for that reason and works hard to shut them down.

settle, then waking again at the slightest sound. The lack of sleep leaves her brittle, and she has

workers through the door, and once angrily shoved away the hand of a friend who touched her shoulder
in concern. These moments embarrass her, but in the instant they feel necessary, as if she must
react before something worse happens.

entirely if there is any chance of reports about disasters or violence. In her studio she keeps
herself occupied constantly—cleaning brushes, rearranging supplies, putting on podcasts she barely

emotionally rich for her, has become more mechanical. She says she no longer trusts her feelings and
has come to believe she will never feel normal emotions again. More than once she has said that what
happened destroyed her understanding of how the world works; she no longer experiences public
