Nadine is a 21-year-old Middle Eastern American woman who came to treatment several months after a
military trauma that she still describes in concrete, sensory fragments rather than as a story she
can easily tell straight through. She had enlisted young and had been proud of being able to handle
technical work under pressure. During her service, one nighttime incident stayed with her in a way
that made the dark itself feel altered afterward. She recalls being outside after dark in a high-
alert setting when the atmosphere shifted abruptly from routine vigilance to immediate danger. What
remains most vivid to her is the combination of darkness, the strain in her body as she tried to
move quickly and stay prepared, and the sense that the night concealed threat in every direction.
Since then, she has felt that once the sun goes down, safety becomes uncertain and too much can
happen before she has time to react.

In the period after the trauma, Nadine returned to civilian life and resumed work as a database
administrator, a role that suited her in some ways because of its order and predictability. She
spends much of the day managing systems, checking for errors, and making sure data are where they
are supposed to be. Even so, the structure of the job has not quieted her sense that things can
become dangerous very quickly. She has become especially careful about anything that feels like
physical strain. If a task leaves her winded, raises her heart rate, or creates the kind of bodily
tension she associates with being on alert, she pulls back from it. She avoids workouts that used to
seem ordinary, takes stairs only if she feels she has no alternative, and is quick to stop any
activity that makes her body feel taxed. To her, this is not simple preference. She has come to see
bodily stress as a warning sign, too close to the state she was in that night, and she does not
trust what might happen if she lets herself get there again.

Her apartment has gradually become the place where this need for control is most visible. Nadine
cleans and organizes repeatedly, often far beyond what is necessary. She wipes down counters that
are already clean, straightens objects until they line up exactly, and reorders drawers and shelves
after she has already finished. When she describes this, she does not frame it as excessive so much
as necessary. If everything is in place, if surfaces are clean and nothing is out of order, she can
breathe a little easier. Disorder unsettles her. A cup left out, shoes by the door, papers not
stacked properly can create a sharp internal agitation, as though small signs of disarray mean she
is no longer fully on top of things. After work she may spend long stretches cleaning instead of
resting, and if she tries to stop before it feels complete, she becomes tense and distracted,
circling back to the task until the room looks exactly right.

Night has become the clearest line in her life. Nadine avoids going out after dark whenever she can.
She schedules errands for the morning or early afternoon, declines invitations that would keep her
out in the evening, and watches the clock in winter when daylight fades early. At work, this means
she tries to leave before sunset and becomes uneasy if a database problem threatens to keep her in
the office late. On one recent evening, a systems issue required the team to stay past normal hours.
As the windows darkened, she found it increasingly hard to concentrate on the troubleshooting in
front of her. She kept thinking about getting to her car in the dark, about open parking lots and
the vulnerability of being outside at night. She left as soon as she could, embarrassed by how
urgent it felt, then spent part of the night cleaning her apartment kitchen and reorganizing a
hallway cabinet until she felt settled enough to sleep.

Over the weeks and months, these habits have narrowed her life. She is single and has grown more
isolated without fully intending to. Friends have stopped asking her out for evening meals as often
because she usually says no or leaves early. Colleagues know her as competent and meticulous, but
some have noticed she is unwilling to stay late or join after-work gatherings. Nadine is aware that
her world has become smaller and more controlled, yet she also feels certain there is a reason for
each rule she has made. What frightens her most is not simply remembering the military incident, but
the idea of being caught off guard again—outside at night, physically strained, without any way to
impose order.

She sought help after realizing how much effort it was taking to organize her life around these
limits. A recent stretch of late-day work demands and repeated comments from others that she seemed
constantly on edge forced her to see that she was structuring nearly every day around avoiding
darkness, avoiding bodily strain, and restoring order whenever she felt unsettled. She arrived to
treatment wanting relief, though cautiously. She says she hopes to feel “normal” enough to go
through a day without planning around sunset or cleaning to calm herself, but she is afraid that if
she lets go of any of these precautions, she will be exposed again to the same kind of danger she
faced before.
