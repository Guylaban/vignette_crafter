cornered and degraded. He remembers being close enough to register his partner’s body odor and
sweat, remembers the clothes worn that day vividly enough that he later stopped wearing similar ones
himself, and remembers pain in specific parts of his body that still returns with the memory. The
fact that this happened inside a marriage, in a place that should have been safe, has remained
especially hard for him to absorb.

In the immediate aftermath, Ravi did what he had long done as a medic and as a soldier: he kept
moving, minimized, and tried to contain it. But the experience did not settle into the past. When it
comes back, it does not feel like recollection. He says there are moments when he is no longer
merely remembering but is back in that room, with the same bodily alarm and the same sense of being
trapped. At those times he feels pain in the same areas that were hurt during the assault, as if his
body is repeating the event even when his mind is trying not to. The smell of sweat on another
person, especially in enclosed spaces, can bring this on abruptly. So can seeing people connected to
his partner or the relationship. Even the presence of police has become charged. He does not
describe this abstractly; he describes scanning parking lots, stiffening when a police cruiser is
nearby, and feeling his chest tighten at the sight of uniforms or official authority, as if exposure
to them means being pulled into something dangerous and uncontrollable again. Underneath this is a
bitter conviction that the people responsible have faced no consequences.

These reactions have narrowed his life. He has begun avoiding clothes that resemble what he wore
during the assault, passing over certain shirts without fully explaining why even to himself at
first. He no longer exercises, despite having once depended on physical training as part of military
life. The combination of exertion, sweat, and heightened bodily sensation feels intolerable to him
now. He also avoids people. At work and outside it, he keeps interactions brief and functional,

door, a piece of equipment dropping, or a sudden voice nearby can cause an immediate startle
response that leaves him embarrassed and angry with himself. He has come to feel safe only in a very
limited number of places, and even there he struggles to settle. At night he has difficulty falling
asleep because he feels he must stay alert. Sleep seems to require surrender, and surrender feels
unsafe.

Over time, Ravi’s interpretation of himself has grown harsh and despairing. He has come to believe
there is something wrong with him as a person, though he struggles to say exactly what. The fact

than injury. He speaks with particular pain about meaning draining out of his life. Work had once

through the motions of duty, but he says nothing feels meaningful anymore.

longer ignore: increasing isolation, persistent sleeplessness, and the sense that his world had
contracted to a few “safe” places and rigid routines. He began to recognize that he was arranging

work—while feeling constantly on edge anyway. Coming to treatment, he is ashamed, guarded, and

same time, he is hoping for some relief from the immediacy of the memories and from the conviction
that his life has lost its meaning. What frightens him most is the possibility that this is now
permanent: that he will remain alert, hemmed in, and inwardly marked by something he still cannot
