David is a 47-year-old North American man who has worked for years as a city bus driver, a job that
once suited him for its routine and the steady, familiar rhythm of moving through the same
neighborhoods each day. He is single and lives alone. When he speaks about the event that brought
him into treatment, he does so haltingly, and even in the first sessions he often covers his face
with both hands as if to block something out. Talking about it at all is difficult for him because
the memory does not stay in the past; as soon as he begins to describe it, he feels it come sharply
to mind again.

The traumatic event involved police. On the day it happened, David had been on duty and was pulled
into a police encounter that left him feeling exposed, powerless, and unsafe. What stands out in the
way he recounts it is not a detached sequence of facts but the force with which certain moments
still hit him when they appear in his mind without warning. The sight of officers near his route, a
patrol car at an intersection, or even the suggestion that he should explain what happened to
someone can bring the whole thing back suddenly. He describes these moments as if the memory
ambushes him. He can be in the driver’s seat, focused on traffic, and then catch sight of police
activity ahead and feel his mind pulled back before he has any chance to prepare himself.

In the weeks after the incident, David noticed himself changing in ways that felt sensible to him at
the time. He had come to believe that the world was dangerous in a more global, permanent way than
he had understood before. He was increasingly certain that people could not be trusted, and this was
not limited to police. The feeling spread outward. He found himself pulling away from other people
generally, declining invitations, keeping conversations short, and spending more time alone in his
apartment. He also felt, in his own words, cut off from the sense of community he used to have.
Driving the bus had once made him feel part of the city’s daily life; over time he experienced
himself as separate from it, watching people get on and off while feeling no real connection to
them.

At work, his symptoms became woven into ordinary parts of the day. Police presence was especially
difficult because he encountered it regularly on city streets. If he saw officers near a stop or
standing beside a cruiser, he would feel his body tense and his attention narrow. On some days the
effort to keep driving safely while these reactions were happening left him struggling to
concentrate. He noticed he was more irritable with passengers and other drivers than he had been
before. Small frustrations could set him off into angry outbursts, and he was troubled by how
quickly his temper now came forward. He did not describe this as losing control without reason; from
his perspective he was on guard in a world that had shown itself to be unsafe, and he felt
constantly braced for something to go wrong.

Outside work, David developed habits meant to get him through the day and the night. When memories
came up suddenly, he would cover his face with his hands, as if shutting out the image might shut
down the surge that came with it. In his apartment he often turned on loud music or the television,
using sound to drown out his thoughts. Silence felt risky. Quiet left too much room for the event to
come back to mind. He also became preoccupied with checking doors and windows. He would lock them,
then return to check again, and sometimes again after that, unable to settle until he had reassured
himself. Even then the relief did not last long.

These changes narrowed his life over months. Avoiding people meant he had fewer conversations and
less support, and his sense of mistrust deepened into a daily stance toward the world. His work
environment, once familiar, now contained repeated reminders of what had happened, especially
whenever police were visible along his route. He began to feel that nowhere was fully safe and no
one was fully reliable. By the time he sought help, he was exhausted by how often the memory came
back and by the effort of managing himself around it. What finally brought him in was the
recognition that his life had become organized around getting through the next trigger—keeping his
head down, shutting people out, turning the volume up, checking the locks again. What frightens him
most is the sense that this is now how the world is: dangerous, untrustworthy, and stripped of the
connection he once felt to other people. What he hopes for, though he says it cautiously, is to be
able to drive his route, go home, and live through an ordinary day without feeling ambushed by the
past.
