Tane is a 56-year-old Māori American detective who came to treatment after years of telling himself
that whatever happened in childhood was long over and should have stayed there. He grew up with a
caregiver whose physical abuse was unpredictable and frighteningly intimate. What remains clearest
to him is not a neat story he can tell from beginning to end, but moments that still feel as if they
are happening now: the sudden presence of an adult body too close to his, the sense of being
trapped, and a burnt smell in the air that seemed to fuse with fear. When he tries to explain the
abuse out loud, he often starts firmly and then loses the thread, stopping mid-sentence with his jaw
tight and his hands clenched, as if the memory fragments faster than he can organize them. What
comes through most strongly then is rage. He says he can go from trying to describe one scene to
feeling a violent surge of anger in his body before he has even found the words.

In the years afterward, Tane built a life around competence and control. He became a detective, a
role that fit the part of him that believed danger was always near and that survival depended on
noticing what other people missed. Even now, he is superalert in ways colleagues experience as
thoroughness and intensity. He scans rooms automatically, tracks exits, notices movements in
peripheral vision, and has difficulty settling at night because some part of him insists he needs to
stay alert. Falling asleep feels like letting his guard down. He lies in bed listening for sounds in
the house and checking the air, his body keyed up long after he wants rest.

The reminders that catch him are often humiliating in their speed. A burnt smell—toast left too long
in a break-room toaster, scorched coffee, something singed from a street vendor’s grill—can bring
back the full memory without warning. His body reacts before he can think: chest tight, stomach
dropping, skin hot, muscles braced as if impact is imminent. He has also noticed that certain people
unsettle him immediately when they resemble the victims he encounters in his work. On cases
involving abuse, if someone’s face, size, posture, or expression echoes those victims in just the
wrong way, he feels himself pulled out of the present and flooded with the old memory. Because he
works in investigations, these moments are not rare abstractions; they happen in interview rooms, on
scene visits, in hospital corridors, in photographs spread across a desk.

Over time he has narrowed his life in practical ways that make sense to him. He avoids other people
when he can, not because he thinks of himself as shy, but because contact feels effortful and
unpredictable. Since his divorce, that withdrawal has become more pronounced. Outside work he keeps
to himself, declines invitations, and leaves social situations early. He also avoids wearing clothes
that resemble what he associates with the abuse. He does this with little ceremony—setting shirts
back on the rack, discarding items, choosing alternatives quickly—but with a private certainty that
certain fabrics or styles are simply not tolerable on his body.

When memories come, he tries to detach from them, to go numb and distant until the wave passes. He
describes it as stepping away internally, as if he can watch from somewhere else and not fully be
there. Yet this strategy has left him feeling cut off even in moments that should be good. One of
the more confusing features for him is that positive emotion itself can become unsettling. If he
begins to feel warmth, pride, closeness, or enjoyment, he often stiffens, suspicious of it, as
though feeling good makes him vulnerable or means he has missed something dangerous. He does not
present this as a theory so much as a lived alarm: when things seem safe, he trusts himself less.

Tane has come to believe that the abuse ruined his understanding of how the world works. He says,
with a flat conviction, that whatever ideas he once had about adults protecting children or life
having any basic fairness were shattered long ago. He is harshest with himself. The persistence of
his reactions has become proof, in his mind, that he is a lousy coper. He compares himself
unfavorably to an imagined person who would have mastered this by now, and he carries a deeper,
older conclusion that the abuse happened because of the sort of person he is. He knows how severe
that sounds when said aloud, but he does not say it for effect; it is one of the beliefs that has
sat with him the longest.

He finally sought help after a recent cluster of reminders at work and at home left him feeling less
able to contain things in the ways he usually does. He noticed he was sleeping even less, avoiding
more people, and struggling to speak coherently when he tried to tell any part of his story. What
frightened him most was not only the force of the reactions, but the possibility that this was
simply his character laid bare. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not to become a different man, but
to be able to sleep, to stay present when reminders hit, and to feel something good without
immediately going on guard.
