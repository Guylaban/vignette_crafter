Nadia is a 43-year-old married Palestinian American pharmacist who came to treatment after years of
keeping her distress tightly contained behind a competent, quiet exterior. When she speaks about her
childhood, she does so in fragments at first, then with sudden flashes of clarity that seem to
overtake the room. She grew up with a caregiver whose drinking shaped the atmosphere of the home.
The smell of alcohol was never neutral for her; it meant unpredictability, a change in voice, the
scrape of a chair, orders barked across the room, and the immediate sense that she had to get
herself ready. She remembers being told what to do in a sharp, commanding tone and feeling her body
go rigid even before she understood what would happen next. The physical abuse itself returns to her
not as a distant past event but with a sharpness she finds hard to describe. She says those moments
feel “more real than regular memories,” as if they are happening in front of her rather than being
remembered. At other times she can recount pieces of what happened in a flat, detached way that
frightens her for a different reason, as though it happened to some other girl and not to her.

As an adult, Nadia built a life that from the outside looks stable. She is married, works long
shifts as a pharmacist, and is known at work as careful and dependable. But the environment of the
pharmacy confronts her in ways that make sense only when her history is understood. She is acutely
unsettled by being in the workplace itself, especially when the pace is fast and someone is speaking
to her from behind or issuing instructions quickly. A supervising pharmacist or store manager giving
routine directions can provoke an immediate surge in her body, and she reacts as if something
dangerous is about to happen even when she knows, on another level, that nothing is wrong. If a
customer comes to the counter with alcohol on their breath, the smell can seize her attention
instantly. In those moments she loses track of what she was counting or entering into the system,
and her concentration narrows around scanning for danger. She has learned to step away, ask a
technician to take over, or busy herself in the back when possible. She also avoids interacting with
authority figures whenever she can, especially anyone in uniform or anyone whose role gives them
obvious power. At work this means delaying conversations with supervisors, becoming overly
deferential, or rearranging tasks to avoid direct contact, which has begun to affect how confident
others see her.

Over the years, Nadia’s sleep has become another battleground. At night she finds it hard to let
herself drift off because some part of her feels she needs to stay alert. She checks sounds in the
house, listens for movement, and remains tense long after her husband has fallen asleep. She knows
she is exhausted, yet the idea of fully relaxing feels unsafe. During the day this leaves her
mentally foggy, and when memories break through they can flood her so forcefully that she has to
stop whatever she is doing. In session she has described starting to recall a particular scene and
then feeling overtaken so completely by emotion that she cannot continue speaking. On other
occasions she goes abruptly blank and numb, describing the same events without any feeling at all.

Nadia has come to organize her life around constriction. She avoids situations that might make her
feel happy or hopeful because those states feel exposed and almost foolish, as if letting herself
want anything good invites harm. Her husband has tried to plan dinners out or small weekends away,
and she often finds a practical reason not to go. She does not describe pleasure as comforting
anymore; she describes it as something she cannot trust. Beneath this is a bleak set of beliefs she
carries with conviction. She has come to believe that bad things happen and she cannot stop them,
that she has no real power when it matters, and that life itself has lost its meaning. Most
painfully, she speaks about having failed in her most important role in life, though she struggles
to elaborate at first, saying only that she feels she has failed at being the kind of person,
partner, and family member she should have been.

There are also moments she is ashamed to admit. When thoughts of the abuse press in and she cannot
quiet them, she sometimes hurts herself physically to interrupt them. She does not describe this as
wanting to die; she describes it as wanting the memories to stop, or wanting something she can
control for a few seconds. What finally brought her to seek help was a recent incident at the
pharmacy when a supervisor gave her a series of brisk instructions while a customer nearby smelled
strongly of alcohol. Nadia froze, made an error in a routine task, and then had to retreat to the
restroom, shaken and humiliated. Afterward she sat in her car and thought, with a kind of exhausted
certainty, that nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore and that she could not keep living in
constant readiness. Even so, what frightens her most about treatment is the possibility of being
overwhelmed by what she remembers. What she hopes for, cautiously, is not happiness exactly, but the
chance to stop feeling as though danger is always one breath away.
