Daniel is a 37-year-old divorced White American man who works as a database administrator for a
regional insurance company. He described the worst period of his life as the months when a serious
illness turned ordinary bodily sensations into signs of possible death. During the acute phase, what
he remembers most clearly is struggling for breath. He can still picture the hospital room with
painful sharpness: the plastic tubing, the IV pole beside the bed, the clipped voices of staff
moving quickly around him, the moment a nurse told him to stay still while another needle was
prepared. He remembered the fear less as a thought than as a bodily certainty that he might not get
enough air in time. The medical equipment around him, especially needles and drips, became bound up
with that sense of helpless exposure. Even now, when he talks about that period, he returns to the
image of lying there and monitoring every inhale, waiting to see whether his chest would loosen or
tighten.

After he was discharged, Daniel expected the fear to ease once he was back in his apartment and had
returned to work. Instead, reminders of the illness kept breaking into daily life. If he noticed
even mild breathlessness climbing stairs to the office or hurrying across the parking lot, his body
reacted as though the medical crisis were happening again. He would go still, shoulders tightening,
attention narrowing to his chest and throat. What frightened him most was not just the sensation
itself but the rush of fear and dread that came with it. A passing shortness of breath could bring
on an intense wave of horror, and he found himself bracing immediately, as if danger had already
arrived. He described these moments as his body “going on red alert” before he had time to think.

Because so much of the illness had unfolded around examinations and procedures, medical settings
became especially hard for him to tolerate. At first he postponed routine follow-up visits, telling
himself he was too busy with work or that the doctors were only being overly cautious. Over time the
avoidance became more organized. He would drive a longer route to avoid passing the hospital. If he
heard an ambulance nearby, he felt a jolt through his body and would grip the steering wheel until
it passed. Seeing a needle at a pharmacy clinic, an IV stand on television, or even a medical exam
room door left open in a primary care office could make his heart pound and his breathing turn
shallow. He came to believe that once he stepped back into a medical setting, he would be flooded by
the same panic and physical alarm he had felt during the illness.

Daniel also began trying to manage his body in ways that made him feel less vulnerable. He became
strict and controlling about food, not out of concern about weight, but because limiting and
carefully regulating what he ate gave him a sense that he could keep his body stable. Meals became
rule-bound and narrow. If coworkers invited him out for lunch, he often declined, preferring the
safety of food he had planned himself. He also stopped exercising. Before the illness he had gone on
occasional runs, but now anything that increased his breathing felt dangerous. The sensation of
being winded was too close to what he had experienced when he was sick. So he avoided exertion, took
elevators instead of stairs at work, and stayed seated for long stretches at his desk, telling
himself it was safer not to provoke his body.

These patterns have affected his life in accumulating ways. As a database administrator, his work is
sedentary and detail-focused, which has made it easier for him to hide how constricted his world has
become. Still, his avoidance shows up there too. When the company scheduled an annual employee
health screening, Daniel spent days feeling tense and irritable and then found a reason to miss the
appointment. He felt ashamed that something as ordinary as a brief exam could unsettle him so much,
yet he was certain that being in a room with medical instruments would make him lose control. His
divorce predated the illness, but he now feels the isolation more sharply; there is no one at home
to witness how much effort it takes to keep himself calm, and he has become increasingly private
about what he is dealing with.

By the time he sought help, Daniel was living in a state of constant readiness, as though his body
might betray him at any moment. He reported strong surges of fear, anger, and shame about how
reactive he had become, especially when a simple sensation like breathlessness could undo his whole
day. What finally brought him in was realizing he was overdue for medical follow-up and could not
make himself schedule it. He said he was afraid that if he faced another examination, the room, the
equipment, and the sensation of not getting enough air would hit him all at once. At the same time,
he said he was tired of organizing his life around escape and hoped, cautiously, that treatment
might help him be in his own body without feeling as if danger were always one breath away.
