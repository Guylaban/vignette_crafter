Tane is a 24-year-old pharmacist who came to treatment months after the death of someone he loved,
carrying a sense that his life had split into a before and an after that he could no longer join
together. He is a single young man from an Oceanian family living in the United States, and when he
speaks about the event he does so in fragments, as if the worst parts refuse to line up in order. He
can say where he was, that there had been drinking, that other people were acting badly, and that
someone he loved was harmed, but when he tries to make it into a clear story he loses the thread. At
times he goes quiet and looks almost detached from what he is saying, describing it later as
remembering it “like it happened to somebody else.” At other moments, the same memory floods him
with guilt so sharply that he cannot stay with it for long. He has come to believe that he should
have done something differently, noticed something earlier, prevented what happened somehow, even
though when he tries to explain exactly how, his account breaks apart.

What remains vivid are not tidy facts but sharp impressions that seize him in the present. The smell
of alcohol is especially potent. In the pharmacy where he works, he braces himself when customers
approach the counter smelling of liquor, and even the scent of alcohol-based products can jolt him
before he has had time to think. He describes his body as reacting first: his chest tightens, his
muscles go rigid, and he feels suddenly prepared for danger even when he knows he is standing under
fluorescent lights at work, filling routine prescriptions. He is embarrassed by how often this
happens. A customer speaking too loudly, acting entitled, or ignoring basic rules can provoke the
same surge. What disturbs him is not only the fear but the moral charge of it. Seeing people
misbehave, especially in ways that feel selfish or reckless, stirs something immediate and physical
in him. He is certain in those moments that people can do terrible damage without caring, and his
body seems to respond as if that damage is about to happen again.

Funerals and cemeteries have become unbearable terrain. In the period after the death, he attended
grief-related gatherings because he felt he should, but each one left him shaken. The sight of
floral arrangements, rows of chairs, subdued clothing, and mourners greeting one another was enough
to make him feel unreal and trapped at the same time. Eventually he began finding reasons not to go.
When relatives or family friends mentioned memorial services, he told them his work schedule at the
pharmacy was too demanding. He has avoided cemeteries altogether. This has narrowed his world in
ways other people have noticed. Family members have read his absences as distance or indifference,
and Tane has not been able to explain that what stops him is not lack of love but the fear of what
happens inside him when grief is publicly marked.

Over time he has felt less and less like the person he was before. He says he used to be happy and
easy with people, but now he moves through days with a constant sense of misery and strain. Even on
ordinary shifts he feels keyed up, his body tense and ready, as though danger might emerge from
nowhere. He knows that many of these moments are false alarms, but that knowledge arrives after the
reaction, not before. He has started to think of his life as destroyed by what happened, not in the
dramatic sense of everything collapsing at once, but in the slow, deadened sense that nothing feels
meaningful anymore. The work he trained for feels mechanical. He counts tablets, counsels patients,
answers questions, and goes home, all while feeling cut off from the reasons any of it should
matter.

What finally brought him to seek help was not a single crisis but the accumulation of these moments
until they seemed to define every week. Recently, during a shift, a customer who smelled strongly of
alcohol became argumentative at the counter, and Tane felt his body surge into alarm so fast that he
had to step away and let a coworker take over. Afterward he was shaken by how little it took and by
how completely it overtook him. He began to fear that this was how his life would remain: tense,
joyless, organized around avoiding reminders and dreading the next reaction. At the same time, some
part of him still hopes that if he can finally face what happened without going numb or collapsing
into guilt, life might recover some meaning, and he might feel like himself again.
