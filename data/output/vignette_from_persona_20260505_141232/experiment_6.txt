Marisol is a 21-year-old animator who came to treatment after years of trying to keep her life
narrowed to whatever felt most controllable. She grew up in a Spanish-speaking Latin American family
in the United States, and when she describes what happened in childhood, she does so in sharp
fragments rather than a full story. The scene she returns to most often is an evening in the family
apartment when she witnessed a violent fight at home. She remembers an adult man in the room, the
sudden change in his body, the violence in his arm as he hurled an object across the kitchen, and
the split second before it struck the wall. She remembers the sound first as much as the sight: the
crack of something hard hitting, raised voices, the scrape of movement, the sense that anything in
the room could become dangerous without warning. When the memory comes close, it does not feel
distant or softened by time. It feels more real than ordinary memory, as if she is back inside the
room rather than remembering it from outside. Sometimes, when she gets close to it, she can smell
the apartment as it was then, and that smell arrives so suddenly that she feels caught off guard by
her own mind. Just as often, she cuts the memory off before it fully unfolds, pushing it away at the
point where the scene feels about to become unbearable.

As she got older, Marisol came to believe that bad things can happen fast and that she has very
little power to stop them. What troubles her most is not only what she witnessed, but the sense that
the people responsible never truly faced consequences. She speaks about that with flat anger: the
adults moved on, excuses were made, and no one repaired what had happened inside her home. Over time
she grew certain that she could not deal with even minor upsets the way other people seemed to, and
that if things began to go wrong, they would keep going wrong while she stood there unable to stop
them.

These beliefs show up in ordinary places. In her animation program and freelance work, she spends
long hours at a desk in shared studio spaces, surrounded by people shifting in their chairs,
gesturing over screens, dropping supplies, and moving quickly behind one another. If someone nearby
makes an abrupt movement, she reacts instantly, her body jerking before she has time to think. If
something is tossed across a room, even casually, she feels a rush of danger that seems out of
proportion to what everyone else is noticing. Certain sound effects, reference audio, or even the
sharp noise of an object hitting a surface can set her on edge for the rest of the day. Visual
reminders are difficult too. When coursework or online feeds include photos that resemble scenes of
domestic conflict, or images that in some way connect her to the person who harmed her family, she
looks away or closes the tab immediately. She avoids anything that reminds her of him, not because
she thinks it is irrational, but because in the moment it feels like the safest way to keep herself
from being pulled back into that childhood room.

Marisol lives tightly now. She is often watchful, scanning who is around her and how they are
moving. That constant readiness spills into irritability. She snaps more quickly than she wants to,
especially when she is startled or feels cornered, and she has frightened herself with how sharp her
anger can sound. She also tries to create control where she can. Food has become one of those
places. She restricts her eating strictly, describing it less as a wish to change her body than as
one domain where rules can be exact and nothing unexpected happens. Planning is another place where
she pulls back. She avoids making plans for the future, even positive ones, because imagining what
comes next leaves her feeling exposed rather than hopeful. In her mind, planning means trusting that
life will hold steady, and she does not trust that.

The consequences have begun to accumulate. Her work depends on collaboration, but she stays guarded
in studio settings and keeps interactions brief. Friends have stopped asking her to commit to things
far in advance because she so often says she is not sure. She feels ashamed of how brittle her life
has become, yet also convinced that loosening her grip would be dangerous. She finally sought help
after realizing that the same patterns were following her into every part of adulthood: at her
workstation, online, in friendships, in the way she eats, and in the way her body reacts before she
can think. What frightens her most is the possibility that she will always be this vulnerable to
sudden danger, always one sound or movement away from feeling like a trapped child again. What she
hopes for, though she says it cautiously, is to be able to live in the present without feeling that
the next upset will prove she cannot handle her own life.
