Mai is a 35-year-old Vietnamese American physical therapist who came to treatment after months of
telling herself that what happened was over and that she should be able to get on with her life. She
is married now, but the trauma she described began in a previous romantic relationship marked by
escalating physical abuse. In the sessions, when she approached the memory directly, her account
repeatedly broke apart. She would begin with an ordinary detail—the apartment, the argument, the
feeling that she needed to leave—and then lose the thread, stop mid-sentence, and press her hands
together as if bracing herself. What did come through clearly was the bodily immediacy of the abuse.
She remembered being grabbed and overpowered when she tried to pull away, the shock of physical
touch turning instantly from familiar to dangerous, and the feeling of being forced into stillness
while her own wishes no longer mattered. She also remembered the temperature on her skin with
disturbing vividness: the cold surface against her arm and the abrupt contrast of air on her body
during the assault. Those sensations remained lodged in her memory in a way that felt more alive
than narrative, so that later a certain chill on her skin could bring the whole scene back with a
force that made it seem not remembered but current.

In the weeks after leaving that relationship, Mai had expected fear and anger, but what took hold
most strongly was humiliation. She had come to believe that the abuse exposed something defective in
her, something about the way she acted that had made it happen. She did not say this abstractly; she
said it as if it were settled fact. If she had not said certain things, if she had noticed the signs
earlier, if she had not stayed as long as she did, then maybe none of it would have happened. She
spent long stretches mentally revisiting moments before the assaults, trying to identify the exact
point at which she should have acted differently. At other times she found herself dwelling on the
version of herself from before the abuse—more relaxed, more open, less watchful. She spoke about
that earlier self almost as if she were talking about another woman she had lost.

These experiences became woven into her daily life at work. As a physical therapist, Mai spends her
days in close proximity to patients, guiding movement, applying manual support, and being touched in
return as part of routine care. That setting repeatedly confronted her with the very sensations she
feared. A patient’s hand reaching suddenly toward her forearm, even in a benign and expected way,
could make her whole body tense. The coolness of a treatment room, or a draft of air across exposed
skin while adjusting a patient’s position, could stop her for a moment with the feeling that she was
back in the apartment. When this happened, the memory did not arrive to her as a story from the
past. It happened all at once, with such emotional force that she had to fight to remain in the
room. On several occasions she excused herself abruptly, went into a supply area, and waited for the
wave of panic and shame to pass. She could not explain to colleagues why she seemed suddenly distant
or flustered, and the inability to put what happened into words deepened her sense that something
was wrong with her.

Outside work, she became sharply vigilant around other people, especially men whose tone, posture,
or nearness felt even vaguely controlling. In ordinary interactions she scanned for who might be
dangerous, where the exits were, whether someone might block her path or insist on something she did
not want. Situations that involved pressure or expectation became especially charged. What
frightened her most was the feeling of losing autonomy again, so even minor moments—someone standing
too close, someone insisting she do something quickly, even affectionate touch she had not
initiated—could flood her with alarm and anger that seemed to arrive before thought. At home this
strained her marriage. Her husband was described as caring and patient, but Mai found herself
recoiling from touch she had once welcomed, then feeling ashamed for doing so. She wanted closeness
and at the same time felt compelled to protect herself from it.

By the time she sought help, the cost had become harder to ignore. She was exhausted by how much of
her mental life was spent looking backward—either to the abuse itself or to the person she believed
she had been before it. She was tired of rehearsing how it could have been prevented, tired of being
ambushed by cold air or casual contact in the clinic, tired of starting to tell the story and
feeling her mind scatter under the weight of it. What finally brought her in was a recent moment at
work when a patient gripped her wrist unexpectedly during an exercise and she froze so completely
that another therapist had to step in. Mai left that day convinced she was unraveling. She came to
treatment afraid that the humiliation had marked her permanently and that there was something
fundamentally damaged in her, but also hoping, cautiously, that she might one day be able to feel
present in her own life again rather than perpetually pulled back into what happened.
