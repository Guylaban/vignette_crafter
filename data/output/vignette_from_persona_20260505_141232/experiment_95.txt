At 42, Linh Tran described the illness that nearly killed her as something that still did not feel
entirely like her own life. She was a married Vietnamese American woman working as a project
manager, used to deadlines, spreadsheets, and the steady competence of being the person others
relied on. The medical crisis interrupted that identity abruptly. She recalled becoming critically
ill and being rushed into intensive treatment, where much of her experience narrowed to fragments:
the sting and sight of needles entering her skin, clear bags hanging from metal poles, tubing,
antiseptic smells, and the cold that seemed to live everywhere in the hospital. Even now, when she
tried to tell the story from beginning to end, she could only do it in pieces. Some moments were
sharply preserved—the glint of a needle, the sight of a drip chamber, the sterile chemical smell in
the room, the cold air against her skin—while other stretches were blank. She would stop in session
and say that the whole thing sounded too extreme to have really happened to her, as if she were
describing another woman’s catastrophe.

In the weeks after she survived and returned home, Linh expected gratitude and relief, but what
stood out instead was how often the emotional force of the illness arrived without warning. At times
she would suddenly feel the same terror and helplessness she remembered from the hospital, yet have
no picture in her mind to go with it. The feeling would surge through her body before she could
place it. At other times a specific image would snap into focus with uncomfortable clarity: a
needle, a bag of fluid, the clinical brightness of the room. She had begun to believe that something
about the experience had damaged her not only physically but fundamentally. She was certain her body
had been permanently weakened. Ordinary sensations were hard for her to trust. If she felt tired,
chilled, or slightly unwell, her mind went quickly to the conviction that the illness had changed
her for good.

Linh also spoke with anguish about having failed in what she saw as her most important role. Before
the illness, she had taken pride in being dependable at home and at work. During the medical crisis
she had needed care from her husband and others, and afterward she could not stop measuring herself
against the version of herself who had managed everything. She had come to see the episode as proof
that her life had been destroyed in some essential way. She still went to work, but the world of
work had become crowded with reminders she had not anticipated. As a project manager, she spent much
of her day in a heavily air-conditioned office where the sudden chill on her arms could bring her
back to the hospital at once. Delivery staff sometimes passed through with disinfectant on their
hands, and the smell was enough to make her stomach drop. When colleagues discussed a health issue
or someone returned from a medical appointment with a bandage, Linh found herself looking away
quickly, her mind already filling with the sight of drips and needles.

Over months, she developed rigid ways of trying to keep herself together. She tried to erase the
memory whenever it surfaced, cutting off images, changing the subject in her own mind, telling
herself not to go there. She watched her thoughts closely, scanning for any sign that she was losing
control or “going crazy.” If a wave of fear came over her without an image attached, that frightened
her especially; she took the sheer intensity of feeling as evidence that her mind was no longer
reliable. Medical settings became particularly difficult. She avoided hospitals, would not go near
ambulances if she could help it, and postponed physical examinations. Even routine checkups felt
impossible because the sight of medical equipment, especially needles or IV drips, seemed too close
to the moments she remembered most clearly. What looked to others like simple avoidance made sense
to Linh as self-protection: she was trying to prevent herself from being overwhelmed, from
confirming her fear that her body was damaged beyond repair, and from facing a story she still could
barely believe belonged to her.

These habits began to narrow her life. At work she quietly delegated tasks that might take her near
the building’s first-aid station and made excuses when wellness events were held onsite. At home,
her husband urged her to follow up medically, but their conversations often ended in tension because
Linh felt he could not understand how impossible it was to walk back into anything resembling that
world. By the time she sought help, she was exhausted by the effort of policing her mind and
arranging her life around reminders of illness. What finally brought her in was the recognition that
she could no longer separate fear of medical cues from fear of herself—fear of what might happen if
she remembered too much, and fear of what it meant that so much remained blank. More than anything,
she wanted some way to live in her body without feeling that the worst had already happened and
would somehow happen again.
