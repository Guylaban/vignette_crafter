Kwame Mensah, a 77-year-old African American man who had come to the United States as a refugee
decades earlier, described the defining trauma of his life as a sequence of sharp visual moments
rather than a story he could tell straight through. When he spoke about it in session, he did not
offer a flowing narrative so much as a handful of fixed images: armed men shouting orders, a crowd
forced into movement, the stunned faces of people around him, the terrifying certainty that
disobedience was impossible. What remained with him most clearly were not conversations or
explanations but pictures. He could see a soldier’s raised arm, a line of frightened civilians, a
body on the ground, the dust in the air. These moments came back to him as isolated snapshots, each
one vivid and complete in itself, but disconnected from anything before or after. At times he said
he could recall what happened and yet feel almost nothing while doing so, “as if it was another man
standing there,” and that emotional distance unsettled him nearly as much as the images themselves.

In the months and years after resettlement, Kwame built a life that looked solid from the outside.
He married, established himself professionally, and spent many years working as a software engineer,
a role that suited both his intelligence and his preference for control and predictability. But his
daily life gradually narrowed around experiences that seemed to pull him back toward the old threat.
Being given orders was especially difficult. Even ordinary instructions could land with unexpected
force. In meetings, if a manager spoke abruptly or told him, “Do this by the end of the day,” he
felt a sudden internal jolt. It was not simply annoyance at being managed; it was the particular
tone of command that caught him. For a moment, the office disappeared behind one of those old
pictures, and he found himself rigid, watchful, and desperate to get away from the interaction. He
would often respond tersely, then keep his eyes on the screen, trying to bury himself in code until
the feeling passed.

He had also become increasingly careful about exposure to anything that resembled the circumstances
of his displacement. News coverage of war, refugee camps, military conflict, or commemorations of
political violence left him shaken, even when he insisted he was “just watching.” As he grew older,
these moments became harder to contain. Sometimes the first sign was a strange sense of unreality,
as though he were no longer fully present in the room. He described these episodes as feeling
detached from himself, slightly outside his body, with the world taking on a faraway quality. That
state frightened him enough that he began arranging his life around avoiding it. He went out less
and less unless absolutely necessary. His wife noticed that errands he used to handle without
difficulty were postponed or delegated. Invitations were declined. He told himself he preferred the
quiet of home, but in practice he was trying to stay away from situations where he might encounter
commanding voices, crowded public settings, or reminders of upheaval.

Avoiding people became another quiet pattern. Over time he stopped attending community gatherings
and kept even casual social contact to a minimum. In part this was easier to explain as age,
tiredness, or a wish for privacy. But he knew that being around others increased the chance of
feeling exposed, cornered, or abruptly transported into those visual fragments. He had learned to
keep his mind occupied almost constantly. At home he moved quickly from one mental task to
another—reading technical articles, checking email, tinkering with old programming problems,
watching videos, scrolling headlines until he realized too late what he had opened. Silence and
idleness felt risky. If his mind was not filled, the pictures could arrive.

This pattern had consequences in his marriage as well as his work life. His wife experienced him as
physically present but often elsewhere, especially when she asked direct questions about his past or
suggested they go out together. He would withdraw, become flat, or answer in a way that sounded
distant and impersonal. He knew she wanted closeness and did not want to hurt her, but he could not
bear the combination of being pressed, feeling detached, and sensing those old scenes gathering at
the edges of awareness.

What finally brought Kwame to treatment was not a new trauma but the recognition that his world had
become too small. After becoming distressed by a trauma-related news segment and then feeling unreal
and cut off for the rest of the evening, he saw how much of his life was now organized around
staying away—away from public places, away from people, away from unstructured moments in his own
mind. He came to therapy worried that these images would never loosen their hold, and frightened by
how easily a simple instruction from another person could make him feel, for an instant, like the
powerless young man in those pictures. At the same time, he said quietly that he was hoping for
something modest and profound: to be able to live in the present with his wife, to leave the house
without calculating every risk, and to remember what happened without feeling either engulfed by it
or numb like a stranger to his own life.
