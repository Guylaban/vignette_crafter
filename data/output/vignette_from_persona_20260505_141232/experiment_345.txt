Mele was 43 when she came to treatment, a Pacific Islander American woman who had worked for years
as a physical therapist and had always thought of herself as steady in emergencies, practical with
injured bodies, and careful with other people’s pain. She was living with her partner when the
accident happened, and even now she struggled to tell it in a single sequence. What came back to her
were pieces that arrived with unusual force: the sharp smell of alcohol on another person’s breath,
the sudden shift in the room, the sight of a body falling the wrong way, and the immediate knowledge
that someone had been seriously hurt and that her own action had been part of it. When she tried to
explain it aloud, especially if someone asked direct questions, she would falter, skip over moments,
then become overwhelmed by shame. She could say that she had not done enough to stop what happened,
but beyond that her account broke apart.

In the first days afterward, Mele replayed isolated fragments rather than a full memory. Those
fragments felt more real than ordinary remembering. She did not describe them as “thinking about
it,” but as being pulled back into it so completely that for a moment the present dropped away. The
smell of alcohol was especially potent. If a patient came into the clinic carrying the scent of beer
from the night before, or if she passed someone on the street who smelled of liquor, she could feel
herself jolt inside as if the accident were happening again. At times she would freeze and scan the
room; at other times she would feel such a rush of fear, guilt, horror, and shame that she had to
excuse herself. Talking about the event was no easier. If her partner asked how she was doing, or a
coworker referred to accidents, liability, or injuries caused by another person, she could feel
panic rising before any details were spoken. She had come to believe that if she let herself think
about the event directly, she would not be able to handle what would follow.

Over the following months, her world narrowed in ways that were obvious both at home and at work. In
the clinic she became preoccupied with where she stood in relation to the door, subtly arranging
herself to stay near an exit in treatment rooms and staff meetings. She noticed possible weapons or
dangerous objects everywhere—metal tools, heavy equipment, scissors, even ordinary items that
suddenly seemed capable of doing harm. This constant scanning left her tense and distracted around
patients. On difficult days she would leave work and spend money impulsively, buying clothes,
household items, or expensive skincare products she did not need. She described the spending as a
way to feel some brief interruption in her own head, a momentary comfort before the shame returned.
She also began taking drugs, not in a dramatic way she volunteered proudly, but quietly and
defensively, as one more thing she did to get through evenings when she felt she could not bear
being with her own thoughts.

Her sense of herself changed alongside these habits. Mele said more than once that the person she
had become was unrecognizable to her. Before the accident she had trusted herself around injury; now
every trauma-related story, professional training discussion, or news item about someone being
seriously hurt could leave her shaken. She could still perform many of her duties as a physical
therapist, but the setting itself had become full of reminders: patients recovering from accidents,
clinical conversations about mechanisms of injury, the smell of antiseptic mixed with traces of
alcohol from someone’s breath, the metal instruments in treatment spaces. Colleagues noticed that
she had become withdrawn and abrupt. At home, her partner saw that she avoided any real conversation
about the incident and went quiet or left the room if it came up. The avoidance did not bring relief
so much as postpone another collapse into shame.

reliving, intense negative emotion, spending, drug use, and hypervigilance had begun to affect her
work and her relationship. What finally brought her in was not one dramatic crisis but the
accumulation of moments in which she no longer felt like herself: standing by the clinic door with
her heart pounding, smelling alcohol on someone nearby and feeling herself thrown back into the
accident, hearing her own voice fail when she tried to explain what had happened. She was frightened
of what would happen if she ever let the memory come fully into focus, and at the same time she was
desperate for some way to live without feeling that she would never forgive herself for not doing
more.
