Margaret Weiss is an 80-year-old retired-not-retired social worker who still works part time with
older adults at a community agency, and she describes her life as divided into a before and after
that she never expected at her age. The trauma she speaks about happened in the context of a late-
life romantic relationship that had seemed, at first, companionable and safe. She had known
loneliness and was not naïve about disappointment, but she had still believed that by this stage of
life people were more settled, more decent, less dangerous. The assault that now anchors her fear
took place in his home after an argument escalated behind a closed door. What remains with her is
not a smooth story but a set of fixed images: his face changing, the sudden movement of his body
blocking her way, the door slamming, his hand on her, the angle of the room as she tried to orient
herself, the sound of another door opening somewhere in the house. When she tries to tell it, the
sequence breaks apart. She can offer only separate pictures, and when she reaches them she is
overcome not only by fear but by a grief that feels larger than the incident itself, grief for the
person she had been and for the ordinary faith in people she feels was taken from her.

In the weeks that followed, Margaret found herself thinking again and again about why this had
happened to her. She could not stop returning to the question, as if some explanation might make the
event fit into a moral world she recognized. Instead, the world itself began to feel altered. She
had come to believe that her understanding of how people behave, of what intimacy means, of what
safety in familiar company should look like, had been completely destroyed. Most painful to her was
the conviction that people simply cannot be trusted. This did not stay confined to thoughts about
her former partner. It spread outward, especially toward men, and even toward men she knew. If a
male maintenance worker arrived at her apartment building unexpectedly, or if a male colleague
lingered after a meeting until the room had emptied, she felt her body brace before she had time to
think. Being alone with men became intolerable in a way she could not dismiss as irrational, because
to her it felt proven. Even related people could set this off; a visit from a nephew or a family
friend did not automatically register as safe simply because they were known.

Doors became especially potent. The sound of a door opening behind her at work, or a heavy office
door slamming down the hall, could pull her up short and leave her heart racing. In the agency
building where she meets clients, she now notices every entrance the moment she walks in. Before she
sits down in a waiting room or an interview office, she scans for exits, checks where she would go,
and keeps herself positioned so she is not trapped. She describes herself as constantly on guard,
watchful in grocery stores, elevators, hallways, and church basements, anywhere an unexpected male
presence or the abrupt movement of a door might occur. Colleagues have noticed that she prefers to
leave her office door slightly open and that she is quicker than before to relocate conversations
into more public spaces.

At night the injury remains close. Margaret has trouble falling asleep and often wakes after only
short stretches, already tense. She has begun structuring her evenings around not dreaming. She
avoids certain times and situations that she believes make nightmares more likely: lingering alone
in the dark living room, dozing in her armchair, letting her mind drift before bed. She keeps
herself occupied instead, sometimes sorting papers from work or leaving the television on longer
than she wants, because the quiet feels dangerous. Alongside this, she tries forcefully to erase the
memory altogether. If one of the images intrudes, she tells herself to stop, to clear it out, to put
it somewhere inaccessible. Yet when it returns, it does so as those same isolated pictures rather
than a narrative she can place in order.

She also speaks, with visible shame, about feeling dirty because of what happened. She knows how
stark that sounds, especially as a seasoned social worker who has spent decades helping other people
name injustice accurately. Still, the feeling persists. It leaves her scrubbing her hands longer
than necessary after work and changing clothes quickly after difficult days, not because she thinks
this makes logical sense but because contamination is how the experience sits in her body.

What finally brought Margaret to seek help was the recognition that her life had narrowed around
these precautions. She still values her work and does not want to withdraw from it, but she has
started turning down home visits if she believes a man may answer the door, and she worries this is
changing the kind of social worker she can be. More quietly, she is frightened by how thoroughly
mistrust has entered her relationships and by the possibility that the world will never feel
recognizable again. What she hopes for is modest but urgent: to sleep, to enter a room without
immediately plotting escape, and to be able to remember what happened without being shattered by
grief.
