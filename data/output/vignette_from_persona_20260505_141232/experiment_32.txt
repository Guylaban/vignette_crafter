Ethan is a 32-year-old park ranger who had spent most of his adult life feeling at home outdoors,
confident in his body, and used to handling situations alone. That changed after an assault in which
he was tortured, an event he still struggles to describe in any complete way. When he tries to tell
it, he gets stuck. He can give isolated pieces with striking clarity—the feel of being confined, the
sound of a door, the sharp certainty that he was at someone else’s mercy—but then the narrative
breaks apart and he loses the thread. Some stretches are vivid enough that he feels almost pulled
back into them, while other parts are blank. What troubles him nearly as much as the memories
themselves is that he cannot seem to put the worst of it into words. In session he has stopped mid-
sentence, gone silent, and shaken his head as if language itself fails at the point where the fear
becomes most intense.

In the weeks after the assault, Ethan found himself looking at his body differently. He had come to
believe that it had been permanently damaged by what happened, even when he could not point to one
clear injury that explained the feeling. He watched himself for signs that something had changed
irrevocably. At the same time, he was unsettled by how abruptly his body responded to things around
him. He would jolt, tense, or feel a rush of alarm before he had consciously registered what had set
him off. That loss of a sense of control frightened him. He was certain that reactions like these
meant something was seriously wrong with him, and at his worst moments he worried that he was going
crazy.

Trust narrowed quickly after the assault. Ethan had always worked in a role that required
coordination with other staff, visitors, maintenance crews, and law enforcement, but he now found
himself assuming that other people could not be relied on. If a coworker said they would handle
something, he felt little relief. If someone was late to meet him on patrol or a response from
headquarters took longer than expected, his mind went immediately to the possibility that something
bad was happening. A routine delay—a radio call not returned right away, a visitor taking too long
to come back from a trail, a colleague arriving ten minutes late to relieve him—did not feel routine
to him. He experienced those moments as dangerous gaps in which harm might already be unfolding.

Because his job as a park ranger involves vehicles constantly—driving service roads, riding with
coworkers between trailheads, transporting supplies—his avoidance has become especially disruptive.
Since the assault, driving or even being a passenger has felt intolerable. He has rearranged duties
when he can, volunteered for tasks that keep him on foot near the station, and made excuses about
preferring to stay local. When he cannot avoid a vehicle entirely, he is visibly tense and
withdrawn. This has begun to affect how others see him at work, though he rarely explains why. Ethan
tends to prepare intensely for any interaction, rehearsing what he will say before speaking to
supervisors, coworkers, or even visitors asking ordinary questions. He tries to script conversations
in advance so he will not be caught off guard, lose his train of thought, or reveal more than he
intends.

At home, he has become fixed on checking doors and windows. He goes back repeatedly, testing locks,
scanning entry points, then circling back again even after telling himself he has already checked.
The ritual gives him only brief reassurance. He lives alone, and the apartment that once felt like a
place to recover now feels like a place he must secure over and over. His emotional life has
constricted in parallel. He describes numbing his feelings rather than letting himself fully
experience them. Friends have noticed he seems flat or absent, and he has allowed several
relationships to fade because staying detached feels safer than trying to explain what is happening
to him.

Over the months, the combined weight of these changes has left Ethan more isolated and less
effective in parts of his work he once managed easily. What finally brought him to seek help was not
one single crisis but the recognition that his world had become organized around anticipation and
control: rehearsing before every interaction, avoiding vehicles in a job built around mobility,
checking locks late into the night, and bracing for catastrophe whenever anything ran behind
schedule. He is frightened by how quickly his body reacts and by how impossible it still is to tell
the story of the assault from beginning to end. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not to forget what
happened, but to stop feeling as if he is still living at the edge of it and to find out whether his
reactions mean he is broken, or whether there is some way back to a life that feels like his own.
