At 30, Daniel is a married North American man who has worked for years as a carpenter, spending most
days on job sites where his hands are busy, his body is in close quarters with other workers, and
minor cuts and scrapes are part of the visual landscape. He came to treatment after many months of
trying to get through on his own following a traumatic incident in which he witnessed another person
suffer a severe injury. When he speaks about that day, the account stalls and fragments. He can
circle around it, but he cannot seem to put the worst of it into words. What comes across instead is
how total the experience was: the sight of visible wounds, the bodily closeness of the scene, and a
distinct taste that has stayed fused to the moment ever since. Even now, he does not describe it as
a memory in the ordinary sense. He says that when it comes back, it is just there all at once, as if
he is inside it again.

In the immediate aftermath, Daniel tried to return to work and act as though he could absorb what
had happened the way men on construction crews are often expected to absorb hard things. But
ordinary moments began to break open without warning. A coworker brushing past him in a narrow
framed hallway, a hand landing suddenly on his shoulder, even casual physical contact from someone
trying to get his attention could make the whole scene return instantly. It was not only visual. He
would feel as though all of his senses had been pulled back into that moment, with no distance
between then and now. At other times, seeing injuries on other people—even routine job-site cuts,
split knuckles, a bandaged hand, blood from a nail puncture—hit him with the same force. There was
also a specific taste linked to the event that could ambush him unexpectedly while eating or
drinking, and once it appeared in his mouth he felt transported back before he had time to prepare
himself.

Because these episodes arrived so suddenly, Daniel became preoccupied with getting away from
anything that might set them off, though he could not predict what would do it. At work he started
angling for tasks he could do more independently so he would not have people moving close around
him. If someone approached from behind, he would tense before he even understood why. When a
coworker held out an injured hand or joked about getting banged up on the site, Daniel found reasons
to step away. His occupation made this especially hard; construction is tactile, crowded, and full
of visible bodily wear. Over time, the effort to get through the day narrowed his world. He also
tried not to think about the event at all. If his mind drifted toward it, he would cut the thought
off abruptly, change the subject, turn on the television, or keep himself moving until he was
exhausted.

At home, his wife noticed that he was eating differently. Daniel began using food in a blunt,
repetitive way, eating far past hunger when he felt unsettled, as though being full might press down
whatever had been stirred up. He described it less as comfort than as something to do to get through
the evening. There were also moments when he tried to make himself go blank on purpose. When he felt
a memory gathering, he would deliberately attempt to leave his body, stare fixedly, and make
everything around him go dim, as if distance could be manufactured by force. These spells frightened
his wife, who experienced him as present one minute and unreachable the next.

As the months passed, Daniel had come to believe that the trauma had ruined his life in a lasting
way. He no longer trusted his own mind because the memory could seize him without warning and
because he could not say, in words, what he had witnessed. This inability felt like a personal
failure to him, further proof that he could not rely on himself. He was certain something in him had
been permanently altered for the worse. He compared himself constantly to how he had been before and
saw only loss: less steady, less dependable, less like the husband and worker he thought he was
supposed to be.

What finally brought him in was not a single dramatic collapse but the accumulation of strain. Work
had become a place of constant vigilance rather than competence, and home had become organized
around managing his distress. After one recent episode triggered by seeing another person’s injury
on the job, followed later that night by the reappearance of the same sickening taste while eating,
he told his wife he could not keep pretending this was passing. In seeking help, Daniel is
frightened that he has been permanently damaged and that the life he had before is gone for good. At
the same time, he is hoping—cautiously, almost against his own expectations—that there might be some
way to live in his body, at work, and with his wife without feeling that the worst moment of his
life is always waiting to return.
