Amina Rahman was 32 when she came for treatment, a South Asian American woman who worked as a
hospital chaplain and described herself as someone who had once been able to sit with other people’s
suffering without losing her footing. She was divorced, living alone, and said that before what
happened she had moved easily through busy hallways, waiting rooms, and family gatherings, taking
comfort in conversation and in being a steady presence for others. When she tried to speak about the
event itself, though, her speech changed. She would begin with, “I know what happened,” then stop,
press her hands together, and look down as if the rest of it existed somewhere just beyond language.
She said she could not find words for it, that whenever she tried to explain clearly what had been
done to her she lost the thread and could not finish. The memory came to her less as a story than as
fragments in her body.

The torture had involved men in uniform holding and restraining her while sounds around her became
charged with danger. In recounting it, Amina could only offer pieces: the sight of military or
police-style clothing, the feeling of being pinned and unable to move her arms, the particular
noises that told her what was about to happen before she could see it. Those details remained vivid
in a way the rest of the narrative did not. She could not give a smooth chronological account, but
she knew with certainty how exposed and overpowered she had been. What frightened her most in
remembering it was not only pain but the total loss of control in the presence of people marked by
authority. Even in session, if she attempted to say more, she often seemed to hit an internal wall,
shaking her head and saying, “It’s there, but I can’t make it into sentences.”

In the months afterward, Amina’s world narrowed around situations that matched those fragments.
Sounds that resembled those from the detention setting could stop her in place. A slammed metal door
on a hospital unit, the clipped rhythm of heavy boots in a corridor, or an authoritative voice
carried through an intercom could make her body tense before she had fully registered why. Seeing
police officers in the emergency department or security staff in dark uniforms made her instantly
vigilant; she would look for another route, lower her eyes, and feel an urgent need to get away.
Being touched unexpectedly was especially difficult. If a patient’s family member, distraught and
seeking comfort, grasped her forearm suddenly, or if a crowded elevator pressed people against her
on all sides, she felt a flash of panic that was hard to conceal.

Her work as a chaplain brought these moments into ordinary days. She began avoiding the hospital’s
open atrium and the uncovered plaza outside the entrance, explaining to herself that those spaces
left her too visible and unprotected. Instead she took longer indoor routes, waited in side
hallways, or lingered in stairwells until she could move without crossing broad exposed areas.
Colleagues noticed that she turned down requests to meet families in public waiting spaces and
preferred smaller rooms with doors. She also withdrew from people she had socialized with before the
trauma. Invitations from old friends went unanswered. Seeing them felt unbearable not because of
anything they had done, but because they belonged to a version of her life that no longer felt
reachable, and she dreaded the possibility that they would ask what had happened and she would fail
again to tell it.

When the memories pressed too close and she could not stop thinking about the event, Amina sometimes
hurt herself physically to interrupt the mental images and bodily sensations. She described this not
as wanting to die, but as a desperate way to make the thoughts stop, to replace something
unspeakable with a pain she could control and understand. She kept this hidden, ashamed and
frightened by how often the urge came after a day with too many sounds, too much exposure, or an
encounter with uniforms at work.

By the time she sought help, the strain was visible in both her personal and professional life. Her
world had become organized around detours, refusals, and silence. She was still showing up to the
hospital, still trying to care for patients, but she felt herself bracing through each day. What
finally brought her in was a recent moment in the hospital entrance when two uniformed officers were
speaking near the atrium and a volunteer reached out to guide her by the elbow. Amina froze, then
fled to a supply room and could not return to her rounds for nearly an hour. She said she was afraid
that if she kept living this way her life would shrink even further, and just as afraid that if she
tried to talk about the torture directly she would fall apart or find, once again, that no words
would come. What she hoped for was modest but deeply felt: to be able to remain present in her own
life without every sound, uniform, or sudden touch turning the world back into that place.
