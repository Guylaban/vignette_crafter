Kwame is a 46-year-old African American man who works as a curriculum designer for a large public-
school district. He is married and had been used to thinking of himself as steady, thoughtful, and
composed, someone who spent his days shaping lesson plans, revising materials, and moving between
meetings with teachers and administrators. The event that now returns to him happened on an ordinary
workday evening, after he had left the district office with a laptop bag full of drafts and notes.
He had been pulled over by police while driving home. What began as a traffic stop escalated
quickly. He remembers the flashing lights filling his rearview mirror and then the officer’s voice,
already sharp before Kwame had fully understood why he had been stopped. When he reached, as
instructed, toward the center console where his registration was kept, the officer shouted, drew his
weapon, and ordered him to freeze. Within moments Kwame was out of the car, pushed against it, and
handcuffed. He recalls the pressure of metal against his wrists, the heat of the hood against his
cheek, and the terrifying certainty, in those minutes, that a small movement or misunderstood word
could get him killed.

What remains most vivid is not a coherent story he can easily tell from beginning to end. Instead,
the memory comes back in pieces that feel too large for words. In the first weeks after it happened,
when his wife asked him to explain what the stop had been like, he would start and then fall silent.
He could describe fragments—the lights, the yelling, the gun—but then his face would tighten and he
would stop talking altogether, as if language failed him right where the fear had peaked. He has
told his therapist that he cannot seem to put the worst part into words. At times there is not even
a picture in his mind, only the same surge of dread flooding his body, as if the emotional force of
the event arrives before any image does. A sound behind him, a sudden command barked across a
hallway, or the flare of police lights on a nearby street can bring that feeling down on him so
strongly that he has to sit still and wait for it to pass.

Over the months that followed, Kwame became markedly watchful. He noticed every patrol car on the
road and tracked officers’ movements with exhausting precision whenever he passed them in parking
lots, near gas stations, or outside the schools he visited for work. In school buildings he began
choosing windows and doorways carefully, scanning the hall before stepping out of conference rooms.
If a security officer entered a campus office unexpectedly, Kwame’s body reacted before he had time
to think; his shoulders jumped, his chest tightened, and his heart pounded hard enough that he
sometimes lost track of what had just been said in a meeting. Colleagues occasionally saw him
startle at a slammed door or at the crackle of a radio and would pause, confused, while he tried to
recover and continue discussing curriculum maps as though nothing had happened.

His job has made these moments difficult to avoid. Part of his role involves visiting schools across
the district, and some campuses have visible police presence or school resource officers near the
front entrance. On those mornings, Kwame has found himself sitting in his car for several extra
minutes, gripping the steering wheel and steadying his breathing before he can make himself go
inside. He does not tell coworkers why. He has come to believe that if he lets the memory open
fully, he will be swallowed by it. Even in therapy, when he begins to approach the moment the
officer drew the gun, his voice trails off and he asks to stop because the emotion arrives too fast
and too intensely.

At home, his wife has noticed how sharply he reacts to sudden noises and how often he positions
himself to keep an eye on the street through the front window. He is not describing elaborate
theories about danger; it is more immediate than that. He feels on guard, as if his body has decided
that being less than fully alert would be reckless. What frightens him most is the force of the
reactions themselves—the way he can be pulled into terror by a siren, flashing lights reflecting off
another car, or an authoritative voice raised without warning. He sought help after a recent school
visit where a police officer turned abruptly toward him in a hallway and greeted him, and Kwame
recoiled so visibly that a teacher asked if he was all right. He was ashamed by how exposed he felt,
and shaken by how quickly his body had acted as though the stop were happening again. Coming into
treatment, he says he wants to be able to speak about what happened without shutting down, and he
wants, more than anything, to stop living as if the next ordinary interaction could turn lethal in
an instant.
