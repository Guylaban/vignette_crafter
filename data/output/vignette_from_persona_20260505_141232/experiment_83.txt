Marisol Herrera, a 61-year-old married bus driver, came to treatment more than a year after
witnessing a fatal road accident that still felt to her as if it were unfolding in front of her. She
had been driving her regular city route on an early weekday morning, moving through a busy
intersection she knew well, when a car coming too fast entered against the light and struck another
vehicle broadside. From her high seat she saw the impact clearly: the violent sideways jerk of
metal, the shattered glass, the immediate confusion of horns and screams. She brought her bus to a
stop and ran toward the cars. What stayed with her most was the blood on one victim’s face and shirt
and the terrible sense, in those first seconds, that everything had spun beyond anyone’s control.
People were shouting, traffic was backing up, and Marisol kept trying to do something useful while
feeling she could not make the scene obey her hands. Even now, when she speaks about it, what
returns is not a distant story but the crash as a total sensory event—the sound of the impact, the
smell of hot metal and fuel, the sight of blood, the panic in her own body—as if it is all happening
again in the present.

In the weeks afterward, she found that ordinary moments on the road could throw her back into that
intersection. If she passed cars crumpled on the shoulder after even a minor collision, her chest
tightened before she had time to think. She would grip the steering wheel and see, for an instant,
not the actual scene before her but the original vehicles slamming together. Blood was especially
powerful. A passenger with a cut finger, a television image glimpsed in a waiting room, even red
staining on a work rag could bring a wave of nausea and alarm. At other times there was no picture
at all—only the same overwhelming terror and helplessness flooding her body without warning. She
would suddenly feel as she had on the street that morning, her heart pounding, her stomach dropping,
her mind insisting that something irreversible was happening right now.

Marisol had come to believe that she had failed people that day in a way she could never repair. She
said she should have protected them, should have stopped the chain of events somehow, should have
done more once the crash happened. Though she knew other drivers and paramedics had arrived, she was
certain that her own inaction was part of what made the memory unbearable. What frightened her most
was the thought that the same thing could happen again while she was behind the wheel and that,
again, she would not be able to control it. On her route she became highly alert to intersections,
impatient drivers, and the movements of other cars edging forward too fast. Seeing one vehicle drift
too close to another was enough to fill her mind with crash imagery.

Over months she developed private habits that she did not tell anyone about at first. Before
starting her bus, she silently repeated phrases in a fixed order, and if she lost track she started
over. At red lights she sometimes counted under her breath or repeated short prayers, convinced that
if she completed them correctly she might prevent another terrible accident. She knew part of her
considered this irrational, but another part felt she could not risk stopping. She also recoiled
from being touched. When passengers brushed past her shoulder or a coworker placed a hand on her arm
in the depot, she flinched sharply. At home, her husband noticed that she stiffened even with
ordinary affectionate contact, and she struggled to explain why. Anything that reminded her of
someone lost became hard to bear as well. She avoided funeral conversations, memorial photos, and
roadside shrines, turning away quickly because she felt herself pulled toward the grief and back
toward the crash.

These changes narrowed her life. At work she began switching routes when she could to avoid the
intersection where she had witnessed the collision, and when that was not possible she approached it
in rigid silence, already braced. Coworkers saw her as more withdrawn. At home she spent more time
alone and said less to her husband, partly because she did not want to be touched and partly because
she feared that speaking about the accident would make it come alive again. Beneath this was a
darker belief she had not said aloud for months: that she was no longer sure her life was worth
living if this was how she was going to feel, trapped in a moment she could not change.

She finally sought help after a recent near miss on her route, when two cars clipped each other in
front of the bus. No one was badly injured, but the sight of the damaged vehicles brought everything
back so forcefully that she had to pull over and let another driver finish her shift. She was shaken
by how completely the past overtook the present and by how ashamed she felt afterward. Coming to
therapy, Marisol said she wanted most to stop living as if the crash were still happening, but she
was afraid that if she let go of her vigilance or her rituals, something awful would happen again
and once more she would fail to protect someone.
