Amina is a 21-year-old African American woman in surgical training who came to treatment after
months of telling herself she should be able to handle this on her own. She was raised to value
discipline and composure, and those qualities had helped her move quickly into a demanding hospital
environment. The event she returns to happened during a violent natural disaster at night, when
severe weather escalated suddenly and left her trapped in darkness with the sound of wind, impact,
and structural strain around her. She remembers the air pressure changing, the lights going out, and
the abrupt confusion of trying to understand what was failing around her while hearing objects
strike the building. What has stayed with her is not a single image as much as the sense that
everything became dangerous at once and that there was nothing she could do to stop it. She recalls
being awake in the dark, listening for the next crash, bracing for something worse, and feeling
small against a force that could not be negotiated with.

In the weeks afterward, reminders began to catch her off guard. Weather forecasts became difficult
to ignore, but what unsettled her most was not any storm in the abstract; it was weather that felt
too much like that night—the same heavy air, the same shift in wind, the same ominous feeling that
the world was changing around her. When rain and wind pick up in the evening, she feels her body
react before she has finished the thought. Her chest tightens, her muscles tense, and she becomes
acutely alert to every sound in the room. Waking in darkness has become its own problem. If she
opens her eyes at night and the room is black, she does not orient calmly to her apartment. For a
few moments she is back in that atmosphere of danger, listening hard and waiting. Even ordinary
noises can make her jolt—a door closing in the hospital corridor, metal instruments clattering onto
a tray, the sharp beep of equipment. She knows these are routine sounds, but her body still reacts
as if they signal immediate threat.

This has been especially difficult in surgery, where the environment is full of sudden sounds,
bright intensity followed by long stretches in windowless rooms, and overnight call shifts that
leave her waking in the dark in a call room. She has become watchful in a way that goes beyond
professional vigilance. She scans hallways, checks weather updates repeatedly, notices changes in
light and sound that other people seem not to register. During tense interactions with senior staff
or supervisors, she has found herself becoming overly agreeable, quick to say yes, eager not to
create friction. In those moments she does not experience it as a choice so much as a way to stay
safe. If someone seems irritated or demanding, she becomes especially compliant, trying to prevent
anything from escalating.

Amina has also begun organizing her life around not feeling too much. She tries hard to control her
emotions, particularly at work, where she is determined that no one will see her as rattled or weak.

work she has cut out exercise, even though it used to help structure her week. She says that getting
her heart rate up now feels wrong, too close to the bodily surge that comes when she is reminded of
the disaster. She has told herself it is better not to provoke that feeling. Over time, this has
narrowed her life. She goes from the hospital to home, keeps her routines tight, and avoids
situations that might leave her physically keyed up or emotionally exposed.

The way she talks about herself has shifted as much as her habits have. She has come to believe that
the disaster ruined the trajectory of her life, that something fundamental was broken that night.
She is ashamed of how little she trusts herself now. Before, she believed she could think clearly
and act effectively under pressure; now, when she startles or feels her body flood with fear, she
takes it as evidence that she cannot rely on herself. What frightens her most is the conviction that
bad things can happen without warning and that she is powerless to stop them. When storms roll in or
she wakes suddenly in darkness, that belief feels absolute.

She finally sought help after a run of overnight shifts during severe weather, when she noticed
herself dreading both the forecast and the dark call room more than the work itself. After startling
hard at a routine noise in the operating suite and then feeling humiliated by how intensely her body
had reacted, she went home convinced that she could not keep pretending this was just stress. She
presented for treatment torn between fear and hope: afraid that her life has already been destroyed
by what happened and that she will never again trust herself under pressure, but hoping there may
