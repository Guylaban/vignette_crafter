Marisol is a 66-year-old Latina woman, divorced, who has spent most of her adult life working with
language. As an editor, she is precise, observant, and accustomed to noticing what others miss. In
session, that same careful attention appears in the way she recounts the years that divided her life
into before and after. She was a civilian in a war zone when the event she returns to most often
occurred. What remains most vivid is not an abstract sense of danger but a sequence of sharply
defined images and sounds: armed men in uniform moving through the street, the sudden concussive
crack of an explosion nearby, and one particular offender whose face she can still picture with
disturbing clarity. She remembers the way the uniform seemed to fill her vision before she
registered anything else, and then the bang itself, so loud and immediate that for a moment it
seemed to erase thought. Even now, when she speaks about it, she describes that moment as if it
still sits just to one side of the present.

In the period after she left the war zone and built a life in the United States, she tried to treat
these memories as something that should have receded with time. Instead, certain moments in ordinary
life continued to strike her with the same force. Seeing a man who resembles the offender can stop
her cold. It is not that she merely remembers; for a few seconds she feels as if her body has
already decided there is danger before she can reason with herself. Uniforms are especially potent.
A police officer standing near a subway entrance, a military dress uniform on television, even a
formal security uniform in a government building can leave her tense and watchful. She finds herself
tracking where the person is, how close they are, and whether she can leave. Loud bangs are equally
difficult. Construction noise outside her apartment, a car backfiring in traffic, a heavy object
dropped unexpectedly in the office can jolt her into immediate alarm. She does not experience these
as neutral sounds that startled her more than most people; to her they carry the atmosphere of that
earlier moment, as if they might signal the same kind of catastrophe unfolding again.

These experiences are woven into her daily life as an editor. Her office occupies part of an older
downtown building where renovations have been intermittent for months. On days when workers are
active, hammering and sudden blasts from construction equipment reverberate through the walls.
Marisol has begun timing her arrival to avoid the busiest periods and sometimes carries work home
rather than remain in the office when the noise is unpredictable. She has also become acutely aware
of uniforms in professional settings. If a police officer is posted in the lobby or outside a nearby
event, she feels her concentration evaporate. She keeps her head down, moves quickly, and later
struggles to settle back into line edits and manuscript comments. Colleagues know her as reserved
and exacting, but few know how much effort it takes for her to remain outwardly composed in these
moments.

Night is the hardest part of her day. Marisol says plainly that she does not feel safe after dark in
the way other people seem to. Evening brings a shift she can feel in her body: more listening, more
checking, less belief in reassurance. Friends and family have told her many times that she is safe
now, that she lives in a secure building, that nothing is happening outside her windows, but she
finds that such statements do not land. She can understand them intellectually and still feel
certain that safety is fragile or possibly illusory. What frightens her most is the sense that
danger can arrive without warning, announced only by a sound or a figure appearing where it should
not be. Because of this, she wakes repeatedly during the night and checks. She listens for noises in
the hallway, looks toward the windows, makes sure the locks are secure, and scans the apartment
before trying to lie down again. She does not describe these checks as excessive; from her point of
view they are the only way to be responsible when nighttime makes her feel exposed.

Over the years this pattern has narrowed her life. She sleeps poorly, arrives at work tired, and
guards her evenings carefully. She dislikes being out after dark and rarely accepts invitations that
would require returning home late. The loneliness of her divorce feels sharper in the nighttime
hours, when there is no one else in the apartment to confirm or disconfirm what she senses. What
finally brought her to seek help was not a new trauma but the recognition that the fear has remained
unchanged despite decades of trying to master it on her own. Recently, after a loud bang from street
construction woke her and she spent the rest of the night moving from room to room checking that she
was safe, she realized how fully her life was organized around the possibility of danger. She comes
to treatment wanting, above all, to sleep through the night and to believe safety when it is
present, though part of her is still afraid that letting her guard down would be a mistake she could
not undo.
