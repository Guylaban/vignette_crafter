Mariam is a 30-year-old Middle Eastern American woman who came to treatment after years of living as
though danger could break through at any moment. She is divorced and works nights as a bartender, a
job she chose partly because the space behind the bar feels defined and contained. She can keep her
back near the shelves, know where the exits are, and watch the room without having to stand exposed
in the middle of it. Open spaces trouble her. Crossing a wide parking lot after a shift, or stepping
out onto an empty sidewalk before dawn, leaves her feeling visible and unprotected in a way she
struggles to explain.

The event she returns to is from childhood, when she witnessed domestic violence in her home. What
remains most vivid is not a story she tells in sequence but a set of fragments that arrive with
force: the sound of a door slamming hard enough to shake the frame, her father’s body moving
suddenly across the room, her mother stepping back, the speed with which an ordinary evening became
dangerous. She remembers watching movements before she understood words, learning to read the lift
of an arm, the turn of a shoulder, the change in pace across a room. Even now, those moments feel
stored in her body more than in language. When she tries to describe what happened, she often stops
mid-sentence and says she knows exactly what happened but cannot find the words. If she pushes
herself to continue, she becomes flooded and has to stop altogether, ashamed and frustrated that
something so central in her life still seems unspeakable.

In the years after, Mariam came to believe that people cannot be trusted, especially when they seem
calm. Her mistrust is not abstract; it lives in constant small calculations. She watches for sudden
movements from customers leaning over the bar, from men reaching quickly into their pockets, from
strangers shifting sharply in line at the liquor distributor. Her body goes on immediate alert
before she has fully registered why. At work she is known as composed and efficient, but beneath
that control she tracks who is entering, who is drinking too fast, who might become unpredictable.
If a patron raises an arm too quickly to wave her down, she flinches internally and then covers it,
tightening her face and forcing herself into an even tone.

She tries hard to control her emotions and rarely lets herself cry, shake, or visibly lose
composure. It matters intensely to her not to come undone in front of anyone. She has come to doubt
her own judgment and says she cannot rely on herself to do the right thing when it counts. That
self-doubt appears in ordinary decisions as much as in emergencies. Before leaving home, she checks
the locks and windows repeatedly, then doubts whether she checked correctly and circles back again.
At night, once she is in bed, she often gets up to confirm the front door is locked one more time.
She tells herself this is the only way to be prepared.

Preparation occupies much of her thinking. On her drive to work, she mentally rehearses worst-case
scenarios: a fight breaking out at the bar, someone following her to her car, an intruder getting
into her apartment, a man misreading a brief interaction and becoming aggressive. She runs through
what she would do, where she would stand, what she could grab, how she would get out. She
experiences this as necessary rather than excessive; without rehearsing, she feels foolish and
exposed. Still, the habit leaves little room for rest. By the time a shift begins, she is already
tense.

Over time her life has narrowed around a small set of places that feel tolerable: behind the bar, in
her apartment after the locks are checked, and in a few familiar corners of her routine. Elsewhere
she feels unsteady. During busy service, coworkers sometimes ask her to cross the floor to clear
tables or help on the patio, and she dislikes the open layout there, feeling too visible and too far
from cover. Her divorce sits in this same landscape of mistrust and self-doubt. She does not offer a
simple explanation for it, only that living closely with another person never felt truly safe and
that she was always bracing, second-guessing herself, and waiting for a shift she might miss.

What brought her to seek help was not one new crisis but exhaustion. After a recent shift, a
customer made a sudden movement while arguing with someone beside him, and Mariam found herself so
intensely on guard that she had to step into the stockroom and stand still until the surge passed.
Later, alone in her apartment, she checked the locks again and again and realized she no longer knew
what “enough” would feel like. She came in saying she is tired of living inside constant readiness.
What frightens her most is the possibility that she will never be able to trust anyone, including
herself. What she hopes for, cautiously, is to feel safe in more than a few narrow places and to
speak about what she witnessed without becoming overwhelmed or losing control.
