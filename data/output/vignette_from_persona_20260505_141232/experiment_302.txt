Anna Weber is a 51-year-old married woman who works as a civil servant in a county records office.
She describes her life as divided into before and after the chemical exposure. The incident happened
during a routine workday in a municipal building when a noxious chemical release spread through part
of the offices. What stays with her is not a single tidy narrative but a series of sensory
fragments: the sudden shift from an ordinary morning to confusion, people talking over one another,
voices giving directions she could not make out, and the growing sense that something dangerous was
in the air while no one around her seemed able to explain clearly what was happening. She remembers
the atmosphere as chaotic and unpredictable, with coworkers moving quickly, bits of incomprehensible
chatter in hallways, and the frightening feeling that she did not know where safety was or what her
body had already been exposed to.

When Anna tries to describe the event now, she often begins, then stops. She says she “loses the
thread,” as if the memory breaks apart as soon as she reaches for it. In session she can name the
setting and the fact of the exposure, but when asked what happened next, she falters, becomes
visibly strained, and says she cannot seem to put it into words. She is aware of how frustrating
this is. Part of her wants to tell it clearly, if only to prove to herself that she understands what
happened, yet when she deliberately tries to remember, the event feels oddly inaccessible. What
comes back are flashes of confusion and alarm rather than a coherent account.

In the months that followed, Anna became increasingly uneasy in situations that felt at all
unpredictable. At work she began dreading spontaneous conversations in the corridor or unexpected
changes in routine. Before even ordinary interactions she now rehearses what she is going to say,
sometimes several times, because she feels she must be prepared for every possible turn. If a
colleague stops by her desk without warning, especially if more than one person is talking at once,
she becomes tense and struggles to follow the exchange. Incomprehensible chatter is particularly
unsettling to her. Hearing overlapping voices in the break room or muffled conversation from a
nearby office can bring back the same sense she had during the exposure, that something important
and threatening is happening around her and she cannot make sense of it quickly enough.

Her world has narrowed around places she experiences as controlled and knowable. She says she only
feels safe in a very limited number of places, mainly at home and in a few familiar areas of her
workplace where she knows the routines and the people. Strangers approaching her in ordinary
situations—a person stepping too close in a government lobby, someone asking a question in a parking
lot, even an unfamiliar visitor entering her office—can feel immediately threatening. She watches
doors, startles at unexpected sounds, and becomes acutely alert when she hears anything loud or
abrupt. A dropped file cart in the office, a raised voice in the hallway, a sudden clang from
building maintenance can make her heart race before she has time to think.

She has also become rigid about physical boundaries. Any form of touch feels intolerable to her,
even benign contact. She stiffens if someone reaches toward her arm to get her attention and has
learned to position herself carefully in rooms so that others cannot brush against her. Her husband
has noticed how much she pulls back from contact, and this has become a painful source of distance
between them. Anna does not describe this as rejection of him so much as a feeling that her body
cannot tolerate being approached.

Medical settings are among the places she avoids most consistently. Hospitals, ambulances, and
physical examinations have come to feel bound up with the exposure and its aftermath. She delays
routine care and has canceled appointments rather than walk into a clinic. Even passing an ambulance
on the street can leave her rattled for hours. She knows this avoidance complicates practical parts
of life, but to her these places represent the same uncertainty and loss of control that terrified
her during the event.

Talking about the exposure is itself a trigger. If coworkers mention “that day” or if her husband
asks her to explain what still frightens her, Anna feels trapped between wanting to answer and
knowing she may not be able to. The effort to speak about it can leave her ashamed and agitated when
the words fail. By the time she sought help, the strain was showing in both work and home life: her
rehearsed interactions were making her seem distant, her safe world had become very small, and her
husband was increasingly worried by how much she avoided touch, noise, strangers, and medical care.
What finally brought her in was the realization that she was organizing nearly every day around
preventing surprise. She says she is most afraid that she will never again feel safe anywhere beyond
a few tightly controlled places, and most hopes that she might one day be able to face ordinary
conversations, ordinary sounds, and ordinary appointments without feeling that danger is already
underway.
