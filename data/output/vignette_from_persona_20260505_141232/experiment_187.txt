At 63, Tane had spent most of his adult life in other people’s emergencies. As a community
organizer, he was used to tense public meetings, calls from frightened tenants, arguments in church
basements and school auditoriums, and the steady work of helping neighborhoods respond when systems
failed them. He was divorced, lived alone, and had long taken pride in being the person who stayed
calm when everyone else was upset. The accident that now divided his life into before and after
happened on an ordinary workday while he was driving between meetings. Traffic had slowed near an
intersection where police vehicles had already gathered around an earlier incident. He remembered
the blue uniforms at the roadside, the jerking line of cars, and someone behind him leaning on a
horn. Then came a burst of shouting from outside his driver’s side window—angry voices he could not
place now, only the sharp force of them—and the violent impact of another vehicle hitting his car
hard enough to spin it.

What remained with him from that day came in broken pieces rather than a full story. Some fragments
were painfully exact: the sight of a police officer running toward the wreck, the metallic smell
inside the car, a sudden pressure in his chest as he struggled to breathe, his hands shaking
uncontrollably while someone yelled for him not to move. Other stretches were simply gone. He knew
there had been people pulling at the door, questions being asked, and later a sequence of medical
checks and phone calls, but there were parts he could not retrieve no matter how hard he tried. In
the first sessions he would stop midsentence and press his fingers together as if trying to hold
onto language. He would say he knew what happened, that it was there somewhere, but when he tried to
describe it the words just would not come. This inability to tell it straight unsettled him almost
as much as the crash itself.

In the months afterward, his world became dotted with moments that made no sense to people around
him but felt immediately dangerous to him. At organizing meetings, if a resident raised their voice
in frustration—even in the measured, controlled way people do when they are trying to be
heard—Tane’s body reacted before he could think. His shoulders tightened, his heart began pounding,
and he felt the same internal jolt he had felt in the car when angry voices flared nearby. He could
tell himself he was in a recreation center or nonprofit office, not trapped behind a steering wheel,
but his body did not seem to believe him. He had come to see his own physical reactions as an enemy
presence, something disloyal inside him that kept dragging him back toward danger even when he was
objectively safe.

Police uniforms were especially hard. His work regularly brought him into contact with city
officials, security staff, and officers stationed outside public events. The dark uniforms, badges,
and heavy belts could pull him abruptly into the clearest surviving shard of the accident scene: the
officer moving toward his window while he sat disoriented and pinned in place. On those days he
found reasons to remain at the back of the room, to let younger colleagues handle check-ins,
permits, or conversations that involved uniformed personnel. He did not explain much. He simply said
he was tired or not feeling up to it. Over time, others noticed that he was less present at the
meetings where confrontation was most likely. A colleague took over several neighborhood forums
because Tane had begun leaving early after arguments broke out, and he felt ashamed watching his
role shrink in the work that had once defined him.

He also carried a hard private conviction about the crash. He believed it happened because of the
way he had acted, though when he tried to explain exactly what he should have done
differently—driven another route, reacted faster, not gone out that day—his reasoning circled
without relief. What stayed firm was the conclusion that he had failed in some essential way. That
belief sat alongside a deadened emotional state that frightened him for different reasons. Friends
from church and longtime organizers checked on him, but he often felt as if he were answering from a
great distance. He could describe what others expected him to feel more easily than he could feel
it. He said he was “fine,” then sat alone afterward sensing almost nothing, disturbed by how empty
he had become.

He finally sought help after a public meeting on neighborhood safety. A disagreement in the room
escalated only mildly, but one man’s angry tone froze him where he sat. When he turned, he saw two
officers at the back wall, and his mind filled with the same unwanted images of the wrecked car and
the officer at his window. He left before the meeting ended and sat in his car for nearly an hour,
unable to steady himself enough to drive. What frightened him most was not only that the memories
still broke through, but that he no longer trusted his own body or his own account of what had
happened. He came in hoping, cautiously, that he might find words for the blank and broken parts of
that day, and afraid that if he did not, the life he had built around being dependable and present
would continue to narrow around him.
