David is a 42-year-old ship captain who came to treatment after more than a year of trying to manage
on his own what he still calls “that blast.” He is married and had spent most of his adult life
feeling steady at sea, used to responsibility and to making decisions that affected other people’s
safety. The event that now dominates his inner life took place during a man-made disaster aboard a
commercial vessel moving through a busy port corridor. In his account, the danger arrived with
shocking force and human culpability. There was a sudden explosion on board, a violent bang that
seemed to tear through the metal around him, followed by shouting, objects being hurled by the force
of the blast, and loose equipment skidding and crashing across the deck. He remembers seeing one
crewman’s gear thrown sideways and hearing another man yell for instructions. In the confusion that
followed, emergency responders came in uniforms that he now notices instantly anywhere he goes. What
has stayed with him most sharply is not only the sound and impact, but the conviction that this
happened because someone’s decisions were reckless and that the people responsible have never truly
been held to account.

In the weeks afterward, David tried to return to his routines as if discipline would settle him.
Instead, ordinary moments began to acquire the feeling of threat. A slammed container door,
construction noise on the docks, even a muffled bang from equipment could stop him cold, his body
reacting before he had time to think. Seeing police or military uniforms in the port, at gas
stations, or outside public buildings pulled him back to the responders and the immediate aftermath
of the explosion. Object throwing became especially difficult to tolerate. If a deckhand tossed a
line too sharply, if someone at home threw keys onto the counter, or if he saw a stranger angrily
fling something in a parking lot, David felt a jolt of alarm that made the scene seem suddenly
dangerous.

Over time, his world narrowed in practical ways. He had come to believe that he could not rely on
other people, a view that had settled into his work as much as his home life. On the ship he checked
crew members repeatedly, watched faces for signs of recklessness or volatility, and scanned
constantly for the kind of person who might cause harm. He was vigilant around unfamiliar workers in
the port and in any new setting where he could not quickly judge who was safe. He began turning down
certain duties and delegating or altogether dropping responsibilities he would once have taken on
without hesitation, especially tasks that meant entering unfamiliar facilities or crowded
operational areas. This had consequences for his standing at work; colleagues noticed that he was no
longer willing to go ashore for some meetings or inspections, and his wife noticed how much effort
it took him to leave the predictable spaces he felt he could control.

His need to assess danger shaped even small decisions. In restaurants during shore leave, David
insisted on the seat with the clearest line to the door and the fewest people behind him. In waiting
rooms, he sat at the edge rather than in the center, visibly tense until he had mapped the room.
Unfamiliar places felt intolerable because, as he put it, he could not assess the danger fast
enough. Nights became their own battleground. He had difficulty falling asleep because some part of
him remained convinced he needed to stay alert. He lay awake listening for sounds in the house or
replaying whether doors were locked, telling himself that letting his guard down would be
irresponsible.

At the same time, he avoided his own thoughts with determination. David said that if he let himself
think too directly about the explosion, he would not be able to handle what came up. When images or
fragments pressed in, he pushed them away by keeping busy, walking the decks, checking things twice,
or, increasingly, gambling. What began as occasional time at casinos during port calls or online
betting at home became a regular way to distract himself and produce a different kind of adrenaline,
something he could enter on purpose rather than have forced on him. His wife knew about the money
they had lost before she understood why he kept returning to it.

What finally brought David to seek help was a convergence of strain at home and work. After freezing
during a loud bang on the docks and later refusing an assignment in an unfamiliar port area, he saw
that his attempts to manage were altering the way he captained and the way he lived with his family.
He arrived for treatment guarded, choosing his chair carefully and watching the hallway when anyone
passed in uniform outside the clinic window. He was adamant that the people responsible had never
faced consequences, and there was bitterness in the way he said it. What frightened him most was the
possibility that if he stopped staying alert, something catastrophic would happen again, or that if
he let himself fully think about the disaster, he would come apart. At the same time, he said he was
tired of living as if every bang, every stranger, and every room had to be survived.
