Ethan is a 34-year-old Navy officer who came to treatment after months of feeling increasingly cut
off from his life, his marriage, and the parts of himself he used to recognize. He described the
turning point as a natural disaster during a deployment-related posting near the coast, when a
violent storm surge struck at night and overwhelmed the area with almost no sense of warning once
conditions changed. He remembers the event less as a narrative than as a series of sharp, fixed
images: dark water forcing its way into buildings, bodies pressed together in confusion as people
tried to move through tight spaces, emergency lights catching on faces, and the disorienting mix of
salt, fuel, and alcohol on the breath and clothes of civilians who had been evacuated from nearby
bars and restaurants. In his memory, he is wedged low and sideways in a cramped space, bracing
himself and trying to keep hold of a surface while the structure shuddered and people shouted over
each other. Even now, when the memory comes back, his body seems to return there first; his
shoulders tense, his torso curls and locks, and he feels himself pulled back into that same bent,
compressed position before he has fully realized what he is remembering.

In the weeks afterward, Ethan found that certain moments seemed to seize him without warning.
Passing through crowded places was particularly difficult. In a packed grocery store aisle or a busy
base corridor, he would suddenly become acutely aware of how close other bodies were, how little
room there was to move, and he would feel a jolt of alarm that made immediate sense to him even if
he could not explain it aloud. Specific locations also carried a charge. Walking near the
waterfront, entering low-lit service hallways, or stepping into buildings whose layout reminded him
of where he had been trapped could make him feel as though the past were not fully over. Smell
affected him too. When he caught the odor of alcohol on someone in public or at a social gathering,
it brought back those nighttime images with unnerving force. He did not describe these memories as
coherent stories. They were mostly visual, vivid snapshots, and at times he would say that what
happened felt so extreme that it was hard to believe it had actually happened to him, as if he were
describing something he had seen rather than lived through.

Over time, Ethan's world narrowed. He began avoiding circumstances that reminded him of the
disaster, especially being in the dark. If his wife suggested an evening out, he would push for
staying home or insist on brightly lit places. He stopped meeting up with friends, including other
officers he had once been close to, because bars, restaurants, and crowded gatherings felt
intolerable and because he no longer knew how to be with people who seemed able to relax. His
withdrawal had practical consequences. Friendships thinned out. Invitations slowed. At home, his
wife experienced him as physically present but emotionally absent, and arguments began to circle
around his refusals, his silences, and his inability to explain why ordinary social plans felt
impossible.

Ethan made sense of these changes in harsh, personal terms. He had come to believe that he deserved
to suffer because of what happened. He did not elaborate this as a grand theory so much as a
repeated inner verdict, one he delivered to himself whenever he noticed himself struggling or
pulling away from others. Alongside that, he spoke with striking certainty about having lost
something essential in himself. He said he used to be a happy person and now felt miserable almost
all the time. What unsettled him most was not only the distress itself, but the sense that he no
longer belonged anywhere. Although he remained in uniform and continued functioning in his duties,
he felt set apart from the people around him, including the Navy community that had once given him a
clear identity. In work settings, especially in crowded operational spaces, he could perform, but
internally he felt separate, as if everyone else were moving through a world he could no longer
fully enter.

By the time he sought help, Ethan was living with a persistent sense of isolation that had become
difficult to hide. He came in after his wife told him she felt she was losing him and after
recognizing that he had started organizing too much of his life around what he could avoid. He spoke
carefully, often pausing when the memory became too immediate and his body tightened into that
familiar braced posture. What he feared most was that this estrangement—from friends, from his wife,
from any feeling of belonging—was now simply who he was. What he hoped for, though he said it
tentatively, was to believe that the disaster had happened to him without it having to define the
rest of his life.
