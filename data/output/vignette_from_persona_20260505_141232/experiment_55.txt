childhood, she did so in a steady, matter-of-fact way that contrasted with how quickly her body
tightened whenever she came close to certain details. She grew up in a home where a caregiver

the repeated atmosphere surrounding it: the sound of that person moving through the house, the

felt when her own name was called in that voice. Even now, she speaks as though the danger lived in
sound before it lived in contact. As a child, hearing footsteps, a sharp shift in tone from another
room, or her name spoken in a certain way meant she had only seconds to brace herself. The voice
itself became inseparable from what followed.

Over the years, those sensations did not remain in childhood. Amina says that in adult life she can
be thrown back into the same state by auditory reminders that other people might barely register. If
she hears a voice with a similar cadence or edge, she feels herself go rigid before she has even
thought through why. Hearing a name connected in her mind to the caregiver can stop her cold. She
described moments on job sites when someone calls out a name across a hallway, or a customer raises
their voice from another room, and she feels a flash of alarm that makes the rest of the interaction
difficult to track. The sounds are what seize her first. She does not describe them abstractly; she
describes the bodily certainty that something bad is about to happen.

In the years after leaving home, Amina tried to build a stable adult life. She married, established
herself in a trade, and became known as competent and reliable. But alongside that, she found
herself dwelling again and again on what had been done to her. She can spend long periods replaying
the caregiver’s actions and the failures of the adults around her, turning over who should have
protected her, who ignored what was happening, and how they got to go on with their lives while she
carried it. She knows these thoughts go in circles, but she also feels pulled back to them, as if
letting go would mean excusing what happened.

Her work as a plumber puts her in situations that sharpen some of these difficulties. She enters

certain interactions or keeping exchanges as brief as possible. She does not experience these people
as neutral. Being near someone in authority stirs a familiar sense of exposure, and she would rather

she hears a related name, she finds reasons to step away, take a call outside, or ask someone else
to finish the job.

has come to believe that what happened says something fundamental about the kind of person she is.

there must have been something in her that made her a target. She said, in different ways, that

not feel like proof of strength to her. They sit beside a heavier conviction that she is damaged at
the core.

comfort she seems to take in the life they have built together.

moments. Recently, after hearing a voice at work that closely resembled the caregiver’s, she found

how immediate it still was, and how quickly she returned to the old conclusion that there must be
something fundamentally wrong with her. She came to treatment feeling worn down by a life organized
around bracing, stepping away, and silently replaying what others had done. What she fears most is
