At 71, Tane had spent years being known as the steady one: a married man who showed up for work on
time, counted change carefully, and preferred routine. He worked as a cashier in a busy American
grocery store, standing for long shifts under bright lights while automatic doors opened and shut
all day and families passed through his lane. What brought him to treatment now was not a single
recent event but the way one witnessed trauma had remained with him in scattered, jagged pieces,
returning in the middle of ordinary moments until his world felt narrowed to scanning, bracing, and
enduring.

When he speaks about what happened, the account breaks apart. He had witnessed a traumatic incident
involving others, and even now he cannot give a smooth beginning, middle, and end. He starts with
one fragment, loses the thread, pauses, and shakes his head. He says he only remembers parts of it,
“like missing frames,” and the missing sections trouble him as much as the pieces he does recall. He
knows there was a baby crying. He remembers a door opening hard, then slamming. He remembers the
atmosphere changing instantly, the feeling in his body that something was terribly wrong, and other
people there who should have intervened or protected those at risk. But when he tries to explain the
sequence clearly, he falters. His wife has heard him begin the story many times at the kitchen table
only for him to stop midway, staring past her, frustrated and ashamed that he cannot make it into a
coherent narrative even for himself.

What has remained especially fixed for Tane is not just the scene itself but his conviction about
what it meant. He has come to believe that the people or institutions who should have protected
those involved failed to do so. He speaks with quiet bitterness about authorities and systems that,
in his view, let it happen and then allowed it to pass without anyone truly being held accountable.
He returns to this point with more certainty than he can muster about the factual sequence. To him,
the most intolerable part is that those responsible faced no consequences. That belief sits heavily
beside a more private change he struggles to describe: he feels emotionally blunted, “dead inside,”
as if something in him shut down afterward and never fully came back online.

Over the following months, these reactions embedded themselves into daily life. At work, the store
became a place of constant jolts. The automatic entrance doors, the back room door swinging open, a
stockroom door slamming unexpectedly—each sound could make his whole body react before he had time
to think. If a customer moved suddenly near the register, reaching across the conveyor belt or
stepping quickly into his space, he flinched as though immediate danger were present. He began
watching people closely in line, tracking their faces, hands, and posture for signs they might do
harm. He knew this made him look stern and distracted, but he felt unable to relax his guard. Even
moments that were only vaguely uncomfortable could set him on edge; a shift in noise, an unsettled
feeling in the aisle, the sense that something was off in the environment was enough to make him
brace.

Babies were especially hard. When an infant cried in his checkout line, he felt his chest tighten
and his attention narrow. He would try to keep scanning groceries and speaking politely, but
internally he was no longer in the present moment. He could not always say exactly what image or
thought had been triggered, only that the sound went through him like an alarm. Over time he began
quietly adjusting his habits where he could. If another cashier was free, he might signal for a
switch when a family with a crying baby approached. On breaks he stayed in the stockroom longer than
necessary, away from the front entrance and its constant opening doors. At home, he withdrew into
silence more often, leaving his wife to interpret his distance as best she could. He was not quick
to anger; rather, he seemed absent, flattened, and unreachable.

The accumulation finally became harder to ignore after a recent shift when a child started crying
near the entrance just as the outer doors banged open and someone moved abruptly beside him. Tane
recoiled so visibly that the customer in front of him asked if he was all right. He finished the
transaction with shaking hands and later sat in his car unable to settle. What frightened him most
was not only the reaction itself but the sense that after all this time he still lived as if danger
might break in at any second, while the event remained something he could neither fully remember nor
fully tell. He came for help because he is tired of feeling hollow and watchful, and because he
wants, if not a perfect account, then at least some way to live with what happened without every
door, every cry, and every sudden movement making the world feel unsafe again.
