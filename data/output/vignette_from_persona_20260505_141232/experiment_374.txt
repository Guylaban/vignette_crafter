Mele was 35, a married woman of Pacific Islander background who worked as an accountant in a mid-
sized office, and by the time she came for treatment she described living as if danger were always
just a few seconds away. The event she returned to again and again had happened after an ordinary
workday. She had stayed late closing out month-end figures, left the office tired and hungry, and
was walking toward her car when she became aware of footsteps behind her in the parking structure.
At first she told herself not to overreact, but the sound came faster and closer. Before she could
turn properly, a man was on her. She saw his face at close range as he demanded her bag and phone.
When he grabbed at her and she felt his hand on her unexpectedly, her body seemed to lock. She
remembered the cold concrete smell of the garage, the metallic taste that filled her mouth, and the
way she had ended up held in a twisted, braced position while trying not to provoke him. Even now,
when the memory returned, her shoulders tightened and her body seemed to fold into that same
defensive posture as if the robbery were happening again. Part of what unsettled her was that the
scene felt both brutally vivid and strangely unreal; she could describe details of his face and the
taste in her mouth, yet would also say that it was so extreme it was hard to believe it had really
happened to her.

In the weeks afterward, Mele found herself replaying the robbery in ways that felt punishing and
endless. She dwelt on what she had done wrong: why she had stayed late, why she had not asked a
coworker to walk with her, why she had not reacted differently when she heard the footsteps. She
went over and over how it could have been prevented, as if there must have been one correct decision
that would have erased the whole thing. The more she revisited it, the more convinced she became
that it had happened because of the sort of person she was—too careless, too slow, too unprepared.
Alongside that was a broader conviction that the world itself was dangerous, that what had happened
to her was not an exception but proof of how exposed everyone really was.

At work, where she had once been known as steady and meticulous, the environment itself became full
of moments she dreaded. If she heard footsteps behind her in the hallway or parking garage, her
whole body went rigid before she could think. When a colleague passed too close or touched her
shoulder unexpectedly to get her attention, she recoiled, embarrassed and shaken. On one occasion
she caught sight of a man near the building entrance whose build and face resembled the offender’s,
and she froze long enough that another employee had to ask if she was all right. She began lingering
at her desk until others were leaving so she would not walk out alone, then on other days asked her
husband to pick her up rather than go to her car by herself. These habits gradually narrowed her
routines and made her feel less competent, especially in a job that depended on her being composed
and focused.

She also described a constant inability to settle, even in places other people insisted were safe.
At home with her husband, with the doors locked, she still could not truly relax. If he was running
late and did not answer a text right away, she did not think “traffic” or “busy”; she went
immediately to the sense that something bad must be happening. Reassurance did not land. She said
that even when people told her she was safe, she could not make herself believe it. Her body
remained braced, as if safety were only a story other people could still accept.

When memories surged, Mele sometimes tried deliberately to blank out or leave her body rather than
feel what was happening inside her. She described staring into space, going numb on purpose, trying
to disappear from herself for a few moments. These episodes frightened her too, because they added
to the sense that the robbery had altered something fundamental. She was troubled not only by the
memory, but by what she believed it said about her—that she had failed, that her actions had brought
it on, that she could not trust either the world or her own judgment.

What finally brought her to treatment was the accumulation of these moments. A coworker had come up
behind her at the office and lightly touched her arm while asking a routine question, and Mele
jerked away so sharply that she spilled coffee and burst into tears. She was mortified, and later
that night she told her husband she could not keep living in a state where footsteps, delays, or a
passing resemblance to one man could undo her entire day. Coming in, what frightened her most was
the possibility that she would never again feel safe enough to inhabit her own body or move through
ordinary places without scanning for danger. What she hoped for, carefully and almost skeptically,
was to be able to leave work at the end of the day and believe she was simply going home.
