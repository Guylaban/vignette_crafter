Nadia Haddad is a 51-year-old married hotel manager who came to treatment after months of telling
herself that what happened was over and that she should have been able to move on. The event she
returns to began during a weekday shift at the hotel, when a strong chemical odor started spreading
through a lower corridor near service areas and housekeeping storage. At first it registered as
something sharp and wrong in the air, stronger than ordinary cleaning products, the kind of smell
that catches in the throat and makes the body react before the mind has caught up. As she moved
through the building trying to understand what was happening, the odor intensified. Staff were
confused, guests were alarmed, and the atmosphere changed quickly from routine workplace pressure to
something that felt dangerous and out of control. Nadia remembers the smell most vividly: harsh,
sterile, and invasive, close to the antiseptic scent she now says she cannot tolerate. The confusion
of the scene, the urgency in people’s voices, and the sense that the building itself was no longer
safe have stayed with her in a way that feels immediate rather than past.

In the weeks afterward, she expected to feel shaken for a while, but instead she found that
reminders caught her off guard in very physical ways. When she encountered hospital or antiseptic
smells, even briefly, her body reacted before she could reassure herself. Walking past a clinic
entrance, catching the disinfectant scent in a restroom, or entering an area of the hotel just after
deep cleaning could bring a rush of bodily alarm. She described her heart pounding, her muscles
tightening, and a wave of dread that made her want to leave immediately. Because the original
exposure happened at work, the hotel itself became more complicated for her. She did not think of it
simply as her job anymore. Under stress, especially during busy shifts with complaints, staffing
problems, or unexpected maintenance issues, she could feel herself become keyed up in a way that
seemed out of proportion even to her. It was not just pressure; it was the sense that something
could suddenly go wrong again.

Her daily environment now confronts her with reminders she cannot fully control. As a hotel manager,
she is expected to move through guest floors, inspect housekeeping standards, respond to operational
problems, and remain composed during moments of disruption. Instead, Nadia has found herself
hesitating outside recently sanitized rooms, sending other staff to check areas with strong cleaning
smells, or lingering in her office longer than she once would have. She has become acutely alert to
loud or unexpected sounds in the building—the crash of a dropped tray, a slammed service door, a
sudden alarm, raised voices over a radio. Each one jolts her. She knows these sounds are common in
hotel operations, yet she experiences them as if her whole system has been primed for danger. On
difficult days, a loud noise in the corridor can leave her shaky and breathless, embarrassed in
front of employees she is meant to supervise.

Nadia also describes her nights as increasingly difficult. She lies in bed tired but unable to
settle, feeling that she has to stay alert. As she explains it, falling asleep means letting her
guard down, and some part of her remains unconvinced that it is safe to do that. She listens for
noises in the house and outside, noticing every creak or sudden sound. If something startles her,
she feels the same surge through her body that she gets at work. Her husband has noticed that she
startles more easily and paces the house late at night, though Nadia tends to minimize it and say
she is just not sleepy yet. She has become frustrated by how much energy it takes to get through a
normal day after another restless night.

Over time these patterns have narrowed her world. Stress at work now feels less manageable, not
because she lacks experience, but because pressure, chemical smells, and sudden noises all seem to
land on the same already taut system. She has tried to compensate by becoming more controlled and
watchful, but that has made her feel less like herself with staff and at home. What finally brought
her to seek help was a recent incident at the hotel when a strong disinfectant smell from a cleaned
area, followed by a loud bang from a service cart, left her visibly shaken in front of employees.
She was disturbed not only by the intensity of the reaction but by the realization that the
workplace she has spent years competently running now feels unpredictable to her. Coming to
treatment, Nadia says she wants to be able to sleep without feeling she must keep watch and to walk
through her hotel without bracing for the next smell or sound. What frightens her most is the sense
that her body still acts as though the danger is happening now.
