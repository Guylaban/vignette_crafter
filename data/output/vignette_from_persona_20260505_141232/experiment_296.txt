images rather than remembering something she had lived through. At the same time, she said the whole

as much as the memory itself.

In the days after she was released, Sophie tried to return quickly to work at the firehouse, telling
herself that routine would make things normal again. Instead, ordinary parts of her day began to
feel charged. The clang of equipment, a door slamming in the station, even a sudden voice behind her
could make her whole body jolt before she had time to think. She was embarrassed by how violently
she startled at sounds that did not seem to affect anyone else. Around the crew she tried to laugh
it off, but inside she was already braced, scanning for the next noise. Even when she was home alone
in her apartment with the locks checked and the lights on, she could not settle into the feeling of
being safe. She would sit rigid on the couch, muscles tight, telling herself there was no danger
while her body refused to believe it.

What unsettled her most were the moments when the past seemed to take over the present. In crowded
spaces, especially when strangers moved too close around her, she would suddenly feel as if she were
back in captivity. Once, stopping at a grocery store after shift change, she found herself hemmed in
near the checkout line, carts pressed close and voices overlapping. For a few seconds she was no
longer in the store. Her heart surged, her breathing changed, and she was acting as if the event
were happening again, trying to angle her body toward an exit without fully knowing why. Afterward
