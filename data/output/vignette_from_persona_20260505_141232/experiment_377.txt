Daniel Weber is a 45-year-old married American journalist who came to treatment after years of
trying to manage on his own what he still calls “that time I was held.” While reporting abroad, he
was taken captive and kept for a period in a small room by armed men who controlled when he could
sleep, eat, move, and speak. What remains most vivid to him is not an orderly story but a set of
sensory moments that feel as if they are still happening. He remembers being forced down onto a
chair and restrained, the sharp strain through his neck as his head was yanked back and held there
during questioning. He remembers the closeness of other bodies in the room, hands gripping his
shoulders and jaw without warning, and the stale smell of liquor on one captor’s breath when the man
leaned over him to talk. In his recollection, these details are not distant. The scene returns “like
a film,” as Daniel puts it, complete and moving, and when it comes back he feels the same terror he
felt then, as if his body has not understood that the captivity ended.

In the months after his release, Daniel tried to return to work and to the life he had with his
wife. He told himself that as a journalist he should be able to keep going, file copy, tolerate
pressure, and stay composed. Instead he found that memories forced themselves into his mind without
his wanting them. He could be sitting at his desk editing an interview transcript and suddenly the
image of the chair, the hands, the room would start replaying with a terrible immediacy. When this
happened, he did not experience it as remembering in the ordinary sense; it felt more like being
seized by the event again. What frightened him most was how quickly terror returned. He was certain,
in those moments, that he was about to be overpowered, even when he was in his own office or at
home.

Over time he became increasingly watchful in ways that shaped his days. In the newsroom, where
phones rang, doors slammed, and conversations rose unexpectedly behind him, Daniel stayed on edge. A
dropped camera bag or a burst of laughter from across the room could make him jolt before he had
time to think. He often reacted as if danger were present even when nothing was wrong, scanning the
room and tracking movement before he could settle himself. He began choosing a seat with a clear
view of the door in editorial meetings and avoiding crowded press events when he could. He knew
colleagues noticed that he startled easily at loud or sudden sounds, but he could not stop
monitoring the environment for what might happen next.

His specific triggers are woven into ordinary moments. Neck pain is one of the strongest. If he
wakes with stiffness after sleeping badly, or if long hours bent over a laptop bring on an ache, he
is abruptly pulled back to the position in which his head was forced and held. The pain does not
merely remind him; it seems to open the whole scene. Physical touch can do the same. His wife
placing a hand unexpectedly on the back of his neck, a colleague brushing past him in a hallway,
even a routine pat on the shoulder can produce an immediate surge of alarm before he recognizes
where he is. He has tried to explain to his wife that it is not about her, but the recoil has
nonetheless changed the texture of their marriage. The smell of alcohol is especially potent. On
assignment receptions, in restaurants, or passing someone on the street who has been drinking, he
catches the odor and is instantly back in the room with a captor leaning close enough for Daniel to
smell liquor on his breath.

These experiences have accumulated into a narrower life. Daniel now structures his work to reduce
the likelihood of being caught off guard, preferring phone interviews over in-person events and
leaving noisy gatherings early. At home he is tense around sudden sounds and remains superalert even
in safe settings, listening for shifts in the house and reacting before he has assessed what he
heard. He has come to believe that his mind can turn on him without warning, that memory can break
through at any moment and trap him in the worst part of his life.

He finally sought help after a recent incident at work when a coworker came up behind him to get his
attention just as neck pain had been building from a long day at the computer. Daniel spun around in
panic, heart racing, disoriented and ashamed, then had to leave the office after the familiar film
of the captivity began replaying in full. What he fears most is that he will remain at the mercy of
these moments, always one sound, one touch, or one smell away from terror. What he hopes for,
cautiously, is not to erase what happened but to be able to live beside it without feeling dragged
back into captivity each time his body or surroundings echo the past.
