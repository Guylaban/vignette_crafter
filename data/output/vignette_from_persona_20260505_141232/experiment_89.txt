life working as a welder. He describes himself as someone who was used to hard physical work, long
shifts, noise, heat, and the ordinary risks of a shop floor. The event he keeps returning to
happened while he was driving home after work. Another car struck his vehicle with enough force that
he was trapped in the seat, twisted and pinned in a way his body still seems to remember better than
his mind can control. What has stayed with him is not a single picture but the whole collision
arriving at once: the violent impact, the angle of his torso jammed against the seat belt and door,
the noise of traffic around him, and the smell in the other car when people came near enough for him
to notice it. He recalls registering alcohol on someone’s breath or clothes at the scene, and that
detail remains especially potent to him now, as if that smell carries the entire crash inside it.

In the weeks after the collision, Tavita noticed that certain moments did not feel like remembering
at all. If he passed a roadside accident and saw damaged cars, even briefly, he did not experience
it as a distant reminder. He felt himself pulled back into the wreck with such force that his whole
body seemed to assume the same position it had been in when he was trapped. He would stiffen, lean,
and brace without meaning to, as though he were once again folded into metal and unable to move
freely. When this happened, he also felt pain in the same parts of his body that had hurt during the
crash. He spoke about this with a kind of frustrated certainty: it was not just that he thought
about the pain; he felt it, sharply and specifically, in the same places.

The same thing happens with sound. Ordinary car noises can abruptly overtake him—the screech or thud

work, where the shop opens onto a road and cars can be heard intermittently through the day, he
finds himself going tense before he can think through why. On some days a sound from outside is
enough to stop him mid-task. For a moment the welding bay is gone and the crash feels completely
real across all his senses at once. He is back in the vehicle, his body set in that trapped posture,
pain flaring where he was injured, hearing traffic as danger rather than background noise. Because
his trade requires steadiness and concentration, these episodes have become especially disruptive.

indoor tasks, and taking routes to work that reduce the chances of passing fresh accidents on the
road.

The smell of alcohol is another powerful jolt. In everyday life it catches him off guard in ways he
never anticipated: on a customer who comes into the shop after lunch, on someone standing too close
at a gas station, even faintly on a passerby. He does not describe this as simple dislike. What
frightens him is how instantly the smell seems to collapse time. The crash is not something that
happened then; it is happening again, with the same sensory force and the same pain. He has become

damaged vehicles, reacting to traffic noise before his wife is even aware of it. He has not built an
elaborate explanation for these reactions so much as a practical one: certain things are dangerous

day. What finally brought him to seek help was a recent moment when he saw two cars pulled over

to the crash. What he fears most is that these moments will keep ambushing him at work, on the road,
and in front of his wife, and that he will never again be able to trust his own body when memory
takes hold.
