the memories do not come as a smooth story. They arrive in jagged fragments: the flash of his hand
reaching across the kitchen counter, the metallic glint of a heavy flashlight by the door, the
bathroom mirror catching part of his face behind her, her own breathing suddenly too loud. She says
it is like looking at a stack of photographs with missing pictures in between. There are pieces she
can see with unbearable clarity and gaps she cannot bridge, and when one image surfaces she feels
the same terror in her body that she felt then, as if she is back in the apartment and has only
seconds to work out what is about to happen.

What stands out in her account is how often the violence was preceded by small shifts she learned to
monitor. She remembers looking into mirrors to locate him without turning her back, trying to tell
from his posture or where his hands were whether she needed to get out of the room. In those moments
she became alert to ordinary objects as possible weapons. A lamp, a belt, a glass bottle, a kitchen
utensil, the flashlight near the door—everything in the room had to be assessed for what it could
become. She had come to believe that safety depended on noticing first, before he moved, before his
expression changed, before her mind had even caught up. Even now, she describes her body as reacting
before she has had time to think. If someone enters a room too quickly or if she notices a hard
object in another person’s hand, her shoulders tense, her chest tightens, and she is already
scanning exits and distances before she can tell herself where she is.

In the years after leaving that relationship, these responses did not stay confined to memories.
They became part of how she moves through the world. Linh checks mirrors repeatedly, in store
aisles, in parking garages, in the reflective glass of building doors, using them to see who is
behind her and whether anyone is approaching. She constantly scans her surroundings for threat and
finds herself tracking people with particular intensity, trying to judge who might be dangerous. She
notices potential weapons immediately in any setting. When she enters a tutoring session in a
family’s home, she is aware within seconds of the scissors on a desk, a heavy paperweight, a knife
block visible from the kitchen. At the library, while a student works through algebra problems, part
of her attention is fixed on who is walking between the shelves, who is lingering too long, what
objects are within reach. She knows this makes it hard to settle fully into teaching, and she
sometimes shortens sessions or shifts them online when she feels too exposed.

Linh experiences this not simply as nervousness but as evidence that she has been permanently
changed for the worse. She says she used to feel part of the world, able to trust people at least
provisionally, but now she is certain you can never know who will harm you. A stranger’s politeness
does not reassure her; familiarity does not reassure her either. What frightens her most is the
sense that danger can be hidden inside ordinary relationships, exactly where other people assume
safety. She often feels like an outsider even in benign settings, including among other parents and
tutors, watching herself from a distance as they chat casually while she is silently mapping exits,
reflective surfaces, and the objects on the table.

This has affected her marriage in quiet but accumulating ways. Her husband experiences her as
distracted in restaurants because she keeps checking mirrored walls or windows and choosing seats
that let her monitor the room. She has become reluctant to attend social gatherings in unfamiliar
homes, especially if she cannot quickly see all the rooms or who is coming and going. She does not
describe this as caution that can be relaxed; to her it feels like the minimum required to prevent
being caught off guard.

She sought help after a recent tutoring session in a suburban home. A student’s father came into the

thought, her body had lurched into alarm. The image of the flashlight by her former apartment door
hit her with such force that the rest of the evening broke into disconnected snapshots. She finished
the session, drove home shaking, and then sat in her car unable to stop checking the rearview
mirror. What brought her in was not only how intense that moment was, but the sinking conviction

she says she still hopes for something modest but profound—to be able to teach, to sit with her
