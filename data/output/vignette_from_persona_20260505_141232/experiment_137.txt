Denise is a 50-year-old single woman who has spent most of her adult life working as a jeweler, much
of it at a bench under bright task lighting, handling tiny clasps, stones, and metal settings that
require steadiness and concentration. When she describes the assault, she does so unevenly. Certain
images are fixed with painful sharpness, while the rest seems to fall away. She can tell a therapist
about the instant she recognized the man’s face and the sick lurch that followed, and then there are
stretches she cannot account for at all. She says it is as if the memory comes in jagged pieces: a
hand, a smell, the shock of realizing what was happening. If she gets too close to the parts she
cannot fully place, she cuts herself off. She has learned to push the memory away before it reaches
any ending she can bear to think about.

In the months after the assault, what stayed with Denise was not a coherent story but these abrupt
returns of sensation and image. Fragments would come without warning and vanish just as quickly,
leaving her unsettled and ashamed that she could not “just remember it normally.” She had come to
see this as further proof that she was, in her words, “a lousy coper.” Rather than viewing her
reactions as something anyone might struggle with after being assaulted, she took them as evidence
of personal inadequacy. What frightened and humiliated her most was not only that she had been
attacked, but that she now felt altered by it. She often described feeling dirty or contaminated, as
though something of the assault remained on her no matter how much time had passed. Alongside that
was a deadened emotional state she found hard to explain: long stretches in which she felt flat,
unreachable, and cut off from herself.

Her daily life narrowed around vigilance. Denise says that strangers approaching her in ordinary
situations—a person stepping too close in a store aisle, someone entering behind her, a customer
moving toward her unexpectedly—can make her feel immediately threatened. She does not experience
this as an overreaction; to her it feels like simple realism, as though danger has become visible in
places other people move through carelessly. Whenever she enters a room or building, she checks
where the exits are before she can settle. This happens automatically at supply stores, at the front
counter of the jewelry shop, in waiting rooms, restaurants, and even office buildings she has
visited many times. She now feels safe in only a very limited number of places, mainly those she
knows intimately and can control. Outside those spaces, she remains braced.

Her work has become one of the clearest places these changes show up. In the jewelry studio, Denise
is especially alert to who comes in and how close they stand to her bench. If a stranger lingers or
approaches from an angle she cannot easily track, she feels her body tense and her attention shift
to where she could get out. There have been days when a wave of nausea has come over her so suddenly
that she has had to step away from a repair in progress and go sit in the back until it passes. She
notices the same nausea when something reminds her of the offender, especially if she sees him or
someone she thinks might be him. The recognition does not have to last long; even the possibility is
enough to turn her stomach and leave her shaken for the rest of the day.

Other reminders catch her in less obvious ways. Conversation about people losing loved ones can
affect her strongly. Hearing others talk about bereavement seems to touch something raw in her, and
she becomes unsettled without always being able to explain why. The reaction feels immediate and
bodily, as though the discussion has opened a door to something she has worked hard to keep shut.
She then goes quiet or withdraws, not wanting to be pulled further into thoughts she does not feel
able to manage.

Over time, Denise has organized her life around a small map of places she can tolerate and many more
she would rather avoid. The cost has been practical and personal. Running errands takes more
planning. Ordinary encounters feel like ordeals. At work she has become more guarded, less available
for casual conversation, and more likely to retreat to the back room when she is overwhelmed. Being
single, she manages much of this alone, and that solitude has made her emotional numbness feel even
starker.

What finally brought Denise to seek help was the sense that her world had become too small and too
governed by threat. She is tired of feeling contaminated and ashamed of how she has responded. At
the same time, she is frightened of what might happen if she lets herself remember more fully. She
wants relief from the constant scanning and the nausea and hopes she might one day feel present in
her own life again, but she is afraid that if she stops pushing the memory away, it will overwhelm
her completely.
