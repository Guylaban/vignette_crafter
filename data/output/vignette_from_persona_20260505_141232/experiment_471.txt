Thomas is a 52-year-old American film director of European background who came to treatment after
months of feeling increasingly estranged from himself in the aftermath of a life-threatening
accident. He had been on location for a night shoot when the accident happened, working late with a
small crew on a rural road closed for filming. A vehicle movement had to be reset, lights were being
repositioned, and in the confusion of shouted cues, engine noise, and people moving through patches
of darkness, Thomas found himself suddenly in the wrong place at the wrong time. He has never found
a satisfying way to tell the story from beginning to end. When he tries, the account breaks apart.
What comes instead are sharply defined images: the brutal white spill of production lights against
black surroundings, a vehicle where it should not have been, the instant of realizing it was coming
toward him, the flash of metal, the ground rushing up. He says the memory is “all pictures,” as if
the event exists in frozen frames rather than words, and those frames remain painfully clear.

In the weeks afterward, Thomas was disturbed by how immediate those images felt. He did not describe
a vague recollection but scenes with the kind of detail he would normally notice professionally:
angles, contrast, glare, movement. That precision frightened him. He could not make the event cohere
into a narrative he could master, and this left him circling around it mentally, asking why it had
happened to him at all. He found himself returning to that question when he was alone in the
morning, in the car, or after work when the house had gone quiet. There was no answer that settled
him. Instead, the repeated searching seemed to leave him more aware of how abruptly life had split
into before and after.

Since the accident, darkness has become especially hard for him to tolerate. On sets, he now avoids
being present for night work when he can delegate it, and if he cannot, he stays close to banks of
light or finds reasons to step away while scenes are being prepared. The combination of darkness and
sudden sound is particularly potent. A dropped piece of equipment, a slammed production van door, an
unexpected call over a speaker can make his whole body jolt before he has time to think. Crew
members have noticed him whip around at noises that would once have been routine for him. He scans
constantly now, listening for what might happen next, noticing every shift in sound on set and in
ordinary life. Even at home, a pan clattering in the kitchen or a neighbor’s car backfiring can make
him jump hard enough to spill a drink or lose his place in conversation.

Thomas has tried to manage this in ways that make sense to him. He keeps himself busy, filling the
day with edits, scheduling decisions, emails, and streaming something in the background when he is
not working, anything to prevent his mind from settling where the images can return. When a location
scout proposes a dim warehouse, a tunnel, or an exterior night sequence, he often reshapes the plan
or sends someone else. He tells himself this is practical, but he is also aware that he is arranging
his life around not being in circumstances that remind him of the accident. Over time this has
narrowed both his work and his relationship. His partner has watched him stay up later with
television on, then hesitate to walk through a dark hallway without switching on multiple lights.
Thomas feels exposed by this, ashamed not only of the reactions themselves but of what he thinks
they say about him.

What weighs on him most is not only fear but the judgment he has come to make about himself. He had
always thought of himself as composed under pressure, someone other people relied on when conditions
were chaotic. Now he sees himself startling at ordinary sounds, avoiding darkness, and failing to
say plainly what happened, and he has come to believe this means he is inadequate. He says his
reactions show he is “a lousy coper.” More than once he has told his therapist that he scarcely
recognizes himself. The person he knew—a director able to work through noise, unpredictability, and
long nights—feels far away from the man who is now constantly on guard.

He finally sought help after a recent night shoot when a metal stand crashed to the ground behind
him. He spun around so violently that the set went silent, and for a moment he could not speak.
Later, sitting in his car and staring at his own reflection in the dark windshield, he felt the
familiar thought with unusual force: I am not myself anymore. He has come to treatment because he
does not want his life to keep shrinking around these moments, and because what frightens him most
is the possibility that this unrecognizable version of himself is permanent.
