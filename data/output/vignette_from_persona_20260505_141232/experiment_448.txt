Marisol Alvarez is a 34-year-old married woman who came to treatment after months of telling herself
she should be able to handle things on her own. She works as a prison guard, a job she once took
pride in for its structure and steadiness, but she now moves through her shifts with a constant
sense that danger can break through at any moment. The fear feels familiar to her. It reaches back
to the period when, as a young woman fleeing violence and instability in her home country, she
traveled with family members and other refugees under the control of armed men and local officials
who decided where people stood, sat, waited, and moved. One night that remains sharply fixed in her
mind, the group was stopped in a crowded holding area by uniformed men shouting instructions. The
air was sour with sweat and alcohol. Marisol remembers the slurred voice of one guard barking
orders, the sound of boots scraping, people crying, and the way everyone around her became instantly
obedient because disobedience felt unthinkable. She can still hear those commands in her head as if
they are happening beside her.

She describes that memory as painfully clear, not blurred by time. When it returns, it does not feel
like a distant story she is recounting but like a scene that arrives in full detail, with the
shouted words and harsh tones coming back first. If she tries to say more once it starts, she
quickly becomes flooded and has to stop. In early sessions she would begin to describe the holding
area, then fall silent, press her hands together, and say that she could hear them again. What
frightened her most was not only what had happened then, but how easily the feeling of helplessness
still took over her body now.

Over the years, she had come to believe that the world is fundamentally dangerous and that people
are not what they seem. She notices herself scanning others for signs of hidden threat, especially
anyone wearing a uniform or giving directions in a clipped, authoritative tone. This has become
particularly difficult in her workplace. At the prison, uniforms are everywhere, including her own,
and orders are part of every shift briefing. When a supervisor gives a sudden instruction from
behind her or an inmate moves abruptly in her peripheral vision, Marisol reacts before she has time
to think, with a jolt of alarm that leaves her heart racing and her muscles tight. She knows,
afterward, that nothing has actually happened, but in the moment her body is certain she is in
immediate danger.

She has adapted her days around this sense of threat. In the staff room she chooses seats with a
wall behind her and a clear view of the door. In meetings she positions herself where no one can
approach unseen. At home, in restaurants or family gatherings, she does the same, to the point that
her husband has noticed she cannot simply sit wherever there is space. Unexpected movement nearby
can make her flinch hard enough to embarrass her, and she is left feeling exposed and ashamed, as if
she has revealed something she cannot control.

Certain sensory cues are especially potent. The smell of alcohol can instantly pull her back to that
night with the uniformed men, especially if it comes off someone’s breath unexpectedly. At work, if
she catches that smell on a coworker during the end of a shift, she becomes tense and watchful for
the rest of the hour. To manage what happens inside her, she often turns up loud music or the
television when she gets home, trying to drown out the replay of voices in her head. She also keeps
her distance from authority figures when she can, limiting casual contact with supervisors and
avoiding situations where she might be singled out or directly instructed unless absolutely
necessary. This has begun to affect her standing at work; she worries she seems difficult or
detached, though to her it feels safer not to be too visible.

When she cannot sleep, she dwells on how the event might have been prevented, mentally reworking
each choice and each moment of obedience, as if there had been some correct action that would have
changed everything. These thoughts leave her exhausted and no more settled. She says she no longer
feels like the person she was before all of this, or even the person she expected to become in
adulthood. The uniform she wears for work adds another layer of disorientation: sometimes she
catches sight of herself and feels like she has become part of something she once feared.

What finally brought Marisol to seek help was a recent incident at work when a supervisor called out
a sharp instruction and another officer moved suddenly at her side; she spun and recoiled so
dramatically that several colleagues stared. Afterward she sat in her car shaking, overwhelmed by
the sound of the command still echoing in her mind. She came to treatment frightened by how quickly
she can feel transported into danger, and hoping, cautiously, that she might find a way to live in
her marriage, her work, and her own body without always bracing for the next threat.
