At 28, Amina had been in the United States only a few years, married, and working long days as a
tailor when she finally came for help. In the first meetings she spoke carefully and sparingly, as
if each sentence had to be checked before it was safe to release. What she did describe about the
torture was concrete and vivid in a way that made clear why ordinary moments now felt perilous. She
had been held in a closed room and subjected to repeated abuse while unable to leave. What stayed
with her most sharply was the bodily certainty of being trapped: the position of the door in
relation to her, the sound of movement outside it, the feeling that danger could begin again at any
second and that she would have no route out. Even now, when she spoke of it, her eyes moved first to
the office door before returning to the conversation.

In the months after, Amina found that this sense of immediate threat did not remain in the past. She
could be in the tailoring shop, bent over a hem or measuring a client, and suddenly feel as if she
were back in that room. These episodes came on without warning. For a few moments the shop around
her seemed to fall away, and she reacted as though the torture were happening again. The whir of a
sewing machine behind her or the sound of someone stepping into the workroom could coincide with a
bodily jolt so strong that she would stop mid-stitch and turn sharply toward the door. She did not
describe these moments as memories in the ordinary sense; to her they felt alarmingly present, as
though the past had broken through and replaced where she actually was.

Outside those episodes, Amina lived in a state of relentless watchfulness. As soon as she entered
any room or building, she automatically searched for exits. She did this in stores, in relatives’
apartments, in restaurants, and in the clinic waiting room. At work she had developed the habit of
choosing her station based on how clearly she could see the entrance and whether there was another
way out through the back. If customers or coworkers shifted furniture and blocked a pathway, she
noticed immediately. She said she could not settle until she had mapped the doors in her mind. Even
then, she remained superalert, tracking who came in, where they stood, and whether anything in the
room limited her movement. This constant monitoring shaped her day so thoroughly that it no longer
felt optional; it was simply what she had to do in order to be anywhere.

Over time, Amina organized more and more of her life around staying away from what felt dangerous
inside her as well as around her. She avoided situations likely to stir strong emotion, including
family gatherings where people might ask personal questions or talk sentimentally about the past.
She kept conversations practical and brief, and if she sensed herself becoming upset, she would
leave the room or busy her hands with mending, pressing, or folding fabric. Her husband had begun to
experience her as distant, not because she did not care for him, but because she could not bear the
feeling of becoming emotionally opened up. Strong feeling seemed to her less like something to move
through than something that could overtake her.

She also avoided relaxation. This was one of the things she said people least understood. Others
suggested rest, tea, prayer, music, or lying down after work, but relaxing made her feel exposed. If
her body softened even slightly, she became uneasy, as though letting her guard down would invite
something terrible to happen. At home, she kept herself occupied late into the evening with small
sewing tasks or cleaning, preferring fatigue to the vulnerability of calm. Rest did not register as
relief; it felt dangerous.

Her avoidance extended to care. Although she had physical complaints and had been urged by her
husband to see a doctor, Amina stayed away from medical settings and delayed psychological help as
well. Being examined, asked questions, or sitting in a closed office with no control over what might
happen felt intolerable. She had told herself repeatedly that she should manage on her own. By the
time she presented for treatment, the strain had become harder to contain. Flashbacks were
interrupting her work more often, and her constant scanning in the tailoring shop left her exhausted
by the end of the day. She came in only after her husband offered to accompany her and after she
confirmed more than once where the exits were. What frightened her most was not only the return of
the torture in these abrupt, engulfing moments, but the possibility that she might never again feel
safe enough to stop watching. What she hoped for, cautiously, was not to forget, but to be able to
sit in a room, finish a seam, and come home to her husband without feeling as though danger were
still right beside her.
