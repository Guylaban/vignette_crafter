Kwame is a 68-year-old widowed African American policy analyst who came to treatment after months of
telling himself that surviving should have been enough. The illness that changed his life began
abruptly. He had been at work, reading through a dense policy brief and preparing notes for a
meeting, when he was hit by a sudden wave of physical collapse that did not feel explainable or
manageable. What remained most vivid was not a medical summary but the immediate conviction that his
body had turned against him without warning. In the hospital, surrounded by alarms, urgent voices,
and the sight of clinicians moving quickly around him, he believed he might die. He remembers
moments of watching people speak over him and around him while his attention narrowed to his own
breathing and the frightening sense that he could no longer rely on his body to do what it had
always done automatically. For Kwame, the terror was not abstract. It was the realization, in real
time, that the threat was inside him.

In the weeks after he was discharged, he expected to feel shaken and then gradually relieved.
Instead, the experience seemed to remain unfinished. At times he could describe what happened in a
flat, detached way, almost as if he were reporting on another man’s hospitalization. He would
recount the sequence of events to his adult daughter or to his physician with a strange numbness
that unsettled him; he noticed that he sounded calm while feeling cut off, as though the memory
belonged to a stranger. At other times the opposite happened. A bodily sensation, a pause in his
breathing, or the effort of trying to remember a detail from the hospital would bring the whole
experience forward with crushing immediacy. Then the illness no longer felt like something in the
past. It felt current, as if he were once again inside that hospital bed with danger unfolding
around him. When that happened he became so emotionally overwhelmed that he had to stop talking, sit
down, and wait for the surge to pass.

As the months went on, Kwame became increasingly preoccupied with the idea that his body was
fundamentally unreliable. He had come to believe that he could no longer trust it, not even in
ordinary moments. If his heart rate shifted, if he felt tired, if his stomach tightened, he did not
take these as passing sensations. To him they were warnings that another crisis could begin at any
moment. What frightened him most was that his body continued to react as though danger were still
present even when he knew, rationally, that he was sitting safely in his own living room. This left
him feeling betrayed by himself. He said more than once that his body was acting like an enemy, and
that no amount of reassurance fully reached the place in him that expected catastrophe.

That expectation shaped his daily life. At home, where he used to enjoy quiet evenings reading or
reviewing reports, he found himself unable to settle. He stayed alert to every internal shift, every
change in tension, every sign that something might be wrong. Even in his own house he felt on guard.
He noticed that he sat upright, shoulders tight, listening to his body rather than relaxing into the
room. Sleep and rest no longer felt restorative because his system never seemed to stand down. He
was distressed by how tense he felt all the time, as though he were poised for bad news.

The effects showed up at work as well. Kwame had spent decades in policy analysis and took pride in
his precision, but now concentration came in fragments. During meetings he would lose the thread of
discussion and find himself pulled inward by a sensation in his chest or by the return of the
hospital memory. While trying to read long documents, he would discover that he had scanned the same
paragraph repeatedly without absorbing it. This was humiliating to him. He had always seen himself
as measured, intellectually steady, and dependable. Now he worried that colleagues might notice the
lapses, though he said little about them. His world became smaller and more effortful, organized
around managing the possibility of danger in his own body.

Alongside this was a more private despair. Kwame had begun to think that he would never feel normal
emotions again. Since the illness, his inner life seemed split between numbness and overpowering
fear, with little room for ease, pleasure, or the ordinary warmth he remembered from before. As a
widower, he had already learned to live with loss; this felt different, as though something basic in
him had gone missing. He sought help only after recognizing that the pattern was not loosening with
time. What finally brought him in was not one dramatic incident but the accumulation of living in a
constant state of internal watchfulness, struggling to think clearly, and feeling estranged from
himself. He said he wanted to know whether it was possible to live in his body again without feeling
hunted by it, and he was deeply afraid that the answer might be no.
