forced down. What has stayed with him most is not a single blow or sentence but the experience of
being trapped in full view of other people, unable to predict what the officers would do from one
second to the next. He came away from it with the conviction that danger can arrive without warning
and that once it begins, no one slows down long enough to hear him.

In the weeks afterward, Dara found that his mind kept circling back to the incident even when he did
not want to think about it. He would replay what he had said, whether he moved the wrong way,
whether he should have kept quieter, spoken sooner, looked less frightened. He repeatedly blames
himself for what happened, returning again and again to the idea that if he had handled those few
minutes differently, the police would not have turned on him. His wife has heard variations of the
same question late at night—why did I do that, why didn’t I just stay still, why didn’t I know what
they wanted? Even when she tells him that what happened was not his fault, he continues to go over
it as if there must be some mistake he can still locate and fix.

At the same time, he works hard not to think about the event at all. He has become skilled at
cutting off conversations, changing the subject, and keeping himself occupied with tasks that leave
no room for reflection. On job sites he throws himself into measuring, sanding, carrying, anything
concrete and physical. If an image from that night starts to come up, he tries to push it away
before it forms fully. He sometimes uses drugs to blunt himself further, especially on evenings when
the thoughts feel close and he is afraid of where his mind will go if he lets it settle. He does not
describe this as wanting to get high so much as wanting to get out from under his own head for a
while.

His sense of danger now extends far beyond encounters with police. In any room, Dara looks first for

he becomes tense and distracted, scanning movements around him rather than listening. He says he
knows other people do not understand why this matters so much, but for him being unable to see who
is coming or where someone is standing feels intolerable. At work this shows up in small but
noticeable ways. In clients’ homes he positions himself carefully, often choosing spots where no one
can approach from behind. If a homeowner or contractor steps unexpectedly into his space while he is
bent over a cabinet or framing, his body stiffens immediately.

He is also on edge when anything takes longer than expected. If his wife is late coming home, if a
text goes unanswered, if a supervisor disappears for longer than planned on a job, his mind jumps
quickly to the sense that something bad is happening. Waiting has become especially difficult
because it resembles the worst moments of the police encounter, when he did not know what was about
to happen next and every second seemed to hold threat. He can sit in his own living room and still
feel unable to relax, even when he tells himself he is safe. His body remains taut, his attention
fixed outward, as if safety is temporary and can be revoked without notice.

Over months, these habits have narrowed his life. His wife has grown frustrated and worried about
how often he seems mentally elsewhere, either stuck in self-accusation or shutting down any mention

something small—his wife running late—and escalated because he was certain something had gone wrong

for, cautiously, is not to forget what happened, but to be able to sit in a room, wait for someone
he loves, and feel like the danger is not already on its way.
