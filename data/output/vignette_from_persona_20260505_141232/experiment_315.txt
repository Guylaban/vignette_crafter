Richard Hale is a 65-year-old American man, married for many years, who has spent most of his adult
life working as a prosecutor. He describes the event that stays with him as an encounter with police
violence that took place late one evening after he left the courthouse district. There had been
shouting on the street nearby, the kind of public disorder he had spent a career trying to address
through the law, and he remembered turning toward it with the reflexive attention of someone used to
stepping into conflict. Uniformed officers were already there. What has remained most vivid for him
is not a clean sequence but a set of sharply felt moments: police moving in with force, bodies
crowding together, people yelling over one another until the words blurred into useless noise, and
the sickening recognition that the authority he had devoted his professional life to working
alongside could become physically dangerous in an instant. He recalls trying to make sense of the
scene while voices overlapped around him, the chatter becoming incomprehensible, and feeling both
trapped by the noise and fixed on the officers themselves. Since then, police presence has not felt
neutral to him. What unsettles him is not simply the memory of force, but the sense that order can
collapse into abuse without warning.

In the weeks after the incident, Richard found himself reacting strongly to situations that seemed,
to others, ordinary. When people behaved badly in public—arguing aggressively in a store line,
shoving past others on a sidewalk, refusing to follow basic rules—he did not experience it as mere
rudeness. He had come to feel that misbehavior was the opening moment before something worse, a sign
that events could turn suddenly and that someone with power might respond violently. He also noticed
how quickly he became tense when he heard overlapping voices he could not decipher. In restaurants,
courthouse hallways, and even family gatherings when several conversations happened at once, the
sound could lose meaning and turn into the same unnerving wall of noise he remembered from that
night. He would stop tracking the conversation in front of him and begin watching the room instead.

This has become particularly difficult in his work. As a prosecutor, Richard still spends much of
his day in court buildings full of security officers, police witnesses, defendants, victims, and
frustrated family members. Passing uniformed officers in the corridor can make his whole body
tighten before he has time to think. He finds himself studying their posture, their hands, the
distance between people, the tone of nearby exchanges. When a crowded hallway fills with fast, half-
heard conversation, he scans automatically for where danger might emerge. He does the same in
parking garages, elevators, and courthouse entrances, watching other people closely for any sign
that they might harm him. He knows this makes him look distracted, and colleagues have noticed that
he positions himself near exits and pauses before entering busy spaces.

At home, Richard has fallen into habits that he describes as his way of getting through the day. If
the house is too quiet, or if his mind starts circling back to the event, he turns on the television
loudly or puts on loud music, preferring to fill the space with controlled sound rather than be
caught by stray noises outside or by his own thoughts. He repeatedly asks his wife if he seems all
right, if she thinks he is overreacting, if she believes he is safe. In the moment, reassurance
helps briefly, enough for him to steady himself, but the need returns. He has become embarrassed by
how often he asks, yet he also feels reluctant to stop because he is never fully convinced by his
own judgment.

Sleep has grown increasingly fragile. Richard says nights are the worst because there is less to
occupy him and more room to listen. He has trouble falling asleep and often wakes during the night
already alert, as if he has been called to attention. Once awake, he checks the house, looks through
windows, and listens for movement. During the day he remains superalert, on guard in a way that
leaves him fatigued but unable to relax. His wife has told him he is harder to reach, less present,
always tracking something over her shoulder or across the room.

What finally brought Richard to seek help was a recent incident at work. In a courthouse corridor,
as several people spoke at once and two officers approached from the far end, he felt himself lock
onto the scene so completely that he could not focus on the conversation he was having with a
colleague. He later went home shaken and asked his wife over and over whether she thought he was
losing control. He is seeking treatment now because he is frightened by how narrowed his world has
become inside places that used to feel familiar. What he fears most is remaining in a state of
constant watchfulness, unable to trust what he sees around him; what he hopes for is to move through
his work and his marriage without feeling that danger is always about to step forward wearing the
face of authority.
