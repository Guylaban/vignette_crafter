Tane is a 63-year-old married man of Māori background who has worked for many years as a financial
analyst, a job that once suited his patience, concentration, and preference for orderly routines. He
describes his life as having divided sharply after a night-time car accident that he experienced as
unmistakably life-threatening. He had been driving home in the dark when another vehicle came fast
into his path. What has stayed with him most vividly is not a tidy sequence but fragments that still
arrive with the force of the original moment: the sudden sound of cars around him, the enclosed
darkness beyond the windshield, and a sharp smell from the crash scene that he can still identify in
an instant before he has time to tell himself where he is. Even now, when he speaks about it, he
does so as if some parts are not properly filed away in the past. He says that certain moments do
not feel remembered so much as re-entered.

In the weeks after the accident, Tane tried to return to normal by going back to work and keeping
family life steady with his wife. Outwardly, he resumed many responsibilities, but he found himself
moving through the world as if danger were close by and likely to return without warning. He came to
believe that he had to be on guard all the time. Entering any room, whether it was a conference
space at the office, a restaurant, or even a medical waiting room, he found himself automatically
scanning for exits before he could settle into whatever he was there to do. He did not describe this
as a choice so much as something his body insisted on. He often says that his body reacts before his
mind has even caught up, as if some part of him has already detected a threat.

That sense of instant alarm is most obvious around the triggers that now structure much of his day.
In the parking garage beneath his office building, the dimness and enclosed dark spaces can make his
chest tighten before he reaches the elevator. The sound of cars outside the building, especially
when engines rev suddenly or tires pass close to the curb, can pull him out of spreadsheets and
meetings and place him back in the accident so abruptly that for a moment it feels as though it is
happening right now. At times, when this occurs, he hears in his head the sounds from that night
with an almost immediate clarity. He is not merely thinking about the crash; he is listening to it
again. Certain related smells are especially potent. If he catches them unexpectedly, whether near
traffic, in a garage, or around a roadside stop, the full memory can come back without warning,
vivid and complete enough that he has to stop and orient himself.

Over time, Tane has organized his life around the possibility of being caught off guard. He is
certain that constant vigilance is necessary because you never know what can happen next. At night
he often wakes and checks whether he is safe before he can settle again. His wife has noticed him
listening intently in the dark, sometimes getting out of bed to check the house, not because he has
heard anything definite but because he feels he cannot simply assume safety. During the day he tries
hard not to let his mind dwell on the accident. He has come to believe that if he thinks about it
directly, he will not be able to handle what follows. Because of that, he cuts off conversations,
changes the subject when his wife asks how he is doing, and throws himself into numerical work when
he senses the memory approaching.

These habits have gradually narrowed his world. At work, he prefers rooms where he can sit near the
door and becomes tense in longer meetings held in windowless conference rooms. He times his arrival
and departure to avoid lingering in the darker parts of the garage, and he is more distracted by
traffic sounds than he used to be. At home, his wife experiences him as present but guarded, as
though part of him is always monitoring something just beyond the moment they are sharing. Tane
sought help only after recognizing that this stance had not eased with time. What finally brought
him in was not one dramatic crisis but the accumulation of nights spent waking to check for safety,
days interrupted by sudden sensory reminders, and the growing sense that he was living braced
against the next disaster. He says he wants relief, but what frightens him most is the possibility
that if he lets his guard down or allows himself to think fully about the accident, he will lose
control of something he has spent years trying to contain.
