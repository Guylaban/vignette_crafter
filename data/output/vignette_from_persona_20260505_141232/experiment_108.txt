At 78, Richard had spent decades building a life around order, logic, and predictability. He was a
database administrator for a regional insurance company, working mostly from home now after years in
an office, and he had always taken quiet pride in being the person who could find the error no one
else could see. Divorced for many years and living alone, he had been managing the routines of older
age with the same methodical discipline until a life-threatening illness shattered his sense that
carefulness could keep him safe. What stayed with him most was not a single dramatic image but the
bodily immediacy of that period: the rush of fear when his breathing changed, the sense that
something inside him had turned without warning, and the hospital environment that became fused in
his mind with mortal danger. When he thinks about that time now, he does not describe it as
remembering in any ordinary sense. He says it can feel as if he is dropped straight back into it, as
though the illness is happening again in the present tense. At those moments he can smell the sharp
hospital odors again, the sterile chemical scent and the stale dryness of recirculated air, and the
memory has a vividness that feels more solid to him than what he ate that morning or what he did the
day before.

In the months after he survived the illness, Richard expected gratitude or relief to arrive and
settle things. Instead, he found himself living as if the emergency had never ended. Even inside his
own house, where he used to feel most at ease, he remained on guard. He checked the rooms without
quite admitting to himself that he was checking them, paused to listen to his breathing, noticed
every shift in temperature, every flutter in his chest, every sensation that might mean his body was
failing again. He was troubled by how quickly fear could overtake him, but the fear was accompanied
by anger and a quieter shame that he could not simply “get over” something that doctors told him was
over. He had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous in a way he had not
understood before, not only because illness could strike without warning, but because ordinary life
now seemed to sit on top of catastrophe like thin ice.

Richard’s workday gave these reactions repeated opportunities to intrude. Sitting at his home
workstation, monitoring overnight data jobs and responding to emails from younger colleagues, he
could suddenly be pulled into the sensory immediacy of the illness by something as small as the
smell of disinfectant after wiping down his desk, or the plastic-medical smell that lingered when he
opened a cabinet where supplies from that period were still stored. For a moment the room no longer
felt like his study; it felt as if danger had returned in full. He would go still, scanning his body
and his surroundings, certain that he had to stay watchful. Even when no new symptom emerged, his
body’s readiness itself frightened him. He had come to think of his body as an enemy of sorts,
something that kept reacting as if he were still under threat, something he could not fully trust
even when test results were normal and he was sitting safely at home in front of familiar screens.

Over time, this stance of vigilance narrowed his life. He was less willing to relax into evenings,
less likely to let himself drift into absorbed concentration, because being unguarded felt reckless.
He kept his phone close, moved through the house with an ear tuned to any sign that something was
wrong, and experienced his own home as a place where he must remain prepared rather than a refuge.
The emotional shift was just as stark. Richard said he had lost faith broadly and painfully: not
only in the fairness of life, but in the idea that safety could be earned or that there was any
dependable order beneath events. He spoke less of belief in a higher power than of a more general
collapse of trust. Life, to him, now seemed arbitrary and hostile. That outlook had made him more
distant with others. Though still competent at work, he kept interactions clipped and practical,
less interested in the casual reassurances people offered because they sounded to him like things
said by people who had not yet learned how quickly everything could change.

By the time he sought help, what troubled him most was the sense of being unable to leave the
illness in the past. He was tired of memories that arrived with such force they felt more real than
the present, tired of being ambushed by smell, tired of feeling watchful in his own living room. He
did not come in describing a wish to become carefree; that seemed impossible to him, maybe even
foolish. What he hoped for, more modestly and more urgently, was to stop living as though survival
had never actually happened. What frightened him most was that this constant readiness meant he
would spend whatever years remained to him braced against danger, unable to believe in safety even
for an hour.
