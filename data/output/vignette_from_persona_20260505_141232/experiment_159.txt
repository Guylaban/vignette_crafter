Nadia is a 51-year-old Middle Eastern American woman who came to treatment after years of trying to
arrange her life around a sense of danger she could never fully explain to other people. She is
divorced and works as a social ministry worker, a role that asks her to meet with vulnerable
families, sit with them in private offices, and move through shelters, church basements, and
community buildings where doors open and close behind her all day. In childhood, the person who
physically abused her was a caregiver inside her own home, and what remains most vivid is not a
story she can tell from beginning to end, but the bodily certainty of being trapped with an adult
who could turn on her without warning.

When Nadia speaks about those years, her words thin out and then stop. She says she knows what
happened, but she “can’t get it into language right.” Instead, memory arrives in fragments that take
hold of her body before she has time to think. She will suddenly feel herself pulled back into the
same position she was in then—shoulders tightened, body braced, attention fixed on where the door is
and whether she can get to it. What comes with those moments is not sadness so much as a surge of
furious heat. She has said more than once that whenever the past comes close, what floods her is
rage: rage at the caregiver, rage at being small, rage at having had nowhere to go.

Over time, she built a life that tried to prevent any repetition of that trapped feeling. Even now,
in ordinary settings, Nadia often reacts as though danger is already present. If someone steps into
a hallway behind her, if a door shuts suddenly, if she realizes she is in a room with one other
person and no easy exit, her body responds before she can reason with it. She turns quickly, scans,
looks behind her again, then again, checking whether someone is following or approaching. She knows
there are many moments when “nothing is actually wrong,” but that knowledge does not stop the jolt.
What frightens her most is how instantly she can feel cornered.

At work, these habits have become woven into her day. Nadia tries to position herself near exits in
every room she enters. In case conferences she chooses the chair closest to the door and keeps part
of her attention on the hallway outside. During home visits and meetings with male parishioners or
clients, she has become adamant about not being alone with another person; she finds reasons to keep
a colleague nearby, leave a door open, or move the conversation into a more public area. She
experiences these choices as necessary, not excessive. To her, being alone behind a closed door with
one other person carries the old logic of childhood: once the door is closed, anything can happen
and no one will know.

The cost has grown more visible over the past year. Nadia has begun avoiding work itself on some
days, especially when her schedule includes one-on-one meetings in unfamiliar settings. She has
called out, rearranged appointments, or lingered outside buildings trying to steady herself before
going in. Coworkers have noticed that she is tense and easily angered. When someone startles her
from behind or questions why she needs a certain seat, she can snap sharply, her voice rising with
an intensity that surprises even her. Later she feels embarrassed, but in the moment it feels as if
she is defending herself against something immediate. The same vigilance follows her outside work;
she checks behind her in parking lots, in grocery store aisles, even walking from her car to her
apartment.

Nadia’s divorce is part of the background of her life now rather than something she centers, but she
describes a long-standing difficulty tolerating confinement, unpredictability, and the feeling of
being alone with someone who has power over the atmosphere in a room. She has become increasingly
aware that much of her day is organized around preventing that feeling from arising. Still, she has
not experienced these patterns as symptoms so much as precautions that make sense.

What finally brought her to seek help was a recent incident at work. She was meeting with a client
in a small office when the client shifted in his chair and the door clicked more firmly shut. Nadia
instantly felt her body lock into that old posture, as if she were back in the room where the abuse
happened. She could not find words for what was happening; all she knew was that she was furious, on
edge, and desperate to get out. She ended the meeting abruptly and spent the rest of the day shaken
and humiliated. Coming to treatment, Nadia says she wants to be able to do her job without feeling
hunted or trapped. At the same time, she is afraid that if she lets herself come closer to the
memories, the rage in them will be too much to contain.
