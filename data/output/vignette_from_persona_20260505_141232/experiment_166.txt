Arjun Patel is a 36-year-old accountant who came to treatment after years of living as though danger
could break into an ordinary room at any moment. He grew up in an Indian American household where,
as a boy, he repeatedly witnessed violence between the adults in his home. In the memory that
returns most forcefully, he is small and standing just outside a kitchen doorway late at night,
hearing an argument rise in sharp, clipped bursts before a crash of metal and glass. He remembers
his mother crying out, the sudden scrape of a chair, his father’s face set in a way that made the
whole room feel unsafe, and the sense that the air itself had turned electric. He has never been
able to tell that scene in a neat sequence. When he tries, his words stall. He can describe
flashes—the sound of something striking the wall, his mother’s arm pulled up to shield herself, his
own body frozen in the hallway—but then he goes quiet, jaw tight, breathing shallow, as if the
memory is happening in the room with him rather than being spoken about from a distance.

What he says most clearly is that the terror comes back whole. It is not, for him, a sad
recollection or a bad childhood story. If something reminds him of that night, he feels the same
surge he felt then: pounding heart, heat in his chest, shoulders locking, stomach dropping. He is
often caught off guard by how little it takes. A male voice rising sharply in the office break room,
the slam of a conference-room door, dishes clattering in the apartment next door, or hearing a
couple arguing in a parking lot can pull the memory over him without warning. In those moments he is
no longer simply Arjun, a middle-aged professional in a pressed shirt carrying a laptop bag through
a normal day. His body reacts first, before he can think: he becomes tense, alert, scanning faces,
exits, and changes in tone. He says he lives “braced,” as if some part of him is always preparing
for the next blow to land somewhere nearby.

That constant readiness has shaped the way he moves through his life. At work, where he is respected
for being meticulous, he notices every shift in other people’s moods. If two coworkers start
speaking sharply to one another, he stops hearing the content of the conversation and starts
tracking volume, posture, and whether someone is blocking the doorway. He prefers to keep his office
door open so he can monitor what is happening in the corridor, but loud footsteps or a sudden knock
can make him jolt in his chair. He arrives early because the quiet feels safer, and he stays later
than necessary to avoid crowded elevators and the unpredictability of other people at the end of the
day. Colleagues know him as controlled and somewhat remote; few realize how much energy he spends
simply trying to get through a workday without his body surging into alarm.

Over the years, Arjun has come to believe that bad things happen and he will not be able to stop
them. He speaks about this with a weary certainty rather than drama. Sitting in traffic, hearing
raised voices outside his apartment, watching a stranger’s expression harden, he feels the old
helplessness settle over him: whatever is coming, he will be trapped in it again. He also carries a
bitter sense that the people who caused the terror in his childhood were never truly held
accountable. In his mind, the fact that life went on, relatives stayed silent, and no real
consequences followed remains a profound wrong. He returns to that unfairness often. It leaves him
with the feeling that violence can erupt, reshape a child’s life, and then simply be absorbed into
the family as if nothing happened.

He describes himself as having once been an easy, cheerful child, and now speaks of being almost
permanently miserable. The divorce two years ago sharpened this view of himself. He does not present
it as a direct explanation, only as another loss that made his life feel smaller. He says his ex-
wife used to tell him he always seemed on edge, that even during ordinary disagreements he looked as
though he was waiting for something terrible. Since living alone, the apartment has become both
refuge and trap: he listens for sounds through the walls, stiffens at arguments from neighboring
units, and paces after hearing a door slam in the hallway.

What finally brought him to seek help was an incident at work when a client meeting turned heated.
Nothing physical happened, but one man struck the table with his palm and raised his voice. Arjun
felt the room tilt into that old terror so suddenly that he could barely speak. He spent the rest of
the day shaken, ashamed, and unable to finish a report that should have taken an hour. In the weeks
after, he found himself dreading the office and feeling even more tightly wound. He came to
treatment frightened that this is simply who he is now—watchful, unhappy, and never safe—and hoping,
cautiously, that he might learn how to live without feeling as though the worst moment of his
childhood is always about to happen again.
