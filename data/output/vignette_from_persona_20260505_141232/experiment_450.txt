Amina is a 25-year-old African American woman, single, and early in her career as a surgeon. When
she speaks about the deployment that still returns to her in fragments, she does not tell it as a
clean story. She circles it, stops, and says that the worst part is that she cannot seem to put the
frightening event into words. What comes back to her is not a sequence she can explain so much as a
set of sensations that arrive whole: the deafening concussion of an explosion, the chopping sound of
aircraft overhead, the feel of a particular rough military fabric under her hands and against her
skin, and the immediate certainty that she was about to die.

During her military service, Amina was caught in an attack that unfolded with overwhelming speed.
She remembers being in uniform, moving through a tense, enclosed operational setting, and then the
sudden blast. The bang was so abrupt and forceful that it seemed to erase everything before it. At
almost the same time there were helicopters overhead, loud enough that the sound felt physical,
vibrating through her chest and skull. She recalls grabbing at herself or the ground, aware in a
sharply sensory way of the coarse texture of the material under her palms. The fear from that moment
remains what is most vivid to her now. When she tries to describe it, she says she is right back
there, with the same terror she felt at the time, as though no time has passed.

In the months after returning home, Amina found that the memory did not stay in the past. It could
be brought back instantly and without warning by something in her surroundings. A helicopter
crossing low over the hospital helipad could stop her in the middle of walking between surgical
floors, her body going tight before she had even consciously registered the sound. A sudden bang in
the hospital—a dropped metal tray, a slammed door, equipment clattering—could produce the same
effect, the whole memory rushing in fully formed. Even touch could do it. The stiff, rough feel of
certain fabrics, especially materials that reminded her of military gear or heavy utility clothing,
made her stomach drop. Those moments frightened her not only because they were intense, but because
they seemed to seize her before she could prepare. She had come to believe that danger could arrive
at any moment and that the only sensible way to move through the world was to stay ready.

That belief became visible in nearly every setting she entered. Before going into a restaurant,
waiting room, conference space, or even a colleague’s office, Amina would quickly identify how she
could get out if she needed to. She was always planning an escape route before entering a place,
noting doors, side corridors, stairwells, and anything that might block her path. Once inside, she
preferred to sit with her back against a wall so she could see the whole room. If others had already
taken those seats, she would hover, reposition herself, or remain standing longer than felt socially
comfortable. She was also constantly checking her surroundings for signs of threat. In the operating
room, where vigilance is ordinarily valued, this could blur into something more consuming. Alongside
the normal surgical focus on monitors, instruments, and sterile procedure, part of her attention
remained fixed on the environment itself—the doorway, sudden sounds in the corridor, the possibility
of being caught off guard. She did not describe this as excessive; to her, it felt necessary.

Over time these habits shaped her life. Colleagues noticed that she was particular about where she
sat in team meetings and unusually alert to noise from outside. She avoided hospital spaces where
helicopter noise was loudest when she could, sometimes taking longer routes through the building.
Socially, being single and living alone made it easier to organize her surroundings exactly as she
wanted, but it also meant there were few places where she fully let down her guard. When friends
invited her out, she often chose venues based on whether she could see exits and control where she
sat. She knew this looked rigid, yet what frightened her most was the idea of being trapped, unable
to see, unable to get out.

By the time she sought help, Amina was exhausted by the effort of staying prepared. What finally
pushed her to come in was a recent moment at work when the sound of a helicopter overhead and a loud
bang nearby brought the memory back so suddenly that she had to step away and compose herself in
private. She found herself ashamed that she still could not explain the original event in words
despite how fully she relived it in her body. Sitting in the intake room, she chose the chair
against the wall and glanced at the door before answering questions. She said she wanted help
because she was tired of feeling ambushed by sounds and sensations, and because she feared that if
she could not get a handle on this, her world would keep narrowing to only those places where she
could watch every entrance and plan every exit.
