Amina is a 30-year-old African American woman who works as a school counselor and lives with her
husband. When she describes the night that continues to return to her, she does so in sharp
fragments rather than as a smooth story: the stop by police on her drive home, the sudden change in
tone, the blast of shouted commands, the flash of bodies moving quickly toward her car. She
remembers the smell most vividly. One of the officers leaned close enough that she caught the odor
of alcohol on his breath, and even now that detail stands out to her with humiliating clarity. She
remembers how tired she had been that evening, already worn down before it happened, and how that
exhaustion made her feel slow, foggy, and one step behind what was being demanded of her. She has
come to believe that the way she moved and answered in those moments was part of why things
escalated. In her mind, if she had been quicker, calmer, different somehow, it might not have
unfolded the way it did.

In the days after, Amina told herself she should be able to put it aside. Instead, small moments in
ordinary life began to feel charged. Loud noises were the worst at first. A slammed door in the
counseling office, lockers banging in the hallway, a sudden shout from students during lunch
duty—each could send an instant jolt through her body. She would go rigid before she had even
thought about why. Sudden movements by other people had a similar effect. If a student sprang up
from a chair too fast, if a teacher gestured abruptly while talking in the corridor, she felt
herself snap to attention, watching hands, posture, distance. She knew she was in a middle school,
not at the roadside that night, but she could not get her body to settle. Even at home with her
husband, if he came around a doorway too quickly or reached suddenly for something near her, she
felt herself tense before she could stop it.

The smell of alcohol became another potent moment of dread. At school events where a parent arrived
carrying stale liquor on their clothes or breath, Amina found herself fighting the urge to leave the
room. She would keep her face composed, but inside she was no longer fully in the conversation. She
became intensely watchful, scanning movements, exits, tone of voice. By the end of the workday she
was often depleted, and that exhaustion frightened her too. Feeling very tired did not register as
normal fatigue; it felt dangerous, as if being worn down meant she would again be slow, exposed, and
unable to respond correctly.

Over the following months, her life narrowed. She began avoiding situations that might bring up
strong emotion because she no longer trusted what would happen if she felt too much. She pulled back
not only from difficult conversations but also from potentially joyful ones. When colleagues invited
her to celebratory dinners or her husband suggested a weekend outing that might help her relax, she
often declined. It was hard for her to explain, even to herself, why happiness felt risky, but she
knew that any strong feeling seemed capable of tipping her into a state she dreaded. She also
started avoiding people more generally. At work she kept her office door closed more often and
limited informal chats with staff. At home she answered fewer messages from friends and family. The
result was that she increasingly felt set apart, watching other people move easily through life
while she stood outside it.

Amina speaks about herself with a quiet harshness. She has come to believe there is something wrong
with her as a person, something defective that was exposed by the police encounter and confirmed by
what followed. She blames herself for how she acted during the stop and for not being able to return
to normal afterward. When she sees how alert and tense she remains even in safe places, she takes it
as further evidence that she is not like other people. She can sit on her own couch with her husband
beside her and still feel unable to relax, shoulders tight, attention tuned to every sound outside,
every shift in the apartment.

What finally brought her to treatment was the accumulation of these changes in the setting that
matters most to her. As a school counselor, she is used to being emotionally available and steady
with students, but she began noticing how often she was bracing through the school day, trying to
get through hallway noise, abrupt movement, and interactions with distressed families without
showing what was happening inside. She felt increasingly alone in her work and in her marriage, and
that frightened her. By the time she sought help, what scared her most was the possibility that this
watchful, shut-down version of herself was becoming permanent. What she hopes for, carefully and
without much confidence yet, is not to erase what happened, but to feel safe enough in her own life
to stop living as if the next sudden movement or loud sound means she is back there again.
