Leila Haddad is a 45-year-old divorced auditor who came to treatment after years of trying to keep
her life narrowed to what felt manageable. She had served in a combat zone earlier in adulthood, and
when she describes the event that still organizes so much of her daily life, she does not tell it as
a smooth story. It comes in pieces. She remembers being in an exposed area with others from her
unit, the feeling of being visible from too far away, the sharp awareness that there was nowhere
solid to disappear to. Then there is the sound: a sudden explosion, a bang so forceful that even now
she says certain noises seem to arrive in her body before her mind can place them. She remembers
scent in a way that feels strangely vivid compared with the rest—someone nearby had been wearing a
strong cologne, or perhaps it drifted from a passing body in the moments before everything changed.
Around those details, the memory breaks apart. She can recall fragments, isolated images and
sensations, but not a full sequence. There are parts she cannot retrieve no matter how hard she
tries, and what she does have feels like disconnected pieces that refuse to form a complete picture.

In the months after she returned home, Leila found herself circling the same thought: if that day
had not happened, her life would have unfolded differently. She imagines versions of herself who
stayed easier with people, who did not lose her marriage under the pressure of becoming watchful and
hard to reach, who did not shape every outing around exits and concealment. What grips her most is
the conviction that she should have protected others and failed. Because she cannot remember the
event in a continuous way, her mind returns again and again to what she might have missed, what she
should have done sooner, whether some different movement or decision could have changed who was
harmed. Alongside that is a harsher belief that the event says something about her character—that
this happened to her because of the sort of person she is. She does not describe this as a passing
guilty thought but as something close to a verdict.

Leila works as an auditor, a job that in some ways suits the part of her that notices details,
tracks risk, and feels calmer when she can anticipate problems. Even there, the habits she has built
around danger shape her day. When she arrives at a client site, she does not feel settled until she
has identified every exit. In conference rooms she chooses seats near doors and avoids broad glass-
walled spaces or open atriums whenever she can, feeling too exposed in them, too easy to see and too
difficult to defend. If a meeting is scheduled in a large open office area, she may ask to move it
under some practical pretext. Walking through parking lots or lobbies, she checks behind herself
repeatedly, alert to the possibility that someone could be following her. She scans rooms
automatically, noting who enters, where people are standing, and what would allow the fastest
escape. She knows others experience this as excessive. To her, it feels like the minimum required to
be anywhere at all.

Her sense of safety has contracted over time. She feels safe only in a very limited set of places,
mainly her home and a few familiar settings where she already knows the layout. Outside those zones
she remains braced. Waking from nightmares is often the worst time. On those mornings she rises with
the residue of the dream still clinging to her, already flooded before the day has started, and it
can take hours before she can tell herself she is in the present. A bang from construction, a car
backfiring, or any explosion-like sound can jolt her into immediate alarm. A specific perfume or
cologne is similarly destabilizing; if she catches it in an elevator or on a client, she may lose
the thread of conversation and feel herself pulled toward those broken fragments from the combat
zone, trying and failing to see what comes next.

What has frightened Leila almost as much as the memories themselves is her body’s response. She has
come to believe that her body is, in some sense, against her—that it keeps reacting as if she is
still under threat even when she knows, intellectually, that she is safe. She experiences this as a
betrayal and as further evidence that something in her is fundamentally altered. She sought help
only recently, after waking from a nightmare before an important audit and finding herself unable to
enter a large client facility without first standing outside mapping exits and scanning the
perimeter. She was ashamed that a routine workday had become so difficult, and shaken by how small
her world had become. In coming to treatment, she says she wants, more than anything, to feel that
she can be somewhere other than her narrow safe zones without constantly preparing to flee. At the
same time, she is afraid of what might happen if she stops watching, and afraid that if the missing
parts of the memory ever come into focus, they will confirm what she already suspects about her
failure.
