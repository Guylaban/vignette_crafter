Evan is a 26-year-old detective who came to treatment months after leaving a marriage that, from the
outside, had looked stable. He described the violence as something that happened behind closed doors
and often after his wife had been drinking. What remained clearest to him was not a single episode
but a pattern of nights in which the sharp smell of alcohol reached him before anything else,
followed by the sense that he had to read the room perfectly and stay alert. In one assault she
struck him hard enough to leave visible injuries, and he remembered registering, with a detached
kind of precision, the sight of his own wounds afterward. Even now, when he speaks about it, the
memory arrives with unusual clarity. He does not describe it vaguely or as if it were distant; he
can hear specific sounds from the event in his head as though they are occurring again in the room
with him, and the detail of those moments is so sharp that he sometimes stops mid-sentence when it
breaks in against his wishes.

In the weeks after the abuse ended, Evan expected that returning to work and keeping a disciplined
routine would steady him. Instead, reminders began appearing everywhere. As a detective, he is
frequently around victims, suspects, and crime scenes, and he found himself reacting strongly to
visible injuries on other people. A bruised witness, a split lip during an interview, a victim’s
bandaged face in the fluorescent light of the precinct—these moments could pull him abruptly out of
whatever he was doing. He would feel a wave of nausea rise before he had even fully understood what
he was looking at. The smell of alcohol was just as potent. Walking past someone reeking of liquor
in a hallway, entering an apartment where stale alcohol hung in the air, or catching it on the
breath of someone he was interviewing could change his whole body in an instant. He came to dread
that sudden internal shift: the sick feeling in his stomach, the involuntary flood of memory, the
sense that the past had forced its way into the present again.

At home, his wife is no longer there, but marriage has not left him feeling connected to anyone. He
told the therapist that he has come to believe people are not what they seem, and he says this
flatly, not as bitterness but as something he now considers simple fact. What unsettles him most is
how fully that belief has spread. It is not just romantic trust that feels damaged; he says he has
lost faith in people generally, in life having any basic fairness, and in the idea that there is
some benevolent order underneath what happens. He experiences himself as set apart from others now,
permanently outside ordinary closeness. When colleagues joke around or talk easily about their
weekends, he feels he is imitating normal participation rather than belonging to it.

Over time, he has developed ways of getting through his days that make sense to him but have
narrowed his life. He avoids relaxing because letting his guard down feels dangerous. If he comes
home exhausted, he keeps the television on, scrolls his phone, or paces instead of settling into the
couch or trying to rest. The idea of being calm feels exposed, as though something bad could happen
the moment he stopped bracing. He also describes numbing his feelings deliberately. He moves through
work with an efficient, shut-down quality that colleagues have noticed, and he prefers that to
feeling anything fully. Physical contact has become especially difficult. He avoids being touched in
any form, even casual contact that other people would barely notice. At work he steps back if
someone reaches for his shoulder, and in his personal life he has become stiff and distant, which
has deepened his sense of being separate from everyone else.

What finally brought Evan in was not a single crisis but the accumulation of these moments. He had
recently been on a case involving an injured victim, felt nausea almost immediately, and then spent
the rest of the day hearing sounds from the abuse in his head while trying to conduct himself
professionally. That episode frightened him because it made him feel less in control in the one
place he most prides himself on functioning well. He is seeking help now because he does not want
every encounter with injury, alcohol, or unwanted closeness to feel hijacked by what happened. At
the same time, he is wary of treatment. Part of him is certain that trusting another person is a
mistake, and he fears that if he stops holding himself tightly together, he will be left undefended.
What he hopes for, though he says it cautiously, is to feel less cut off from life and less ambushed
by memories he never invited in.
