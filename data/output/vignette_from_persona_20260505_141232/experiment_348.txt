Mele was a 46-year-old married woman of Pacific Islander background who had made her living for
years as a musician, performing regularly in clubs, at community events, and in recording sessions
around her city. She described her life before the illness in practical terms: late nights after
performances, long rehearsals, travel with instruments, and a strong sense that her body would do
what she asked of it. The event she returned to again and again in treatment was the period when
that confidence broke. She became critically ill with a life-threatening respiratory condition that
escalated quickly enough for her to experience, in vivid detail, the sensation that air was no
longer reliably available. She remembered the smell of antiseptic and plastic tubing, the sharp
medical smells in the examination room, and the humiliating panic of trying to draw breath while
staff moved around her. What stayed with her was not only that she might die, but the terrifying
immediacy of her body failing in front of other people while she could do nothing to correct it.

In the months that followed, she found that ordinary bodily sensations no longer felt ordinary. If
she climbed stairs carrying equipment to a rehearsal space and noticed even slight breathlessness,
her body reacted before she had time to think. Her chest tightened, her heart raced, and she would
stop where she was, scanning herself for signs that the illness was happening again. She did not
experience these moments as overreactions; to Mele, they felt like her body knew something dangerous
before her mind could catch up. Smell was especially potent. The odor of disinfectant in a backstage
restroom, the sterile scent of hand sanitizer, even certain hospital-like cleaning products used in
venues could pull her back into the atmosphere of the examination room. She would go still, her
attention narrowing, feeling for her breathing and trying to work out whether she was safe.

Medical settings became almost intolerable. She postponed physical examinations, avoided hospitals
whenever she could, and turned down routine follow-up care unless her husband pushed the issue. Even
passing an ambulance on the street made her stomach drop. Undergoing a procedure, or even imagining
one, brought back too much: the helplessness, the smell, the sensation of fighting for air while
strangers watched. She told herself she was being sensible, that there was no reason to put herself
through that again unless absolutely necessary. Over time, this narrowed her world. There were only
a few places where she felt fully safe—mostly her bedroom, one corner of the living room, and a
familiar rehearsal area at home where she could sit upright, control the air, and leave if she
needed to. Outside those spaces, she remained alert in a way that exhausted her.

At night she often woke abruptly and checked whether she was safe. Sometimes this meant sitting up
to assess her breathing; sometimes it meant orienting herself to the room and listening for any sign
that something was wrong in her body. These awakenings were not dramatic, but repetitive and
wearing. By morning she often felt ashamed of how vigilant she had become. She had come to believe
that her reactions since the illness showed that she was a lousy coper. She compared herself harshly
to the version of herself who used to perform under pressure and travel without a second thought.
Now, before a show, she could find herself wondering whether she could trust herself to make the
right call if something happened onstage—whether she would ignore a real symptom, panic over a
harmless one, or freeze. That self-doubt made accepting performance opportunities feel fraught.

Her thinking often circled around how life would have been different if the illness had never
happened. She imagined a cleaner timeline in which she was still working freely, taking gigs without
checking routes to make sure she would not pass the hospital, and saying yes to recording sessions
in medical buildings or commercial complexes with that unmistakable antiseptic smell. These thoughts
did not comfort her; they sharpened her sense of loss. Alongside them was a punishing private
refrain that she deserved to suffer because of what happened. She did not elaborate this as a
coherent moral argument so much as a felt conviction that her suffering somehow fit her, that
weakness exposed in the illness had revealed something inadequate in her.

By the time she sought help, the impact on her marriage and work had become difficult to ignore. Her
husband had begun accompanying her to appointments she could not quite bring herself to attend
alone, and she had started declining some professional commitments because unfamiliar venues did not
feel safe enough. What finally brought her in was a recent incident before a performance when she
caught the smell of disinfectant in a dressing room, felt suddenly short of breath, and had to step
out while the rest of the ensemble waited. She was frightened not only of getting sick again, but of
living inside an ever-shrinking map of safe places and mistrusting herself more each time her body
surged into alarm. At the same time, she still hoped she might recover enough steadiness to work,
sleep, and breathe without constantly checking whether danger had already begun.
