long before her divorce, she had taken a leave from graduate study to work with a humanitarian

overseas.” What happened there has remained sharply present to her in ways that do not feel like the
past. She recalls one distribution day near the anniversary that now marks her calendar each year
without her wanting it to. Armed men in uniform had been moving through the area while civilians
crowded around aid supplies. The scene had the heat, dust, and urgency of too many bodies trying to
get food and medicine at once. In the middle of that confusion there was shouting, the crack and
percussion of gunfire, people dropping and surging, and the metallic chaos of military vehicles and
commands barked nearby. She remembers swallowing something handed to her in haste—an oral
rehydration solution or ration with a distinct artificial taste—while trying to keep working and
realizing, all at once, that terror had overtaken the crowd.

the memory comes back, she hears the sounds first: the shouted orders, the shots, the crying, as if
they are occurring in the room with her. At other times the whole scene arrives at once and feels
completely real across all her senses, so that for a moment she is no longer in her chambers or
apartment but back there, tasting that same sharp, chemical sweetness in her mouth and feeling the
same terror she felt at the time. What unsettles her most is not simply remembering but the total
conviction of the reliving. She knows intellectually where she is, yet her body and mind respond as
though the event is happening again.

life. Bailiffs, courthouse security officers, and police witnesses are routine features of her work,
but she notices their uniforms immediately, often before she has registered anything else about

transported her, and she has thrown it away after a single sip, disturbed by how fast the courtroom
and the present can disappear.

although she does not describe herself as lonely so much as cut off, she feels set apart from others
