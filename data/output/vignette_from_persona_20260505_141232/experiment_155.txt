and fear, and she was practiced at sitting still in the presence of suffering. What she had never

one part of her life sealed off, a part that began when she was forced to flee her home as a refugee
and that still entered her days without warning.

orderly. She remembers being transported in crowded conditions, surrounded by fear and uncertainty,
with armed men nearby. The sight of weapons remains especially charged for her, not as an abstract
sign of danger but as something tied to the moments when other people controlled where she would go,
how she would move, and whether she would be safe. What stayed with her most strongly were not only
images but sounds: sharp commands, shouting, the metallic presence of weapons, the noise of
frightened people packed close together. Even now, when the memory intrudes, she sometimes hears
those sounds in her head as if they are occurring again. At other times, what unsettles her is how
impossible the whole thing feels. She can know, in one sense, that it happened, while another part
of her recoils from it as too extreme to belong to her own life. She has said more than once that it
feels unreal, as if it happened to someone else, and yet it arrives in her mind with a force that
leaves no room for doubt.

Over the years, these experiences settled into the ordinary routines of her life in the United
States. Riding the bus became particularly difficult. Public transport placed her in close proximity
to strangers, limited her control over when she could leave, and exposed her to sudden shifts in
movement and noise. If a passenger stood abruptly, reached into a bag too quickly, or turned sharply
in the aisle, Abena felt herself go instantly on guard. She watched people closely for signs that
they might harm her, tracking hands, posture, tone of voice. Even after stepping off the bus, she
often looked behind her repeatedly, checking whether anyone was following her. She understood that
other people might not notice these behaviors, but to her they felt necessary, the only sensible
response when safety could change so quickly.

At times the first sign that she was struggling was not fear exactly but a dissociative sensation, a
beginning sense of detachment, as though the world had gone slightly far away or she herself had
become unreal. That feeling frightened her in its own right. Once it began, she became afraid of
what might come next if she let herself think too much. She had come to believe that if she allowed
the memories in fully, she would not be able to handle them. For that reason she developed a
disciplined silence around her refugee experiences. Even with her husband, she kept details to
herself. In church settings, where others sometimes spoke openly about violence, displacement, or
war, she redirected the conversation toward prayer, practical needs, or scripture rather than her
own history. People experienced her as private and composed; few understood how much effort went
into staying that way.

Her avoidance extended beyond conversation. Abena favored concealing clothing, garments that covered
her body and did not draw notice. She described wanting to move through public space without being
seen, without inviting attention. There was a weary resignation in the way she explained this, tied
to a deeply painful view of what had happened to her. She spoke of having been treated not as a
person but as an object, and that sense had never fully lifted. Alongside it sat a bitter conviction
that what happened was profoundly unjust and that nothing had ever truly been done about it. The
wrong remained, in her mind, unanswered.

reopening the past. But the strain had become harder to contain. Practical limits had begun to
affect her ministry: hesitating to take the bus to church events, arriving already tense after
scanning fellow passengers, feeling shaken by ordinary movements in crowded fellowship halls. What
finally brought her in was not a single dramatic collapse but an accumulation of moments in which
she felt the old terror pressing into the present and her world narrowing around it. She is seeking
help now with caution and considerable dignity. What frightens her most is that speaking of the past
may overwhelm her completely. What she hopes, quietly, is that she might remain herself while no
longer having to live as though danger is always one quick movement away.
