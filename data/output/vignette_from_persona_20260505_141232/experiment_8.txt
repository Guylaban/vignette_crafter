Minh Tran was 35 when he became seriously ill, a married Vietnamese American man who had always
thought of himself as dependable, careful, and productive. He worked as a UX designer for a tech
company, spending most days at a screen mapping user flows, joining video calls, and keeping track
of timelines and revisions. The illness began in a way that still felt frighteningly ordinary to
him: a period of feeling unwell that stretched on longer than it should have, followed by a rapid
escalation that ended in emergency care. What stayed with Minh was not a single dramatic image so
much as the lived sense that his body had become dangerous territory without warning. He remembered
waiting for updates, for test results, for someone to explain why things were taking so long, and
with each delay becoming more certain that something catastrophic was unfolding just out of view. By
the time the danger had passed, he no longer experienced waiting as neutral. Any delay had come to
feel like the beginning of terrible news.

In the months afterward, Minh did what he could to put the whole episode behind him. He did not want
to think about the illness, the hospital, the uncertainty, or the helplessness of being told to
wait. If his wife tried to bring up how frightening that period had been, he would go quiet or
change the subject. He told himself there was no point dwelling on it, but privately he also felt
unable to tolerate what came up when he let his mind drift there. He avoided hospitals whenever
possible, looked away when ambulances passed in traffic, and postponed routine physical
examinations. Even seeing a medical building from the car window could make his body tighten before
he had consciously registered why. He described himself as being tense all the time, shoulders
raised, stomach clenched, as if he were bracing for something to go wrong again.

That vigilance began to spread into ordinary parts of his life. At work, if a teammate took too long
to reply on Slack, if a product review meeting ran over, or if a loading screen stalled during
testing, Minh felt a jolt of alarm that far exceeded the moment. The felt logic was immediate: when
things take longer than expected, something bad is happening. He knew other people did not react
this way, and he could hear how disproportionate it sounded when he tried to explain it, but
reassurance did not land. Even when his wife told him he was fine, or a doctor’s office later
confirmed that a delay was just administrative, he found it impossible to feel safe. His mind did
not settle with information; his body remained keyed up, prepared for danger.

He also started avoiding anything that made his body feel strained. He stopped exercising in the
ways he once had, and he became wary of exertion, heat, rushing, or anything that made his heart
pound or his breathing change. The sensations themselves felt loaded now, too close to the bodily
vulnerability he had discovered during the illness. He planned his days to minimize physical stress
and became more rigid about conserving energy. This narrowed his life in ways that were hard to
admit. He was less spontaneous with his wife, less willing to go out if it meant walking far or
being away from home too long, and more likely to stay in controlled environments where he felt he
could monitor himself.

Alongside this, Minh’s view of himself had changed in a painful way. He had come to believe there
was something wrong with him as a person, not just with his body. Surviving the illness had not left
him feeling fortunate or resilient; instead, he felt defective and weak. He compared himself
unfavorably to other people who seemed able to handle medical uncertainty or bodily discomfort
without falling apart inside. What troubled him most was how emotionally flat he had become. He
described feeling dead inside, as though the version of him that used to enjoy things, engage
easily, and feel present with his wife had receded. He still went through the motions of work and
marriage, but much of it felt mechanical.

By the time he sought help, the strain was showing in concrete ways. He had delayed another physical
examination despite knowing it was overdue, and the anticipation alone was affecting his sleep and
concentration. At work he was finding it harder to manage routine project uncertainty because every
unanswered message and every delayed decision stirred the same dread. His wife had begun to say that
she missed him, that even when he was home he seemed far away. Minh came to treatment not because he
felt hopeful, exactly, but because he was tired of living as if danger were always one step ahead.
What frightened him most was the sense that he could never trust his own body or believe anyone who
told him he was safe. What he wanted, quietly and with some shame, was to stop feeling so weak and
to feel alive in his own life again.
