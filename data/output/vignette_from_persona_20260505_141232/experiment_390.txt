Ramesh Patel is a 79-year-old South Asian American man who came to treatment after years of carrying
his war experience in near silence. He is divorced and still works shifts as a warehouse worker, a
fact he describes with a kind of stubborn pride. He says work gives him “something to do with my
hands,” though over time it has also become one more place where the past appears beside him without
warning. When he first spoke about the trauma, he did not begin with abstract statements about war.
He began with one scene: the noise, the confusion, and the certainty in that moment that he would
die. In combat, he found himself surrounded by sudden blasts, shouted commands, and the sight of
other men falling around him. What remained with him most was not a single neat memory but a series
of sharply preserved fragments—the force of the sound, the air seeming to split, the feeling that
nowhere was safe, and the terrible awareness that survival depended on reacting instantly while
terrified.

In the weeks after he returned, Ramesh did what many people around him expected: he carried on. He
worked, married, raised a family, and spoke very little about what had happened. Yet the war never
settled into the past for him. He describes the memories as if they are stored in pieces, arriving
suddenly and with more force than he thinks they should. A bang in everyday life can feel to him
like more than a noise; for a moment it is as if his body has already decided where he is before his
mind can catch up. He does not talk about these episodes dramatically. Instead he says, with
frustration and embarrassment, that he “still reacts like it’s happening.” The sense of present
danger has remained close to the surface for decades.

At the warehouse, this is especially hard. The setting is full of the kinds of moments that catch
him off guard: pallets dropped too quickly, metal striking concrete, engines revving, voices raised
across long aisles. Younger coworkers may laugh off the noise, but Ramesh feels his whole system
tense. He scans automatically, noticing exits, movement, and anything out of place. He prefers tasks
that let him keep some distance from others and some control over his surroundings. If a section of
the warehouse is particularly loud or crowded, he finds reasons to stay away from it. He tells
himself this is just common sense, but over the years it has narrowed his world. Coworkers know him
as reliable but reserved. He joins no one for breaks if he can help it, and when people ask ordinary
questions about his past, he redirects the conversation. The result has been a life that looks
steady from the outside while becoming increasingly constricted.

His divorce sits in that same history of distance. He does not offer easy explanations, and he does
not present himself as a victim of his symptoms. Still, as he talks, it is clear how much of himself
he learned to hold back. He had come to believe that speaking about combat would bring something
unbearable too close, and that if he let himself feel what was there, he would lose control. What
frightened him most was not only remembering the war but the possibility of being overwhelmed by it
in front of someone else. So he kept to routines, kept conversations shallow, and relied on work and
endurance rather than closeness.

Now, at 79, he is struck by how little this private discipline has changed what he lives with
inside. He reports severe PTSD symptoms overall, and what troubles him is how immediate the threat
still feels despite the passage of time. Recently, after a loud crash at work, he found himself
shaken enough that he had to stop what he was doing and steady himself against a shelf. He was
deeply rattled by the thought that even now, in an ordinary warehouse in America, his body could
respond as if he were back in combat. That incident, more than any abstract wish to “talk to
someone,” brought him in. He says he is tired of organizing his life around what might set him off,
tired of feeling as though danger is always one sound away. At the same time, he is wary of
treatment. Part of him fears that revisiting the war will make it more vivid, not less. What he
hopes for, though he says it cautiously, is simple: to feel older than the war at last, and to get
through a workday without bracing for battle.
