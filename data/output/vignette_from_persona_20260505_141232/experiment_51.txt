Nadia is a 24-year-old single Middle Eastern American woman who works as a tailor in a small
alterations shop, where most days are spent bent over hems and seams, listening to the whir of
sewing machines and the stop-and-start sounds of customers entering from the street. She traces the
beginning of her current difficulties to a life-threatening motor vehicle collision several months
ago. She had been riding in a car when another vehicle came at them fast enough that there was only
a fragment of time to register it. What remains most vivid to her is not a coherent story but a
sequence that returns with brutal clarity: the sudden recognition that the car was about to be hit,
the impact, the sense of having no control, and the certainty in that instant that she might die.
Even now, when the memory comes, it does not feel to Nadia like remembering in the ordinary sense.
It arrives as though the scene is happening again in the present, with the same terror flooding back
so completely that for a moment she is no longer in the room she is standing in.

In the days after the crash, Nadia found herself going over the collision repeatedly, as if a film
had lodged in her mind and had started playing without her consent. Sometimes she would be pinning a
sleeve or measuring a customer’s waist and the scene would begin again in her head, vivid and
immediate, carrying the same frightened conviction that disaster can come without warning. She had
come to believe that this was not a freak event but evidence of how the world really is. To her, the
lesson of the crash felt obvious: the world is dangerous, people are not what they seem, and safety
depends on staying especially careful because you never know what can happen next.

That belief shows up in the way she moves through ordinary settings. In the alterations shop, Nadia
prefers to position herself where she can see the front door and as much of the room as possible. If
she has to sit with her back exposed to the entrance, she becomes tense and distracted, glancing up
repeatedly. In waiting rooms, cafés, or family gatherings, she looks for a chair against the wall
before she can settle. She scans faces, doorways, traffic outside windows, and any sudden movement
nearby. She experiences this not as a habit but as a necessity; what frightens her most is the
thought of being caught unaware again. Even at night, when she tries to sleep, some part of her
feels she needs to remain alert. She lies in bed listening for sounds in the apartment and in the
street, delaying sleep because fully letting go feels unsafe.

Nadia has also stopped driving and avoids being a passenger whenever she can. At first this meant
turning down social invitations that required a ride across town. Then it began to affect work. She
has missed chances to help with fittings at a second location because she could not make herself get
into a car. When relatives or coworkers offer her a lift, she imagines the route ahead in
catastrophic detail, mentally rehearsing crashes, impact points, ambulances, and what she would do
if the driver lost control. She tells herself that thinking through the worst-case scenario is the
only way to be prepared. If she does have to travel by vehicle, she spends the trip watching
intersections, other cars, and the driver’s movements, bracing at changes in speed and checking
constantly for danger.

Over time, these patterns have narrowed her life. Friends have noticed how often she declines
invitations, and at work she is more distant, interrupted by moments when her attention is pulled
away by the replay of the collision. She is embarrassed by how quickly terror returns when something
reminds her of that day, yet she is also certain that her vigilance is justified. In her mind,
relaxing would be naive. By the time she sought help, Nadia was exhausted from sleeping lightly,
tense from constant scanning, and frustrated by how much effort it took to get through a normal day
in the shop. What finally brought her in was the recognition that the crash was still happening
inside her whenever the memory surfaced, and that more and more of her decisions were being
organized around avoiding cars and staying on guard. She says she wants her life back, but she is
afraid that if she lets herself feel safe, even briefly, something terrible will happen again.
