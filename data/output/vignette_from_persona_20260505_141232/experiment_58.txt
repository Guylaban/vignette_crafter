his family had long relied on for steadiness, practical judgment, and patience. He is married and
had been used to moving through long hospital shifts with a clear sense of purpose. That changed
after he was mugged while walking back to his car after an evening shift. The parking structure was
dim and mostly empty, and he remembered noticing footsteps that seemed to echo behind him before he
reached his vehicle. When he turned, a man closed the distance quickly, demanded his wallet, and
struck him when he hesitated. Ravi remembered the event not as a flowing sequence but as stark
visual fragments: the shine of concrete under fluorescent light, the attacker’s hand, the angle of
the man’s face, the hard ground rushing up. He was hit and hurt in the struggle, and what stayed
with him afterward was not only fear but the bodily memory of where he had been injured. When the
memory came back, he felt pain again in those same places, as if his body were replaying the assault
along with the images.

In the weeks that followed, Ravi found that the mugging lived in his mind as pictures rather than a
story. He would say it was like looking at photographs: disconnected but vivid, frozen images that
arrived without warning. A glimpse of a shadow in a reflective surface, the sense of footsteps
behind him, or entering a parking lot at the end of a shift could bring back the same visual frames.
At those moments he did not simply remember what had happened; he felt it in his body, with a
painful echo of the blows he had taken. What frightened him most was how quickly ordinary moments
could become charged. Passing a mirrored wall at the hospital, he would catch himself checking it
repeatedly, using the reflection to make sure no one was coming up behind him. If he walked down a
corridor or through the staff garage, he kept looking over his shoulder, certain that failing to do
so would leave him exposed.

Ravi’s days became organized around trying to stay ahead of danger. He scanned entrances, corners,
and parking areas automatically, searching other people’s posture and movements for signs of threat.
In the cafeteria, in meetings, even at family restaurants, he chose his seat based on where he could
see the door and protect his back. At work, this meant he stopped sitting where colleagues usually
gathered during breaks and instead positioned himself at the edge of the room, where he could watch
who came in and out. Coworkers noticed he seemed distracted or tense, though Ravi did not know how
to explain that he felt he had to keep checking. Mirrors became part of the same routine. He looked
into them not out of vanity but because they offered another angle on the space around him, a way to
monitor what might be behind him.

When he was not watching his surroundings, he worked hard to keep his mind occupied. He filled
silence with tasks, extra charting, television in the background, phone games, anything that might
prevent the images from surfacing. At home, his wife saw him move from one activity to another
without settling. If there was nothing immediate to do, the stillness became intolerable. Nights
were the hardest. He had trouble falling asleep, and when he did sleep, he woke easily, alert and
listening. Bed no longer felt like a place where he could let down his guard. Fatigue followed him
into his shifts, and he found himself relying even more on routines of checking and mental busyness
just to get through the day.

Alongside this, Ravi had come to believe something about the attack said something fundamental about
him. He was certain there was something about him that made it happen, as if he had carried some
weakness that others could detect. He did not describe this as a passing thought but as a conclusion
that had settled heavily into his view of himself. From there, his sense of the future narrowed. He
told himself that nothing good could happen anymore, that whatever life he had before was over, and
at times he felt with bleak certainty that he had no future at all. These beliefs sat beside the
practical facts of his life—his marriage, his job, his responsibilities—but did not loosen in the
face of them.

and how to leave. At the hospital, he was still functioning, but each shift felt like an exercise in
vigilance rather than care. What finally brought him in was not a single new crisis but the
realization that he could no longer imagine continuing like this: sleeping poorly, policing every
environment, filling every spare second so the images would not return. He came to treatment wanting
relief from the constant sense of danger, but also with a quieter hope he could barely trust—that he
might one day walk to his car, sit in a room, or lie down beside his wife without feeling that he
had to be on guard.
