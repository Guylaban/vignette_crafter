had narrowed himself into since surviving a terrorist attack. He worked as a bank teller in a busy
downtown branch, and the rhythm of the job suited the way he now lived: constant transactions, short
exchanges, no silence if he could help it. He had begun volunteering for extra shifts, staying late
to balance drawers, covering coworkers’ weekends, and taking on every tedious task he could find. He
told himself he was being responsible, but he also knew what happened when he went home too early
and the apartment became quiet. In the quiet, his mind drifted back to the day of the attack, and he
would immediately try to push it away, changing the subject in his own head, turning on the
television, scrolling his phone, doing anything not to think.

The attack had happened in a crowded public area while he was out with his then-wife on an ordinary
afternoon. What stayed with him was not only the terror and confusion of the violence itself, but
the moments when he reacted in ways he could not reconcile with the person he believed himself to
be. In the chaos, he had made split-second decisions to get away from danger, and afterward he
became fixed on what those actions said about him. He had come to believe he had failed some
essential test of character. He did not describe himself as someone who had simply been terrified;
he described himself as someone who had acted against his own values. That idea lodged hard. He had
once thought of himself as steady, dependable, the kind of man who could be counted on in a crisis.
Since the attack, he was certain that image had been exposed as false.

Over the following months, Minh became increasingly watchful in public. Walking from the employee
parking lot to the bank entrance, he repeatedly glanced over his shoulder, scanning to see whether
anyone was following him. If a customer lingered too long at the counter or if footsteps matched his
pace on the sidewalk after work, his whole body tightened. He did not frame this as anxiety so much
as necessity; from his point of view, failing to check would be careless. At night he rarely slept
through. He would wake abruptly and move through the apartment checking locks and looking around to
make sure he was safe. Even after convincing himself no one was there, he remained alert, listening
for sounds in the hallway, unable to settle fully back into sleep.

His marriage deteriorated in the same period. Minh withdrew from emotional closeness with a
determination that confused and hurt his wife. When she tried to ask what he was carrying, he shut
down or said he was tired. Physical and emotional intimacy both became difficult. Being close to
someone meant being known, and being known felt dangerous when he no longer trusted himself. He had
come to believe that if he could act as he did during the attack, then he could not rely on his own
character or judgment. He was ashamed of how quickly he became irritated, too. Minor frustrations at
home could set off sharp, angry responses that seemed to come out before he had time to stop them.
The divorce left him with a further sense that his life had split into a before and after, and that
the version of himself that had once sustained a relationship was gone.

At work, the strain showed in flashes. Minh remained outwardly competent with customers, but
coworkers noticed he was tense and easily provoked in the break room, snapping over small mistakes
or abrupt noises. He compensated by working harder. The bank became the place where he could keep
moving and not be alone with his thoughts. He took pride in being reliable there, yet even this had
a brittle quality. He no longer felt capable in the way he once had; the confidence that had defined
him was missing, replaced by the sense that he was only managing by staying busy enough not to feel.

By the time he sought help, Minh was trapped between exhaustion and vigilance. He was sleeping
badly, spending little time with anyone outside work, and structuring his days around not thinking.
What finally brought him in was the realization that the strategies he relied on were shrinking his
life without giving him any real sense of safety. He was scared that this was permanent—that he had
become an angry, mistrustful man who could not depend on himself and could not be close to anyone
again. At the same time, he still held a quiet hope that if someone could help him face what
happened without being swallowed by it, he might recover some version of the person he used to
recognize as himself.
