Elena Morales is a 78-year-old widow who works at a call center, spending long shifts with a headset
on, listening to strangers describe billing problems, family emergencies, and losses that spill into
conversations not meant for them. The event that divides her life into before and after happened
years ago during a terrorist attack in a busy public area she had been passing through on an
ordinary day. She remembers the crowd first, then a sudden violent rupture that turned familiar
space into panic and confusion. In the aftermath there were people screaming for relatives, calling
names, asking who had seen whom. What remains most sharply with her is not a neat narrative but
fragments: the sense of danger, the cries of people who had lost loved ones, and the pain that
seized her neck as her body locked with fear. When she tries to describe what happened, she stops
and presses her fingers to her throat or the base of her neck. She says she knows what happened, but
the words do not come. There are stretches she simply cannot account for, blank places she has gone
over again and again without being able to fill them in.

In the weeks that followed, Elena found that certain moments could pull her back into the emotional
force of that day without warning. If a caller at work began talking about a death in the family or
about losing someone suddenly, her body reacted before she could think. Her neck would stiffen and
ache, and she would go quiet, gripping the desk until the call ended. Colleagues noticed that she
sometimes asked to transfer calls that sounded “too personal,” though she rarely explained why. On
especially difficult days, even hearing other agents nearby speak to grieving customers was enough
to make her feel as if something terrible were pressing in around her again. She also discovered
that talking directly about the attack itself was nearly unbearable. When family members once asked
her to tell them what happened, she felt trapped by the effort; what frightened her was not only the
memory but the fact that she could not make it into a story. That inability left her ashamed and
frustrated, as if failing to speak about it meant failing to master it.

Over time Elena narrowed her life carefully. She stopped going anywhere near the place where the
attack occurred, even when doing so would have been the most convenient route. She planned errands
around whole sections of the city and told others she was “too tired” to accompany them if there was
any chance of passing nearby. As anniversaries connected to the event approached, she became more
vigilant and withdrew further. She avoided news coverage, declined invitations, and tried to keep
the date from being mentioned aloud. She did not want family to sit with her and remember. She had
come to believe that opening herself emotionally to others on those days, or really on most days,
would leave her exposed to more pain she could not bear. Since being widowed she had already known
loneliness, but after the attack she kept even affectionate concern at a distance. If a daughter or
granddaughter tried to draw her out, Elena would change the subject, insist she was fine, or end the
visit early. Emotional closeness felt dangerous, as though caring openly for people only set the
stage for losing them.

The meanings she gives the event are harsh and fixed. Elena is certain that she cannot stop bad
things from happening to her. At her age, she says, she should have some peace, but instead she
feels that catastrophe can arrive anywhere, without warning, and leave her unable to protect herself
or anyone else. She has also come to believe she failed in her most important role in life. Though
she does not elaborate easily, the thought carries the weight of being a mother and matriarch who
should have been able to keep family safe, steady, and reassured. Instead, she sees herself as
someone who became frightened, withdrawn, and unavailable. When she looks ahead, she sees little
future beyond enduring days carefully and avoiding what might upset her. Hope feels remote to her,
almost naive.

What finally brought Elena to seek help was not a single crisis but the accumulation of these
constrictions. At work, more calls seemed to involve bereavement or frightened family members, and
she found herself dreading entire shifts because she did not know what voice or phrase might catch
her off guard. Her world had grown smaller, and her family had begun telling her they missed her
even when she was sitting right beside them. She came to treatment still struggling to put the
attack into words, still unable to account for parts of it, and still bracing whenever her neck
starts to hurt, as if her body is warning her that danger is near. What she fears most is that this
is all that remains of her life now: avoiding, enduring, and waiting for the next blow. What she
hopes, quietly and with difficulty, is that she might find some way to live in the present without
feeling so defenseless.
