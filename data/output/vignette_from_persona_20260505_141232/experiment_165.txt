Lina is a 19-year-old Southeast Asian American woman who works as a hairdresser in a busy salon, and
when she speaks about the event that brought her to treatment, she does so in a way that shifts
abruptly between stark visual detail and flat distance. Several months earlier, she had been driving
with another young person in the car when a moment on the road ended in a serious collision and
grave injury to the other person. She returns to fragments rather than a smooth story: the sudden
sight of cars colliding, the violent angle of impact, shattered light, the other person hurt because
she was behind the wheel. At times she describes these scenes as if she is looking at photographs
she cannot put down. At other moments she recounts what happened in a strangely emotionless voice,
saying it can feel as though it happened to someone else, only for that detachment to collapse and
be replaced by overwhelming grief. What remains most fixed for her is the conviction that she should
have protected the other person and failed.

In the weeks after the accident, Lina found that unwanted thoughts forced themselves in without
warning. She could be folding towels at the salon, listening to dryers and chatter, and suddenly see
the image of cars hit by accident as vividly as if it were happening again. Loud noises became
especially potent. A metal cart clanging, a blow dryer dropped on the station floor, a car horn
outside the salon windows could send a shock through her body before she had time to think. She
describes her heart pounding, her muscles locking, and a wave of physical alarm that made no sense
to anyone around her but felt immediate and total to her. Even when she knew she was standing in a
safe room with coworkers she liked, she could not relax. She has begun scanning for exits the moment
she enters any building, including the salon where she has worked for over a year. She notices the
front door, the back room, the alley exit, and keeps track of how quickly she could get out.

The changes spread into her daily life. Because driving had become unbearable, Lina stopped driving
altogether and avoided being a passenger whenever she could. Getting to work became complicated,
requiring rides, careful timing, and occasional missed shifts when she could not bring herself to
get into a vehicle. At the salon she tries to hide how much effort this takes. When clients or
coworkers become impatient, she quickly becomes excessively agreeable, saying yes too fast,
apologizing even when she has done nothing wrong, and trying to keep everyone pleased. She says that
when she feels the slightest hint of tension from another person, she becomes compliant
automatically, as if smoothing things over might prevent something worse. This has altered how she
works with clients; she overaccommodates requests, gives up breaks, and lets others set the pace
even when she is exhausted.

Dates connected to the accident have also taken on special significance. As those days approach,
Lina becomes preoccupied and withdrawn. She avoids mentioning them, avoids places or conversations
that might remind her what day it is, and tries to get through them as if they were ordinary. She
does this deliberately, because she is afraid of what will happen if she lets herself fully register
the date. Even so, the memories come in pictures. She sees the road, the collision, the injured
person. Sometimes she feels nothing while recalling it, which frightens her in its own way;
sometimes grief arrives so strongly that she has to stop what she is doing.

What she has come to believe since the accident is bleak and deeply personal. She is certain she
failed in the most basic responsibility to protect someone else. She also says she cannot rely on
other people, a belief that shows up in how little she asks for from anyone, including her partner.
She accepts rides reluctantly, worries others will let her down, and keeps much of her distress to
herself. Alongside that is a shaken sense of her own body. She says she no longer trusts it: her
hands, her reactions, her judgment all feel unreliable to her now. That fear is sharpened whenever
her body surges into panic at a loud sound or reminder, as if it might betray her again without
warning.

By the time Lina sought help, the strain had become visible. Transportation problems were affecting
her work, and at the salon she was spending as much energy managing herself as cutting and styling
hair. What finally brought her in was the sense that her world was getting smaller: fewer places she
could go, fewer rides she could tolerate, fewer moments in which she felt safe. She wants relief
from the constant alertness and the sudden images, but what frightens her most is the possibility
that the accident says something permanent about her—that she is someone who failed another person
and can no longer trust either others or herself.
