At 39, Arjun Patel came to treatment as a married man who had spent years building a reputation as a
careful, imaginative interior designer, someone clients trusted to make homes feel livable after
upheaval. The loss that marked him began unexpectedly with the death of a loved one, an event he
still spoke about in a clipped, halting way, as if letting himself linger on it might pull him back
into it. What remained most vivid was not an orderly narrative but the force of being confronted
with death where there should have been ordinary life, and the sense that one moment had split time
into before and after. Even now, when he was caught off guard, he could suddenly feel as if the
experience were happening again rather than being remembered, his surroundings thinning out while
his body braced for something terrible that had already happened.

In the first months afterward, Arjun found that reminders of loss seemed to gather everywhere. If
someone mentioned a relative who had died, or a client casually referred to “losing” a parent, he
felt a sharp internal shift. He would go still, then restless. He described moments when
conversation in a room continued around him but he was no longer fully in it; for a few seconds he
was back inside the original shock, with the same physical jolt and the same certainty that
something irreversible had just occurred. He noticed that his body reacted before his mind could
catch up. His chest tightened, his shoulders stiffened, and he became ready for danger before he
could even tell himself what had triggered him. That suddenness frightened him. It made the world
feel unreliable, as though grief could break through any surface without warning.

Over time, he began arranging his life to reduce the chances of that happening. He avoided funerals
altogether, even for people he cared about, telling himself he would only make things harder for
others if he went. Cemeteries and grief-related gatherings became places he would not enter. The
anniversary of the death became especially loaded. As the date approached each year, he grew
increasingly tense and preoccupied, watching the calendar with dread. Then he would try not to look
at it at all. He stayed busy, worked late, and sometimes planned distractions so he would not have
to acknowledge the day. He had come to believe that if he let himself notice the anniversary
directly, he would lose control of his reactions.

His efforts to stay prepared took another form as well. Arjun spent long stretches mentally
rehearsing worst-case scenarios. If his wife did not answer a call right away, if a family member
was delayed, if a client texted that they needed to “talk,” his mind leapt ahead into disaster. He
told himself this was practical, that imagining the worst might soften the blow if something
happened again. Instead, it left him perpetually keyed up. He checked details, revised plans, and
tried to anticipate bad news before it arrived. At work, where much of his day involved meeting
clients in homes and discussing the intimate meanings attached to spaces and belongings, this became
especially difficult. When clients spoke about redesigning a room after a death in the family, or
wanting to “move on” after losing someone, Arjun felt his body surge into alarm. He sometimes became
abrupt in meetings, snapping over small changes or reacting sharply to assistants when materials
were late. The irritability embarrassed him afterward, but in the moment he felt cornered and raw,
as if he had to push something away before it overwhelmed him.

These habits narrowed his life in ways that became visible to his marriage. His wife saw that
certain conversations ended abruptly, that invitations involving memorial services were refused
immediately, and that the days around the anniversary brought a familiar tension into the house. He
was more easily angered, more likely to withdraw, and less able to tolerate ordinary uncertainty.
What frightened him most was not only the pain of the loss itself but how quickly he could be
transported back into it. He was certain that if he let down his guard around reminders of death,
the experience would return full force.

He finally sought help after another anniversary season left him on edge for weeks and after a work
meeting in which a client’s casual mention of a funeral left him briefly disoriented and then
unusually sharp with everyone around him. The incident unsettled him because it happened in the
middle of a normal workday, in the kind of conversation he could not reliably avoid. Sitting in
therapy, Arjun said he was tired of organizing his life around what he could not bear to encounter.
At the same time, he was afraid that speaking directly about the loss, or even marking its
anniversary openly, would make the whole thing feel present again. What he hoped for, cautiously,
was not to forget, but to be able to hear about loss, to face certain dates, and to move through his
work and marriage without feeling ambushed by the past.
