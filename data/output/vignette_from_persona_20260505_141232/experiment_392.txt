Ravi Patel is a 64-year-old warehouse worker who came to treatment after months of worsening nights,
saying that darkness now feels less like the end of the day than the beginning of something
dangerous. He is married and has spent years on steady physical work, unloading shipments and moving
pallets in a large distribution warehouse on the outskirts of town. He described the traumatic event
as a period of captivity that began during an evening shift, at almost the same hour that still
troubles him now. He had been finishing work when he was forcibly confined by others and kept
overnight in a dark enclosed space. What stands out in his account is not elaboration but the
sensory precision of what remains with him: the darkness, the helpless waiting, and the repeated
awareness that he could not get out or protect himself. At one point during that captivity, he
became acutely aware of snakes nearby, or believed they were nearby, and since then even casual
conversation about snakes or a photograph of one is enough to make his whole body tense. He still
speaks about that time carefully, as if getting too close to the memory might undo him. He has come
to believe that if he lets himself think about it fully, he will not be able to handle what comes
up.

In the weeks after the event, Ravi noticed that the approach of the same time of day brought a shift
in him even when nothing was happening around him. Late afternoon moving toward evening became
charged. At work, as the light outside the warehouse bay doors started to fade, he would find
himself scanning the floor behind him and turning suddenly to check whether anyone was there. The
warehouse itself, with its dim aisles and shadowed corners, became hard to tolerate once daylight
dropped. He stayed on edge through the last part of his shift, certain he needed to remain
superalert. When coworkers walked up behind him unexpectedly, he would jerk around before he even
knew he was doing it. He did not explain much to them. He only knew that his body behaved as if
danger was close and could come from behind.

Night became the hardest part of the day. Ravi reported that he finds it much harder to feel safe at
night than during daylight, and this has shaped his routine at home. He delays going to bed for as
long as he can, lingering in the living room with lights on, dozing in a chair rather than lying
down in the dark. Waking in darkness is especially difficult; when he opens his eyes and the room is
black, he is immediately alert, checking his body for signs that something is wrong, running his
hands over himself, noticing every ache or sensation as if it might mean injury or danger. He has
also begun repeatedly asking his wife to tell him he is okay. Sometimes he asks directly whether the
doors are locked, whether everything is fine, whether he seems normal. The reassurance helps only
briefly, and he often asks again. His wife has become worried and worn down, especially because
neither of them is sleeping well anymore.

Over time, Ravi’s world has narrowed around these habits. He avoids lying in the dark, avoids images
or talk of snakes, and avoids letting his mind settle on what happened. He is not dismissive about
the event; rather, he seems frightened by the possibility of remembering too much. What frightened
him most in session was not only the event itself but the sense that it proved something unbearable:
that bad things can happen to him and he cannot stop them. He said this with the flat certainty of
someone who no longer experiences safety as dependable. Alongside that, he admitted with difficulty
that there are times he is not sure his life is worth living anymore. He did not describe a wish to
die so much as a bleakness about whether life can feel livable again if every evening turns into a
period of watchfulness.

What finally brought Ravi to seek help was a recent stretch of nights when he had hardly slept and
began dreading the end of each workday. One evening in the warehouse, as the shift moved toward
closing time, a coworker joked about finding a snake in a shipment, and Ravi felt himself go cold
and immediately looked behind him, then around the loading area, as if he had been transported back
to that enclosed darkness. He went home shaken, stayed awake most of the night, and kept asking his
wife to reassure him that he was safe. By the time he came for treatment, he was exhausted and
discouraged. He said he wants help because he cannot keep living with every night feeling dangerous,
but he is also afraid that talking about what happened will make it all come back in a way he will
not be able to bear.
