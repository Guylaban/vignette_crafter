David is a 51-year-old widowed school principal who came to treatment after months of trying to
organize himself around a fear he could not neatly explain to other people. The traumatic event was
a life-threatening illness that unfolded in the clinical world he now goes to considerable lengths
to avoid. He described stretches of that period as strangely inaccessible when he tries to call them
up on purpose, as if the worst parts will not line up into a coherent story when he reaches for them
directly. What remains sharply present are sensory fragments and bodily states. He remembers the
smell of antiseptic in treatment rooms and, at other moments, the sour, medicinal smell of alcohol
swabs and hand sanitizer just before someone touched him or prepared a procedure. During
examinations he had lain still in a rigid, exposed position, acutely aware that his survival
depended on other people and on a body that no longer felt dependable. What frightened him most was
not just the possibility of dying, but the realization that his own body could turn dangerous and
incomprehensible to him.

In the months after he recovered enough to return to work, David expected to feel grateful and
resume his old life. Instead he found that ordinary moments at school could abruptly take on the
force of the hospital. A custodian cleaning a hallway with a sharp disinfectant, a nurse opening an
alcohol wipe in the front office, even stepping into the school health room could stop him cold. At
those times he did not necessarily see a full picture in his mind. More often the feeling arrived
first and all at once, with the same intensity he had felt when he was critically ill. He would go
tense and still, and he noticed his body seeming to brace into the same position he had been in
during examinations, as if he were back on a table waiting for something to be done to him. Because
the memories were so difficult to retrieve deliberately, these moments felt even more unsettling to
him; he could not tell himself a complete story and set it aside. He was left with the conviction
that his body knew something catastrophic before his mind could catch up.

David had once thought of himself as composed, socially capable, and dependable under pressure,
qualities that had served him well as a principal. Since the illness, he has come to feel that “the
person I’ve become” is someone he barely recognizes. He no longer trusts his body, reading normal
sensations with suspicion and feeling that it could fail him again without warning. Alongside that
is a quieter but equally painful belief that he cannot rely on himself. In meetings with teachers or
parents, he now rehearses conversations in advance, sometimes word for word, before any interaction.
He does not describe this as perfectionism so much as self-protection: if he scripts what he will
say, perhaps he will not falter, freeze, or reveal how unsettled he feels. Even brief unscheduled
conversations in the hallway can leave him internally scrambling.

Outside work his world has narrowed. Widowed before the illness, David had already been accustomed
to managing much of life alone, but over time he cut off nearly all contact with friends and
extended family. Texts go unanswered, invitations are declined, and phone calls are allowed to ring
out. He says it feels easier not to explain himself, easier not to be seen as altered or fragile.
The isolation has had visible consequences. Colleagues have noticed he no longer joins staff dinners
or community events, and a sister who used to speak with him weekly now hears from him only
sporadically. At school he avoids situations that might bring him close to medical cues. He
delegates student hospital visits to assistant administrators, takes routes that bypass the nurse’s
office, and has put off his own physical examinations despite knowing they are important. The idea
of sitting in an exam room, smelling antiseptic, waiting for a clinician to begin, feels
intolerable.

What finally brought David in was a recent medical appointment he had delayed and then forced
himself to attend. As soon as he entered the clinic and caught the smell of alcohol and
disinfectant, the emotional force hit before any clear image did. His body tightened into that
familiar frozen posture, and afterward he sat in his car unable to drive for several minutes, shaken
and ashamed. That episode frightened him not only because it was intense, but because it confirmed
how little command he feels he has over himself and over his body. By the time he sought help, he
was living with the sense that his life had become organized around avoiding reminders and
concealing how changed he feels. What he fears most is that he will never again be able to trust his
body or recognize himself in the person he has become. What he hopes for, cautiously, is to be able
to enter a medical office or walk past the school nurse’s door without feeling as though he is back
in the worst moments of his illness.
