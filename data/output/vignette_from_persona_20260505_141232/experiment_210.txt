Nadia is a 34-year-old Middle Eastern American woman who came to treatment after more than a year of
trying to manage on her own. She is divorced and works long days as an agricultural worker, moving
between fields, packing sheds, and shared work areas where conversations travel easily and people
are constantly coming and going. The event she returns to began during a workday when she witnessed
a violent trauma involving another person. What remains most vivid to her is not a single orderly
memory but the atmosphere of danger around it: the sudden shift in people’s faces, the sense that
something was wrong before anyone said it out loud, the sound of a baby crying nearby while adults
began shouting over each other. She remembers watching someone who had seemed ordinary moments
earlier become frightening, and afterward she could not shake the feeling that threat announces
itself in small, unreadable changes that other people miss until it is too late.

In the weeks after, Nadia told herself she had to stay strong and get on with work. She made a
deliberate effort to think in bright, disciplined phrases—everything is fine, it could have been
worse, just focus on the good—because she felt that allowing herself to dwell on what she had seen
would be dangerous. When images or fragments of the scene came into her mind, she pushed them away
quickly and sharply, as if refusing them entry. She described doing this almost automatically,
especially at night or on the drive to work. At the same time, she began cleaning with an intensity
that surprised even her. She would come home and reorganize kitchen shelves, wipe counters
repeatedly, sort and re-sort drawers, and continue long after the apartment was already clean. The
repetition gave her a brief sense that things could still be put in order.

Her world gradually became organized around moments that felt charged. At work, if two coworkers
lowered their voices or exchanged a look she could not read, Nadia’s body would tense before she had
fully registered why. She found herself watching for people who seemed “off,” studying their
movements, tone, and expressions. She had come to believe that people are not what they seem and
that the most dangerous person in a setting may be the one everyone else is overlooking. Ordinary
conversations in the fields or break area could suddenly become unbearable if they turned toward
violence, crime, family tragedy, or anything that echoed the circumstances of what she witnessed.
When that happened, she would walk away to check equipment, volunteer for a different task, or busy
herself with sorting produce rather than remain where she had to hear it.

Babies’ cries were especially potent. In public, in workers’ housing nearby, even hearing a baby
from a passing car could stop her mid-thought and bring back the helpless, exposed feeling of that
day. She did not describe this in abstract terms; she said it felt as if the air changed around her,
as if she were again bracing for something terrible to unfold in front of her. Because agricultural
work often places her near other families and laborers, these moments were difficult to avoid
entirely. On days when she heard a baby crying near the packing area or in the distance from
neighboring housing, she became preoccupied and distracted, then ashamed that she could not simply
ignore it.

Over time Nadia’s view of herself in the world narrowed and hardened. She spoke with sadness about
having lost faith—not only in other people but in life itself, and in the sense that there is any
larger goodness watching over events. Since her divorce she had already felt unmoored, but after the
trauma that feeling deepened into a conviction that she no longer belonged anywhere. She no longer
felt fully at ease with coworkers, did not want to confide in family, and found little comfort in
the beliefs or community practices that had once steadied her. Being around others often left her
feeling separate, as if everyone else were participating in a world whose basic safety she no longer
accepted.

What finally brought Nadia to seek help was not one dramatic crisis but exhaustion. She was tired of
spending so much energy forcing herself to think positively, tired of batting memories away, tired
of cleaning late into the night for a few minutes of relief. More than once at work she had caught
herself leaving a conversation abruptly because the topic turned in a direction she could not bear,
and she worried others were noticing her withdrawal. She came in saying she did not want to live
like someone always scanning, always organizing, always pretending. What frightened her most was the
possibility that she would never again trust anyone or feel that she belonged anywhere. What she
hoped for, though she said it cautiously, was to feel less on guard in her own life.
