Moana Te Rangi is a 65-year-old widow who has spent years working as a cybersecurity analyst,
someone accustomed to scanning for vulnerabilities and staying several steps ahead of danger. The
accident that now divides her life into before and after happened on her drive home one winter
evening. A truck ran a red light and struck the driver’s side of her car with enough force to crush
the door inward. She remembers the impact not as a sequence but as violent images: the flash of
headlights too close, shattered glass suspended in the air, the warped frame of the car pinning her
in place. There was the sharp smell of gasoline and hot metal, and a pain in her side and leg so
immediate that even now, when the memory returns, she feels pain again in those same places as if
her body is replaying it with her mind. She has said that when she thinks about it, she can smell
the scene before she can form words for it.

In the first months after the crash, Moana found herself going over and over why it had happened to
her. She could spend an entire evening circling the same questions without relief, replaying the
ordinary decisions of that day—what time she left work, whether she should have taken another route,
why she was the one in the intersection when the truck came through. What stayed with her most
strongly was the sense that it was deeply unfair. She had been following the rules, coming home from
work, living carefully, and yet she was the one whose body was broken open by someone else’s
mistake. She speaks about the accident with a simmering disbelief that nothing could undo what was
done and that nothing adequate had been done about it since.

As her physical injuries healed, her trust in her body did not return with them. She had come to
believe that her body was no longer reliable, that it could fail her suddenly and leave her trapped
again. A twinge in her hip, a moment of stiffness, even ordinary fatigue could feel like proof that
she was not safe inside herself. She also became acutely self-conscious around other people. In
meetings at work, especially when she had to walk into a conference room after a bad night, she felt
exposed, as if colleagues could see the accident on her just by looking at her face or the way she
carried herself. No one had to say anything directly; her conviction was that the crash had marked
her in a way other people would detect immediately.

Moana’s memories have remained strikingly sensory. They come mostly as pictures rather than a full
story: the interior of the car caved in, the windshield fractured into a white web, the impossible
nearness of the truck. Sometimes they are accompanied by smell so vivid that she checks the room
around her, certain for a second that gasoline is present. At those times the pain in her side and
leg flares as well, and she instinctively braces herself. She has tried hard to erase the memory,
pushing it away the moment it begins to form, refusing photographs from that time, cutting off
conversations if anyone mentions crashes or road safety. More broadly, she has started avoiding
anything that might stir feeling at all, not only distressing things but positive ones. She turns
off films when the music swells, declines family gatherings that might become emotional, and keeps
her days narrowed to work tasks, meals, and television she does not care about. To her, any strong
feeling seems risky; once emotion starts, she does not trust where it will lead.

Night has become the most difficult part of each day. Moana finds it harder to feel safe after dark
than in daylight, even in her own home. She delays going to sleep because she feels she needs to
stay alert. When she does lie down, she listens for sounds in the house and outside it, as if
vigilance itself might prevent something from happening. She wakes repeatedly and checks whether she
is safe—looking at the doors, the windows, the hallway, orienting herself to where she is before
trying again to rest. By morning she is often exhausted, then sits at her workstation reviewing
security alerts with the strained concentration of someone who has not really slept. The irony of
spending her days monitoring digital threats is not lost on her; she notices how easily a minor
system anomaly can jolt her, and she has begun turning down optional on-site days because commuting
and being in the office leave her feeling too exposed.

Over the past year the cost of living this way has become harder for her to ignore. She is more
withdrawn from the people who care about her, less present in the parts of life that once gave her
steadiness, and increasingly tired from nights spent on guard. What finally brought her to seek help
was not a new event but the realization that the accident was still arriving in her life as smell,
image, and pain, and that she was organizing entire days around not feeling and not remembering. She
says she is frightened that this is simply who she is now: a woman who cannot trust her body, cannot
sleep, and cannot stop carrying the unfairness of what happened. At the same time, she is
hoping—carefully, almost reluctantly—that there may be some way to live without constantly scanning
for danger and without needing to erase her own mind to get through the day.
