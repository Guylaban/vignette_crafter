At 80, Minh Tran still worked full-time as a data scientist for a large hospital system, a role that
suited the orderly, quiet habits he had built over decades. He was a widower and lived alone, and
most of his days were structured around code, meetings, and long hours in front of multiple screens.
The event that remained with him came from much earlier in his life, when he had worked as a relief
worker in a war zone. He spoke about that period carefully, as if too much detail might pull him
somewhere he did not want to go, but certain scenes returned with relentless clarity. He remembered
being inside a crowded aid station after shelling, with civilians packed into a concrete building
that had already taken damage. People were injured, frightened, shouting over one another, and
supplies were running out. What stayed with him most sharply was the feeling of being trapped with
no good choices left, hearing people beg for help he could not give, and watching some men push past
the weakest to seize food and medical supplies for themselves. Even now, what struck him as
unbearable was not only the danger but the human behavior inside it: the selfishness, the disorder,
the way panic made people cruel.

In the years after he left, the memories did not settle into the past in the way he had expected.
When they came, they came as if they were occurring again. He described them not as recollections
but as a film that started playing without his consent. If he was reminded of the war zone, he did
not simply think about it; for a few moments he felt back inside that building, with the noise, the
pressure, and the certainty that there was no way out. He said the worst times were after sleep. If
he woke from a nightmare, even one he could not fully remember, he would sit upright in bed already
tense, his heart pounding, and for several seconds or minutes the room did not feel entirely like
his bedroom in the present. Those mornings could spoil the whole day, leaving him irritable and
ashamed of how shaken he still was.

Over time he came to believe these reactions meant something was wrong with his mind. At his age, he
told himself, a man should have control over memory and temper. Instead he found himself feeling
sudden surges of fear, anger, guilt, and disgust that seemed too large for the situations in front
of him. He had become frightened not only of the world but of himself. He was certain the world was
dangerous in a basic way, that beneath ordinary appearances people could turn selfish or violent
very quickly. Just as troubling to him was the thought that one day he might lose control of his own
anger and do something terrible. He had never described acting violently, but the force of his
irritation scared him. A rude remark in a store, someone shoving ahead in line, a colleague behaving
carelessly in a meeting—these moments could flood him with the same moral outrage he felt in the aid
station, and he would have to clamp his jaw shut and leave before he said more than he intended.

His daily life had narrowed around these reactions. At work he prepared excessively for even
ordinary interactions, rehearsing what he would say before meetings with younger colleagues, before
asking for a correction, even before brief conversations with reception staff. He wanted to
eliminate the possibility of being caught off guard, of saying something sharp, of discovering there
were no good options once an interaction had begun. In conference rooms and hallways he constantly
scanned his surroundings, noting exits, voices, sudden movements, and shifts in tone before he could
attend to the actual conversation. He also avoided news coverage of wars, humanitarian disasters,
and refugee crises. Because his job required long hours online, this avoidance had become effortful
and deliberate; he scrolled past headlines quickly, muted keywords, and asked coworkers not to
mention graphic events near him. He knew this made him seem detached, especially in a hospital
system where global suffering was often discussed, but he felt he had no choice.

These habits affected his work and relationships. Colleagues experienced him as formal and exacting,
and he knew some avoided him after he snapped during minor disputes. He spent less time with friends
from his temple community because public irritability embarrassed him, and after his wife died there
was no one at home to absorb the tension or gently interrupt it. What finally brought him to seek
help was a recent morning after a nightmare when he arrived at work already on edge. During a
meeting, a coworker brushed aside concerns about data errors with what Minh experienced as
carelessness, and he felt a burst of anger so strong that he had to leave the room abruptly. He
stood in the hallway, body rigid and ready, scanning the space as if danger were nearby, and was
shaken by how quickly the past had seemed to overtake the present. He came to treatment frightened
that he was going crazy or becoming an angry old man no one could trust, but also hoping that the
memories might begin to feel like something that happened long ago rather than something that was
still happening now.
