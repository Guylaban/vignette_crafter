to believe it actually happened to him.

Years earlier, Thomas had been sent to report on a man-made disaster, arriving in the aftermath of a

and the professional reflex to observe closely. He remembers the visual field first: twisted debris,

remains with him is not a coherent account but a sequence that returns like film clips: the ruined
setting, the shock of not knowing whether another blast or attack could come, the sense that anyone
nearby might still be in danger. The unpredictability of those hours lodged in him as much as the
horror did.

In the weeks afterward, Thomas tried to continue working. He told himself he had seen hard things
before. Yet ordinary moments began to split open. If a related photo appeared in a newspaper
archive, on television, or on a colleague’s screen, he felt himself pulled out of the present. The
memory did not come to him as a thought so much as a replay, vivid and repetitive, as though the
same scene were projected again and again behind his eyes. At times, when he remembered it, he felt
as if it were happening right now. He could taste that same flavor in his mouth and would go still,
his body reacting before he had words for what was happening. He became wary of anything
unpredictable—a sudden change in plans, an unexpected noise, a disruption in a routine
assignment—because uncertainty itself felt charged.

As the years passed, his life narrowed around precautions that felt necessary to him. In
restaurants, interview settings, and even at family gatherings, Thomas always chose a seat with his
back to the wall so he could see the room. He scanned automatically for heavy lamps, letter openers,
kitchen knives, anything that could be used as a weapon if something went wrong. At home, especially
at night, he listened for sounds that might mean someone was entering. He knew, on one level, that
his apartment was locked and quiet, but he could not relax in it. Safe places did not feel safe in
his body. When something startled him or he felt threatened, he did not leap into action; instead he
froze completely, as if stuck in place while his mind raced.

reasons to pass those assignments to younger reporters, then felt ashamed of needing to. Over time
his avoidance spread beyond work. He had come to believe that if he let himself really think about
the event, he would not be able to handle it. So he pushed thoughts away, changed channels, closed
browser windows, and steered conversations elsewhere. The costs accumulated quietly. He spent money

the purchases mattered, but because for a short while they interrupted the inner pressure. His
apartment filled with unopened packages, a visible record of his attempts to comfort or distract
himself.

What frightened Thomas now was not only the memory but what he believed it had done to him. He was

bearing witness and making sense of events for other people. The divorce had preceded the trauma,
but his solitary life made the emptiness more obvious; there was no one in the apartment to
interrupt the long evenings of scanning, replaying, and waiting.

directly at what happened, he will come apart completely—and that the deadened, watchful life he has
now may be all that remains.
