Kwame is a 55-year-old married African American man who has made his living for decades as a
painter, moving between residential jobs and commercial interiors, accustomed to long hours on
ladders, the smell of primer, and the steady satisfaction of leaving a room transformed. Before the
death of his loved one, he understood himself as dependable, practical, and able to keep going
through difficulty. He now speaks about life as divided into before and after.

The death came unexpectedly and with a violence that still feels unfinished to him. His loved one
collapsed suddenly at home. Kwame remembers the rush of trying to understand what was happening, the
helplessness of being in the house while everything changed in minutes, and the arrival of the
ambulance. What remains with him most vividly are not smooth, complete memories but stark visual
fragments: emergency lights flashing against the walls, the shape of people moving quickly through
the house, the sight of his loved one being taken away, the doorway, the stretcher. At other times
there is no picture at all, only the full force of the moment crashing back into him. He described
episodes in which grief hits with such intensity that for a few seconds it is as if his body has
remembered something his mind cannot fully bring into view. When he does deliberately recall the
day, he is overwhelmed by grief so quickly that he often stops himself mid-thought.

In the months after the death, these experiences began threading themselves into ordinary parts of
his life. Because his work takes him into other people’s homes and around active neighborhoods, he
cannot fully control what he encounters. If an ambulance passes a job site with its siren on, he
feels his whole body tighten at once. He becomes rigid, watchful, and distracted, his attention
pulled away from the wall in front of him. Even being at work itself has become loaded. Standing in
a room with drop cloths spread out and paint supplies arranged, he can suddenly feel a wave of
emotion that seems to come from nowhere and everywhere at once, as though the ordinary act of
working has become entangled with the day his own home turned into an emergency scene. On some jobs,
clients talk casually about illness, funerals, or someone “losing a loved one,” and he feels himself
shut down internally. He does not experience those conversations as sympathetic small talk; to him
they land with a rawness that makes it hard to stay present.

At home, where he once expected comfort, Kwame has found himself unable to settle. Night is
especially difficult. He says it is harder to feel safe after dark than during the day, and his body
rarely seems to stand down from readiness. He notices his muscles staying tense, his breathing
becoming shallow, and a constant sense that he must be prepared to react, though he cannot say to
what. He checks the house more than he used to and remains alert even in familiar rooms. His wife
has noticed that he does not fully relax into the couch in the evenings and that he listens closely
to sounds outside. Being inside his own home no longer guarantees the feeling of being protected.

As time has passed, Kwame has come to speak about himself in altered terms. He says he no longer
knows who he is. The version of himself he trusted—the man who could handle what needed handling,
who knew his place in his family and his work—feels inaccessible to him now. Around other people he
often feels cut off, as if he has been moved into a separate category from everyone still living
ordinary life. Even when he is with coworkers or beside his wife, there are moments when he feels
profoundly apart, unable to imagine that they can understand the force of what returns to him. He
has come to believe that the trauma destroyed his life, not only because of the loss itself but
because the life that followed has felt unrecognizable.

This has had visible consequences. On painting jobs he has become quieter and more easily derailed
when something in the environment reminds him of the death. At times he tries to avoid conversations
that might turn toward bereavement, stepping away or changing the subject, which has made him seem
distant to customers and less available to coworkers. At home, his guardedness has narrowed family
life; evenings are less easy, and his wife experiences him as physically present but difficult to
reach.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was the accumulation of these moments rather than a single
crisis. He has grown frightened by how little peace he feels, especially at night, and by the sense
that grief can overtake him without warning, sometimes as pictures and sometimes as pure emotional
force. He is afraid that this is who he is now: a man permanently cut off from himself, from other
people, and from any sense of safety. At the same time, he comes to treatment hoping there might
still be some way to live in his home, do his work, and remember his loved one without feeling that
each reminder destroys him all over again.
