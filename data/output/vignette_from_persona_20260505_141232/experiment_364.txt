Mika grew up in a Pacific Islander family in the United States and spent most of his adult life
building himself into someone others trusted: a military aviator when he was young, then later an
airline pilot, a husband who was steady, competent, and hard to rattle. The event he returns to in
treatment happened during combat decades ago, on an evening mission that had stretched into dusk.
The failing light had already made everything uncertain. He was moving with others near a landing
zone when helicopter noise pressed low overhead, loud enough to vibrate in his chest and jaw. In the
confusion around the aircraft, there was abrupt movement, shouting, bodies dropping and turning, and
a tactile detail that fixed itself in him with unusual force: the rough weave of military gear and
straps grinding against his hands and forearms as he grabbed, braced, and tried to orient himself.
He remembers the hour of it as much as anything—the particular slide from late day into night, when
shapes were still visible but safety was gone. When he recalls it now, the same terror comes back
with such force that he does not experience it as a story about the past. It is immediate, bodily,
and overwhelming.

In the years after leaving combat, Mika did what he had always done with fear and uncertainty: he
kept functioning. He transitioned into commercial aviation and built a life with his wife around
schedules, flights, and routines that looked successful from the outside. Yet he had begun to notice
that certain things could pull him out of the present instantly. The sound of helicopters was the
clearest. Even when he knew exactly where he was—driving on a freeway, standing on an airport
tarmac, hearing a medical helicopter pass over a hotel on an overnight—his body reacted before he
could think. Sometimes the memory arrived whole, without warning, as if his surroundings had opened
a trapdoor under him. A rough, scratchy texture could do it too: a certain kind of webbed strap,
coarse upholstery, stiff fabric rubbing against his skin. He found himself tugging at collars,
avoiding particular jackets, and recoiling from materials he could not easily explain to other
people. Dusk was often the worst part of the day. As evening approached, he became more uneasy, as
if the hour itself carried danger. At night he felt markedly less safe, even at home with the doors
locked.

He has tried to describe to his wife that the memory does not feel like an ordinary memory. It can
seem as though it belongs to someone else, to a man he recognizes but cannot fully claim as himself.
At other times it breaks over him with total emotional force, and that split leaves him unsettled
and ashamed. He has come to believe there is something wrong with him as a person because he cannot
file the event away the way he thinks other men did. He says he used to be happy, easier in himself,
more open to pleasure. Now he experiences himself as chronically miserable, and he speaks about that
change as if it were permanent damage.

The world, in his view, has also revealed itself plainly. He is certain it is dangerous, and he
lives accordingly. When he enters a restaurant, briefing room, hotel lobby, or airport terminal, he
automatically scans for exits before he takes in anything else. On layovers, he chooses seats with a
view of the door and notices who comes in, who stands suddenly, who moves too fast. Sudden movements
by other people put him immediately on guard; even a fellow pilot rising quickly from a jump seat or
a flight attendant stepping unexpectedly into the galley can make his body tense. These habits have
become woven into his working life. In airports full of aircraft noise and constant motion, he
remains outwardly composed, but the effort is considerable. At home, his wife has learned that
evenings can make him remote and watchful. She has also learned not to reach for him abruptly after
dark.

What finally brought him to seek help at 63 was not a single dramatic collapse but the growing sense
that his world had narrowed around vigilance, dread, and a private conviction of defectiveness.
Recently, after hearing helicopter rotors during an evening hotel stay before a scheduled flight, he
was hit so suddenly by the full memory and terror that he spent the night unable to settle, acutely
alert to every sound in the corridor, repeatedly checking where the exits were. He completed the
next day’s responsibilities, but the strain frightened him. He is coming to treatment because he
does not want the rest of his life to be organized around danger and because he misses the man he
believes he used to be. What frightens him most is that this is simply who he is now: a man who can
never feel safe after dark, never stop bracing for sudden movement, and never get far enough away
from that evening to know it is over.
