Marisol is a 54-year-old Latina combat engineer who came to treatment after years of trying to keep
herself tightly controlled at work and at home. She has been married for decades and still speaks
about the military with pride, but when she describes the deployment that stays with her, her
account breaks apart into sensory fragments rather than a single narrative. She can say where she
was assigned and that her unit was working around damaged roads and explosives, but when she tries
to explain what happened on the day that most haunts her, she loses the sequence. What remains
immediate to her are flashes and bodily impressions: the sudden sight of fire, the feel of intense
heat striking her skin, men shouting over one another, and the sense that everything had turned
dangerous in an instant.

She remembers being in the middle of a mission environment that had already taught her to expect
hidden threat. Then came a blast-related incident she still cannot recount cleanly from beginning to
end. She recalls flames rising fast enough that they seemed to swallow the scene, the heat on her
face and arms, and the voices around her turning into shouted commands and panic. Even now, when the
memory comes, she does not first think it through as a past event. She hears it. In her mind there
are the same shouted words and urgent tones from that moment, as if the sound has returned before
she has any chance to prepare herself. She says it can happen “out of nowhere,” with something in
her surroundings pulling the whole thing back in an instant before she even realizes she has been
triggered.

After she returned, the world did not feel ordinary again. Marisol had come to believe that danger
was not exceptional but everywhere, waiting behind appearances. She noticed that her body rarely
settled; she stayed tense, shoulders tight, muscles braced, as if she might need to react at any
second. Silence became almost as unsettling as noise. If a room, a street, or a worksite went
unusually quiet, she felt a rush of certainty that something bad was about to happen. In those
moments she would start scanning automatically—doorways, corners, who was standing where, what could
ignite, what did not look right. As a combat engineer she is often in environments where alertness
is part of the culture, and that made it easy for this constant checking to blend into her work
life. But coworkers noticed that she was doing more than being careful. If a training area was too
still, or if someone suddenly raised their voice across the site, Marisol’s attention would snap
away from the task in front of her. She struggled to concentrate and sometimes had to ask for
instructions to be repeated because her mind had already shifted to searching for threat.

Certain moments in daily life became especially hard to manage. A burst of flames from a grill, the
sight of a controlled burn, or even heat from machinery hitting her skin in a particular way could
bring back the whole scene so fast it felt as though past and present had collapsed together.
Shouting was particularly potent. At work, if supervisors or crew members called out loudly over
equipment noise, she could feel her body surge before she had time to think. At home, even raised
voices from a television in another room could leave her withdrawn and on edge. She found she could
not tell herself a coherent story afterward; she only knew that she had been back there again.

Over the months and years, Marisol shaped her life around reducing strain. She began avoiding
anything she thought might be stressful, not because she saw herself as fragile but because she
believed pressure made everything harder to contain. She turned down situations that felt
unpredictable, kept her routines narrow, and tried not to engage emotionally with things that might
stir her up. She describes this as numbing herself, flattening her feelings so she could get through
the day. Her husband experienced her as increasingly distant. She was physically present but hard to
reach, and when he asked what was wrong she often said she did not know how to explain it.

Alongside this was a harsh private view of herself. Marisol had come to believe there was something
wrong with her as a person because she still reacted this way after so many years. She also returned
again and again to the conviction that what happened was profoundly unfair and that nothing
meaningful had ever been done about it. That sense of injustice sat beside her broader belief that
the world is dangerous, that safety is temporary at best and never to be trusted.

She sought help only recently, after noticing that her concentration at work had become harder to
hide and that her life had narrowed to managing the next reaction. What finally brought her in was
not one dramatic crisis but the accumulation of too many moments when flames, heat on her skin, or
someone shouting transported her without warning. She says she is tired of living as if danger is
always just about to break through the silence. What frightens her most is the idea that this is
simply who she is now; what she hopes, cautiously, is that she might one day be able to remember
what happened without feeling as if it is happening again.
