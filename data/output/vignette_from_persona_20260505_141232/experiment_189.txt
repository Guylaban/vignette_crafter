Amina is a 72-year-old widowed woman who has spent most of her adult life working outdoors, and even
now, as a forester, much of her week is organized around early starts, tree lines, access roads, and
long hours in relative isolation. She came to treatment speaking plainly and without embellishment
about having lived through war as a civilian many years ago, but the scenes she described were
sharply defined. She remembered nights most vividly: being awake in darkness, unable to see who was
moving outside, waiting for the next blast after hearing a sudden explosion somewhere nearby. In her
account, the danger had often announced itself through sound before anything else—a bang in the
distance, then the understanding that more could follow, that people’s intentions were unreadable,
that someone approaching in a hurry or lingering strangely might not be safe. What remained with her
was not a single abstract memory of “the war,” but a set of moments in which darkness, explosive
sounds, and uncertainty about other people all meant immediate threat.

In the years after she left that environment and built a life in the United States, Amina did not
speak much about those experiences. She worked, raised her family, and later continued on after
being widowed, becoming known as competent and steady. But she noticed that certain moments did not
feel as though they belonged only to the past. Waking in the dark was especially difficult. If she
opened her eyes at night and the room was unlit, there was an instant in which her body seemed to
behave as though she were back in a place where darkness meant vulnerability. She would listen hard,
orient herself, and feel compelled to check the room, the hallway, the windows. She did not describe
this as unreasonable; to her, it felt like doing what was necessary before relaxing. Until she had
looked around and reassured herself about where she was, she could not settle.

Loud bangs had a similar force. A car backfiring, construction noise, a slammed metal gate, or
distant fireworks could stop her mid-thought. She said the problem was not simply disliking noise.
It was the particular quality of an explosion-like sound, abrupt and percussive, that made her feel
as if danger had broken into the present. On those days she often turned on the television or played
loud music as soon as she got home, filling the house with a steady wall of sound. She explained
that if there was enough noise she had chosen herself, she did not have to wait tensely for the next
unexpected bang. The sound gave her something to hold onto. It also meant that evenings in her home
were rarely quiet.

At work, these habits took on a practical shape. In the forest and at trailheads, she found herself
continually surveying her surroundings—tree line, vehicles, movement on access roads, figures she
did not immediately recognize. If someone was standing near the gate too long, watching rather than
passing through, or moving in a way she read as furtive, her attention fixed on them. She had become
someone who tracked people closely, not out of curiosity but because she felt she had to know where
a potential threat was at all times. Colleagues experienced her as highly alert and meticulous, but
Amina knew how effortful it was. A routine site inspection could become exhausting because so much
of her attention went into scanning for signs that a person might be dangerous. If she encountered a
suspicious-looking visitor near dusk, she was more likely to cut the task short, return to her truck
early, and keep checking her mirrors on the drive out.

Over time, these patterns narrowed her life. She avoided being in unlit places longer than necessary
and organized her evenings around keeping the house bright and noisy. She was less comfortable with
neighbors she did not know well and less willing to linger in public places after dark. Since her
husband’s death, the nights had felt longer and more exposed, and there was no one else in the house
to check a sound or tell her she was safe. She did not present herself as frightened in a general
way; rather, she was certain that danger can arrive without warning and that the safest course is to
stay prepared. What troubled her was how relentless that preparedness had become.

She finally sought help after a recent stretch in which she woke repeatedly in darkness and found
herself moving through the house to inspect doors and windows before she was fully awake. Around the
same time, a bang at a worksite left her shaken enough that she abandoned the rest of the day’s
field tasks. She said she was tired of living as if every dark room, every explosion-like sound, and
every suspicious person required the same immediate readiness. What frightened her most was not only
the return of those moments from the war, but the possibility that this would be how the rest of her
life would feel—guarded, scanning, and never fully at ease. What she hopes for is modest and very
clear: to sleep through the night without waking into threat, and to do her work in the woods
without always searching for danger in every sound and every stranger.
