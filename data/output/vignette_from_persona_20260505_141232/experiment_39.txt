When Minh Tran talks about the night his wife died, he does not tell it straight through. The memory
comes to him in jagged sections, as if pieces of glass had been swept into a pile but never fitted
back together. He is 68 now, a widowed Vietnamese American man who has spent decades working as a
public defender, and he can still summon only fragments with painful clarity: the ring of his phone
after hours, the rushed drive to the hospital, the sight of his wife on a bed behind a curtain, and
above all the sounds. He hears them again in his head when the memory comes—the monitor’s alarm,
hurried voices in the corridor, a nurse calling for assistance, his own wife’s strained breathing
before it stopped. What returns most forcefully is not a coherent sequence but those sounds and the
crushing grief that follows them. Even in session, he falters when he reaches that point, lowering
his head and pressing his hand against his mouth as if bracing against a blow.

Before her death, Minh had been known among colleagues as measured and unflappable, someone who
could sit through chaotic arraignment calendars and heated client meetings without losing his
balance. In the months afterward, he found that sense of steadiness gone. He was often seized by
powerful feelings that seemed to arrive all at once—fear, anger, guilt, and a kind of horror at how
abruptly a life could disappear. Sometimes he felt ashamed by how undone he remained, especially
because his work had exposed him to tragedy for years. He had come to believe that the world around
him contained hidden danger in ordinary people, and he watched others with a tense, appraising focus
that he recognized as excessive but could not switch off. In courthouse hallways, in elevators, in
the lobby outside interview rooms, he found himself scanning faces, clothing, posture, hands. If a
man raised his voice nearby or moved quickly toward him, Minh’s body reacted as though something
terrible were about to happen. His heart surged, his shoulders tightened, and for a moment he was
certain he was not safe, even when nothing was actually wrong.

That vigilance followed him home. At night he checked the front door, then checked it again, then
the windows, then returned to the door because he could not hold onto the feeling of certainty that
the lock had caught. He described standing in his darkened living room, looking at the deadbolt,
touching it repeatedly, trying to quiet the sense that some threat had been left unguarded against.
The house, once shared with his wife, had become both emptier and more fraught. Small sounds—a shift
in the building, footsteps outside, a car door slamming—could set off an immediate alarm in him. He
knew, even in the moment, that he was reacting beyond what the situation called for, but that
knowledge did not prevent the rush of danger.

Minh’s efforts to get through the evenings had narrowed his life over time. He stopped watching
television news and would quickly turn off films or programs if a hospital scene came on or if
someone on screen cried out in panic. He avoided articles about medical crises and stories of sudden
loss. He said he could not bear anything that resembled “that corridor, those sounds.” After work,
instead of cooking as he and his wife once had, he often ate continuously through the evening,
grazing on takeout and snacks past the point of hunger. He did not describe this as comfort so much
as a way to blunt the sharpness of his mind, to give himself something else to do while trying not
to hear the monitor and voices returning.

These habits began to affect his work. As a public defender, he spent long hours in crowded,
emotionally charged settings, and the courthouse gave him constant occasions to feel on edge. He
started lingering at his office door, checking the lock before leaving for court, then coming back
once more to make sure. In client meetings, particularly with agitated or distressed defendants,
part of his attention drifted from the legal conversation to monitoring whether someone in the room
might become dangerous. Colleagues noticed he was more withdrawn at lunch and less willing to join
the group in the break room when the television was on. At home, there was no one to interrupt the
repetitive checking or the solitary eating, and the evenings stretched out around the spaces where
his wife used to be.

What finally brought Minh to seek help was not one dramatic collapse but the accumulation of these
moments, along with a recent episode at work when a raised voice in the hallway sent a jolt through
him so suddenly that he had to grip his desk until he realized no one was coming for him. Later that
night, after checking the locks several times and finding himself in tears when a medical drama
flashed across the screen before he could turn it off, he understood that he was living in constant
expectation of danger while carrying grief he could not approach without being overwhelmed. He comes
to treatment afraid that the sounds of that night will always be waiting for him in broken pieces,
and hoping, cautiously, that he might someday remember his wife without feeling as if the loss is
happening all over again.
