what happened quickly and clinically, he would pause if the room carried any trace of aftershave and
his face would go flat, as though he had stepped away from himself.

In the weeks afterward, Omar returned to work and tried to behave as though discipline alone would
carry him through. He is used to being competent with bodies, confident in treatment rooms, and
reassuring with injured patients. But he found that his own body no longer felt like a reliable
place to live in. If he noticed pain anywhere near the part of his body that had hurt during the
assault, his attention narrowed immediately. He would check himself repeatedly for signs that
something was wrong, scanning for injury, tension, or some indication that his body remained damaged
or vulnerable. He knew, intellectually, that he was looking too often, but he did not feel able to
stop. He had come to believe that he could not rely on himself anymore, not on his instincts, not on
his body, not even on his reactions from one moment to the next.

His work environment became unexpectedly difficult. As a physical therapist, he spends his day in
close proximity to people, adjusting posture, guiding limbs, demonstrating stretches, and moving
around treatment tables in shared gym space. Unexpected motion in his peripheral vision began to
jolt him. If a patient stood up suddenly beside him, or a coworker crossed quickly behind him, Omar
reacted as if something dangerous were happening before he could think. He also became alarmed when
touched unexpectedly. A hand on his shoulder from a colleague, once an ordinary gesture, now made
him flinch sharply and turn with visible fear. He felt embarrassed every time this happened,
especially because his profession depends on calm physical presence. He began positioning himself
carefully in rooms so no one could come up behind him, and he kept a close watch on movement around
him in ways that left him exhausted by the end of the day.

The smell of certain colognes was one of the hardest things to manage because it could not be
controlled. In the clinic hallway, in an elevator, even briefly from a patient arriving for an
appointment, one specific kind of scent could pull him into a frightening nearness to the assault.
He did not always see images so much as feel that he was back in the same danger, with his chest
tightening and his attention collapsing around the smell. At those moments he would mentally push
the event away or tell himself that it had happened to someone else, as if distance were the only
way to remain standing. He avoided thinking about the assault outside those moments too. If his mind
started to approach it at night or while driving home, he cut the thought off immediately, turned on
music, or forced himself back to practical tasks.

At home, the strain fell most heavily on his marriage. Sexual intimacy with his wife became fraught,
not because he did not care for her, but because sexual situations themselves had become loaded with
danger. He would tense when intimacy began, monitor his body for pain, and then feel ashamed for
withdrawing or going emotionally blank. What frightened him most was not only the possibility of
feeling trapped again, but the sense that his emotional life had changed permanently. He told
himself that he would never feel normal emotions again, that something essential in him had gone
flat or gone missing. Alongside that was a deep shame about what had happened to his body. He could
not bear to think of it directly for long, and when he did, the shame was immediate and total.

By the time he sought help, Omar was functioning through effort rather than ease. He was still going

reminders, monitoring his body, and bracing against sudden touch or movement. What finally brought
him in was a recent moment at the clinic when a patient’s husband, wearing a cologne very close to
the one from the assault, approached him unexpectedly from the side and touched his arm to get his
attention. Omar recoiled so sharply that both of them stared at him. He finished the interaction,
but afterward sat alone in a supply room feeling shaken and deeply ashamed. He came to treatment
afraid that this was who he was now: a man cut off from his own emotions, unable to trust himself,
unable to inhabit his body without shame. At the same time, he was hoping, quietly and almost
reluctantly, that there might still be a way to feel present with his wife, steady at work, and less
afraid of ordinary closeness.
