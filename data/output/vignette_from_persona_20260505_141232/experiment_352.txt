speaks about the war zone she lived through as a civilian, her account becomes halting in a way that

clarity, but when she tries to put the experience into words she often stops, presses her lips
together, and shakes her head as if language will not come. What does come are flashes: an image
like a still photograph, a fixed frame she cannot alter or soften. She remembers bodies packed close
together in hiding places, the air stale and hot, the smell of sweat hanging heavily around
frightened people who were trying not to be noticed. Even now, when the memory catches her, it is
not only visual. She can sometimes smell it again as if the odor were present in the room with her.

In the months after the war, and then across the many years since she came to the United States and
built a life, those moments never settled into the past for her. She married, worked, and aged, but
she had come to feel that what happened remained visible on her, as though other people could tell
just by looking at her that she had lived through something terrible. In ordinary interactions she
became increasingly careful and deliberate. Before meetings, phone calls, or even casual exchanges,
she rehearses what she will say, running through possible conversations in advance so she will not
be caught off guard or reveal too much of herself. At work this has become woven into the shape of
her day. Before entering a conference room for an audit meeting, she pauses outside to compose exact
phrases in her mind. Once inside, she looks for the seat that lets her keep her back against the
wall and her eyes on the whole room. If that seat is taken, she feels exposed and unsettled, and it
is hard for her to follow what anyone is saying.

The workplace has become one of the clearest places where her fear is activated. The close air of
offices, long meetings, and the ordinary body odor of people seated near one another can bring back
the atmosphere of those wartime spaces with alarming force. She may catch a trace of sweat in an
elevator or conference room and instantly feel herself pulled toward those photographic images and
that remembered smell. At those moments she also becomes acutely alert to where everyone is
positioned. She checks behind herself in hallways, turns to see whether anyone is there, and notices
exits before she notices people. She is certain she is not safe unless she can see the room.

What troubles her and confuses her most is that danger is not the only thing that can set her on
edge. At times when she feels unexpectedly happy or relaxed—sitting quietly with her husband,
finishing a task successfully, or having a pleasant lunch break—she becomes tense almost
immediately. The ease itself feels wrong, as if letting down her guard invites something awful to
return. She does not describe this as a chain of reasoning so much as a certainty in her body:
relaxation is unsafe.

Over the years her mind has circled the event relentlessly. She goes over and over how it might have
been prevented, replaying decisions, movements, and missed chances as if there were still an answer
hidden somewhere in the details. She also returns to the question of why it happened to her at all.
These thoughts do not resolve into anything that comforts her; they simply recur, especially at
night and after difficult days at work. Alongside this, she has found herself taking too many risks,
doing things that could cause harm, in ways that feel out of keeping with the careful professional
she has otherwise always been.

Emotionally, Mereana describes a narrowing of life. She has come to believe she will never feel
normal emotions again. The moments of warmth or happiness that do emerge are quickly overtaken by
fear or estrangement, leaving her convinced that something essential has been permanently altered.
More recently, this has darkened into thoughts that her life may not be worth living anymore. She
does not present these thoughts dramatically; rather, they are spoken with a weary conviction that
has frightened her husband and, finally, herself.

She is seeking help now because the strain has become harder to hide. At 75, still trying to
function at work and at home, she feels exhausted by the constant scanning, the rehearsing, the
circling questions, and the sense of being marked in ways others can see. What she hopes for is
modest but deeply important to her: to sit in a room without needing the wall at her back, to go
through a workday without the smell of someone’s sweat transporting her elsewhere, and to feel that
a moment of peace can remain simply a moment of peace. What frightens her most is the possibility
that this is all she has left—that the war has stayed inside her so completely that she will never
again feel fully alive.
