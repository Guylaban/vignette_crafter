At 54, Mark Jensen had spent most of his adult life in the woods. As a forester, he was used to
reading terrain, judging distances, and making decisions that affected other people’s safety. Before
the accident, that competence had been central to how he understood himself: steady, practical, the
one others trusted. He was married and had built his life around work that required calm attention
and confidence in his own judgment. The event that brought him into treatment shattered that sense
of himself in a way he still described as almost unbelievable.

The accident happened during a workday in close quarters with another man. Mark had been engaged in
routine forestry work when a decision he made resulted in another person being seriously injured. He
returned again and again to the physical arrangement of the moment: how close they were standing,
where his own body had been positioned, how quickly the injury happened once things went wrong. In
recounting it, he did not speak in broad summary. He would suddenly stiffen in his chair and hold
his body as though he were back there, braced in the same posture he had been in at the instant of
the accident. What came back to him was not only an image but a bodily reliving of position and
tension, as if his muscles still knew the exact stance of that moment.

In the days after, he found himself seized by memories of the injured man’s face and body. Reminders
of the victim were especially potent. If he caught sight of someone built similarly, moving in a
similar way, or if anything specifically called that man to mind, Mark felt his stomach drop.
Physical proximity became difficult in a way that had not been true before. If someone came too near
him, especially unexpectedly, he felt a jolt of alarm and a powerful urge to shift away. Around
strangers this was worse. Even in ordinary situations, a person approaching him could feel
dangerous, and he would watch closely for any sign of possible harm. He knew on some level that he
might be standing in a safe place, but he could not relax into that knowledge. His body remained
tight and ready, scanning other people rather than settling.

Physical pain also became a trigger. Pain in part of his body could abruptly pull him back to the
accident, as though the sensation opened a door to the memory. When that happened, the recollection
came with intense emotion. Sometimes what surged up first was rage—raw, immediate, and hard for him
to understand, because it appeared alongside overwhelming grief. He spoke of being furious when the
memory hit, then almost in the same breath overcome by sorrow about what had happened to the other
man and what had been taken from both of them. These reactions did not feel chosen. They arrived
with force, leaving him feeling shaken and ashamed afterward.

Over the following months, Mark’s confidence in himself eroded. He had come to believe there was
something about him that made the event happen, as if the accident exposed a defect in his character
rather than a terrible outcome of a single moment. He no longer trusted his own judgment. Tasks that
had once felt automatic now seemed loaded with the possibility that he would make another
catastrophic mistake. At work, this was especially painful. Forestry had defined him, but in the
same forests that had once felt familiar, he now felt fundamentally altered. Being near coworkers in
the field could make him acutely aware of distance, bodies, and risk. He monitored where people
stood and how they moved, and when anyone came near him unexpectedly he would tense and pull back.
The work setting that used to confirm who he was now confronted him with the conviction that he was
no longer capable of doing the things that had always defined him.

At home, his wife saw the cost even when he tried not to speak about it. Mark was more guarded, less
able to settle in his own house, and increasingly preoccupied by the sense that he could not rely on
himself. He did not describe this as simple guilt. It felt larger and more final: proof, to him,
that he was not the man he had thought he was. What frightened him most was not only remembering the
accident, but the possibility that the accident had revealed something permanent—that he was unsafe
to trust, unsafe to stand close to, no longer fit for the work and identity he had built his life
around.

By the time he sought help, the strain of living this way had become too difficult to contain. He
was exhausted by staying on guard around other people and by the way memories overtook both his body
and emotions. He came in wanting some way to be near others without bracing, to be in his own body
without being thrown back into that moment, and to know whether there was any path to living as
himself again. What he feared most was that there was not—that the accident had permanently stripped
him of the steadiness and competence he once counted on.
