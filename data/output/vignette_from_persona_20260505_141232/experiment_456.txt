Miriam is a 48-year-old African American rabbi who came to treatment months after surviving an
explosion at a community gathering held in a synagogue social hall. She speaks about the event in a
measured, pastoral voice that breaks only when she reaches the moment of the blast. It had been an
ordinary evening program, crowded and noisy, with congregants moving between folding chairs and
buffet tables while she circulated from one conversation to another. She remembers noticing small
things that now feel charged with meaning: an unfamiliar person lingering near an exit, a metallic
crash from the kitchen that made several people glance up, her own brief thought that she should
check whether everything was all right. Then came the explosion—an abrupt, violent bang that tore
through the room and transformed the hall in an instant. People screamed, dropped to the floor, and
ran. Miriam recalls shouting for people to get down and trying to direct others toward a safer
doorway, but what remains with her most sharply is the conviction that she did not do enough. In the
aftermath, when others told her she had helped, she could not take it in. She had come to believe
that if she had been more alert, more decisive, more careful, she might have stopped what happened.

In the weeks that followed, loud bang sounds became unbearable in their precision. A car backfiring
outside her office, a heavy door slamming in the synagogue corridor, a dropped stack of prayer books
hitting the floor with a crack—each brought her back to the social hall before she had time to
think. She would freeze and then immediately begin scanning the room, her eyes moving to exits,
windows, and the faces of whoever was nearby. Even during services, standing before her
congregation, she found herself checking the back doors and watching for unusual movements. She is
clear that this does not feel excessive to her. She is certain she has to be especially careful
because you never know what can happen next.

Her work has kept her in exactly the settings that unsettle her most. As a rabbi, she cannot avoid
communal life entirely, but she has narrowed it. She no longer lingers after events when congregants
want to talk in groups, especially if conversation turns to the explosion. If someone begins to
mention that night, or related incidents in the news, she finds a reason to step away, ask an
assistant to handle a question, or redirect the room back to logistics. News coverage of bombings or
attacks has become something she avoids as well; if a television is on in a waiting area and the
story shifts to an explosion, she looks away or leaves. Her husband has noticed that evenings are
the hardest. At night she makes repeated checks of locks, windows, and the dark spaces outside the
house. Reassurance does not help. When he tells her they are safe, she hears the words but cannot
believe them. Darkness now feels like exposure rather than rest.

Another change has unfolded more quietly inside her marriage. Miriam avoids sex, not because she has
stopped caring for her husband, but because closeness and vulnerability feel intolerable. She does
not fully explain this to him; she only says she is tired, preoccupied, or not ready. Over time this
has created distance between them, adding a private loneliness to the public composure she maintains
in her role. She feels watched easily now, even in ordinary situations—crossing a parking lot after
an evening meeting, standing alone in the sanctuary, noticing someone seated in the last row after
services. She turns to check, sometimes more than once, trying to determine whether there is a
threat she has missed.

What frightens Miriam most is not only that another attack could happen, but that goodness itself no
longer feels possible. She says, with a kind of stunned finality, that nothing good can happen to
her anymore. The statement sits alongside her ongoing responsibilities, as if she is carrying out
the forms of hope for others while no longer expecting it for herself. She sought help after a
recent Shabbat event, when a dropped catering tray produced a sharp explosive clang and she abruptly
stopped mid-sentence at the lectern, unable for several moments to continue. She later learned that
several congregants had seen her distress. That incident, and the strain now visible in her
marriage, made it harder to tell herself she was simply being careful. At intake, what she most
wanted was not to feel at peace—she did not trust that as realistic—but to stop living as though
danger were always one sound away. What she feared most was that if she let her guard down even
briefly, she would once again fail to prevent the unthinkable.
