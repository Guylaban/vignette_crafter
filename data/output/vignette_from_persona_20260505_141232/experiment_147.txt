feeling that they had witnessed something shameful and irreversible about him. By the time he sought
help, what weighed on him most was not only how often he felt on edge, but the conviction that what
happened to him had marked him in a way other people could not understand.

The traumatic event occurred while he was traveling in a remote area several years earlier. He was
taken and held against his will by armed men in uniform. What remained with him was not a single
flowing narrative so much as a set of hard, fixed images: the first sight of a sleeve patch and
rifle sling as he realized he was not free to leave; the close view of a door shutting; a face
leaning near his while he was being watched; the angle of a boot beside him as he tried not to move;
the sensation of pain in his body while he was confined and powerless. When he describes the
experience now, it often comes in these still frames. At other times he can recount facts about
where he was held and for how long in a flat, detached way, as if he is describing something that
happened to someone else. He notices that both states are unsettling in different ways: either the
memory strikes him as if it is happening in the present, or he feels nothing and is left disturbed
by his own numbness.

Certain cues in daily life carry an immediate charge because they resemble those moments. Visitors
to the park sometimes strike up conversations about military service or current conflicts,
especially at the trailhead kiosk where Ravi checks permits. He finds himself going rigid while
nodding politely, his attention narrowing onto bits of uniform, insignia, or the cadence of the
conversation. People in uniform are particularly difficult. State troopers, search-and-rescue teams,
even certain utility workers can set him on edge before they have said a word. He becomes acutely
self-conscious when he feels watched, as though being seen too closely means being exposed and
trapped again. On busy days he positions himself so he has a clear line of sight and dislikes having
anyone approach from the side. Unexpected movement nearby can make him jerk, step back, or raise an
arm defensively before he knows what he is doing.

When the experience breaks through more fully, Ravi sometimes has the sense that the captivity is
happening again. In those moments he is no longer standing in a park office or on a trail but back
inside those few terrible images, and he feels pain in the same parts of his body that hurt during
the ordeal. He has learned not to trust reassurance. When his girlfriend tells him he is home and
safe, or when colleagues tell him there is no danger, he hears the words but cannot make himself
believe them. Safety feels to him like something other people talk about more easily than they know.

Over time he has developed small, rigid routines that help him feel less exposed. He tightly
controls what and when he eats, experiencing the discipline as one of the few things that is fully
his to command. At work he is overly agreeable with supervisors, law enforcement partners, and even
demanding visitors, going along quickly with what others want because compliance feels safer than
risking conflict with anyone who has authority. The cost has been quiet but significant. Coworkers
know him as dependable, but some have noticed he never pushes back, even when he is overloaded. At
home, his girlfriend has become frustrated by how little he tells her and by his tendency to shut
down after public incidents that leave him feeling observed.

He is also troubled by thoughts he does not like admitting: vivid fantasies of revenge against the
people responsible for his captivity. He does not describe any plan or intention, but the fantasies
come as a private counterweight to the humiliation he carries. He has come to believe that what

have ended up captive at all. Around other people he often feels fundamentally separate, as if there
is an invisible boundary between him and those who have not been degraded in the same way.

What finally brought Ravi in was the accumulation of these moments at work and the fear that they
were beginning to define his life. He worries most about being seen as broken, especially in

time, he says he wants “to stop living like every movement means something is about to happen,” and
to find some way of being with the memories without either falling into them or going completely
numb.
