Mele was 57, a divorced Pacific Islander American woman who had spent most of her adult life shaping
space for other people. As an architect, she was usually precise and calm, someone who could walk
into a building and immediately register how bodies would move through it, where light would fall,
where a family might gather. The trauma that brought her to treatment was not something that
happened to her body, but to someone she loved, and the places and sensory details around it had
since become fused with a sense of dread she could not shake.

When her loved one was hospitalized after a sudden medical crisis, Mele spent long days moving
between parking garage, elevator, corridor, waiting room, and bedside. What remained with her was
not a single clean memory but a sequence of spaces and sensations: the sharp antiseptic smell that
hit her each time the automatic doors opened, the maze of polished hallways, and the constant wash
of voices around her. Families spoke in hurried fragments, staff gave updates in clipped tones,
overhead announcements crackled, and at moments the chatter around her blurred into sound she could
hear but not organize. She remembered standing still while people around her talked, feeling as if
language itself had broken apart. During that period she had looked to the hospital staff, to
prayer, to the basic order of life, expecting that effort and care would mean something. When her
loved one did not recover, what settled in her afterward was a conviction that none of those things
could be counted on.

In the months that followed, she began to notice how easily ordinary settings turned charged.
Certain physical locations were the worst. If she had to pass the hospital district, or enter a
medical office building for any reason, she felt her body tense before she had fully registered
where she was. The smell of antiseptic in a clinic restroom, a dental office, even on someone’s
hands after sanitizer could stop her in place. In crowded lobbies or public transit, when
conversations overlapped and she could not make out what people were saying, she felt suddenly
unsteady and alert, as if she were back in those corridors hearing words that should have mattered
but were impossible to hold onto. As an architect, she was often expected to visit active sites,
meet clients in large institutional buildings, and consult on healthcare-related renovations. She
began quietly rearranging her work whenever she could, asking colleagues to cover meetings near
medical campuses and inventing scheduling conflicts to avoid walkthroughs that would bring her near
hospitals or ambulances. She told herself these were practical decisions, but over time her world
narrowed and her coworkers noticed she was becoming difficult to pin down.

Outside work, Mele withdrew from friends. Invitations piled up unanswered. She could not bear the
effort of sitting across from people who asked gentle questions or looked at her with concern. She
had come to believe that something about her was now visible, that others could see what had
happened just by looking at her face. If a friend’s expression lingered a second too long, she was
certain they had recognized the damage in her. It felt safer to stay away than to risk that
exposure. Her isolation deepened after the divorce-era friendships she had once relied on began to
fray from repeated cancellations and vagueness.

One of the few things she did relentlessly was exercise. She pushed herself hard, far beyond what
had once been a healthy routine, waking early to run or spending long stretches on the elliptical
until she was exhausted. She described it less as enjoyment than as something she had to do. The
strain and soreness gave structure to hours that otherwise felt empty. She no longer spoke about
buildings, travel, or family milestones with the old spark; she said that nothing in her life felt
meaningful anymore, and she meant it in a flat, settled way that frightened her more than dramatic
despair would have.

She eventually sought help after a work meeting in a medical office complex. She had agreed to go
because she did not want to seem unreliable, but the moment she stepped through the entrance and
caught the disinfectant smell, with voices ricocheting across the lobby, she froze. She left before
the elevator arrived, sat in her car shaking, and then drove home instead of answering calls. That
incident forced a conversation with her supervisor and made her recognize how much of her life had
come to be organized around staying away. In coming to treatment, Mele said she was not expecting to
get back the person she had been. What she hoped for, more quietly, was to walk through a building
without feeling ambushed by it, to sit with a friend without feeling exposed, and to find some
reason to believe that life still held meaning after what she had witnessed.
