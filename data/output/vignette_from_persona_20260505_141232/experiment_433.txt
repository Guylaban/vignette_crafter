Nadia Haddad is a 63-year-old Lebanese American woman who has worked for many years as an auditor, a
job she once valued for its order, predictability, and reliance on careful observation. She came to
treatment after months of worsening sleep, increasing constriction in her daily life, and a growing
sense that her own reactions were no longer under her control. The event she returns to began during
what was supposed to be a simple day trip with her younger niece, whom she had helped raise and
regarded almost like a daughter. They had driven to a wooded state park outside the city, a place
her niece had wanted to visit for the trails and quiet. Near the end of the afternoon, Nadia became
separated from her when her niece went ahead down a path. What happened next is what still comes
back to Nadia in a way that feels not like recollection but like being dropped back into the same
moment. She remembers the trees, the smell of dirt and wet leaves, the muffled stillness of the
area, then the abrupt wrongness of finding her niece trapped and injured in a small locked
maintenance shed near the trail, panicked and calling out. Nadia remembers the metal door that would
not give, the enclosed space, her niece’s terror, and her own frantic, useless attempts to open it
before help arrived too late.

Since then, reminders do not come to Nadia as distant images. She describes them as arriving “all at
once,” with the sounds, smell, visual detail, and bodily panic returning together so completely that
for a few moments she is certain she is there again. The terror is the same terror: her heart
pounds, her chest tightens, and she feels herself become physically braced before she has even had
time to think. What unnerves her most is how suddenly it happens. She can be moving through an
ordinary day and something in the environment will pull the whole scene back without warning. A
locked conference room at work, a small records archive with no easy exit, even stepping into an
elevator if the doors hesitate can make her feel the metal door of the shed again and hear her
niece’s voice. She has started leaving doors propped open when she can, and if a room feels close or
difficult to get out of, she finds a reason not to enter.

The same is true outside. Because the event took place in a wooded natural area, any similar setting
has become charged for her. A tree-lined walking path, the smell of damp earth after rain, the sight
of park signage at the edge of a preserve can bring the scene back so vividly that she has to turn
around. She no longer accepts invitations to picnics or outdoor gatherings in unfamiliar parks. It
is not only that such places remind her of what happened; she has become convinced that in
unfamiliar places she cannot properly judge what danger may be waiting, and that once something goes
wrong she will again be unable to stop it. She plans routes carefully, avoids detours, and keeps to
locations she knows well enough to map exits in her head.

Her work has narrowed around these fears. As an auditor, she used to travel to offices she had never
visited and inspect storage areas, file rooms, and back-office spaces. Over the past year she has
begun refusing assignments in unfamiliar buildings. When she must go, she scans continuously—doors,
locks, stairwells, who is nearby, whether a space feels too isolated. Colleagues have noticed that
she asks to keep meeting room doors ajar and becomes visibly tense if someone closes them. On one
recent visit, seeing a memorial photo of a deceased employee in a lobby display unexpectedly brought
back not only her niece’s face but the unbearable awareness of her as a victim, and Nadia had to
leave the building and sit in her car shaking. She has become embarrassed by these episodes and has
started limiting face-to-face work when possible, which has strained her standing with her team.

Over time Nadia has come to feel altered in ways that trouble her deeply. She says she has lost
faith—in people who say places are safe, in life’s fairness, and in any larger sense that the world
is ordered or benevolent. She had once believed that vigilance and responsibility could protect the
people she loved; now she is certain bad things can happen without warning and that when they do,
she will be helpless. She is also frightened by her own body. Even in settings she knows are safe,
it reacts as though danger is present, flooding her with alarm before she can reason with it. At
night this leaves her lying awake, listening for sounds, replaying possibilities, or waking suddenly
and struggling to settle again.

What finally brought Nadia to seek help was a combination of exhaustion and shame after she panicked
during a routine audit in a locked records room and then spent the night awake, convinced she was
unraveling. She told the therapist she does not want her world to keep shrinking. She is afraid that
if this continues she will stop working altogether and withdraw from everyone, but she is also
afraid that the memories will always have the power to overtake her without warning. More than
anything, she hopes for some way to be in a room with a closed door, or near trees, or in the
presence of loss, and remain in the present rather than feeling herself thrown back into the worst
moment of her life.
