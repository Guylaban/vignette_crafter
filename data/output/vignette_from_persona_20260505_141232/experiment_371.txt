Mateo is a 22-year-old HVAC technician who came to treatment months after an accident at work in
which another person was seriously injured. He described the day in a halting, overly precise way,
as if he had repeated it to himself so many times that the sequence had become worn smooth without
becoming any easier to bear. He had been on a service call in a tight mechanical space, working
alongside another man while equipment was being checked. What stays with Mateo is not a broad story
but a set of moments that still arrive with punishing force: the stale industrial smell in the air,
the sudden shift from routine concentration to something going terribly wrong, and the instant he
understood that the other person had been badly hurt. He returns again and again to what he did not
do quickly enough, convinced that there was one more step he should have taken, one more second in
which he could have stopped it. What torments him most is the belief that he stood there and failed
when it mattered.

In the days after the accident, Mateo tried to keep moving. He went back to jobs, answered texts
from coworkers, and told himself that replaying the event would help him understand it. Instead, he
found himself circling the details relentlessly: where he was standing, what he heard first, what
his hands were doing, whether his body should have reacted faster. He would lie awake running
through tiny variations of the scene as if some overlooked fact might finally explain how it
happened. He had come to believe that his own body had betrayed him in the crucial moment. If his
reflexes, judgment, or coordination had failed once, he felt he had no reason to trust them now. On
service calls he began second-guessing ordinary movements, pausing before touching equipment,
watching his hands as though they belonged to someone less reliable than he used to be.

As time passed, the memory did not settle into the past. It forced itself into his mind without
warning, especially when he was not prepared for it. He could be driving between calls, stocking the
van, or sitting in his apartment, and suddenly the accident was there again. Sometimes what struck
him first was smell. When he thought of that day, or when something in a work environment resembled
it, he could catch the same odor from the scene so vividly that for a moment he felt pulled back
into it. He found this especially disturbing because the memory often seemed oddly detached, almost
as if it belonged to someone else and not to him. He would know intellectually that it was his
experience, but it could feel unreal and estranged at the same time, leaving him shaken and confused
afterward.

At work, Mateo’s world narrowed. As an HVAC technician, he spends much of his day in back rooms,
rooftops, crawl spaces, and mechanical areas where sudden noises and stretches of stillness are
common. He became acutely uncomfortable in unusual silence. In a quiet utility room, when the normal
hum cut out and everything went still, he would feel his chest tighten and brace for something
dangerous to happen. If a smell, a certain kind of enclosed workspace, or a moment of silence caught
him off guard, his body reacted hard and fast: pounding heart, tension, a rush through his stomach,
a sense that he needed to get out immediately. Over time he restricted himself to a very small
number of places where he felt even somewhat safe—his apartment, his mother’s house, and a couple of
familiar worksites he had been to many times. Anywhere outside that small circle felt exposed.

He also began organizing his life around avoidance. He stayed away from dates connected to the
accident, growing tense as they approached and making sure he had plans to be elsewhere or alone.
More broadly, he started avoiding anything he thought might be stressful, not only at work but in
daily life. He turned down difficult assignments, left decisions to others, and stopped going to
places where he might feel pressured or trapped. The consequences accumulated quietly. Coworkers
noticed that he hesitated over jobs he once handled without much fuss, and he worried he was
becoming someone they would not recognize. He felt that way himself. The version of Mateo he carried
in his mind was capable, steady, and useful under pressure; the person he now encountered in the
mirror seemed foreign, diminished, and hard to respect.

By the time he sought help, Mateo was exhausted by the effort of managing his days. What finally
brought him in was not one dramatic collapse but the realization that his life had become organized
around staying within safe edges and mentally revisiting the worst moment of it. He is frightened
that this is who he is now: someone who cannot trust his own body, cannot leave the event in the
past, and cannot forgive himself for failing to stop another person from being hurt. At the same
time, he still hopes there might be a way to be at work without feeling ambushed by silence, smell,
and memory, and to see himself as recognizably Mateo again.
