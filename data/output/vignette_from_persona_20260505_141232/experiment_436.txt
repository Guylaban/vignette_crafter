Ethan is a 33-year-old prosecutor who came to treatment after more than a year of trying to force
himself back into a life that, from the outside, still looked intact. He is married, known at work
for being composed in court, and had prided himself before the attack on being someone who could
think clearly under pressure. The event that now organizes so much of his inner life happened on an
ordinary weekday evening after he had left the courthouse and stopped at a crowded downtown
restaurant to meet a colleague. He remembers noticing a group at the bar, the smell of spilled
liquor and beer hanging in the room, and then a violent blast that seemed to split the space apart.
Even now, when he tries to describe it, the account breaks up. He can say there was a bang, then
heat, then screaming, then people on the floor, but when he tries to put the sequence together he
loses it. He has told his wife that it is like holding shards instead of a story.

What is clear to him is the sense that there was a moment in which something terrible might still
have been stopped if he had moved faster, shouted sooner, pulled someone down, done anything more
than what he did. He remembers seeing people looking around in confusion just before the explosion
and has come to fix on that instant with merciless scrutiny. In his mind, he was the one who should
have recognized danger, should have protected people, should have acted. He does not talk about this
as a vague regret. He says it as a verdict: he failed. He will sit with his jaw clenched and say
that other people were injured and he walked out alive, and that he will never forgive himself for
not doing more to stop what happened.

In the weeks afterward, Ethan returned to work quickly, partly because the routines of prosecution
felt like the only thing in his life that still had rules. But the world no longer seemed populated
by ordinary people. In courthouse hallways, in elevators, in the coffee line, he found himself
studying faces with a hard, suspicious attention. He had come to believe that people were not what
they seemed, that danger could sit unnoticed in plain sight until the second it declared itself. He
did not say this dramatically; it showed up in small, relentless ways. He watched hands, bags,
jackets, the timing of someone stepping too close. If a stranger lingered in the back of a
courtroom, Ethan tracked him instead of listening fully to testimony.

His symptoms are tied to specific moments in his daily life. Because the attack happened after a
long workday, exhaustion has become one of the states he fears most. When he is very tired,
especially after long trial preparation or late nights reviewing files, his guard feels thinner and
the memory comes in harder. He has learned to dread that drained, heavy feeling in his body because
it seems to leave him less able to keep the scene away. The smell of alcohol is another immediate
jolt. Passing by the courthouse bar association events, walking through a restaurant with his wife,

remember the attack then; for a few moments it feels present, as if the room around him has been
overrun by that earlier room. Loud bangs are worst of all. Construction noise near the courthouse, a
slammed metal door, a car backfiring outside chambers—his body reacts before he can think, and the
explosion is there again with a vividness that feels more real than normal memory, sharper and more
convincing than what is actually in front of him.

What troubles Ethan almost as much as the intrusions is that he cannot make the event into a
coherent narrative. He can deliver an airtight chronology in court, but when the subject is the
bombing, his own mind refuses sequence and order. He moves from the smell of alcohol, to the flash,
to someone on the ground, to his own useless hesitation, and then says he cannot trust any of it
because it comes in fragments that feel too alive. At home, his wife has tried to ask what happened,
but he often stops after a sentence or two, frustrated and ashamed. Over time he has withdrawn from
dinners out and from work gatherings where drinks are served. He has stayed later in his office to
avoid joining colleagues, and his wife has noticed how often he insists on quieter routes, quieter
restaurants, quieter evenings.

nearby construction brought the whole scene back with such force that he had to brace himself
against a wall. He was shaken not only by the intensity of it, but by the fact that it happened in
the middle of the workday, in the professional setting where he has tried hardest to appear
