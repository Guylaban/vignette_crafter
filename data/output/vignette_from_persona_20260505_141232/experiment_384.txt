social ministry, visiting families in crisis, organizing food support, and sitting with people
through illness, eviction, and grief. She is married, and before the assault she thought of herself
as someone who could bring steadiness into difficult rooms. She remembers having been warm, hopeful,

still struggles to put into language. Even now, when she tries to describe what happened, her speech
catches. She says she knows exactly what happened, but the memory does not come as a story. It comes
as a handful of images, fixed and separate, each one landing in her mind with the force of a
photograph: a sudden stillness in the room, the sense of being trapped with no easy way out, the
look of the man's face at one instant, the position of her own body, the unbearable awareness of
what was being done to it.

In the immediate aftermath, Marisol did not experience the event as something finished. It returned
in these sharp visual moments, and every time it did she was flooded not only with fear but with
guilt. She found herself thinking, with brutal certainty, that she should have prevented it, should
have known, should have done something different. Alongside that guilt was a corrosive shame about
her body. What had happened to her physically felt, in her own mind, like a stain she could not wash
away. She became increasingly unable to tolerate ordinary ease. Relaxing felt dangerous, as though
letting her guard down for even a moment would invite something terrible. If the house fell
unusually quiet, she would stiffen and scan the silence, convinced that danger was gathering just
outside her awareness. Even when she could tell herself logically that she was at home with her
husband and nothing was wrong, her body would not settle.

Over the months that followed, this vigilance shaped her routines. In her work for social ministry,
delays became especially difficult. If a parish volunteer was late to meet her, if a family she was
scheduled to visit did not answer the door right away, if someone took longer than expected to
return a call, her mind leapt immediately to catastrophe. She would feel sure something bad had
happened. In the car outside an apartment building or in the hallway of a church office, she would
sit braced and tense, unable to use those waiting moments to rest. Silence was just as hard in the
course of her day. She had once found comfort in quiet chapels, in empty offices before others
arrived, in peaceful homes after children had finally fallen asleep. Now unusual silence felt
ominous. In a still room she would feel her chest tighten and her thoughts sharpen toward threat, as
if she had learned that quiet was never neutral.

At home she adopted private habits that made sense to her as ways of staying in control. She became
rigid with food, strictly limiting what and when she ate. The discipline gave her a temporary sense
of order, one small area in which nothing could happen unless she permitted it. She also found
herself turning suffering into a kind of sentence. When memories came and guilt surged, she would
tell herself she deserved to feel bad, deserved hunger, deserved discomfort. It felt wrong to be
soothed. If her husband suggested they sit together and watch a movie, or take a quiet evening on
the porch, she often pulled away from the possibility of ease. To soften even briefly felt unsafe
and undeserved.

These changes accumulated in her marriage and in the person she believed herself to be. Marisol
speaks with grief about having once been a happy woman and now feeling miserable most of the time.
She is not simply sad; she feels altered, as though the version of herself that could laugh easily
and trust the goodness of people has receded from reach. Working in ministry has made this
especially painful. Her role places her in daily contact with need, dependence, and human
vulnerability, and she has come to feel that her old faith in people, in life, and in God has been
badly damaged. She still goes through the motions of service, but inwardly she feels estranged from
the beliefs that once gave her work meaning.

By the time she sought help, the strain of living this way had become difficult to conceal. She was
exhausted by constant tension and by the effort of keeping herself tightly controlled. She was
troubled by how quickly her mind moved to danger in silence and delays, and by how little relief she
felt even in safe places. What finally brought her in was not one dramatic crisis but the
recognition that her world had narrowed around fear, guilt, and shame, and that she could no longer
find words for what had happened without feeling overwhelmed. She says she wants some part of her
life back, though she is frightened that speaking of the rape will make the images sharper and the
guilt worse. What she hopes, quietly and almost cautiously, is that she might someday be able to be
still without feeling threatened, and to inhabit her own body without shame.
