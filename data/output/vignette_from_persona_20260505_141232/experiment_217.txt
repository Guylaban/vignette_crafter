At 66, Meena Patel came to treatment after years of trying to keep herself composed by staying busy,
staying quiet, and not letting her mind go where it wanted to go. She is a married South Asian
American woman who works as a security guard, usually assigned to the lobby and parking areas of a
mid-sized office building. The event she returned to again and again in sessions was the car crash
in which her adult son was fatally injured while she was with him. She described that day in
concrete, frozen fragments rather than as a smooth story: the two of them driving, the sudden impact
of another car hitting them, the sight of the crushed vehicle, and then seeing her son’s body after
the collision. What remained most unbearable was not only that he died, but that she had been there.
Meena had come to believe that she should have protected him and that the fact she did not meant she
had failed in the most basic duty a mother has. She also blamed herself for how she acted in the
moment, turning over small decisions from that day as if some different move from her might have
changed the outcome.

In the weeks after the crash, Meena found that reminders arrived everywhere. If relatives began
talking about road accidents, she would feel her chest tighten before she even fully registered the
conversation. News footage or a glimpse of cars after a fender bender on the street could stop her
cold. Funerals became nearly impossible. At the first memorial gathering she attended after her
son’s death, the sight of mourners and flowers and the movement toward the gravesite made the crash
feel immediate again, as if time had collapsed and she were back beside the wreck. She said that
when the memory came, it did not feel like remembering in the ordinary sense. It felt present,
current, more real than everyday life. She could see the scene with a sharpness that ordinary
memories did not have, and the force of feeling was so intense that she had to shut it down or leave
the room.

Over time, Meena organized her life around not stirring that pain. She tried hard not to think about
the crash and would abruptly push the images away when they intruded. She stopped going to funerals
and avoided cemeteries altogether, even when this caused tension within her extended family, where
attendance at such events carried meaning. She also began steering away from anything that might
lead to disagreement or confrontation. At home, if her husband raised even minor concerns, she would
go silent or leave the room rather than risk an argument. At work, this showed up in specific ways.
As a security guard, she was supposed to address problems directly—visitors parking where they
should not, delivery drivers arguing about access, tenants complaining loudly at the desk—but she
increasingly dreaded interactions that might escalate. She would look for a coworker to step in or
resolve matters as quickly as possible just to end the exchange. She did not explain much to others;
from the outside, it could look like she had become overly reserved. Inside, she felt she could not
bear any more strain.

Her body also no longer felt settled. Meena described herself as constantly tense, as if bracing. On
shift she scanned entrances, the parking lot, and passing traffic with relentless vigilance. Seeing
damaged cars, hearing people discussing a collision, or unexpectedly passing a funeral procession
near the building could bring on an immediate physical reaction—her heart pounding, muscles
tightening, breath shortening, hands shaking. In those moments she felt her whole system surge
before she had words for what was happening. She was certain danger could appear without warning,
and her attention moved restlessly across her surroundings as if she had to catch it first.

What frightened Meena most was not only the memory itself but what she believed it had done to her.
She had begun to think she would never feel normal emotions again. She still cared for her husband
and family, but ordinary pleasure, ease, and warmth seemed absent or unreachable to her, and this
deepened her sense that something in her had been permanently altered. She sought help only after a
recent incident at work, when coworkers were discussing a serious highway accident in the lobby and
she became so overwhelmed by the vividness of the crash image that she had to leave her post. She
was shaken and embarrassed, and later told her husband she could not keep living as though every
reminder might pull her back into that day. By the time she presented for treatment, she was hoping
for some way to be in the world without feeling ambushed by memories, and she was afraid that if
nothing changed, the rest of her life would be defined by guilt, vigilance, and the sense that the
worst moment of her life was still happening.
