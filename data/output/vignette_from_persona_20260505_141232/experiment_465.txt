not only fear but a fierce, burning rage.

impossible to explain to the people around her. She worked in a busy nonprofit office where staff

dropped a binder in the conference room. Colleagues would tell her, gently, that she was safe there,
that the building had security and she was with trusted coworkers, but she found that impossible to
believe. The words did not reach the part of her that had become certain safety could disappear
without warning.

Night became especially difficult. Marisol could move through the day with effort, but after dark
the sense of danger sharpened. In bed beside her husband, she stayed alert to small noises in the
hallway, changes in headlights outside, the creak of the house settling. He would remind her that
the doors were locked and that he was there, but reassurance seemed almost offensive to the
certainty she felt in her body. Night, to her, was when people were exposed, when help felt farther
away, when she was most aware of how little control she really had.

somebody else would have known what to do, would have prevented it, would have fought differently.

changing clothes, she was struck not only by the trigger of nakedness but by contempt for her own
vulnerability. She rushed through showering and dressing, avoiding mirrors and lingering exposure
because being physically uncovered now felt inseparable from danger and humiliation.

because she no longer felt like herself among them. She described a lonely estrangement, as if the
