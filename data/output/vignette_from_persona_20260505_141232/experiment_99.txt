Mele had spent most of her adult life in uniform, and at 71 she was still working as a police
officer, proud of the steadiness people expected from her. She was a married Pacific Islander woman
who had long been the person others turned to in a crisis. That made the day her husband was injured
in a car crash feel all the more unbearable to her, not only because he was hurt, but because she
had been there and believed she had failed in the one thing that mattered most.

The crash happened after an evening out when she and her husband were driving home together. What
remains with Mele is not a story she tells in a smooth sequence but a set of sharp, fixed moments.
She can still see the other car at the instant of impact, the bent metal, the angle of the vehicles
after they were hit. In her mind the memory comes back mostly in pictures, bright and exact, with a
clarity that unsettles her because nothing about it has softened with time. Alongside the images,
she hears again the sounds from inside the car during the collision and immediately after it—the
violent crash itself, her husband’s voice, the confusion of those seconds. When the memory comes, it
is as if the sounds arrive in her head without warning, and she finds herself bracing in the present
as though she is back there.

What troubles Mele most is the meaning she has given to those moments. She has come to believe the
event happened because of the way she acted. She does not offer this as a passing guilty thought but
as something she feels certain of. If she had said something different, noticed something earlier,
insisted on another choice, then maybe it would not have happened. Even more painful is her
conviction that she should have protected her husband and failed. She is a police officer; in her
view, protecting people is what she does. The fact that the person hurt was the man she loves has
made the event feel, to her, like a personal moral failure. Since then she has also felt a broader
helplessness settling into how she sees the world. Bad things, she thinks, can happen without her
being able to stop them, even when she is alert and trying.

In the months afterward, reminders began appearing everywhere. On duty she might come upon cars
damaged in traffic accidents, and the sight of crumpled doors or a smashed front end would pull her
straight back into the visual scene of her husband’s crash. The reaction felt immediate and bodily,
but what she described most vividly was how the image in front of her seemed to merge with the
pictures she already carried. Routine conversation at work also became difficult. Other officers
often talk through road incidents, compare scenes, or review what happened at collisions. When
accident discussions start, Mele feels herself tighten before she can think it through. She knows
exactly where such conversations take her: back to the impact, back to what she believes she should
have prevented.

The smell of alcohol is another potent reminder. Since the crash followed an evening out, even a
brief whiff on someone’s breath or clothing can stop her cold. In public and at work, that odor can
suddenly bring the whole scene into focus with disturbing detail. She then hears again what she
heard that night and sees again what she saw. Because she knows how quickly this can happen, she has
grown increasingly watchful in ordinary settings, scanning for the smell before she fully enters a
room or keeping her distance when she notices it.

These habits have changed the shape of her life. As a police officer, she cannot fully avoid damaged
vehicles or conversations about accidents, but she has begun steering herself away when she can,
leaving others to take the lead or withdrawing from discussion sooner than colleagues expect. At
home, her husband has seen how certain roads, certain scenes by the roadside, or a passing remark
about a crash can shut her down. Their world has narrowed in small practical ways, and Mele feels
ashamed of that too. She is used to being dependable, and now she worries that even in ordinary
moments she is no longer the person she was.

By the time she sought help, what pushed her to come in was not one new crisis but the persistence
of the memories and the certainty with which she still blamed herself. She was tired of being
ambushed by images of wrecked cars, by the smell of alcohol, by conversations she could not bear to
hear through. What frightened her most was the sense that she could not protect the people she loves
and could not stop terrible things from happening. What she hoped for, though she said it
cautiously, was some way to be with the memory of what happened to her husband without feeling she
was reliving it and without carrying the same unyielding verdict against herself.
