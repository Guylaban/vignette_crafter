Rami is a 38-year-old Middle Eastern American man who has worked for years as a hairdresser in a
busy salon, where his days are structured around close attention to other people’s bodies and
appearances. He lives alone and describes himself as having been independent before his illness,
someone who relied on stamina, steady hands, and the ability to stay composed while clients talked
to him from the chair. The event he returns to in telling his story is the period when he became
seriously ill with a life-threatening medical condition and was taken into a hospital for urgent
treatment. What stands out most in the way he describes it is how quickly his body stopped feeling
like his own. He remembers the fear of being examined and handled while not knowing whether he would
survive, the medical procedures that made him feel exposed and trapped, and the sense that danger
was coming from inside him. When he talks about that time, the memory is not always visual.
Sometimes what comes back first is smell: the sharp, clinical odor from the hospital and procedure
rooms, vivid enough that for a moment he feels he is there again. At other times it is pain,
returning in the same parts of his body that hurt during the illness, as though the memory lands
physically before he has even formed a thought.

In the months afterward, Rami found that reminders arrived in ways he could not predict or control.
If he came across photographs from that period—images of himself looking gaunt, pictures sent by
relatives, even medical images saved on his phone—he felt his body react immediately. His chest
tightened, his muscles braced, and he became flooded with fear and dread before he could look away.
He began deleting and avoiding anything visual that might take him back there. Medical settings were
even harder. Routine examinations that other people might treat as inconvenient felt to him like
stepping back into the same threat. Sitting on an exam table, hearing the sounds of equipment,
waiting for someone to touch or assess him, he became intensely tense, as if his whole body were
preparing for catastrophe. He was acutely aware of strong physical reactions when reminded of the
illness, and this frightened him almost as much as the reminders themselves.

Nighttime became another place where the illness returned. Rami sometimes woke from nightmares with
the emotional residue still fully alive, even when he could not immediately picture what he had
dreamt. He would come awake already terrified, ashamed of how shaken he was, his body rigid and
alert in the dark. What unsettled him was that the feeling often arrived without a clear image, just
the full force of horror and helplessness dropping into him all at once. Lying in bed afterward, he
would scan himself for signs that something was wrong again. He had come to believe that his body
kept reacting as if he were still in danger even when nothing around him was unsafe, and this made
rest feel unreliable.

Over time, this changed how he moved through ordinary life. At the salon, clients often wanted to
show him photos on their phones—hair inspiration, family pictures, vacation snapshots—and although
these were usually harmless, he had become wary of images in general. He found himself averting his
eyes quickly or feeling a jolt of alarm when an unexpected hospital-related image appeared in a news
feed or message notification during a quiet moment between appointments. On days when he had a
medical follow-up scheduled, he was distracted at work, shoulders tight, body already preparing for
the examination hours before it happened. Because hairdressing depends on calm concentration and
easy conversation, this tension showed. He sometimes shortened client interactions, kept his talk
superficial, or scheduled around appointments so he could leave quickly. Colleagues noticed he was
more tightly wound, though he did not explain much.

The meaning he has made of these reactions is painful and frightening to him. Rami says he no longer
trusts his own body. The illness taught him, in his mind, that his body can betray him without
warning, and every surge of tension or physical sensation now feels like possible proof of that.
When his body reacts intensely to reminders, he does not experience it as “just stress”; he takes it
as evidence that something is fundamentally wrong in him. At his lowest moments, especially after
waking from nightmares or leaving a medical appointment shaken, he becomes scared that these
reactions mean he is going crazy. What frightens him most is not only the memory of nearly dying,
but the idea that he can no longer rely on his own mind or body to tell him when he is actually
safe.

He sought help after a recent medical examination left him trembling and overwhelmed for hours
afterward, followed by a night in which he woke from a nightmare with the same smell and pain of the
illness pressing in on him. The next day at the salon, while a client held up photos for a haircut
consultation, he felt himself go rigid and had to step away to compose himself. That moment
embarrassed him and made the problem harder to dismiss. Coming into treatment, he says he wants to
feel that his body belongs to him again. At the same time, he is afraid that talking about what
happened, or facing any reminder of it, will confirm his worst fear: that the danger never really
ended, and that his body will always act as if it is about to happen again.
