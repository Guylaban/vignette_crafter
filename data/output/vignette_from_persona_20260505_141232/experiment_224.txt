Elena Morales is a 66-year-old Latina paramedic who came to treatment after months of telling
herself that what was happening to her was just stress and then, more recently, fearing that it
meant she was losing her mind. She has been married for decades and has spent much of her adult life
working in emergency medicine, the kind of work that had long given her a sense that even in
terrible situations there was something practical to do. The event she returns to, though often
against her will, was a period of captivity that left her with a very different understanding of
danger. When she speaks about it, she does so in fragments at first, then stops abruptly, pressing
her hand to her chest and saying it becomes “too much” if she lets herself go further.

During the captivity, what Elena remembers most vividly is not noise or chaos but stretches of
oppressive quiet. She describes being kept in a confined space where long intervals passed in near-
complete silence, broken only by the sense that someone might enter at any moment. In those silent
stretches she had no way to tell what was coming next, no control over what another person might do,
and no way to make it stop. That particular quality of the experience has remained sharply alive for
her. Now, when a room goes unusually quiet, she does not experience it as ordinary calm. Silence
feels loaded, as if danger is gathering just out of sight. She finds herself bracing, listening,
waiting for something bad to happen.

In the months after she was released, Elena expected the memories to settle if she stayed busy.
Instead, they began intruding on her in ways that frightened her. She can be washing dishes, sitting
in her parked car before a shift, or restocking supplies in the ambulance bay, and suddenly the
memory forces itself into her mind. It does not arrive as a deliberate recollection but as something
imposed on her. She says the worst part is how little choice she has in it. Sometimes the image or
feeling comes with such force that she has to stop whatever she is doing. At work she has gone still
in the middle of preparing equipment, overcome by a wave of emotion so intense that she has to turn
away and compose herself before continuing. When she tries to tell the story in a straight line, she
often hears herself say, almost with disbelief, that it feels unreal, “like it happened to somebody
else.” The extremity of it makes it hard for her to accept that it happened to her own life, in her
own body.

Elena has also become a person who scans constantly. Walking from the ambulance entrance to her car
after a shift, she checks behind her repeatedly, certain that someone may be following. She does the
same in grocery store parking lots and even on the short walk from her front door to the mailbox.
Inside her own home, where she used to feel most at ease with her husband, she remains on guard. If
the house becomes too quiet, she notices herself listening intently, shoulders tightened, as though
safety could disappear without warning. She knows on one level that she is home, but that knowledge
does not settle her body. What frightens her most is the feeling that nowhere is reliably safe
anymore.

Over time, Elena has come to believe that the world is dangerous in a way she had not allowed
herself to think before. As a paramedic she had witnessed violence, accidents, and sudden medical
crises, but this feels different to her: less like isolated emergencies and more like proof that
terrible things can happen anywhere and that no amount of competence can prevent them. She has
become convinced, too, that she is ultimately helpless when harm comes. The captivity left her with
the sense that bad things can simply overtake her and that she cannot stop them. These beliefs sit
heavily beside another fear she is almost ashamed to say aloud: that her reactions mean she is going
crazy. She hears herself describing the intrusions, the vigilance, the way silence terrifies her,
and wonders what kind of person cannot even sit in a quiet room without expecting danger.

These changes have narrowed her life. At home, her husband has noticed that she startles into
alertness during ordinary quiet evenings and that she often interrupts herself to look toward a
hallway or turn around as if checking for someone behind her. At work, where efficiency and
steadiness matter to her, she has become more aware of moments when her attention is pulled away by
a memory she did not invite. She has sought help now because the strain of feeling on guard
everywhere, even in her own house, has become exhausting, and because being overwhelmed by the
memory at unexpected moments has begun to shake her confidence in herself. She says she wants to
understand why this is still happening and, more urgently, to know whether she is “going crazy” or
whether there is some way to live without feeling that danger is always just about to arrive.
