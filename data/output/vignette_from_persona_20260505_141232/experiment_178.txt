Minh is a 27-year-old Vietnamese American journalist who came to treatment after months of trying to
hold himself together through work and routine while feeling increasingly unsafe in ordinary parts
of his life. He is in a relationship now, but the trauma he described occurred in a previous
romantic relationship, when arguments with his former partner became physically violent behind
closed doors. What stayed with him most vividly was not a single isolated incident but the
particular atmosphere of those assaults: the sudden shift from tense conversation to being trapped
in a room with someone larger and stronger than him, the sight of a man blocking the doorway, the
blows landing in his torso and arms, and the humiliating disbelief that this was happening in what
was supposed to be an intimate, loving relationship. He remembers one night especially clearly,
after they had taken a bus home and his partner had grown increasingly angry on the ride. Once
inside the apartment, Minh was cornered and hit repeatedly while trying to judge whether speaking,
apologizing, or going silent would make things worse. The helplessness of being shut in with him and
not knowing when it would stop has remained central to how he experiences danger now.

In the weeks after the relationship ended, Minh told himself he should have seen it coming, should
have said the right thing, should have left earlier. He dwells on what he did wrong with a punishing
persistence, replaying conversations and looking for the moment when, in his mind, he failed. He has
come to believe this says something essential about him, that he is inadequate in ways other people
can somehow detect. Alongside that self-criticism is a broader conviction that people cannot really
be trusted and that you never truly know who will harm you. He does not describe this as a theory so
much as a fact that his body already knows.

That sense of danger appears in specific settings. Riding the bus is especially difficult. The press
of strangers, the confined space, and the feeling that he cannot easily leave make his chest tighten
before he has gone more than a few stops. If a man sits too close or if he finds himself alone with
a male source, editor, or rideshare driver, he becomes acutely alert, scanning posture, tone of
voice, and exits. As a journalist, he often has to travel across the city for interviews and events,
and public transportation used to be part of his everyday work. Over time he began rearranging
assignments when he could, delaying field visits, or paying for other ways to get around when money
allowed. He does not present these decisions as irrational avoidance; to Minh, they feel like basic
precautions in a world where safety claims cannot be trusted.

When memories return, they do not feel like memories in the ordinary sense. He says they arrive as
if the abuse is happening now. In those moments he is flooded so intensely that he has to shut the
recollection down or leave whatever he is doing. He also notices pain in the same parts of his body
that were hurt during the assaults, as though his ribs and arms are remembering before he can think.
On difficult days at work, when he is editing a story about violence or waiting for a source who is
late, the delay itself becomes ominous. If someone takes longer than expected to answer a message or
arrive for a meeting, he quickly assumes something bad is happening. He knows other people may see
this as an overreaction, but certainty comes fast and feels immovable.

At night, Minh rarely settles fully into sleep. He wakes and checks his surroundings to confirm that
he is safe, sometimes more than once, because reassurance from others does not reach the part of him
that remains on guard. He has also developed quieter ways of coping that have narrowed his life. He
never allows himself to feel or express anger. Anger seems dangerous to him, something that could
tip an interaction toward harm, so he turns it inward and becomes more self-blaming instead. He has
also begun restricting food when distressed. He describes not eating as a way to get through the
day, to keep himself contained and less emotionally exposed, though it has left him distracted and
depleted.

By the time he sought help, these patterns had started to affect both his work and relationship.
Missed meals, poor sleep, and the strain of constant vigilance made it harder to concentrate on
deadlines, and his partner had noticed how often he became uneasy with delays or withdrew after
being on a bus or spending time around unfamiliar men. What finally brought him in was a recent
assignment requiring multiple in-person interviews and cross-town bus rides; after one trip, he was
overwhelmed by a vivid return of the abuse and had to abandon the rest of the day’s reporting. He
came to treatment frightened that he may never feel safe enough to do his job or trust anyone fully,
but also hoping that the past might one day feel past.
