Mele was a 46-year-old married veterinarian who had spent part of her adult life doing animal health
work in a civilian area that became engulfed by armed conflict. She described the day that stayed
with her as beginning like many others, moving between frightened families, injured livestock, and
the noise of vehicles on damaged roads. She had been traveling with other people when fighting broke
out nearby. The sounds were sudden and overwhelming—sharp blasts, shouting, impacts she could not
place—and in the crush of bodies someone grabbed at her to pull her down or move her faster. What
remained with her was not a single orderly story but a set of jagged moments: the violent noise, the
pressure of hands on her before she understood who was touching her, the sensation of being trapped
in a vehicle with others while danger was somewhere outside, and the certainty, for those minutes,
that she had no control over what would happen next. When she tried to tell it now, the memory did
not come out cleanly. It arrived in broken pieces that did not fully join up, then returned again as
if a film had started in her mind without her permission.

In the months after returning to the United States, Mele found that ordinary life no longer felt
ordinary inside her body. She told herself she was home, married, safe, back to work, yet she moved
through her days as though something could break through at any moment. At night she had difficulty
falling asleep because part of her felt she had to remain alert. She would lie in bed listening for
sounds in the house, the street, the heating system, anything out of place. Even on evenings when
she could say plainly that there was no danger around her, she could not relax into that knowledge.
Her husband noticed that she startled if he touched her unexpectedly in the kitchen or came up
behind her while she was charting, and she would turn sharply before she recognized him. She hated
those moments. They left her flushed with embarrassment, already hearing the familiar thought that
she could not rely on herself, that when something happened she became disorganized and useless.

The same pattern followed her into her work at the veterinary clinic. Mele was highly experienced
and attentive with animals, but she had begun arranging her day in quiet, careful ways. If a
technician reached past her without warning while they were restraining a dog or preparing an exam
room, her whole body tightened. Loud noises in the clinic—the metallic crash of a dropped tray, a
barking eruption from the kennels, a door slamming—could pull her out of what she was doing and
leave her scanning the room before she could return to the task. She watched entrances, listened for
changes in tone, noticed who was moving where. Colleagues saw her as tense and serious; a few had
commented that she seemed unable to settle even during slower shifts. Mele knew it too. She kept
looking for danger even when she disliked herself for it.

Driving became another place where the past intruded. She could drive alone, though rigidly, but
having other people in the car was much harder. The feeling of sharing an enclosed moving space with
others brought back the war-zone ride in a way she could not fully explain. If her husband suggested
a longer drive together or friends offered to carpool, she made excuses, took separate cars, or
insisted on routes she felt she controlled. Over time these habits narrowed her life. She and her
husband went fewer places together, and at work she avoided off-site visits if they involved riding
with others. The consequences were practical and relational at once: more strain, more
misunderstandings, more private effort spent simply getting through routine situations.

When memories came, they brought not only fear but shame. She felt ashamed remembering how little
control she had had, ashamed that she still could not assemble the event into a coherent account,
ashamed of the ways she now reacted to touch and sound. She had come to believe that what happened
to her was profoundly unfair and that nothing meaningful had been done about it. Just as painful was
her conviction that bad things could happen and she would be unable to stop them. In session she did
not present this as an abstract belief; it was the conclusion she drew each time her body reacted
before her mind caught up.

Mele finally sought help after a loud crash at the clinic and an unannounced touch from behind by a
coworker happened within the same afternoon. She spun around, shaken and humiliated, then later sat
in her car unable to start driving home with her husband on the phone asking whether he should come
get her. She said she was tired of living as if she had to stay on watch everywhere. What frightened
her most was the possibility that this was who she was now—someone who could not trust herself,
could not relax, and could not keep bad things from happening. What she hoped for, though she said
it cautiously, was to feel safe in her own life again.
