David is a 53-year-old married social worker who came to treatment after many months of trying to
contain his distress on his own. He described the assault with difficulty, circling it before he
could approach it directly. What happened to him had occurred in an enclosed setting, and that fact
remained central to how he experienced life afterward. He could say that he had been sexually
assaulted, and he could describe flashes of what he remembered, but when he tried to put the
sequence into words he would stall, go silent, and look down. He knew what had happened; he was
adamant about that. But when he reached for language, it seemed to fail him. In session he would
sometimes stop mid-sentence, jaw tight, visibly straining, as if the facts existed clearly somewhere
inside him but could not be spoken without something intolerable coming with them.

When the memory broke through, it did not feel to David like something finished and past. He said
the terror came back in full force, not as a distant recollection but as the same fear he had felt
in the moment itself. Just as immediate was the shame. He did not describe the event from a detached
distance; he seemed to relive it and then recoil from himself for reliving it. What was hardest for
him was not only what had been done to him, but the humiliation attached to remembering it. He had
come to dread the memory for that reason. If an image or bodily sensation surfaced, he would try to
force it away, telling himself not to think about it, pushing it down as quickly as he could. For
short periods that seemed to work, but the effort left him tense and preoccupied.

In the months after the assault, David found that enclosed or confined spaces became especially
difficult to tolerate. Elevators, small offices with the door shut, cramped meeting rooms, and
windowless spaces all took on a particular charge. In his work as a social worker, this began to
shape his day in concrete ways. He started preferring hallway conversations to private office
meetings when he could manage it, and if he had to enter a small interview room he was acutely
uncomfortable from the moment the door closed. He was less able to focus on what a client was saying
because part of his attention remained fixed on the fact of being confined. He did not explain this
openly to colleagues. Instead he adjusted his routines quietly, which over time narrowed how and
where he could work.

Outside work, he became increasingly wary of places he did not know well. Unfamiliar neighborhoods,
buildings, or venues felt dangerous because he could not quickly assess who was around him, how he
would get out, or what might happen if something changed suddenly. He found reasons to decline
invitations that would require going somewhere new. Even ordinary errands became easier if they were
done in familiar locations he had mentally mapped. He also noticed himself repeatedly looking behind
him in public, checking whether someone might be following him. Walking to his car, crossing a
parking lot, or moving through downtown between appointments, he would glance back again and again.
The behavior made sense to him in the moment; he felt he needed to keep watch. But it also meant he
rarely felt at ease anywhere.

A more private pattern took hold as well. David spent long stretches ruminating about revenge,
imagining how he might get even with the man who assaulted him. These thoughts did not bring relief.
They arrived with force and repetition, especially when he was alone, and he found himself
rehearsing scenarios in his mind without ever acting on them. He recognized that he was stuck in
them, but he could not let them go. Alongside this, he became highly reactive when anything was
delayed. If his wife was later than expected getting home, if a colleague failed to answer a message
promptly, if someone was overdue for a scheduled meeting, his mind leapt to the assumption that
something bad was happening. He had difficulty waiting in uncertainty; delay itself had started to
feel ominous.

His marriage had been affected by the strain, though not through dramatic conflict so much as
through his constant vigilance and withdrawal into his own head. His wife could see that he was
somewhere else even when he was with her. He offered practical explanations for his tension, but the
full story remained hard to tell because the words still would not come and because he felt such
profound shame each time he approached it.

He finally sought help after recognizing that his world had become organized around what he could
avoid, monitor, or suppress. The immediate prompt was the accumulation of problems at work, where
even routine parts of his job were becoming harder to navigate without careful control of the
setting. Sitting in treatment, David was frightened of what would happen if he stopped holding the
memories back, and frightened too of remaining as he was: checking, scanning, avoiding, and bracing
for danger. What he hoped for, cautiously, was not to erase what happened, but to be able to
remember it without being flooded by terror and shame, and to move through his life without feeling
that threat was always just behind him.
