Elisabeth is a 73-year-old white American woman who has been married for decades and still works as
a QA engineer, a role she values for its order, documentation, and clear procedures. In the first
sessions, she described her childhood trauma haltingly, often stopping mid-sentence and pressing her
lips together as if language itself gave out. She said she knew exactly what had happened, “every
piece of it,” but when she tried to speak, the words would not come. What she could convey was that
the abuse was physical, repeated, and carried out by a caregiver who was supposed to protect her.
What remains most vivid to her is not a flowing story but a series of still images, “like
photographs.” She can see a doorway, a hand moving quickly, the angle of a room, the expression on
the caregiver’s face just before she understood she was not safe. At other times she can recount
those same moments with a flat, distant tone that unsettles even her; she says it can feel as if she
is describing something that happened to another child.

As a girl, Elisabeth had come to believe that safety could vanish without warning and that trusted
people could turn dangerous in an instant. She was certain, then and now, that she had not been able
to stop what was happening. Alongside that helplessness sits a hard, enduring self-accusation: she
feels she should have done more, though when she tries to explain what more a child could have done,
she falls silent. What wounds her most is not only the violence but the betrayal. The person who
harmed her was someone she had depended on, and that fact remains central to how she understands the
world.

Over the years the abuse did not fade into an ordinary past. Instead, Elisabeth organized her life
around staying alert. She notices people’s movements before she consciously registers why. If
someone near her shifts abruptly or raises an arm quickly, her body reacts first; she startles,
stiffens, and feels instantly on guard before she has had time to think. She described this as
exhausting but nonnegotiable, as if vigilance were the only sensible way to move through the world.
Uncertainty is especially difficult for her. Changes of plan, not knowing who will be present, or
being unable to predict how an interaction will go can leave her feeling exposed. She does not
describe this as mere dislike of ambiguity; to her it carries the charge of danger, because the
worst moments of her childhood also arrived when she did not know what would happen next.

Being alone with men has become one of the clearest examples of this in her present life. She is
married, and she speaks warmly of her husband, but outside that relationship she becomes sharply
uneasy if she finds herself alone with a man. At work, where she is respected for her precision,
this has shaped small but persistent habits. If a male colleague asks to review a defect report in a
closed office, she prefers to keep the door open, suggest a conference room with glass walls, or
delay the conversation until others are nearby. If an unexpected one-on-one meeting appears on her
calendar, she feels her body tense before she has even read the details. She knows these are
ordinary workplace situations, yet what frightens her is the sense of being cornered with
uncertainty and male presence at the same time. Over months and years this has narrowed her ease
with coworkers and made collaboration more effortful than she believes others realize.

Funerals, cemeteries, and grief-related events are also potent for her. The stillness, the
formality, the atmosphere of helplessness around loss all affect her strongly. She has avoided
funerals when she could, and when attendance felt unavoidable, she often remained preoccupied and
distant, scanning who was nearby and wanting to leave quickly. This has had consequences in her
family life. She still remembers occasions when relatives interpreted her absence or abrupt exit as
coldness, when in fact she was struggling to stay present at all.

Now, in her seventies, Elisabeth is seeking help because the effort of remaining on guard has become
harder to carry, not easier. A recent convergence of workplace uncertainty and a grief-related
family event left her shaken by how quickly her body moved into alarm and by how little access she
had to words afterward. She finds this especially painful because she has spent a lifetime being
competent and controlled. What she fears most is that she will always be vulnerable to bad things
happening without warning and that she will never stop blaming herself for not having prevented the
abuse. What she hopes for, cautiously, is not to erase the past, but to be able to speak about it
without going numb or wordless, and to move through ordinary situations—at work, with family, in the
world—without feeling that danger is already in the room.
