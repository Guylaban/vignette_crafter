Anika Patel was 31 when she sought treatment, a married middle-school teacher who had once thought
of herself as steady under pressure, quick with students, and at ease in the warm attention that
often comes with being good at her job. The event she kept returning to had happened months earlier
in an ordinary setting that still felt unbearable to remember clearly. She had been behind the wheel
when another person was seriously injured. In the moments she recalled, the memory did not unfold
like a story with a beginning and end. Instead it came to her as separate images, fixed and
terrible, as if someone had taken photographs inside her mind: a sudden instant of movement, the
other person’s body in the wrong place, the jolt of impact, the stunned stillness immediately after.
When she spoke about it in session, her face changed as though she were no longer remembering but
standing in it again. She would go quiet, grip the arms of the chair, and say that it was not like
thinking about something that had happened in the past. It was as if it were happening right then.

What stayed with her just as vividly was smell. If she tried to describe the accident, or if
something in the day pulled the memory close, she would catch the scent that had been present at the
scene and feel her stomach drop before she had even fully registered why. The smell made the images
seem even nearer, convincing in a way that ordinary memory was not. She had come to dread how little
warning there was when this happened.

In the weeks after the accident, Anika found that the things she had once enjoyed or welcomed became
strangely unsafe. Praise was one of the sharpest examples. At school, colleagues had always spoken
well of her. Parents thanked her, administrators noticed her work, and students were drawn to her
calm competence. Now, if a principal complimented her lesson planning or a parent sent an
appreciative email, she felt a rush of discomfort that bordered on panic. Positive attention seemed
intolerable, almost offensive. She could not reconcile being told she was kind, capable, or
dedicated with the fact that another person had been seriously hurt because of what she had done.
She would deflect compliments quickly, change the subject, or leave the room under some practical
pretext. Even at home, when her husband tried to reassure her or tell her she was a good person, she
became tense and remote.

Positive feelings themselves had also become confusing. If she caught herself laughing with a
colleague in the teacher’s lounge or feeling proud after a successful class, the feeling would
curdle almost immediately. She seemed startled by her own enjoyment, as though happiness itself were
evidence of something wrong in her. It did not feel simple anymore. It felt dangerous, undeserved,
and incompatible with who she now believed herself to be.

Conversations about accidents had become nearly impossible. In the school setting, this happened
more often than she would have expected: students discussing a local traffic collision, coworkers
mentioning bad weather and dangerous roads, a passing comment in the hallway about someone’s fender-
bender. Each time, Anika felt pulled out of the present. She would freeze mid-task, lose track of
what she had been saying, and feel those visual fragments return with such force that the room
around her seemed briefly unreal. She became adept at slipping away from these conversations,
busying herself with copies, closing her classroom door, or redirecting discussion in ways that
others did not always understand. Over time, colleagues noticed that she no longer lingered in the
staff room and seemed absent from the easy social life of the school.

What frightened Anika most was not only the memory but what she had come to believe about herself
since the accident. She was certain the injury had happened because of the way she acted. She did
not speak in generalities about bad luck or mistakes that could happen to anyone; she experienced it
as personal indictment. The conclusion felt settled in her mind: she had done this. Alongside that
conviction was a painful sense that the person she had been before—the competent teacher, the
reliable adult, the woman who could manage a classroom and a life—was no longer available to her.
Standing in front of students still performing the outward tasks of teaching, she felt she was
imitating someone she used to be. She had also begun to feel separate from other people in a more
permanent way. In faculty meetings, at family gatherings, even beside her husband, she experienced
herself as set apart, as though everyone else belonged to the ordinary world and she no longer did.

By the time she came for help, the strain had narrowed her life. School remained the clearest
example: a place that once confirmed who she was had become full of moments to dodge, from praise to
casual discussion, and each dodge left her more isolated. She presented for treatment after
realizing she was organizing entire days around getting through without being pulled back into those
few unbearable mental pictures. What she feared most was that this was now her permanent
self—incapable, guilty, and outside other people’s lives. What she hoped, though she said it
cautiously, was that the memory might one day feel like something that had actually ended.
