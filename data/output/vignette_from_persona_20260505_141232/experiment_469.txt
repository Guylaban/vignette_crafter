María Elena is a 44-year-old Latina woman, divorced, who has spent the last several years in
religious life as a nun. Before entering the convent, she had already lived through the painful
upheaval of a marriage ending, and she describes having sought a life of service and structure
afterward. The event that now dominates her inner life occurred during a public religious gathering
she attended with other sisters, an event held in a crowded civic plaza where worshippers, families,

bodies, and then as abrupt terror when an explosion tore through the crowd. In the moments that
return to her now, she does not recall it as a narrative but as a total sensory assault: the blast,
the force through her body, the metallic smell, people screaming, and the sight of blood on the
ground and on clothing. She sustained a painful injury during the attack, and the pain in that same
part of her body still has a special power over her. When it flares unexpectedly, she is no longer
simply noticing discomfort; she is thrust back into that plaza with a certainty that the danger is
present again.

In the weeks afterward, María Elena found that the memory did not come when she chose it. Instead it
forced itself into her mind in fragments so vivid that she felt she was reliving the attack across
all her senses at once. Blood was especially potent. A small cut in the convent kitchen, blood drawn
at a medical appointment, even seeing red on fabric could bring a rush of horror that felt immediate
rather than remembered. Night brought little relief. She began waking from nightmares already
gripped by the atmosphere of the attack, as if she had surfaced from sleep but not fully left the
scene. In those moments before dawn, lying in her small room at the convent, she would sit upright
and orient herself to the crucifix, the dresser, the window, trying to convince herself she was here
and not there. Yet if the old injury was aching at the same time, she could feel overtaken all over
again.

What has unsettled her almost as much is that when she tries to remember the event deliberately, to
tell it in order or pray through it, the memory becomes strangely inaccessible. She can be flooded
by it without warning, but if she sits with a therapist or spiritual director and tries to recount
exactly what happened from beginning to end, parts disappear. This has left her going over the
details repeatedly in her own mind, searching for coherence, revisiting where she had been standing,
where others were standing, what she saw before and after the blast, as if enough review might
finally make it settle into something she can understand. Instead, the process leaves her exhausted,
frightened, and no more certain.

Over the months, María Elena’s world narrowed. She came to feel safe only in a very limited set of

those spaces she rarely relaxed. If she had to accompany other sisters to Mass at a larger parish,
to a hospital ministry, or even to a grocery store, she found herself immediately scanning for exits
before she could feel remotely settled. She noticed doors, side aisles, stairwells, the width of

she felt trapped, as if the same kind of sudden catastrophe could happen again and she would not be
able to get out. She increasingly declined outings that involved festivals, public prayer services,

She has also become more emotionally removed from other people. Life in a convent ordinarily
involves a closeness of daily routine, shared prayer, and mutual reliance, but María Elena has
pulled back from being known too well. She keeps conversations practical, avoids lingering after
meals, and turns aside gentle attempts by other sisters to ask how she is really doing. What
frightened her most about closeness was not simply being upset in front of others; it was the sense

remains and that she ought, by now, to be stronger.
