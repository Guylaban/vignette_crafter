When Dara was a young man, before he immigrated to the United States and built a long life as a
husband and welder, he was a civilian in a war zone in Southeast Asia. He still struggles to give a
full account of what happened. Even in the first sessions, he described the event in fragments, as
if he were holding separate pieces that would not fit together. He remembered being out in an
exposed area during fighting, hearing vehicles approach and then the sharp, escalating noise around
him, and he remembered running and then not being able to get enough air. After that, his
recollection broke apart. He said there were parts missing, whole sections he could not reach. At
times the memory felt less like his own past than like something that had happened to another person
and somehow been placed in his mind. What unsettled him almost as much as the content was the
strangeness of it: the story was so extreme that even now, at 74, he sometimes found himself
thinking it could not possibly have happened to him, though he knew that it had.

What remained vivid were the moments that matched his current fears. The sound of cars was
especially potent. In the war zone, the approach of vehicles had signaled danger, and in the years
since, certain engine sounds still seemed to seize his body before he had time to think. He would
feel his chest tighten and become acutely aware of his breathing, as if the old panic were returning
through his lungs. Difficulty breathing was not only frightening in itself; it also seemed to place
him back inside those broken, unreal fragments of memory where he had been exposed and unable to
control what was happening around him.

Over the years Dara made a life that, from the outside, looked steady. He married, worked hard, and
spent decades welding, valuing the contained order of the shop floor. Yet even there his symptoms
entered daily life. When car sounds carried in from the lot or the street, he would look up
immediately, scanning, his attention pulled away from the metal in front of him. He described being
superalert all the time, as though some part of him remained on watch. He had increasing difficulty
concentrating, especially when there was noise outside the building. He preferred areas of the
workshop that felt enclosed and disliked tasks that took him into open yards or exposed loading
areas where he felt too visible and vulnerable. He did not explain this much to coworkers. Instead
he adjusted quietly, lingered indoors, and made practical excuses.

At home, nights were often the worst. Dara had difficulty falling asleep because he felt he needed
to stay alert. He would lie awake listening, watching the darkened room, noticing every sound from
outside. His wife knew he slept lightly, but he rarely told her what was happening in his mind. He
avoided talking with others about the war or its aftermath, and when something related came up, he
redirected himself quickly, turning to another subject or forcing his thoughts elsewhere. If news
footage, a documentary, or any related media appeared on television, he either left the room or
fixed his attention on something else, unwilling to let the images settle. He had lived this way for
so long that the avoidance had the feel of habit, but it had narrowed his life. Conversations with
his wife stopped short at the very places where she most wanted to understand him, and he remained
alone with meanings he had never been able to put down in words.

Those meanings were painful and fixed. Dara had come to believe that what happened to him was deeply
unfair and that nothing had ever been done about it. He spoke with bitterness about the people
responsible facing no consequences. Alongside that anger was self-blame that he could not shake. In
the scattered pieces he retained, he focused on the way he had acted in those moments and was
certain that the event had happened because of what he did or failed to do. Even when he
acknowledged how chaotic and dangerous the circumstances had been, the accusation returned to him in
private: he should have moved differently, chosen differently, known something he could not have
known.

He sought help only recently, after noticing how much smaller his world had become. Car sounds
outside the welding shop were affecting him more sharply, related media upset him for days, and his
concentration had become unreliable enough that he worried at work. More than that, his wife had
begun asking him why, after all these years, he still seemed unreachable when certain sounds came or
when he became short of breath and distant. What finally brought him in was not a wish to tell the
whole story at once—he was still unsure he even had the whole story—but a fear that he would remain
trapped between missing memories, constant watchfulness, and a sense of injustice that had never
loosened. What he hoped for, carefully stated, was to sleep without guarding the night and to hear a
car pass without feeling his body return to a place he still could not fully remember.
