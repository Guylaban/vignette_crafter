Marisol Herrera is a 57-year-old physical therapist who came to treatment more than a year after
surviving a catastrophic flood. Divorced and living alone, she had built a steady life around work,
routine, and a strong sense of competence. Before the disaster, she thought of herself as practical
and calm under pressure, the kind of clinician who could steady frightened patients with her voice
and hands. The event that now returns to her began during a period of heavy rain when water rose
through her neighborhood with a speed she still struggles to describe. She remembers standing inside
her home and seeing water where it should never have been, pushing under the door and then climbing
higher, carrying debris and a force that made ordinary rooms feel suddenly unreal. At some point
names shouted outside—the street name, neighbors’ names, the name of the river repeated by emergency
responders and people trying to find one another—became fused with panic. What remains most potent
for her now is anything that evokes that scene: the sight of floodwater on the news, a large body of
water she happens to pass, or even hearing certain names linked in her mind to that day.

In the weeks after, Marisol found that the memory would not come back in an orderly way. At times it
felt as if it had happened to somebody else, as though she were describing a film she had seen
rather than her own life. At other times she was overtaken by the full force of terror and sorrow
without any clear picture at all. She would be sitting quietly and suddenly feel the same surge in
her body—her stomach turning, her chest tightening, grief crashing over her—without an image she
could point to. When she does deliberately try to recall the flood, what comes most strongly is
overwhelming grief. She does not elaborate it into dramatic language; instead she goes quiet, looks
down, and says that remembering leaves her feeling hollowed out.

Marisol has become highly attentive to signs that something bad could happen again. Unexpected or
loud sounds make her jolt immediately alert. A crash in the apartment building, a slammed car door
outside, even a sudden clatter at the clinic can leave her heart racing before she has had time to
think. Nights have become especially difficult. She lies awake listening, feeling that she cannot
fully relax, or wakes repeatedly and scans the room before she can settle again. Over time, safety
has narrowed for her. She says she only feels safe in a very limited number of places, essentially
her apartment and a few familiar rooms at work. Beyond those spaces, she feels exposed and tense.

Her work as a physical therapist brings these difficulties into daily view. She still goes to the
clinic, but not with the same ease. If a patient mentions living near the river, or if she hears the
name of an area associated in her mind with the flood, she feels nausea rise abruptly and has to
focus on staying composed. Visual reminders are difficult too; a rehabilitation room television
showing storm footage or even a conversation about waterfront property can leave her feeling
unsteady. She has increasingly limited her life outside these controlled settings. She goes out less
than she used to, turning down invitations and keeping errands brief and necessary. What seems
sensible to her is staying where she can manage herself, where exits are known and the surroundings
feel contained.

When distress peaks, Marisol tries to make herself go blank. She described deliberately attempting
to leave her body, to become numb and absent until the feeling passes. In the moment this seems like
the only way to get through the wave of nausea, dread, or grief, especially when there is no clear
image attached to it. She also spends long stretches dwelling on who she was before the flood. She
measures herself against that earlier version—more spontaneous, more willing to go anywhere, less
guarded—and the comparison has become painful. The losses feel personal and daily rather than
abstract: she no longer moves through the world with the same trust, and her life has contracted
around a few safe places.

By the time she sought help, Marisol was exhausted by poor sleep and by the effort of holding
herself together at work. What finally pushed her to come in was the realization that her world had
become so small that even an ordinary day could feel like an ordeal if it included the wrong sound,
the wrong name, or the sight of water. She said she is frightened that this is simply who she is
now, someone who can never fully come back into her own life. At the same time, she still hopes she
might regain some part of the woman she remembers being before the flood, and be able to leave home,
work, and rest without bracing for the next wave.
