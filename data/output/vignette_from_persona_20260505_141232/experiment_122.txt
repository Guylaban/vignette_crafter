Rana is a 26-year-old non-binary veterinarian who lives with their partner and had, until last year,
thought of themself as an upbeat, capable person who could manage almost anything. The robbery
happened after an evening shift at the animal hospital where they work. They had stayed late
finishing notes and checking on a dog recovering from surgery, and by the time they left the clinic
the parking lot and side street were mostly empty. As Rana walked toward their car, someone came up
close behind them and demanded their phone and wallet. The encounter was fast and disorienting. What
stayed with Rana was not a clean sequence of events but flashes: the sudden presence at their back,
the pressure to comply immediately, the smell on the person’s clothes and breath, and the sense that
they could not risk doing anything but giving the person exactly what was asked. They remember
becoming almost excessively agreeable, speaking softly, apologizing, handing things over quickly,
trying to seem easy to control because in that moment it felt like the safest possible way to get
through it.

In the days afterward, Rana tried to tell themself it had not really happened the way it felt, or
that it was “just a robbery” and not that bad. When friends or coworkers asked, they would minimize
it, as if shrinking the event could make it less real. But when they tried to describe the robbery
in any detail, they often lost the thread. They would start with leaving work and then get stuck,
unable to continue in an orderly way, embarrassed by how their mind seemed to break apart around the
memory. At unexpected moments, the whole thing would rush back without warning. Walking through the
clinic parking lot at dusk, hearing footsteps come up behind them, or catching a smell that
resembled what they remembered from that night could abruptly pull them back into the scene with a
vividness that felt more like reliving than remembering. Sometimes, even when they were only
thinking about it, they could smell it again and would freeze for a moment, thrown by how immediate
it felt.

Over the following months, Rana’s world became organized around watching for danger. Outside, they
constantly scanned their surroundings, reading every movement in peripheral vision, checking
entrances, parked cars, and the distance between them and strangers. They frequently looked behind
them to see whether anyone was following. The habit became so ingrained that even walking from the
veterinary clinic to their car in daylight required repeated glances over the shoulder. They began
timing their departures to avoid being alone and put off errands that meant going out unless their
partner could come. Their avoidance was not abstract; it changed ordinary life. If a prescription
needed picking up or groceries were needed after work, Rana often postponed it. At the clinic they
lingered indoors until someone else was leaving, even when they were tired and wanted to go home.

Home no longer felt reliably safe either. Rana described feeling on guard even inside their
apartment, listening for sounds in the hallway and remaining aware of the door and windows. What
frightened them most was the conviction that danger could emerge without warning and that only
constant carefulness stood between them and another assault. They had come to believe they had to be
especially vigilant because you never know what can happen next. Alongside that was a harsher
conclusion: other people were not dependable. The robber had been a stranger who came from behind,
but the lesson Rana took from the experience spread widely. They found themself assuming they could
not rely on others to protect them, notice their fear, or keep them safe. Even with their partner,
whom they loved, they often felt they had to manage things alone.

The changes were visible to people around them. Colleagues noticed Rana avoiding late shifts when
possible and becoming tense in the parking lot. Their partner saw how outings had narrowed and how
often Rana’s eyes moved to doors, windows, and the space behind them. Rana also grieved the person
they felt they had been before. They said they used to be happy and now felt miserable much of the
time, not in a dramatic way but as a steady loss of ease.

They sought help only after recognizing how much effort it was taking to get through each day. A
recent evening at work brought it into focus: leaving the clinic, they caught a familiar smell near
the lot, instantly felt the robbery return in full, and stood unable to move until a coworker asked
if they were alright. What brought Rana to treatment was not only fear of another attack but fear
that their life was shrinking around that fear. They want to feel like themself again, and at the
same time they are afraid that if they stop being so careful, something terrible will happen and no
one will be there to help.
