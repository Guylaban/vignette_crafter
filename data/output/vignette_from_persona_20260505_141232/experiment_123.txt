Arjun Patel is a 32-year-old South Asian American man who came to treatment after months of feeling
as if the city had changed into something hostile and unreadable. He is married and works as a
housekeeper in a downtown hotel, where much of his day is spent moving quietly through hallways,
entering and leaving guest rooms, and pushing a cart stacked with linens and cleaning supplies.
Before the robbery, he had thought of himself as careful and hardworking, someone who kept his head
down and got through long shifts without complaint. What happened one evening on his way home has
come to divide his life into a clear before and after.

The robbery took place after a late shift, when Arjun was walking from the bus stop with his work
shoes still damp from cleaning solution and a small envelope of cash in his pocket. A man approached
him quickly, demanded his wallet, and struck him when he hesitated. Arjun remembers the encounter
with an almost unbearable sharpness: the sound of shoes on pavement, the angle of the man’s
shoulders, the closeness of his breath, the shock of being hit, and the exact instant he understood
that the person in front of him might seriously injure him. The memory comes back to him with such
clarity that he does not experience it as distant history so much as a scene that can reappear in
full detail without warning. When it does, he feels pain again in the same places where he was hurt
during the robbery, as though his body has not accepted that the assault is over. He described it as
his jaw and side hurting all over again when the memory returns, even when he is standing safely in
the present.

At the same time, Arjun struggles to put the event into words. In session he circled around it,
stopping mid-sentence and pressing his lips together, saying he knew exactly what happened but could
not explain it properly. The facts are available to him in precise fragments, but when he tries to
tell the story aloud, language seems to fail him. His wife has noticed this at home. When she asks
what is bothering him after he goes quiet, he often says only, “It’s that night,” and leaves it
there, looking frustrated with himself for not being able to say more.

In the weeks after the robbery, his view of other people shifted. He had come to believe that people
are not what they seem, that friendliness or ordinary politeness can hide something predatory. He
was certain that people cannot be trusted, and he spoke about the world now as fundamentally
dangerous. These beliefs show up most strongly in the places where he has the least choice about
being around strangers. At the hotel, when guests approach him in the corridor and ask for towels or
directions, he finds himself watching their hands, their pockets, the way they stand too close. A
person stepping suddenly out of an elevator can make his whole body tense. He no longer experiences
unfamiliar people as neutral; he reads them as potential threats whose real intentions are hidden.

This has altered the way he moves through his days. He lingers in staff-only areas longer than
necessary and is reluctant to walk alone to the bus stop after evening shifts. He has started asking
coworkers to accompany him outside when they can, and on days when no one is available he feels
trapped between staying late and facing the walk home. His wife has experienced the change too. He
asks more questions about where she has been, who was around, whether the door is locked, whether
she noticed anyone following her. To him these are reasonable precautions in a world that has
revealed its true nature; to her, they have begun to feel like the robbery is still living in their
apartment with them.

By the time he sought help, Arjun was exhausted by the persistence and force of the memories and by
the way his body seemed to relive the assault whenever they returned. What finally pushed him to
come in was a recent moment at work when a hotel guest approached him unexpectedly from behind in a
hallway, and he felt the pain flare in his injured side so strongly that he had to lean against his
cart and stop working. He was embarrassed that he could not explain to his supervisor what had
happened. Sitting in the office now, he is most afraid that he will never again be able to look at
other people without suspicion, that he will keep living as if danger is always one step away. At
the same time, he is hoping for some way to speak about that night without freezing, and to move
through his work and his marriage without feeling that the world is waiting to harm him again.
