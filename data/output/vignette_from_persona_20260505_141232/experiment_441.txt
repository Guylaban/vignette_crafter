Arjun Patel was 41 when he came to treatment, a married architect who had spent years designing
orderly spaces for other people while his own life had narrowed around a set of moments he could not
put safely in the past. The traumatic event he returned to most often had happened late at night in
the apartment he had shared with his romantic partner before his marriage. He had already known her
drinking could change the atmosphere of a room quickly, but that night the smell of alcohol hit him
the moment she came close to him, sharp and sour on her breath and clothes. She was speaking loudly,
then rapidly, her words tumbling over each other until he could no longer follow what she was
saying. He remembered the confusion of hearing language as noise rather than meaning, a stream of
incomprehensible chatter that made it impossible to predict what would happen next. When he tried to
calm her and then tried to step away, she became physically violent. What remained with him were not
a smooth sequence of events but a series of still images: her face suddenly close to his, the blur
of a raised arm, the edge of a table, the floor from an angle he had never expected to see, the
smell of alcohol filling the room. When he remembered it now, it was as if he were looking at
photographs developed from the worst seconds of his life, each image carrying the same terror he had
felt then.

In the weeks afterward, Arjun told himself he should have handled it differently. He had come to
believe that in the moment that mattered most, he had not done the right thing. He did not describe
this simply as having made a mistake; he spoke as though something essential in him had been
exposed. He was certain he had failed in an important role, though he struggled to say exactly how
without sounding ashamed. He was a man who had always thought of himself as thoughtful, measured,
and capable of protecting the people around him. Now he doubted his own judgment. The fact that the
person who hurt him had been someone he trusted sat heavily with him. When he spoke about her, the
injury he returned to was not only physical abuse but betrayal. He could not reconcile intimacy and
danger, care and assault, and he no longer trusted himself to read those distinctions correctly.

Over time, his body began reacting in ways that made him feel he was living ahead of his own mind.
He would startle violently at sudden sounds, and there were moments when his body seemed to register
danger before he had even identified anything wrong. If he caught the smell of alcohol on a stranger
in an elevator or walking past a restaurant patio, his chest tightened and his muscles braced before
he had time to think, I’m safe, this is not then. Something similar happened when he was physically
unwell. A fever, nausea, or even the weak, off-balance feeling of a minor illness made him uneasy in
a way he found hard to explain. Feeling physically compromised reminded him too vividly of the
helplessness of that night. At work, this could be particularly unsettling. His architecture firm
often required site visits to noisy construction environments where overlapping voices, radios, and
shouted instructions echoed off concrete. If several people were talking at once and he could not
make out what was being said, he felt the same jolt of confusion he had known in the apartment, and
his body reacted as though he needed to defend himself before his mind caught up to where he was.

Sleep became another battleground. Arjun dreaded the vulnerable period of lying in the dark waiting
to drift off. He said he felt he had to stay alert, as if letting go of vigilance would be
irresponsible. Even beside his wife, who knew he had been through something painful though not
always the full detail, he found it difficult to settle. He listened for sounds in the apartment,
monitored movement in the hallway, and remained tense long after he wanted to be asleep. During the
day he functioned, but with increasing strain. He avoided office happy hours and client dinners
where alcohol would be prominent. He delegated some site visits to younger colleagues when he knew
the environment would be chaotic and loud, and over months this began to affect how available he
seemed at work. At home, his wife noticed he was easily startled and often distant after a poor
night’s sleep, but what he found hardest to confess was not fear alone. It was the conviction that
he could not trust himself, that he had already failed once in a role that mattered deeply, and that
closeness could conceal danger without warning.

What finally brought Arjun to seek help was a recent combination of events that made the problem
impossible to contain. After coming down with a bad viral illness, he felt weak and feverish for
several days, and during that time the old terror came back with unusual force. Soon after, at a
project meeting held in a crowded renovated warehouse, the overlapping voices around him became
unintelligible for a moment and he froze, heart racing, before abruptly leaving the room. He told
his wife that night that he could not keep living as though his body were always preparing for
something terrible. In seeking treatment, what frightened him most was the possibility that this
meant something permanent about him—that he truly could not rely on himself when it counted. What he
hoped for, more quietly, was to be able to sleep without guarding, to walk into his own workspaces
without bracing, and to remember what happened without feeling as though he were back inside those
frozen pictures again.
