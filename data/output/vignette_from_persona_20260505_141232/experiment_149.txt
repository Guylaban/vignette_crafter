At 54, Javier Morales came to treatment carrying himself with the rigid alertness that had become
second nature to him. He is a married Latino man who has worked for many years as a parole officer,
and he described his life now as one organized around watching, anticipating, and bracing. The event
he returned to again and again had happened during an encounter with police violence, an experience
he still struggled to tell in a straight line. When he tried, the account came in fragments. He
could place himself in the scene, could remember the charged stillness before things turned, and
could remember the force used against him, but there were gaps that seemed to swallow the middle of
the story. He would get as far as one piece of it and then stop, saying it was as if his mind shoved
the memory away before he could see the rest. Even when he did speak about it, he often sounded
startled by his own words, as if the event were so extreme that it did not feel like something that
could really have happened to him.

What remained most sharply with Javier was not a coherent narrative but a set of impressions and
meanings that had hardened over time. He had trusted that the people involved, and the institutions
around them, would act within bounds and would protect rather than harm. Instead, he experienced
what he understood as a profound betrayal. He had come to believe that he was failed not only by the
officers who used violence against him, but by the larger system that should have recognized what
had happened and done something about it. In his mind, the unfairness of it sat unresolved. Nothing
had been set right. No one had made it make sense. He spoke about this not in abstract political
terms but personally, with the injured certainty of someone who had relied on rules and roles and
found that they did not hold.

In the months afterward, his days began to narrow around signs of danger. Silence became especially
difficult. He described unusual quiet as intolerable, not peaceful. If a room went still, if a
hallway at work suddenly emptied out, if home became too quiet at night, Javier felt a surge of
certainty that something bad was about to happen. The moment before violence in his own experience
had carried that same kind of unnatural hush, and now silence seemed loaded, as if it were an alarm
only he could hear. He startled easily at abrupt sounds or movements, his body reacting before he
had time to think. More and more, he found himself constantly checking entrances, watching people’s
hands, tracking shifts in tone and posture. He did this at the parole office, in waiting areas, in
parking lots, in grocery stores, and even at home when things were calm.

His work gave these reactions daily opportunities to take hold. As a parole officer, he spends much
of his time with people under stress, people who are angry, defensive, or breaking rules. What
unsettled him most was not simply conflict, but what he experienced as moral disorder: people acting
badly, violating expectations, ignoring boundaries. When clients became belligerent, manipulative,
or openly defiant, he felt himself tighten immediately. He was not just irritated; something in him
read misbehavior as the beginning of danger. On stressful days at the office, when caseload
pressures mounted and voices rose, he noticed his heart pounding and then became frightened by the
sensation itself. A racing heart no longer meant only stress. It felt like the opening seconds of
something terrible. Once he noticed it, his attention narrowed further. He would scan the room more
intensely, listen for changes in sound, and struggle to stay present in the conversation in front of
him.

At home, his wife saw the cost more clearly than he did. Javier did not tell the story often because
he could not get through it without losing parts of it, and because some part of him recoiled each
time he got close. He would stop mid-sentence, go quiet, and say he did not know what happened next,
or that it all sounded unreal when he said it aloud. Daily life was shaped instead by his vigilance.
Quiet evenings were not restful to him. If the house became too still, he would get up to check
doors, windows, or the yard, unable to settle. Stress of any kind—paperwork, an argument, running
late—could leave him keyed up for hours.

By the time he sought help, Javier was exhausted by living in a state of constant readiness and by
the sense that the event remained both unfinished and unacknowledged. What finally brought him in
was the recognition that he was no longer able to move through ordinary workdays without feeling on
guard, and that even his own body had become a trigger when his heart started pounding. What
frightened him most was the feeling that danger could return at any moment, especially in those
silent pauses when everything looked calm. What he hoped for, cautiously, was not to erase what
happened, but to be able to remember it without being overtaken by fragments, and to live without
feeling that every quiet moment was a warning.
