Asha Patel was 80 when she came for treatment, a divorced South Asian American woman who had
practiced dentistry for decades and still worked part-time in a small office she had built a life
around. The event she returned to again and again in the first sessions was a terrorist attack that
had taken place downtown several years earlier, on a weekday afternoon when she had been away from
her clinic between patients. She had gone to a nearby commercial block she used to visit routinely,
and what remained sharpest for her was how ordinary the day had felt until the rupture: the sudden
noise of emergency sirens rising from several directions at once, people shouting, and then the
smell of smoke pushing through the street so quickly that it seemed to arrive before she could
understand what had happened. In her memory, the air had changed first. She remembered the harsh
chemical smell and the burnt odor, the crowd surging unevenly, and the sickening recognition that no
one around her seemed to know where safety actually was. What stayed with her most was the sense
that control had vanished in an instant and that her life now depended on forces entirely outside
her hands.

In the weeks afterward, Asha found that certain moments in daily life caught her before she could
prepare. If an ambulance passed her dental office with its siren blaring, she would go still for a
second, then feel compelled to orient herself immediately: where the front door was, who was in the
waiting room, whether anything outside looked wrong. The smell of smoke was especially potent.
Something as ordinary as a neighbor grilling outside, a faint electrical burning smell from old
equipment, or smoke drifting in from the street could change the texture of the whole day. She would
feel herself pulled back to that block downtown, to the confusion and the certainty that danger
could appear without warning. She did not describe this as a vague nervousness. To her, it felt
practical and necessary to get her bearings quickly, because she had come to believe that a person
who failed to notice danger early was at the mercy of it.

That belief became woven into how she moved through the world. She stopped going anywhere near the
place where the attack had happened, even when doing so would have been the most direct route to
errands or appointments. If friends suggested meeting in that part of the city, she declined and
offered another location without explaining much. Over time her world narrowed in small, cumulative
ways. Areas she had once known well became mentally marked off-limits. She told herself there was no
point testing fate by going back. At work, she developed habits that felt to her like common sense.
In the operatory and waiting room alike, she kept track of entrances and exits almost automatically.
If she attended a professional meeting or even ate in a restaurant after work, she chose a seat near
the door. She noticed where people were standing, who came in late, whether anyone seemed agitated,
and what sounds were coming from outside. She was particularly unsettled in situations where someone
else held obvious power over her movements or choices. Being directed sharply by airport staff,
spoken over by a security guard, or feeling unable to leave a room on her own terms produced a jolt
of alarm that felt out of proportion to others but not to her. In those moments she was certain that
losing control of the situation, even briefly, could be dangerous.

These patterns began to affect her work in subtle but meaningful ways. During busy clinic hours,
when she needed to focus on patients, sirens from the street could pull her attention to the window
or hallway. She would check the corridor, glance toward the entrance, and silently review how
quickly she and her staff could leave if necessary. She remained competent and careful, but the
effort of constantly scanning her surroundings left her tired. Staff noticed that she preferred
doors not be blocked and that she became tense if supplies were stacked in a way that narrowed the
path out of a room. Socially, too, she had grown smaller in her life. She saw fewer people and
avoided invitations that might place her in crowded parts of town or in settings where she did not
feel free to leave.

What brought Asha to seek help now was not a single new crisis but the accumulation of years spent
organizing herself around the possibility of danger. Recently, while walking from her office to her
car, she heard converging sirens and caught the faint smell of smoke from somewhere nearby. She
found herself frozen for a moment, scanning the block, calculating exits, unwilling to move toward
the parking structure until she had looked everywhere twice. The incident unsettled her because it
felt so immediate, as if the old afternoon had not receded at all. She came to treatment wanting
some relief from the constant vigilance that had become her normal. What frightened her most was the
idea of becoming more constrained, of ending her life confined by routes, smells, doors, and other
people’s control. What she hoped for, cautiously, was not to forget what happened, but to move
through her city and her clinic without always feeling she had to be ready to escape.
