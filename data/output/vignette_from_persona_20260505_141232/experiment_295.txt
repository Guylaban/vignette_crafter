Kwame is a 23-year-old urban planner who had been proud, before the assault, of how easily he moved
through the city. He spent his days visiting redevelopment sites, attending community meetings, and
talking with residents about traffic, housing, and public space. The attack happened after an
evening planning meeting in a neighborhood corridor where he often worked. As he was leaving, a man
who smelled strongly of alcohol began arguing nearby, his voice sharp and heated. Kwame remembered
noticing the smell first, then the change in the man’s face as the argument escalated. Someone threw
an object—he can no longer clearly say what it was—and in the confusion the man turned on him. What
remains with Kwame is not a coherent story he can easily tell but a set of frozen images: the glint
of something moving through the air, the man’s angry expression, the alcoholic sourness on his
breath, his own sense that events were already in motion and he could not stop what came next. When
he tries to describe the assault in detail, he loses words. He knows it happened; he can point to
fragments of it with painful certainty, but if he stays with the memory for more than a moment he
becomes flooded and has to stop speaking.

In the weeks afterward, the city that had once felt like his field of work began to feel volatile
and unreadable. At planning meetings, if residents disagreed with one another and someone’s tone
sharpened—even calmly—Kwame’s body reacted before he could reason with it. A chair scraping, a
binder dropped on a table, a quick gesture in his peripheral vision would make him jolt hard enough
to embarrass him. He started watching hands, shoulders, and sudden movement around him, as though
danger might announce itself in a twitch. Colleagues would tell him that a meeting was under control
or that a site visit was perfectly safe, but he found he could not take that in. Other people’s
reassurance seemed flimsy to him. He had come to believe that safety was exactly the sort of thing
people say right before something goes wrong.

What frightened him almost as much as the memories was what they seemed to mean about him. He
dwelled on what he should have done differently that night: whether he should have left earlier,
whether he should have avoided the argument, whether he had missed some obvious sign. These thoughts
circled for hours, often late at night, and never arrived anywhere. He also became convinced that
the people and systems that were supposed to protect him had failed. He had expected that public
space, event security, and the basic structures around civic work would count for something; after
the assault, that assumption felt naïve. More and more, he was certain that bad things could happen
without warning and that when they did, he would not be able to stop them. Even minor upsets began
to feel like proof that he could no longer cope. If a conversation became tense, or a project hit an
obstacle, he felt internally brittle, as though the smallest disturbance might push him past what he
could manage.

Over the following months, Kwame changed his routines in ways that looked practical from the outside
but steadily narrowed his life. He began avoiding any situation that might involve disagreement or
confrontation. At work, that meant speaking less in public meetings, declining to facilitate
contentious sessions, and asking coworkers to take the lead when residents were upset about zoning
or construction. He started choosing site visits that seemed quieter and avoiding community events
where tempers sometimes flared. Being single, he had once relied on friends and colleagues for much
of his social life, but he also pulled back from them, especially if getting together meant bars,
parties, or anywhere he might catch the smell of alcohol. If someone nearby had been drinking, his
body tightened immediately and his attention locked onto exits, gestures, and distance.

At night, he found another way to get out of himself. He started gambling online, telling himself it
was just a distraction, then noticing he sought the jolt of it—the brief, sharp excitement that cut
across the heaviness and agitation. It gave him something immediate to focus on other than the
pictures in his head. Still, afterward he often felt ashamed and more critical of himself, which fed
the same familiar loop of thinking about what he had done wrong.

By the time he came for help, the pattern had begun to affect his work visibly. He was still showing
up, still trying to function as an urban planner, but meetings that once fit him well now felt like
exposure to something he could not control. What finally brought him in was a recent incident in
which a colleague, frustrated but not threatening, slapped a rolled set of plans onto a conference
table. Kwame jerked back so abruptly that the room fell silent. He left before the meeting ended and
later sat in his car unable to explain, even to himself, why his chest was pounding so hard. He said
he was tired of living as if any sudden movement or flash of anger could become another assault. At
the same time, he was afraid that talking about what happened would overwhelm him completely, and
afraid that the larger truth might be that no place is really safe and no one meant to protect him
ever truly did.
