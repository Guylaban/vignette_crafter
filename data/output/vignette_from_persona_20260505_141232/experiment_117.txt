Kwame is a 27-year-old African American man who came to treatment after years of trying to stay
ahead of reactions he could not fully explain to other people. He works long hours as a taxi and
Uber driver, mostly evenings and nights, and described feeling most comfortable when he is moving,
watching mirrors, and keeping his attention split between the road, the app, and the people around
him. He is single and lives alone. In early sessions, he spoke carefully and without much expression
about childhood physical abuse by a caregiver, describing a home where danger often announced itself
in small sensory details before anything was said out loud.

What stays with Kwame most vividly is not a single scene as much as a sequence his body still seems
to know by heart. As a boy, he would hear footsteps coming up behind him or down the hall and
immediately tense, trying to judge from the sound how close the caregiver was and what mood they
were in. He remembers the feel of a particular rough fabric against his skin during assaults, and
the shock of a specific coldness on his body, the kind of temperature that made him flinch before
the blow itself. In telling it, he did not elaborate dramatically; instead he spoke with the
certainty of someone whose fear had once depended on noticing these details quickly and accurately.
Even now, footsteps behind him can make his shoulders lock and his heart start pounding before he
has fully turned around. A rough seat cover, certain upholstery, or a fabric brushing his arm can
bring a rush of alarm that feels out of proportion to the present moment. Sudden cold on his
skin—from air conditioning blowing directly on him, or stepping out into night air after a heated
car—can make him feel as if his body has been pulled backward in time.

As he grew older, Kwame came to believe that something in him had been altered for good. He said
more than once that he feels “changed in a bad way,” as if whatever happened then marked him
permanently. He does not experience his body as reliable. What troubles him is how quickly it
reacts, how it surges into readiness when he knows intellectually that nothing is happening. He has
come to see his body as something that betrays him, reacting as if he is still in danger even in
ordinary situations. When friends or coworkers have told him to relax or reassured him that he is
safe, he has found their words almost meaningless. Safety, to him, does not land as a real
condition; it sounds like something other people are able to believe.

These reactions shape his workday in concrete ways. Driving for hire means strangers are constantly
entering his space, and he is acutely aware of movement and sound behind him. The moment a passenger
approaches the car from the rear or he hears footsteps coming up quickly on the driver’s side, he
feels himself go on guard. He watches people in the rearview mirror, scans their hands, and tracks
every shift in posture or tone. He chooses routes, neighborhoods, and times of day partly around
where he feels he can keep control. If a passenger’s clothing or bag brushes the back of his neck,
or cold air from an open window hits his skin unexpectedly, he has to work to keep his face neutral
and his driving steady.

Over time, Kwame has organized his life around not getting too close to anyone. He avoids emotional
intimacy because closeness feels exposing in a way he cannot tolerate. Relationships have tended to
end once someone wants more openness, more consistency, or more physical and emotional closeness
than he can manage. He distracts himself constantly—music, podcasts, extra shifts, scrolling on his
phone between rides—anything that keeps him from sitting still long enough to feel what comes up. He
also uses drugs, describing them less as pleasure than as a way to blunt himself or get through the
end of a shift. Alongside this, he acknowledges taking risks that even he recognizes could get him
hurt: driving too long, pushing himself when exhausted, sometimes moving through the city with a
kind of defiant carelessness. He is highly watchful but also, at times, reckless.

A painful part of his story is how separate he now feels from other people, including from his wider
community and culture. He spoke about feeling cut off, as if other people belong somewhere he no
longer knows how to enter. Gatherings that might once have offered connection instead leave him
feeling apart, tense, and unreachable. By the time he sought help, that isolation had become harder
to ignore. What finally brought him in was not one crisis but the accumulation of it all: the strain
of spending his workdays on guard, the collapse of another attempted relationship, and the sense
that his world was getting smaller even as he kept driving through the whole city. He said he wants
help because he is tired of living as if danger is always one step behind him. What frightens him
most is that this is simply who he is now; what he hopes, though cautiously, is that he might not
always have to feel at war with his own body.
