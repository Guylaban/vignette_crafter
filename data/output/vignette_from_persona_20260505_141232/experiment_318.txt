At 66, Diane had spent decades in front of cameras and under bright lights, and she still worked
steadily enough as a commercial model to keep a regular schedule of castings, fittings, and long
studio days. She was a widow and lived alone, and when she first described the event that brought
her to treatment, she did so with the flat precision of someone who had replayed it too many times.
Months earlier, she had been driving to a photo shoot when she was involved in an accident in which
another person was seriously injured. In Diane’s telling, the worst part was not only seeing the
immediate aftermath but the unbearable conviction that there had been a moment, however small, when
she should have done something differently. Her mind kept returning to the seconds before impact and
to what followed, searching for the point at which she believed she failed. She had come to believe
that a more competent person, a more alert person, simply would not have gotten into that situation.
When she said this, it did not sound abstract or philosophical; it sounded like a verdict.

In the days after the accident, Diane found that her body no longer settled. Even sitting in her own
living room with the television on low, she could feel herself held tight, shoulders raised, jaw
set, as if bracing for something else to happen. She knew perfectly well that she was at home and
safe, but the knowledge did not translate into ease. She described trying to relax on the sofa and
realizing that her hands were clenched in her lap. She would notice the tension only because it had
become painful. At night she would lie in bed waiting for her muscles to release and feel instead
that her whole body was poised to react. The feeling was not tied to any one place. It followed her
into ordinary moments that used to be neutral.

Her work made this especially visible. In the studio, where she had once moved easily through
directions and quick changes, she began to struggle to hold her attention on simple tasks. A stylist
would explain the order of looks, or a photographer would adjust her position, and Diane would catch
herself drifting, unable to keep track of what had just been said. She found this deeply unsettling
and embarrassing. Modeling had long depended on her ability to stay composed and responsive in fast-
moving environments. Now, under the scrutiny of crews much younger than she was, she felt exposed by
her own lapses. If someone repeated instructions, she heard it as confirmation that she was no
longer reliable. She had become certain that other people could see the accident on her, not
literally, but as a kind of stain. What frightened her was not only being judged for having caused
serious harm to another person, but being seen as the sort of woman who could do such a thing.

That sense of humiliation hardened over time. Diane stopped chatting easily on set and kept
interactions clipped and formal. She began arriving as late as she thought she could without being
unprofessional, preferring less idle time in makeup chairs or green rooms where conversation might
turn personal. She avoided questions about how she had been doing. If a job required prolonged
downtime between setups, she would sit rigidly with a magazine open and absorb almost none of it,
too tense to rest and too distracted to focus. Even among familiar people, she felt she had been
reduced by what happened. She described it as permanent, as though the accident had fixed her in the
eyes of others and in her own.

Alongside this, Diane remained preoccupied with what she had not done. She returned again and again
to the belief that she should have done more to stop what happened, though when she tried to explain
exactly what “more” meant, she could not arrive at anything that relieved her. The lack of relief
seemed only to deepen her self-reproach. She spoke as if forgiveness were not merely difficult but
prohibited. To forgive herself would, in her mind, be to minimize the seriousness of the injury and
her responsibility for it.

By the time she sought help, these patterns had narrowed her life. Work, once a place where she felt
practiced and at ease, had become another setting in which she monitored herself and came up
wanting. At home she could not fully unwind, even in the quietest parts of the day. She noticed her
concentration slipping in conversations, in reading, even in following a recipe, and each lapse
seemed to confirm her harsh comparison between herself and the person she thought she should have
been. She came to treatment after a recent job on which she lost track of repeated directions and
then sat in the dressing area afterward, humiliated and trembling with tension, unable to calm
herself before driving home. What she wanted most was not absolution so much as the possibility of
getting through a day without feeling constantly braced and ashamed. What she feared most was that
this was now her permanent self: the woman who caused irreversible harm and could never stop
reliving her failure to prevent it.
