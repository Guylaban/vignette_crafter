When Ty arrived in the United States years ago from Southeast Asia, she built a life that felt
steady and ordinary. At 52, she is married, works long shifts at a fast-food restaurant, and had
come to value routine, especially the sense that if she worked hard and kept going, life could be
managed. That assumption was badly shaken during the natural disaster she now describes only in
broken fragments. When she tries to speak about it, the account does not come out as a single story.
Instead, it arrives in jagged pieces: the sudden noise, the sense of the world turning violent
without warning, darkness, water and debris where familiar surroundings had been, and the terrifying
certainty in those moments that there was nowhere safe to go. Even in session, when she starts to
approach the memory, she becomes visibly overwhelmed and has to stop, pressing her lips together and
looking down as if to hold herself together. She says the memory does not stay where she puts it. It
forces itself into her mind when she is washing dishes, riding home from work, or trying to lie
quietly in bed.

In the weeks after the disaster, Ty found that nighttime changed first. Daylight felt somewhat more
manageable because she could see what was around her, but after dark she no longer felt she could
truly relax. She began checking doors and windows repeatedly before bed, then lying awake listening
for anything out of place. Sleep became difficult both at the start of the night and after she
finally drifted off. She would wake abruptly and remain alert for long stretches, straining to hear
whether some new danger was approaching. What frightened her most was the feeling that bad things
did not announce themselves until it was too late. Night came to feel like the hour when she was
least protected.

Over the following months, sounds became especially potent. At the restaurant where she works,
unexpected loud noises are unavoidable: the crash of a metal tray, the alarm of a timer, someone
dropping a stack of frozen goods in the back, the sharp hiss and clatter from the kitchen during a
busy rush. Coworkers might glance up and keep moving, but Ty feels her whole body react before she
can think. She goes instantly on alert, scanning, her heart pounding, as if the next second might
bring catastrophe. She tries not to show it, embarrassed by how strongly she reacts, but she has
begun positioning herself away from the noisier stations when she can. On especially hectic shifts
she finds herself distracted, braced, and exhausted by the effort of getting through the day. At
home, a slammed car door outside or an unexpected thud in the apartment building can make her freeze
and listen, fully awake again.

Her own understanding of what has happened to her is bleak and painful. She has come to believe that
something in her emotional life has been permanently damaged. She tells herself that she will never
feel normal emotions again, and there is a flatness to the way she says this, as if joy, ease, and
ordinary comfort now belong to other people. At times she sits with her husband and knows she is
supposed to feel close, grateful, or safe, but instead feels distant from herself and from him. She
does not describe conflict so much as disconnection. She also carries a growing sense that she no
longer belongs anywhere. The life she made in America feels unfamiliar, but the person she was
before the disaster also feels unreachable. At work she smiles at customers and follows the script
of the shift, yet privately feels set apart, as though she is only acting like someone who fits in.

Running through all of this is a conviction of helplessness. Ty is certain she cannot stop bad
things from happening to her. She does not say this dramatically; she says it as if it has been
proven. That belief appears in small decisions as much as large ones. She avoids talking in detail
about the disaster because once the memory begins, it comes in disconnected pieces and the emotion
surges so strongly that she feels she might lose control. She avoids certain conversations, certain
television images, and lingers at work after closing if it means getting home before it is fully
dark. These habits have narrowed her world. She is tired, less present with her husband, and
increasingly worn down by every day requiring vigilance.

What finally brought Ty to seek help was not one new event but the accumulation of nights without
rest and the realization that she was organizing her life around noise, darkness, and the effort not
to remember. She said she could no longer keep pretending she was fine during her shifts while
feeling internally on edge all the time. At the same time, she was frightened that speaking about
the disaster would make the memories come back too strongly to bear. More than anything, she wants
some way to live without feeling under threat after every sound and every nightfall, though she is
not yet sure whether that is still possible for her.
