At 22, Minh was in college in the United States when he joined a relief organization that sent
student volunteers into a war zone to help distribute food, carry medical supplies, and assist
families moving through damaged neighborhoods. He had gone because he believed, with the earnest
certainty of someone young and serious, that when other people were in danger the right thing was to
show up. He still talks about one day there more than the others. He and several other relief
workers had been moving between a crowded distribution point and a damaged school building being
used as a shelter. Armed men in uniform were positioned nearby, and there was a constant sense of
being observed as lines of civilians shifted, stalled, and pressed forward. Minh remembered looking
up and seeing uniforms, weapons, and hard, appraising faces turned toward the crowd. He was aware of
police and military presence not as background but as something to track moment by moment. In that
setting, being seen felt dangerous. He recalled trying to keep his head down, feeling his chest
tighten whenever an officer or soldier looked directly at him, and being struck by how little power
he had to change anything around him.

What stayed with him afterward was not only fear, but a painful conviction that he had failed where
it mattered most. He had wanted to be useful, protective, competent. Instead, when he remembers that
period, he thinks of confusion, armed authority all around him, and his own inability to stop
terrible things from happening in front of him. He had come to believe that he had failed in his
most important role in life. He says this plainly, without dramatics, as if stating an established
fact. Alongside that belief is another that frightens him in a different way: even now, back in the
U.S. and enrolled again as a student, his body reacts as though the war zone is still around him.
His heart speeds up, his muscles tense, and his attention narrows before he has had time to tell
himself he is safe. To Minh, this does not feel like a memory intruding so much as his body becoming
an unreliable ally. He is distressed by the sense that his own body keeps acting as if danger is
present even in ordinary settings.

Over the months after he returned, these reactions began shaping his days on campus. Walking across
the university grounds, he became acutely sensitive to the feeling of being watched. If someone
glanced at him too long in the student union, if he noticed a campus security officer near an
entrance, or if he saw a police or military uniform anywhere in town, his whole body seemed to
brace. He would lower his eyes immediately. Making eye contact felt intolerable because it seemed
too close to that earlier feeling of being singled out under scrutiny. In class, he began choosing
seats where fewer people could see him directly. If he noticed authority figures or uniforms outside
a building, he would turn around and take another route, even if it made him late.

His world narrowed in quieter ways too. He stopped meeting up with people he had socialized with
before he left. Seeing them felt loaded; he could not bear the ordinary expectation of recognition,
questions, or eye contact. Friends interpreted his distance as disinterest, and several stopped
reaching out. Minh did not describe this as loneliness at first. He described it as easier, safer,
less exposing. Still, the cost became concrete. Group work became harder because he avoided looking
at classmates and kept interactions brief. He passed up campus events and informal gatherings. Being
a student meant moving through crowded, public spaces every day, and he managed this by watching
routes carefully, avoiding visible security personnel, and leaving quickly when he felt too
observed.

What frightened him most was how little control he felt he had over any of it. He was certain that
bad things could happen and that he could not stop them. That sense of helplessness attached itself
not only to the memory of the war zone but to the present, so that even safe situations could feel
precarious if a uniform came into view or someone’s gaze lingered on him. By the time he sought
help, he was exhausted by the effort of getting through the day while appearing normal. He came in
after realizing how much of college he was now arranging around avoidance—who he could pass in the
hallway, where he could sit, which paths would keep him away from campus police. He said he wanted
help because he could see his life getting smaller. What he feared most was that this was proof he
had already failed and would keep failing; what he hoped, cautiously, was that he might find some
way to live as a student again without feeling hunted by uniforms, by other people’s eyes, and by
his own body.
