clear quickly that she did not feel composed inside. When she speaks about the combat deployment,

can tolerate. The event she returns to most often happened at night. She had been with others from
her unit in darkness broken only by scattered light and the noise overhead of helicopters moving
through the area. In the confusion and danger of that moment, thoughts about family were close at

comments about family can strike her with unexpected force. What remains most vivid is not a single
orderly narrative but the felt combination of darkness, aircraft sounds, and the immediate sense
that something terrible had happened and that she had been the kind of person to end up there.

In the weeks after returning, Amina found that ordinary life did not feel ordinary. Helicopters
passing overhead would stop her in place. Even when she knew she was in the United States, the chop
of the blades seemed to pull her back into that combat setting so abruptly that her body reacted
before she could think. Darkness became difficult in a way she had never known before. She avoided
dim hallways, hesitated before entering unlit rooms, and kept lights on longer than she used to,
because dark spaces no longer felt neutral to her. Comments that other people made lightly about
family could land hard. A coworker asking whether she was close with her family, a client mentioning
a mother or sibling, someone joking about family obligations—these moments could suddenly make her
feel exposed and unsettled. To others, her reaction might have seemed confusing, but to Amina those
remarks touched something linked to the night of combat itself, as if they opened a door she was
trying to hold shut.

Alongside these reactions, she had come to believe something painful about herself. She was
convinced there was something about her that made the event happen. She did not describe this as bad
luck or war; she described it as personal defect. Somebody else, she felt certain, would not have
gotten into that situation. If another person had been stronger, smarter, or more capable, they
would have avoided it or handled it differently. These thoughts were not occasional doubts but a
settled way she had begun to judge herself. She also spoke with grief about who she used to be.

where she was functioning, she did not experience that functioning as evidence of competence. She
saw it instead as a thinner version of herself, someone getting through tasks without the sense of
strength she once took for granted.

event because being with them meant facing versions of herself that no longer felt accessible.

got close enough, they would see what was wrong with her, or they would expect a version of Amina
she no longer believed existed. She also stopped watching television programs or films, and avoided

gatherings with colleagues after work, especially those that might run late into the evening, and
her isolation became noticeable.

What finally brought Amina to seek help was not one dramatic crisis but the accumulation of a life
becoming smaller while her distress remained large. She was troubled by how much effort it took to

family, and that there might still be a way to live alongside other people without bracing for harm.
