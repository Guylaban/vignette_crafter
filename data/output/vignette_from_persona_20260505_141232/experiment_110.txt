Elaine is a 60-year-old divorced American politician who came to treatment after many months of
trying to manage on her own what she still refers to, haltingly, as “the crash.” Before it happened,
she had been known for her steadiness in public, for moving briskly from committee meetings to
constituent events, for spending long hours in cars driven by staff or occasionally taking the wheel
herself between engagements. The accident occurred after a day that had already been crowded and
tense. She was driving when a collision happened and another person was seriously injured. In the
fragments she can bring up, there is the violent image of cars struck at an angle, the abrupt force
through her body, and a burst of sound that has remained with her more vividly than the full
sequence of events. She remembers shouting—her own voice, other voices, she cannot cleanly separate
them—and the scene has since become fixed less as a coherent story than as jagged sensory pieces.

What has troubled her from the beginning is that when she tries on purpose to remember exactly what
happened, she cannot seem to get hold of it in an orderly way. She can sit in her office and tell
herself to think it through step by step, but her mind goes blank or slips away just when she wants
the details most. Then, at unwanted moments, the event returns in a different form: a flash of
crumpled cars, the sound of shouting in her head, and a sharp pain in her neck that feels so
immediate she instinctively reaches up to brace the area. She describes these episodes as if her
body is telling the story before her mind can. The neck pain is especially unsettling because it
makes the accident feel present again, not past.

The things that set this off have become painfully specific. Raised voices in a corridor outside a
legislative chamber, a staff argument that becomes heated, even the emphatic shout of someone trying
to get another person’s attention can make her whole body react. She feels a rush of fear and shame
before she can think. Seeing images of cars that have been hit—on local news, in campaign materials
about road safety, even in passing glimpses online—can stop her cold. She has also become intensely
alert to pain in her neck. A small twinge after a long meeting or after sleeping badly is enough to
pull her back into the sense that something terrible is happening again.

Since the accident, Elaine has organized her life around not being stirred up. She avoids anything
she thinks could be stressful, which has narrowed both her public and private world. In a profession
built on conflict, debate, and constant exposure, she has started asking aides to screen events more
carefully, declining appearances likely to involve confrontation, and leaving strategy sessions when
the tone sharpens. Colleagues have noticed that she is less available in moments that once would
have been routine for her. She also tries to avoid not only painful feelings but strong feelings
altogether. She keeps conversations flat, steers away from subjects that might upset or move her,
and has become wary of moments that could bring either criticism or warmth. It makes sense to her
this way: if she does not let herself feel too much, maybe she will not lose control in public.

Her avoidance around vehicles has had some of the clearest practical consequences. She no longer
drives, and being a passenger is nearly as difficult. For someone whose work depends on travel from
office to office, district to capitol, this has been deeply disruptive. She postpones in-person
meetings, rearranges schedules to reduce car time, and sometimes chooses not to attend events at all
rather than face the ride. The result has been missed opportunities and quiet strain with staff who
are left to explain her absences. She is aware of the irony that a public figure accustomed to
projecting authority now feels trapped by something as ordinary as getting into a car.

In session, what stands out is how harshly she judges herself. She has come to believe the event
happened because of the way she acted. Even without being able to summon every detail deliberately,
she is certain she should have done more to stop it. She says this with an almost ritual intensity:
that she will never forgive herself for not doing more, that what happened is humiliating, that no
amount of public composure changes what it means about her. The humiliation is not abstract. It
enters the room with her fear that others would see her as reckless, incompetent, or morally stained
if they knew how often she replays the accident or how frightened she is now by roads, shouting, and
her own body.

What finally brought Elaine to seek help was a recent episode after a contentious public meeting.
Voices rose in the hallway outside, and she suddenly heard the shouting from the crash in her head,
felt searing pain in her neck, and found herself unable to focus on the briefing in front of her.
She sat frozen, ashamed that she could not concentrate on work she had done for decades. She came in
frightened by how small her life has become and by the possibility that this is permanent. At the
same time, she is hoping—cautiously, almost reluctantly—that if she can understand why these moments
overtake her, she might reclaim enough of herself to return to work, to travel without dread, and to
live without feeling condemned by a day she cannot undo.
