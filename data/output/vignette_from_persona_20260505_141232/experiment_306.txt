city bus on early and late routes, preferring the predictability of the road to talking much about
himself. What he finally described had happened decades earlier, during a period when he had been
detained and tortured. Even in the first sessions, he did not tell it as one continuous story. He
could give flashes with terrible precision and then stop at the edge of blankness. Some moments were
fixed in him with unbearable clarity: the sound of approaching footsteps before a door opened, the
instant he understood that other people near him were also in danger, the helpless certainty that he
was about to be hurt again. Other stretches were missing altogether. He said it was as if his mind
had kept a few frames in perfect focus and torn the rest out. What frightened him was not only what
he remembered, but how little he could account for in between. He knew there had been more, and the
gaps left him feeling unsteady, as if something essential had been lost but was still close enough
to strike.

When he did remember, the emotional force was immediate. He did not speak of the torture as
something far away that he could revisit at a distance. He said that when it came back, it brought
the same terror. In session, he would stiffen, his breathing would change, and he would look toward
the door before answering the next question. He spoke most painfully about the presence of others
during that period and his conviction that he had failed them. He had come to believe, with the
force of a moral verdict, that he should have done more to stop what happened and should have
protected other people who were there. He did not present this as an abstract regret. To Mark, it
was proof of something permanent about him. He was certain that a stronger man would have
intervened, resisted, found some way to shield others. Because he had not, he called himself weak.
“I failed,” he said in different forms again and again, as though any fuller account of the event
would only confirm what he already knew.

Over the years, these experiences settled into his daily life in ways his family could see even when
he said little. Nights were the worst. Daytime, with its routines and movement, gave him some
structure, but darkness made him feel exposed. He found it harder to feel safe after sunset than at
any other time. In bed he listened closely for sounds in and around the house, straining to detect
whether someone might be entering. A settling floorboard, the rattle of a gate, a car door outside
could bring a surge through his body so strong that he was instantly awake and braced. His wife had
grown used to finding him up at the window or standing still in the hallway, listening. He knew the
house was locked. He knew, in one sense, that he was at home. But what his body seemed to know was
that danger announced itself in sounds before it arrived, and he could not stop himself from
monitoring for it.

The same pattern followed him into work. Driving a bus meant long hours enclosed with strangers
behind him and a constant stream of noise around him—passengers shifting, footsteps on the steps,
the hydraulic hiss of the doors, sudden banging from the chassis or someone striking the side panel
to get his attention. When a sharp sound caught him off guard, he felt the reaction before he could
think: a jolt through his chest, tightening in his muscles, the sense of terror arriving all at
once. He tried to keep his expression flat and his hands steady on the wheel, but he became more
watchful on late routes, especially in the dark, checking mirrors repeatedly and listening to every
movement near the front of the bus. He did not explain this much to coworkers. He told himself he
was just being careful. Still, the strain accumulated. By the time he got home, he was already keyed
up for the night ahead.

At home, his marriage had narrowed around these habits. His wife wanted to know what he needed, but
Mark had little language for it besides saying he could not relax. He avoided fuller conversations
about the torture because he dreaded what would happen if he tried to fill in the missing parts. At
the same time, the parts he did remember punished him. He lived between blanks he could not tolerate
and fragments he could not bear. What finally brought him to treatment was not a new event, but the
growing sense that his life had become organized around fear and self-condemnation. After another
night spent listening for an intruder and another workday shaken by sudden bodily surges at ordinary
sounds, he told his wife he was tired of feeling as if the danger were still inside the house and
inside him. He came in afraid that if he looked directly at the past, the terror would be exactly as
overwhelming as it had been then. He also came in hoping, cautiously, that he might find some way to
live at night, at home, and on his bus route without feeling that he had to stay on guard every
minute.
