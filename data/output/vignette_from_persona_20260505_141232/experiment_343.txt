Amina is a 38-year-old married African American woman who works on a commercial fishing boat along
the Gulf Coast, a job that structures her days around early launches, heavy nets, diesel fumes, and
long stretches of water. She came to treatment speaking about the death of a loved one in a car
crash, returning again and again to the same conviction: that she should have stopped it. The event
happened two years earlier, when her younger brother was driving home after spending the afternoon
with her and her family. They had argued briefly before he left. She had noticed he was upset and in
a hurry, and she has fixed on the moment he picked up his keys as the point when she believes she
failed him. Later that evening, there was a crash. Since then, the details she carries are not
abstract. When people talk casually about accidents, she feels her chest tighten and her body go hot
and shaky, as if she is back in the hours when the family phone calls began and everything narrowed
to dread. If she sees images of mangled cars on the roadside, in news footage, or on her phone, she
feels a jolt in her stomach and has to look away.

In the weeks after his death, Amina became consumed by thoughts of prevention. She replayed the
afternoon in loops: if she had told him to stay for dinner, if she had taken his keys, if she had
apologized differently, if she had gone with him. These thoughts did not feel optional to her; they
felt like the only honest way to face what had happened. She had come to believe that letting go of
the replay would mean excusing herself. What frightened her most was the possibility that her
brother died because of the way she acted and that she had failed in a basic duty to protect someone
she loved. She says she cannot forgive herself for “not doing more,” even though what “more” would
have looked like remains painfully undefined.

Her work has made some reminders hard to avoid. Before dawn, the crew often stops at a gas station
on the way to the docks, and the television above the counter sometimes runs local traffic reports
showing twisted vehicles or discussing fatal wrecks on the interstate. On those mornings, she
becomes physically tense and short with everyone around her. The younger deckhands have learned not
to joke with her when something about a wreck comes on the screen. If one of them starts talking
about a bad accident they passed on the highway, Amina may snap at them to “talk about something
else” with an anger that surprises even her. At sea, where patience and coordination matter, her
irritability has changed the feel of her working relationships. She knows she has become easier to
provoke and harder to approach, and she hates that her husband now describes her as always “on
edge.”

At home, the anniversary of her brother’s death has become especially difficult. As the date
approaches, her mood darkens days in advance. She grows watchful, bracing herself for the day as if
something catastrophic might happen again. She experiences waves of fear, guilt, anger, and shame so
strong that they seem to overtake the whole household. She stays up late most nights, long after her
husband has gone to bed, telling herself she is not tired, though in session she admits that night
is when the replay starts and sleep feels dangerous because it leaves her alone with her thoughts.
She sits with online shopping sites open on her phone, buying things she does not need—clothes,
kitchen gadgets, gear for the boat—describing the spending as a brief numbing comfort and then
another reason to feel ashamed in the morning when the delivery confirmations arrive.

Over time these habits have accumulated consequences. The late nights leave her exhausted on the
water, and the impulsive purchases have caused arguments at home about money. Her husband has tried
to get her to talk about her brother, but she often reacts sharply if he mentions the crash
directly, especially if he wonders aloud whether it could have been prevented. To Amina, that
question is unbearable because she is already asking it every day. By the time she sought help, the
immediate reason was a recent anniversary week when a roadside image of a crushed car on her way to
the docks led to such a strong physical reaction—racing heart, trembling, nausea—that she had to sit
in her truck before she could work. She told the therapist she was tired of living as if every
mention or image of a crash could undo her, but she was equally afraid that if she stopped punishing
herself, she would be betraying her brother. What she hopes for, cautiously, is not to forget him,
but to live without feeling certain that his death is hers to carry forever.
