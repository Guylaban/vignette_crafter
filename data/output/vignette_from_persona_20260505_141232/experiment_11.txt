Claire Moreau was 43 when a medical crisis turned her sense of safety into something fragile and
unreliable. She had been working a busy dinner service as a chef, pushing through the kind of
fatigue and discomfort she was used to dismissing in a hot kitchen, when she became acutely ill.
What stayed with her was not an abstract idea of sickness but the bodily immediacy of it: the sudden
conviction that something inside her was failing, the rush of staff voices around her, the stark
light of the emergency department, the sensation of not being able to trust what her body was doing
from one moment to the next. In her memory, the experience comes back as total sensory reliving. She
does not recall it as a distant narrative but as if she is there again all at once—the brightness,
the sounds, the physical sensations, the fear. At times she says it is hard to believe it actually
happened to her, as though the extremity of it sits outside ordinary life and outside the person she
used to be.

In the weeks after she survived the illness, Claire expected to feel grateful and to return to
herself. Instead, she found that reminders arrived without warning. In the restaurant, a wave of
heat, the metallic smell from the line, or the abrupt clatter of pans could suddenly pull her into a
flashback in which she felt she was back in the medical emergency, not remembering it but living it.
Her body would surge before she had time to make sense of anything. She described her chest
tightening and her muscles bracing as if danger were already present, and only afterward realizing
she was standing in her own kitchen at work. Even at home with her partner, in places she knew were
safe, she could not relax. She noticed herself staying alert to sensations in her body, as though
she were listening for evidence that another crisis had begun.

What frightened Claire most was the feeling that her body had become an enemy. She had come to
believe that it had been permanently damaged by what happened, and that its reactions now meant she
was never truly out of danger. If her heart raced, if she felt tired, if she noticed any unfamiliar
sensation, it seemed to confirm for her that something fundamental had been ruined. She did not talk
about these thoughts dramatically; they had the flat certainty of conclusions she felt she had
already tested. She was certain that her body kept reacting as if the threat were still present, and
to her that meant the threat was not really over.

Alongside the fear was grief. When memories broke through, she did not only feel terror but a heavy,
overwhelming sorrow for the life she thought she had before, for the ease with which she used to
move through a shift, for the confidence she once had in her own strength. She found herself numbing
her feelings whenever she could. If her partner suggested a weekend away, a celebratory dinner, or
anything that might make her feel hopeful or happy, Claire pulled back. She avoided positive
occasions because the lift in mood felt intolerable, almost unsafe, as if allowing herself to enjoy
something would expose how much had been lost or set her up to have it taken away again. Over time,
her partner began to feel shut out by how often Claire answered with “maybe later” and then
retreated into silence.

Sleep became another place where she tried to manage the experience. She stayed up until very late,
long after she was exhausted. She described dreading the vulnerability of settling down and letting
her mind catch up with her. In the late-night hours she would scroll on her phone or sit awake in
the dim kitchen of their apartment, preferring fatigue to the moment of trying to rest. The next
day, she returned to the restaurant depleted, more brittle on the line, and increasingly aware of
how often the environment itself set her body off. She remained at work, but colleagues noticed she
stepped away more often and held herself tensely even during routine prep.

By the time Claire sought help, months had passed in this constricted pattern. She was not coming
because she doubted that something terrible had happened; she was coming because she feared that
this was now her permanent condition. She said, with a kind of mournful resignation, that she would
never be able to feel normal emotions again. What she most wanted was not to erase the memory but to
stop feeling ambushed by her own body and to know whether she could ever stand in her kitchen, go
home to her partner, and allow herself an ordinary moment of calm without waiting for danger to
return.
