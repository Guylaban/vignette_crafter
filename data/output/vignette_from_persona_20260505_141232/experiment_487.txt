mechanic, known in the shop for moving carefully but confidently around engines, grease, and tools,
and for preferring to solve a problem herself before asking for help. She was married and lived with
her husband, but by the time she came for treatment her world had narrowed around a feeling of being
watched that never fully let up.

The stalking had begun in ordinary places that should have felt routine. A man started appearing
where Mai was already going: outside the repair shop at closing time, near the parking lot, along
the route home. What stayed with her was not a single dramatic confrontation so much as the repeated
realization that he was there again. On more than one day she noticed him standing near the edge of
a vacant lot by the shop, the air carrying the damp smell of soil and cut grass, and then later
caught sight of him again after she had left. She remembered turning and seeing him at a distance,
then turning again a few minutes later and still finding him there. The threat came to feel
inescapable because it entered the spaces of everyday life, including the path between work and
home. Even now, when she tries to describe those weeks in detail, Mai often starts, gives one or two
clear images, and then loses the thread. She says she can see certain moments “like pictures” in her
mind—the dark outline of a person near the lot, the uneven ground, the car, the back glance over her
shoulder—but when she tries to connect them into a full account, she stops mid-sentence or pushes
the memory away before she reaches the end.

In the months that followed, those fragments remained vivid while her sense of safety did not
return. The smell of soil or outdoor air could change her whole body in an instant. If she stepped
out of the shop and caught the smell of damp earth after rain, or if she was near a patch of turned
ground, she was no longer simply noticing the weather. She was back in those moments of checking
whether he was there, scanning the edges of the lot, bracing herself before walking to her car.
Because the stalking happened around the ordinary rhythms of leaving work and moving through outside
spaces, outdoor smells became charged for her in a way she herself found hard to explain.

Stress made everything sharper. On busy days at the garage, when customers were waiting and several
jobs were unfinished, she found herself more watchful, more likely to interrupt what she was doing
and look toward the door or the windows. She would walk to the back of the shop for a part and then
glance behind her on the way back, as if confirming that no one had followed her in. She did the
same in parking lots, grocery aisles, and on sidewalks. Looking behind her had become so frequent
that it no longer felt like a choice; to Mai it seemed like the only sensible thing to do. She was
certain that if she stopped checking, she would miss something important.

What unsettled her most was that she did not feel safe even at home. She described being on guard in
her own house, listening for sounds outside, noticing movements near windows, and orienting herself
toward doors without meaning to. There were evenings with her husband when nothing bad was
happening—television on, dinner finished, both of them finally sitting down—and that calm could
itself turn frightening. Feeling happy or relaxed did not bring relief. Instead, it made her tense,
as though letting down her guard would leave her exposed. If she caught herself enjoying a quiet
moment, she would suddenly feel she had made a mistake and begin scanning again, sometimes getting
up to look out or checking behind her even moving from one room to another.

These habits accumulated consequences. At work she remained capable, but coworkers noticed that she
was easily pulled away from a task by something at the edge of her attention. At home, the strain of
being continually alert wore on her husband, who wanted to reassure her but could not talk her out
of what felt to her like a practical necessity. Mai herself was frustrated by how difficult it was
to explain. In sessions, she would begin to describe what had happened, offer a few exact snapshots,
then go quiet, saying she could not finish it and did not want to see the rest.

She sought help only after realizing that the vigilance had not softened with time and that even
moments of peace now felt dangerous. She said she was tired of living as if someone might be just
behind her, even in her own home. What she feared most was never being able to relax without feeling
foolish or exposed. What she hoped for, stated plainly, was to be able to walk out of the shop,
smell the air, go home to her husband, and not have her body act as if the stalking were happening
all over again.
