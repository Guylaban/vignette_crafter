Nisha Patel is a 22-year-old South Asian American woman who came to treatment after months of trying
to push away what happened to her on the job. She is single and had been working as a delivery
driver, spending most days alone in her car, moving between apartment complexes, gas stations, and
front porches. The robbery happened during an evening delivery. She had parked, stepped out with an
order, and was approached before she had time to make sense of what was happening. A man closed the
distance quickly, directing her where to stand and what to hand over. What stayed with her was not
only the theft itself but the feeling of being overtaken by someone else’s commands, her body
complying while her mind lagged behind. She remembered a few sharp, frozen moments rather than a
full sequence: the man’s position near her car, the way he controlled where she moved, and the sound
of his voice telling her what to do. Later, when memories came back, they often arrived as those
isolated visual snapshots, accompanied by the voice she had heard that night, as if the command were
sounding again in her head.

In the days afterward, Nisha told herself it had not really happened the way she thought, or that
maybe it was not that bad. She found herself repeating this when other people asked how she was
doing, and even when she was alone. At other times the event did not come back as a clear picture at
all. Instead, she would suddenly feel the same surge of fear and helplessness in her body, with no
full image attached, and be left trying to understand why her heart was racing. She had also come to
believe that she should have done more in the moment. Even though she had been frightened and
compliant, she judged herself harshly for not stopping it, not resisting, not somehow changing the
outcome. Alongside that guilt was a broader conviction that she could not stop bad things from
happening to her. The robbery had left her feeling that safety could disappear instantly and that
her own actions might not matter.

Returning to delivery work became difficult in ways that felt painfully specific. Being in a
vehicle, whether driving herself or riding with someone else, became something she increasingly
avoided. The car was too closely tied to the robbery scene and to the vulnerable feeling of arriving
somewhere alone. She would delay shifts, cancel plans that required rides, and make excuses to
others rather than admit how frightened she felt. When she did have to go somewhere, she scanned
constantly for people she considered suspicious: someone lingering too long near an entrance,
someone watching her, someone moving toward her in a way she could not predict. She could not settle
in any place until she had identified how she would get out. In stores, apartment hallways, and
pickup locations, her attention went first to doors, stairwells, and gaps between people. Even at
home she found herself waking in the night to check that she was safe before she could attempt sleep
again.

Certain encounters were especially difficult because they echoed the original loss of control. Any
situation in which someone seemed to be ordering her around or forcing her into compliance could
provoke an immediate internal jolt. She noticed this with customers giving abrupt instructions, with
strangers crowding her space, and particularly around police. The sight of police officers was
enough to make her body tense and her mind race, and she struggled to explain why except that
authority itself now felt loaded and dangerous. She was also unable to relax even when she knew,
logically, that she was in a safe place. Friends would tell her to calm down, but calm did not feel
available to her body anymore.

Over the following months her world narrowed. She used drugs at times to blunt the intensity of what
she felt and to get temporary distance from the fear, the wakefulness, and the sudden return of the
man’s voice in her mind. At the same time, she felt increasingly cut off from other people. Being
22, she watched peers move easily through ordinary routines—driving, going out, laughing off small
risks—while she felt separate from them, as if she now lived by different rules. The concrete cost
showed up in her work: avoiding driving threatened her ability to keep doing deliveries, and the job
that had structured her days became another source of dread.

By the time she sought help, what finally pushed her to come in was the sense that she was
organizing her whole life around danger and still never feeling safe. She was exhausted from waking
to check on her surroundings, from constantly locating exits, and from trying to deny an event that
kept returning in pieces and sounds. What frightened her most was the belief that this was now who
she was—someone unable to protect herself, someone set apart from everyone else. What she hoped for,
though she said it cautiously, was to feel less controlled by the robbery and less trapped by the
expectation that bad things could happen at any moment.
