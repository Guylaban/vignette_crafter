Mark is a 57-year-old interior designer who came to treatment more than a year after a life-
threatening car accident that he still describes as if it is happening in front of him. At the time
of the crash, he had been driving home from a client meeting, tired but alert, moving through a busy
intersection on a familiar route. Another car struck the side of his vehicle with enough force to
spin it. He remembers the violent sideways impact, the burst of sound from crumpling metal and
brakes, and the helpless, suspended second in which he understood that he could not stop what was
happening. What remains most vivid to him is not a full, orderly memory but the visual image of
damaged cars locked together at odd angles and the noise around them—engines, tires, horns, voices
he could not make sense of. He survived, but the experience left him with a sense that his body had
failed him at the exact moment he most needed it to keep him safe.

In the weeks afterward, Mark found that ordinary parts of daily life had begun to take on the
quality of warning signals. If he passed a roadside collision, even a minor one, he felt seized by
the same mental picture of smashed vehicles and the same sick certainty that catastrophe could
arrive without notice. Car sounds were especially difficult. The abrupt squeal of brakes outside his
apartment, a horn in traffic, the low grind of engines idling near a loading zone could stop him
mid-thought and pull his attention away from whatever he was doing. As an interior designer, much of
his work depends on traveling to clients’ homes, meeting contractors, and visiting showrooms around
the city. He began noticing how often his workday placed him near traffic: waiting for rides in
front of buildings, standing beside busy streets with fabric samples in hand, hearing vehicles
outside a consultation. He did not describe these moments as simple reminders. To him, they felt
charged, as if the accident were close by and could break through again.

Conversations became difficult in a similar way. When clients, coworkers, or friends mentioned a
wreck on the highway or a family member’s fender bender, Mark’s attention narrowed immediately. He
would go quiet or change the subject abruptly, later feeling embarrassed by how visibly unsettled he
had become. Over time he started steering conversations away from roads, travel, and local news, and
he declined some social invitations if he thought the evening would involve long drives or casual
talk about recent events in town. His world grew smaller in ways that were practical enough to seem
reasonable to him at first. He told himself he was being careful, that there was no point putting
himself in situations that would set him off in the middle of a client meeting. But the accumulation
of these choices altered his work. He scheduled fewer in-person visits, leaned more heavily on email
and remote consultations, and noticed a distance developing with clients who had once relied on his
ease and presence.

Running alongside this was a painful shift in how he thought about himself. Mark had come to believe
that he could no longer trust his own body. The fact that he had been alive and functioning normally
one moment and trapped in violent impact the next felt, to him, like proof that his body was
unreliable and fundamentally vulnerable. He paid close attention to physical sensations and
interpreted them bleakly: tension, fatigue, or a surge of adrenaline did not feel temporary but like
evidence that something in him had been permanently altered. He said more than once that he did not
know himself anymore. Before the accident he thought of himself as composed, aesthetically driven,
and capable of moving through complicated environments with confidence. Now he experienced himself
as wary, easily shaken, and diminished. What frightened him most was the conviction that this was
not a phase but a lasting reduction of who he was. He was certain that he had changed for the worse.

By the time he sought help, these beliefs had settled into the texture of everyday life. A recent
incident finally pushed him to call: while on his way to a design installation, he saw two damaged
cars pulled to the shoulder and was flooded with the old crash image so intensely that he had to
stop what he was doing and step away before meeting the client. He later felt humiliated and deeply
discouraged. In session, he spoke less about wanting to “get over” the accident than about wanting
to understand whether any version of himself was still intact. He was afraid that the man he had
been before the crash was gone for good, and he was hoping, cautiously, that treatment might help
him live in a world of cars, sounds, and ordinary conversation without feeling that disaster was
constantly about to return.
