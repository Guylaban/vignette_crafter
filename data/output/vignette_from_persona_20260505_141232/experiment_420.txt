David is a 57-year-old single man who works as a tutor, meeting students in library study rooms, at
kitchen tables in their homes, and sometimes online when he can arrange it. When he speaks about the
assault, he does so haltingly. He says he knows exactly what happened and at the same time cannot
seem to put it into words. Certain parts are painfully sharp, almost overlit in his mind, while
other parts feel missing, as if the sequence breaks apart when he gets too close to it. What remains
especially clear is being alone with another man in a closed space, realizing he was no longer in
control of what was happening, and the moment physical contact became something forced on him rather
than chosen. That loss of autonomy is what still stands out most starkly to him. He remembers the
feel of the man's hands with distressing clarity; then his account becomes fragmented, and he stops,
saying he can see it but cannot say it.

In the weeks after the assault, David found that ordinary contact no longer felt ordinary. A hand on
his shoulder, someone brushing past him in a crowded hallway, even the casual closeness of standing
near another man could make his body tense before he had time to think. He had come to believe that
his body was no longer something he could rely on. It reacted too fast, too strongly, and in ways
that made him feel exposed. What frightened him most was that danger no longer seemed to announce
itself clearly. He became certain that he had to be especially careful because you never know what
can happen next. In public he began watching other people closely, scanning faces, posture, tone,
and distance for any sign that someone might harm him. Unfamiliar places felt particularly risky
because he could not judge them quickly; if he did not know the exits, the layout, who might be
around, or how close other people would be, he preferred not to go.

These habits began shaping his work. As a tutor, he started refusing new meeting locations unless he
could picture them in advance. If a student suggested a different café or a new branch library,
David often redirected them or offered online sessions instead. When he did meet in person, he
always chose his seat based on how protected he felt, preferring a chair with a clear view of the
room and an easy path out, never wanting to be cornered or surprised by someone approaching from
behind. If a student's father or older brother was unexpectedly home and David found himself alone
with another man in the house for even a brief moment, he became rigid and distracted, his attention
pulled away from the lesson and toward monitoring the room.

Over time, he narrowed his life further to avoid emotional strain. He declined invitations, cut
short conversations that felt too personal, and steered away from situations likely to stir strong
feeling. He also avoided touch in an increasingly deliberate way. He kept more physical distance
from others, stepped back from greetings, and arranged himself carefully so no one would brush
against him. These choices made sense to him; avoiding contact and intense feeling seemed like the
only way to get through the day without being overwhelmed. Smoking became woven into this routine.
He would smoke before going into a tutoring session, after leaving one, and in the pauses between
difficult moments, using the ritual to manage himself when he felt stirred up or on edge.

The cost has been a life that feels smaller and more solitary. David already lives alone, and he now
experiences himself as fundamentally set apart from other people. Around others he often feels
different in a way he cannot bridge, as though everyone else is participating in an ordinary social
world whose rules no longer apply to him. He is especially aware of this when students or colleagues
exchange easy gestures of familiarity that he cannot tolerate. He does not describe himself as
simply shy or private; rather, he feels isolated by what happened and by how much effort it takes to
stay watchful.

He sought help only recently, after noticing how much of his workday had become organized around
safety calculations. He was spending as much energy deciding where to sit, who might be present, and
how to avoid contact as he was actually tutoring. In session, he returns again and again to the same
fear: that if he lets his guard down, if he is trapped, touched, or overpowered even briefly, he
will have no way to protect himself. At the same time, what he hopes for is modest and deeply
important to him. He wants to be able to do his job without constantly scanning the room, to enter a
new place without immediately planning escape, and to feel that his own body is something he can
inhabit rather than something that can betray him.
