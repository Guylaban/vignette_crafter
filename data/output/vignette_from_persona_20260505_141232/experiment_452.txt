Luka is a 25-year-old American man of Eastern European background who works as a salesperson and
lives with his girlfriend. He came to treatment several years after spending time as a civilian in a
war zone, an experience he still speaks about as if it sits just behind the present moment. When he
describes that period, what comes across first is not a tidy account but the atmosphere of it: the
sense that danger could arrive without warning, especially after dark. He remembers nights
punctuated by sudden blasts, the sharp crack of gunfire, the rush to decide whether to stay low,
run, or wait. He had learned to listen for changes in sound with total intensity. Darkness meant not
knowing where the next strike would land, who might be outside, or whether the building around him
would still be standing by morning. Even now, what feels most true to him is that night is when
terrible things happen.

In the immediate aftermath of leaving the war zone, Luka expected that being back in the United
States would make that feeling disappear. Instead, he found that evenings brought the same
tightening in his body and the same certainty that he should not relax. During the day he could
usually tell himself where he was and what year it was; daylight, traffic, and ordinary routines
gave him something solid to hold onto. But once the sun went down, his sense of safety changed
abruptly. He would check the locks more than once, look repeatedly out the windows, and listen for
sounds outside his apartment. He did not describe this as a habit so much as something he felt he
had to do. In his mind, letting his guard down at night would be foolish. He was certain that other
people could afford to feel casual after dark only because they had not seen how quickly a normal
evening could become catastrophic.

Alongside this vigilance, Luka has been carrying strong surges of emotion that seem out of
proportion to whatever is happening around him but feel entirely justified from the inside. Fear is
the easiest for him to name, especially in the evenings, though anger rises quickly too. At times he
feels horror when a night sound catches him off guard, as if part of him is back in those streets
waiting for the next explosion. He also speaks about guilt and shame in a more halting way, as if he
is embarrassed by how much the war still lives in him when other people expect him to be grateful
simply to be safe now. He has come to believe there is something wrong with him for still reacting
so strongly. When his girlfriend notices him tensing at night or asks why he seems on edge, he often
feels ashamed that he cannot just enjoy an ordinary evening with her. On other nights he becomes
irritable, then guilty for snapping, then withdrawn because he cannot explain what has taken hold of
him.

These difficulties have followed him into work. Luka sells in a busy retail environment where he is
expected to stay engaged, track details, and shift quickly from one customer interaction to the
next. He says concentration has become unreliable in a way that frightens him. During the day he can
lose track of what a customer has just asked, find himself staring past someone while trying to pull
his thoughts back together, or realize he has missed part of a sales conversation. If he has had a
bad night beforehand, the problem is worse. He rereads basic information, forgets what he was about
to say, and struggles to stay mentally present long enough to complete simple tasks with his usual
confidence. Coworkers have noticed moments when he seems distracted or far away. He worries they see
him as careless, and that worry itself sits in the background whenever he is trying to focus.

about bedtime because he delays going to sleep, lingering over checks around the apartment or
staying awake longer than he means to. He prefers to keep lights on in parts of the home, which she
experiences as restlessness and distance. Their evenings, which used to be the easiest part of their
relationship, have become shaped by his need to monitor his surroundings and by the intensity of the
emotions that can overtake him without much warning. At work, he dreads late shifts most of all; as
the store approaches closing and darkness gathers outside, he notices himself becoming more keyed up
and less able to think clearly.

What finally brought Luka to seek help was not one new symptom so much as the accumulation of them.

he could not concentrate, he found himself sitting in his car after a shift, overwhelmed by fear and
anger and ashamed that he still did not feel safe. He told his girlfriend he was tired of living as
if every night required defense. In coming to treatment, what he hopes for most is simple enough to
sound almost modest: to be able to come home after work, be with her, and let darkness be only
darkness. What frightens him most is the possibility that he will never stop feeling as if danger
begins when the sun goes down.
