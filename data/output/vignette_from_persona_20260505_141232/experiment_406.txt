Nadia Haddad is a 46-year-old pharmacist who came to treatment after several years of living in the
shadow of the period when her son became critically ill. She is single and has raised him largely on
her own, and when she speaks about what happened, she does so with the precision of someone trained
in medicine and the dread of a mother who once stood close to losing her child. The worst period
began with what seemed at first like an ordinary illness and then turned rapidly into an emergency.
She remembers the rush to the hospital, the ambulance doors closing, the lights in the emergency
department, and the long stretches of waiting while staff moved quickly around her son’s bed. As a
pharmacist, she was accustomed to clinical settings, medication names, and the routines of care, but
this was different: she was no longer the professional behind the counter but a mother watching her
child’s life hang in the balance. The sounds and sights that belonged to treatment became tied to
the fear that he might die.

In the months after he recovered, Nadia found that she could not move through medical spaces as she
once had. The hospital where she had spent those hours became somewhere she avoided unless there was
absolutely no alternative. Even hearing an ambulance pass on the street made her body tense before
she could tell herself she was safe. At work, she could manage the pharmacy counter and the familiar
rhythm of dispensing prescriptions, but anything that edged closer to acute medical care unsettled
her. If a customer came in straight from a hospital discharge, or if conversation turned to
emergency treatment, she became tightly wound and focused on getting through the interaction as
quickly as possible. When colleagues talked casually about physical checkups or urged her to
schedule her own examinations, she put them off. The idea of entering an exam room, hearing
monitors, or seeing staff in motion around a patient felt unbearable. She understood how irrational
that might sound to others, especially given her profession, but to her those places no longer meant
care; they meant the moment everything could be taken away.

Nadia also tried hard to keep the whole experience out of her mind. If images of the hospital or her
son in danger began to surface, she pushed them away, turning at once to a task, a television show,
inventory lists from work, anything that would stop the thoughts from gathering momentum. At the
same time, she found herself circling one question over and over when she was alone: why had this
happened to her and her child? She did not arrive at an answer that comforted her. Instead, the
search seemed to leave her more convinced that something was fundamentally wrong. She had come to
believe that a stronger person would have handled it better, would not still be shaken years later,
would not dread an ambulance siren or a doctor’s office. She spoke of herself with striking
harshness, saying in effect that she was weak, that something about her was defective, that whatever
good future she once imagined had ended in that hospital.

These beliefs now color much of her daily life. Even in objectively safe settings—at home after
locking the door, in her car in the parking lot after work, in the quiet of a Sunday afternoon—she
cannot relax. Her shoulders stay tight, and she remains alert to sound. Ordinary noises that other
people barely notice can make her jump: something dropped in the pharmacy, a sudden car horn
outside, a sharp ring from a phone. The startle leaves her embarrassed in front of coworkers and
customers, and she quickly tries to compose herself so no one will ask questions. Nights are
especially difficult. She has trouble falling asleep, and when she does sleep it is often shallow
and broken. Lying in bed, she tries not to think about the illness, then finds herself drawn back to
it anyway, wondering again why it happened and what it says about her that she has never fully
regained her footing.

Over time, these patterns have narrowed her life. She keeps her distance from hospitals and avoids
making appointments for her own physical care. Her work remains one of the few stable structures in
her week, yet even there she feels changed, more brittle and less confident than before. What
finally brought her to seek help was not a single dramatic crisis but the accumulation of strain:
exhaustion from poor sleep, the constant sense of being on edge, and the realization that she was
arranging more and more of her life around not encountering reminders of that time. She is
frightened that this is simply who she is now, a damaged and weak person for whom nothing good lies
ahead. At the same time, she is hoping—quietly, cautiously—that treatment might make it possible to
be in the world, and in medical spaces, without feeling as though she is back in the worst moments
of her son’s illness.
