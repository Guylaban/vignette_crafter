zoning maps, transit corridors, and community meetings. Before seeking help, she had spent years

life, as if it were something that had happened to another woman with her face and voice but not
really to her.

natural landscape as the setting for terror: uneven ground, brush and tree cover, the sense that
danger could be hidden in any direction. On the day that stayed with her most forcefully, shouting
broke out without warning. Voices rose fast—urgent, panicked, overlapping in a language she only
partly understood—and people began moving abruptly around her. She remembered the shock of bodies
turning, running, crouching, grabbing for cover, and her own instant sense that something
catastrophic was happening. What remained most vivid was not a tidy sequence but fragments: the
volume of the shouting, sudden motion at the edge of her vision, the raw feeling of being trapped in
exposed terrain. When she tried to tell the story now, she would begin with one detail and then lose
the thread, stopping mid-sentence, looking confused and frustrated, as if the scene had broken apart
in her hands.

In the weeks after she returned to the United States, Linh found that ordinary things could pull
that place around her again. Old photos were especially dangerous. It did not even have to be a
picture from deployment; sometimes an image from that period, or simply seeing herself from those
years, was enough to make her feel abruptly transported. She described moments of suddenly acting as
if she were back there, her body tightening and orienting toward escape before she had fully
registered where she actually was. Being in nature could do the same. A weekend hike with her
partner, meant to be relaxing, became unbearable when the trail opened into scrubby, sunlit terrain
that resembled the landscape where she had worked. She went silent, scanned the surroundings, and
then insisted they turn back without being able to explain why. Shouting was the most immediate

setting again.
