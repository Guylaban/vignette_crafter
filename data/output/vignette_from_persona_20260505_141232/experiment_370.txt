Liam is a 19-year-old white American man who came to treatment while still trying to keep up with
his work as a counselor and his relationship, though both had begun to feel strangely distant from
him. The event he returns to when he tries to explain what changed was a sudden, life-threatening
illness that led to an emergency hospitalization. He remembers the speed with which ordinary
discomfort became something frightening and out of control: being rushed into a brightly lit
treatment area, people moving quickly around him, the sight of tubing and needles being prepared,
the cold precision of medical equipment, the antiseptic smell that seemed to coat the back of his
throat. At the time, what overwhelmed him most was the sense that his body had become unknowable. He
had been inside it, trusting it, and then suddenly it seemed capable of failing him without warning.
During examinations and procedures he felt pinned between needing help and dreading every moment of
it, watching hands approach him with needles or IV lines and bracing himself as if something
terrible were about to happen.

In the weeks after he was discharged, he expected the fear to settle once he was home and no longer
in immediate danger. Instead, pieces of the hospital kept intruding into ordinary life. A glimpse of
a needle, a drip stand on television, or the sterile smell of antiseptic in a clinic or even a
public bathroom could make his body surge before he had time to think. He described his reactions as
immediate and physical: his chest tightening, his stomach dropping, his whole body jolting as though
he were back under hospital lights waiting for another procedure. Even routine medical settings
became hard to tolerate. A simple examination room, the sound of equipment being moved, or the
suggestion that he should schedule a check-up could leave him tense and watchful for hours.

Liam had come to believe that his body was no longer reliable. He did not speak about it abstractly;
he meant that he no longer trusted the ordinary sensations of being alive. If his heart beat harder,
if he felt tired, if something ached, his mind went back to the illness and to how quickly things
had once become dangerous. At other times he felt almost betrayed by his own body. What frightened
him was not only the memory of having been ill, but the feeling that his body now kept reacting as
if the threat were still happening even when he knew he was safe. That loss of trust spread into how
he saw the rest of his life. He said that nothing felt meaningful in the same way anymore. Work,
plans, even time with his partner could feel flattened, as if part of him had stepped back from the
future.

His daily routines gradually narrowed around trying not to stir any of this up. Although he had
started therapy, he noticed that after sessions he would do almost anything not to think about what
had been discussed. He would scroll on his phone, put on videos, switch tasks, or mentally cut
himself off the moment hospital images started to come back. At night he often stayed up very late
on purpose. He knew he was exhausted, but going to sleep felt like giving up control, and the quiet
made the memories and bodily tension harder to ignore. The result was that he slept poorly anyway,
lying awake for long stretches or waking easily. During the day he became increasingly reactive to
sudden movement nearby; if someone shifted quickly beside him, he would jerk or tense as if he
needed to protect himself before he had even registered who it was.

These difficulties showed up sharply in the setting where he spent most of his time. As a young
counselor, he prided himself on being steady and present with other people, but he found himself
scanning his environment more than he wanted to admit. If a colleague mentioned a hospital visit, if
a client described a medical procedure, or if he had to pass an ambulance outside the building, he
felt his body kick into alarm. He also began avoiding parts of ordinary health care altogether,
putting off physical examinations and steering clear of hospitals whenever he could. This had
practical consequences: appointments were delayed, and his partner grew frustrated and worried about
how much effort it took to persuade him to attend even routine medical visits.

By the time he sought help in earnest, Liam was worn down by the combination of constant bodily
alertness, poor sleep, and the effort of keeping reminders at a distance. What finally brought him
in was not one dramatic crisis but the recognition that his life had become organized around
avoiding medical cues and then trying not to think about why. He was frightened that he would never
again feel at ease in his own body, and that the emptiness he now felt toward work, relationships,
and the future might be permanent. At the same time, he was hoping for something simple and very
personal: to be able to walk into a medical setting without feeling ambushed from the inside, and to
stop living as if his body were still warning him that catastrophe was moments away.
