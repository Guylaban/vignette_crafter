Amina is a 43-year-old African American woman who works in IT support for a large medical office and
came to treatment after years of carrying a private sense of danger and defectiveness that she had
never fully spoken aloud. She is married and, by her account, outwardly dependable and composed, the
person at work who can usually troubleshoot under pressure. What she described in the first
sessions, however, began much earlier, in childhood, in episodes of physical abuse by a caregiver
that took place in conditions that still feel vividly present to her. When she thinks of what
happened, she does not remember it as a single abstract fact from the past. She remembers being shut
into a small room and the feeling of having nowhere to go. She remembers the particular terror of
hearing a lock and knowing she was contained with no way out. She also remembers being taken into a
natural area where no one else was around, the kind of setting that should have felt open but
instead felt exposed and unsafe. Even now, what stands out to her is not only the pain but the
certainty she had as a child that she had somehow brought it on, that there must have been something
in her that made an adult treat her that way.

As she grew older, Amina did not set these experiences aside so much as organize her life around not
coming too close to them. Talking directly about what happened has always felt dangerous to her. If
a conversation edges toward childhood or abuse, she notices her whole body tighten before she has
even decided what to say. She becomes acutely aware of her shoulders, jaw, and stomach, as if she is
bracing for impact. She said that even when she knows, logically, that she is sitting with her
husband or in a therapist’s office, her body can react as though something bad is about to happen.
The same thing occurs in enclosed spaces. If a conference room door closes during a staff meeting,
especially if the room is small and windowless, she feels a rush of alarm that seems out of
proportion to the moment, but to her it does not feel false at all. Her attention shifts immediately
to the door, to whether it is locked, to how quickly she could leave. She has sometimes missed parts
of meetings because she is tracking the room rather than what her coworkers are saying.

Her work in IT support confronts her with this more often than she would like. She is frequently
called into cramped server closets or small locked offices to fix printers, terminals, or network
equipment. She has developed quiet habits that make sense to her: leaving doors slightly ajar when
she can, positioning herself nearest the exit, asking practical-sounding questions that allow her to
delay going into certain rooms. These strategies have not gone unnoticed. She worries that
colleagues see her as difficult or distracted. On days when she is already tense, she finds it hard
to concentrate on troubleshooting steps she knows well, and then that lapse itself becomes proof, in
her mind, that something is wrong with her.

The same pattern appears outside work. Her husband enjoys hiking and weekend drives, but Amina has
gradually withdrawn from outings in wooded parks or isolated natural areas. She does not describe
nature as generally unpleasant; rather, certain settings abruptly feel too similar to where some of
the abuse occurred. The sight of trees closing in around a trail, the feeling of being away from
other people, can make her body surge into readiness before she can talk herself down. She then
feels ashamed, because she believes other people would simply enjoy the scenery, whereas she
experiences it as threat. Over time she has come to speak about herself in harsh, global terms. She
has said she must be the kind of person these things happen to, that there is something defective in
her character, not just something painful in her past. These beliefs are not fleeting thoughts for
her; they are the explanations she has carried for years.

What brought her now was a recent combination of strain at work and increasing distance at home.
After becoming overwhelmed in a closed office while trying to repair a computer, she had to step out
abruptly and could not easily explain why. Around the same time, she turned down another outing with
her husband rather than risk ending up somewhere that felt too much like before. He urged her to get
help, not because he doubted her strength, but because he could see how much effort it took for her
to get through ordinary days. Amina arrived to treatment frightened of what would happen if she
spoke in detail, certain that revisiting the past might make her lose control, but also quietly
hoping that she might no longer have to live as if danger were always one door, one conversation, or
one stretch of trees away.
