Harjit Singh is an 80-year-old widowed Coast Guard veteran who came to treatment after many years of
living inside an event he still struggles to describe in a straight line. He speaks in bursts and
pauses about the day it happened, on a stretch of rough shoreline during a rescue operation in a
heavily wooded coastal area. What he can say with certainty is that another man was gravely injured
in an accident Harjit believes he set in motion. When he tries to recount it deliberately, the story
breaks apart. He can offer a flash of wet rock under his boots, the sharp smell of salt and pine,
the scream of metal under strain, a body falling in a way that still makes his stomach drop. Then
there is a gap. When he pushes himself to remember more, he says it is as if the memory goes dead on
him. But at other times it returns all at once, with a force that feels present rather than past:
the blare of emergency sirens, the cold air off the water, the color of the trees, the impact in his
chest when he realized someone had been badly hurt.

In the weeks after the accident, Harjit told himself it had not happened the way he feared, or at
least not as badly. He would correct his own mind when it drifted toward the scene, insisting he
should think about the good that came after, the people who survived, the fact that worse outcomes
were possible. He has spent years trying to replace intrusive fragments with forced gratitude and
“positive thoughts,” as he puts it. If an image surfaced, he shoved it away. He came to believe that
dwelling on it was dangerous, that if he let the full picture form he might not be able to contain
it. Yet this effort has left the event in his mind as disconnected pieces rather than a settled
memory, and when it breaks through it does so with a vividness that overwhelms him.

What has stayed most powerfully in his daily life is the sense that he must remain on guard. Harjit
watches entrances, windows, the movements of other people. In any waiting room, restaurant, temple
function, or family gathering, he chooses his seat carefully, preferring a wall behind him and a
clear path out. He does not describe this as habit so much as necessity. He says he cannot relax
unless he knows where danger could come from and how he would respond. Unfamiliar places trouble him
especially. If he cannot quickly understand the layout, he feels exposed and unsettled, and over
time he has narrowed where he is willing to go.

Natural settings have become particularly difficult. A park with dense trees, the smell of damp
earth, a rocky waterfront, even certain stretches of highway where the landscape opens into brush
and shoreline can stop him cold. For a man whose Coast Guard life once tied him to the outdoors,
this has been a painful constriction. He still remains connected to that world, but he avoids
settings that resemble the scene of the accident whenever he can. If he has to pass through such a
place, he becomes tense and watchful, as if something terrible could happen again at any moment.
Sirens are another immediate rupture. Hearing them from a passing ambulance or patrol vehicle can
bring the whole scene back with crushing sensory force, and afterward he may sit in silence for
hours, unable to explain to anyone what just happened inside him.

Talking about the future is unexpectedly difficult for him as well. When relatives ask about plans,
or former colleagues talk about upcoming events, Harjit becomes withdrawn. He does not say much, but
the conversation seems to catch on something painful. He has come to feel that there is something
fundamentally wrong with him, that the accident revealed the kind of man he really is. He speaks of
it not as a terrible mistake but as evidence of defective character. In his mind, what happened fits
with “the sort of person” he is, and that belief carries a heavy shame.

There have also been periods when, instead of pulling back, he has taken needless risks. He is
matter-of-fact about this, as if courting harm made a grim kind of sense. Those moments sit
alongside his caution, both part of how he has lived since the accident. His family has noticed the
changes most in his narrowing routines, his reluctance to go somewhere new, his scanning of every
room, and the way certain sounds or landscapes can make him disappear into himself.

Now, after years of trying to master the memories by dismissing them, outrunning them, or covering
them with forced optimism, Harjit has sought help because the effort is no longer working. He says
he is tired of being startled by sirens, tired of declining invitations because he does not know the
place, tired of feeling that a simple conversation about next month or next year can leave him
shaken. What frightens him most is the possibility that the accident says something final about who
he is. What he hopes, though he says it cautiously, is to be able to remember what happened without
feeling as if he is back there again, and to sit in the world with a little less vigilance.
