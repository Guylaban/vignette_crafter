Daniel is a 55-year-old fashion designer who came to treatment after years of arranging his life
around a sense of danger he could never fully explain, even to himself. He was raised by a caregiver
who physically abused him throughout childhood. When he tries to describe those years, the account
breaks apart. He can circle details—the feel of being cornered in a quiet house, the sudden movement
before a blow, the watchfulness he learned in rooms where nothing was being said—but he cannot put
it into a single clear sequence. At times he speaks about it in a flat, almost detached way, as if
he were describing something that happened to another boy. At other moments, a memory arrives with
crushing guilt, and he is left dwelling on what he believes he did wrong, what he should have
anticipated, how he might have prevented what happened. Just as often, his mind turns outward and
fixes on what was done to him, replaying the cruelty and the unfairness of it until he feels
consumed by the injuries other people have caused.

What remains most vivid is not a neatly told story but a way of sensing danger. As a child, silence
in the house did not mean peace. Silence meant waiting, listening, bracing for the next shift in
mood or the next approach. That association has stayed with him into adult life. In his apartment
now, unusual quiet can make his body tense before he has consciously registered why. He finds
himself listening for footsteps, for a door, for some sign that something bad is about to happen.
Even at home, where he knows he is alone and should be able to relax, he feels on guard. He checks
locks more than once and remains alert to small sounds, as though safety could vanish without
warning.

This constant readiness follows him into work. Daniel has built a respected career in fashion, a
profession that places him in studios, fitting rooms, crowded workspaces, and backstage environments
where people move quickly and touch without much ceremony. A hand landing unexpectedly on his
shoulder when a seamstress is asking a question, or a model brushing past him from behind, can make
him jolt with visible alarm. He knows these moments are ordinary in his field, and the embarrassment
afterward is often sharp. He worries that colleagues notice his reaction and think there is
something deeply wrong with him. He has begun positioning himself at the edge of rooms, preferring
to see people approach. In fittings he is careful about where he stands, less because he can explain
it than because it feels intolerable to be surprised by contact.

Over the years he has come to believe that the abuse permanently changed him for the worse. He does
not speak about this dramatically; rather, it appears in the certainty with which he dismisses the
possibility of feeling normal. He says he has never really trusted people and does not see why he
should. Being close to others feels risky, not only in intimate relationships—his marriage ended
years ago—but in ordinary dependence, in letting anyone see uncertainty or need. He is also
frightened by his own reactions. The startle, the scanning, the way a quiet evening can suddenly
feel ominous, the way memories arrive either numb or unbearable—these have led him to wonder if he
is going crazy.

He has developed small but rigid habits around sleep. Because certain times of day and evening
conditions seem to bring on nightmares, he tries to avoid them indirectly: keeping the television on
late, staying busy, stretching work into the night, resisting the transition into bed when he senses
the familiar dread building. These routines leave him tired and frayed, but he clings to them
because they feel like the only available defense. In the hours when he cannot distract himself, his
mind turns over the same questions: what others did to him, what he failed to do, how much of his
life has been shaped by damage he cannot undo.

He sought help now after a recent period of mounting strain at work and at home. In the studio, he
startled so violently when an assistant touched him from behind that everyone stopped and stared.
Later that week, alone in his apartment, the silence of the place became so threatening that he left
the television and lights on in every room just to get through the evening. What finally brought him
in was less one dramatic incident than the fear that his world had narrowed to managing reactions he
did not understand. He wants, cautiously, to know whether there is a way to live without being on
guard all the time. What frightens him most is the possibility that this is all he will ever be now:
damaged, mistrustful, and losing control of his own mind.
