Mika is a 71-year-old man of Oceanian background who has spent most of his adult life working as a
painter, taking jobs in apartment buildings, storefronts, and church halls across his part of the
city. He lives alone and had been used to a routine built around early mornings, steady handwork,
and long hours moving through public spaces without much thought. The change in him began after a
terrorist attack that occurred during an ordinary workday, when he was painting inside a commercial
building near a crowded pedestrian area. He had gone out to get coffee and was passing near the
entrance when the blast happened. What stayed with him was not an abstract idea of danger but the
exact physical fact of it: the violent force, the abrupt drop of his body, the way he ended up
twisted on the ground and unable for several seconds to understand whether he was alive. He
remembers the smell in the air, the sound of people screaming, and the sight of debris scattered
across the pavement with a clarity that still unsettles him. In his mind there is nothing blurred or
softened by time; the memory arrives sharp, complete, and unwanted.

In the weeks afterward, Mika found that the attack did not remain in the past in the way he expected
it would. At odd moments, without invitation, the scene forced itself into his mind. He could be
rinsing brushes, waiting in line for supplies, or sitting quietly at home, and suddenly he was back
there. What frightened him especially was how much his body seemed to join the memory. When it came,
he felt himself pulled into the same position he had been in after the explosion, as if his torso
were bracing against the pavement again and his muscles had remembered the exact angle of impact. He
would stiffen or hunch without meaning to, caught for a moment in the posture of that day. Because
the memory was so vivid and detailed, he did not experience it as a faded recollection but as
something close and immediate, and he came to feel that the world outside his apartment could turn
lethal without warning.

Over time, Mika had come to believe that neither he nor anyone else could be relied upon. He was
disturbed by how completely he had lost control of his body and mind during the attack, and
afterward he took that as evidence that he could not trust himself to cope if something happened
again. At the same time, the fact that an ordinary public place had become the site of terrorism
left him certain that other people could not keep him safe either. He spoke as if danger were woven
into everyday life now, not confined to particular places or circumstances. Sidewalks, transit
stops, busy storefronts, and office lobbies all felt exposed.

His attempts to manage this have narrowed his life. He tries hard to control his emotions,
especially around other people, keeping his face composed and cutting off any sign that he is
frightened or overwhelmed. When he feels himself react, he clamps down and goes quiet. He also keeps
asking others to reassure him that he is okay. If a neighbor notices he looks shaken, or if a former
coworker calls, Mika finds himself asking in slightly different ways whether he seems all right,
whether what he is feeling is normal, whether he is “still okay.” The reassurance settles him only
briefly. More and more, he has limited going out unless he feels he has to. For a man whose work
once depended on moving from one job site to another, this has had practical consequences. He has
turned down painting work in larger buildings and busy public-facing spaces because leaving home
feels like stepping back into vulnerability. Small jobs have become difficult to accept if they
require travel through crowded areas, and his income and contact with others have both shrunk.

By the time he sought help, Mika had been living in a tightened, watchful way for months. He spent
much of his time at home with his tools neatly arranged, as if preserving a work life he no longer
moved through freely. What finally brought him in was not a single new symptom but the recognition
that his world had become very small and that he was organizing his days around staying inside,
holding himself together, and asking others to tell him he was safe. He is afraid that the danger he
senses everywhere is simply the truth of the world now, and that if he lets his guard down he will
be caught helpless again. At the same time, he says he wants to be able to take a painting job, walk
out the door, and trust his own mind enough to live like a person rather than like someone waiting
for the next attack.
