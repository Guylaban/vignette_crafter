Martin Weber is a 62-year-old married man who has worked for many years as a bank teller, someone
who had long thought of himself as steady, careful, and dependable. The event that now dominates his
life happened in an ordinary setting and in the presence of people he knew, which is part of what
makes it so difficult for him to bear. Some months before seeking help, Martin was involved in an
accident in which he unintentionally caused serious injury to another person. In his account, what
stands out is not only the moment itself but the awful recognition that his own action had set it in
motion. He remembers the immediate surge of terror as he realized what had happened, and alongside
it a sickening certainty that he should have prevented it. The people connected to the injured
person became fixed in his mind as part of the scene, so that now it is not strangers but related
people, familiar faces linked to the event, who feel especially charged.

In the weeks that followed, Martin found that the event did not settle into the past. Instead, parts
of it broke through abruptly. He could be standing behind the counter at the bank, sorting deposit
slips or counting cash, and a fragment of the scene would flash up without warning—just a piece,
vivid and disconnected, there and then gone again. Those moments did not feel like neutral memories.
When the event came back to him, he felt the same terror he had felt at the time, as if his body did
not recognize that it was over. Just as quickly, the guilt would flood in. He would think, with a
harshness he would never use toward anyone else, that he had done this, that a decent, capable man
would not have let it happen. He had come to believe that the accident revealed something
fundamental and shameful about him: that he was inadequate, that he was a lousy coper, and that
whatever had changed in him since that day was not temporary but permanent damage.

These beliefs now color how he moves through even safe situations. Martin says he cannot relax, even
when he knows on one level that nothing is happening. At home with his wife in the evening, he
remains keyed up in his chair, listening for small shifts in the house, unable to let his shoulders
drop. At night he has difficulty falling asleep because he feels he needs to stay alert. He does not
describe this as sensible, exactly; rather, he says that lying down and surrendering to sleep feels
wrong, as if vigilance is the only thing holding disaster back. He notices every creak, every
movement, every sound from outside. What frightened him most was not just the memory itself, but the
sense that he no longer had the basic trust in himself or the world that used to let him rest.

His work at the bank has become one of the clearest places where this change shows. The teller line
requires constant contact with people, and Martin now braces when someone comes too close to the
counter or touches his arm unexpectedly to get his attention. An unprepared touch makes his whole
body jolt before he can think. On busier days, when customers are impatient and the line is long,
his general stress rises and he becomes even more watchful, scanning faces and movement around him.
If someone connected in any way to the injured person might appear—or even someone who reminds him
of them—he feels his chest tighten. He has become superalert, on guard in a way that leaves him
exhausted by the end of the day. He tries to keep himself composed, but privately he takes these
reactions as further evidence that he is not coping the way he should.

At home, his wife has seen him become harder to reach. He is still present, still dutiful, but less
able to settle into ordinary shared routines. If she touches him unexpectedly from behind in the
kitchen or when he is half-absorbed in thought, he startles sharply, and both of them are left
shaken and embarrassed. He judges himself for that too. In his mind, each reaction confirms the same
bleak conclusions: that he is diminished, that he has been altered for the worse, that other men
would have managed this better.

By the time he comes for help, Martin is tired of living in this state of constant readiness and
self-reproach. What finally brings him in is not a single dramatic crisis but the accumulation of
sleepless nights, the strain of holding himself together at work, and the growing sense that his
marriage is being narrowed by his tension and distance. He says he wants to understand why the
memory still arrives with such force and why he cannot simply put it behind him. At the same time,
he is afraid that treatment may only confirm what he already suspects—that the accident exposed
something permanently broken in him. What he most hopes for, quietly and almost skeptically, is to
feel like a safe man in his own life again.
