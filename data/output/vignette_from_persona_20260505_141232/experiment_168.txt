Anna Müller is a 54-year-old married woman who has worked for years as a chef, someone used to heat,
pressure, and close quarters with other people moving quickly around her. When she first began
describing the assault, it was striking how often she stopped and lost words. She could circle
around parts of it, but she could not seem to tell it as a single story. What came instead were
disconnected pieces: the scrape of a chair, a man stepping too close, the smell of stale alcohol,
the shock of realizing that this was someone she had trusted and that she was not safe with him
after all. She spoke of the event as if it lived more in her body than in language. At moments in
session she would stiffen, her shoulders curling and her arms drawing inward, and say that when it
came back to her she felt herself in the same position again, as though her body had gone back there
before her mind could catch up. What frightened her most was not only that she had been assaulted,
but that it had happened with someone she had believed she knew. She had come away certain that you
can never know who will harm you, and that people cannot be trusted.

In the weeks afterward, Anna found that reminders appeared in places that had once felt ordinary.
Certain kinds of conversation became unbearable, especially when talk turned personal or emotionally
intimate, or when other people spoke in ways that edged toward trust, loyalty, or relationships. She
would feel herself going tight and distant, as if she had to get out of the exchange before
something dangerous happened. Emotional closeness itself began to feel risky. Even with people who
cared about her, there was a sense that if she let them come too near, she would be exposed again.
Being alone with men became especially difficult. She did not describe this as a vague discomfort;
to her it felt like common sense. She was convinced that harm could come from anyone, and that the
worst part was never seeing it clearly until it was too late.

These fears entered her workday in concrete ways. In the kitchen, where staff moved fast behind one
another with knives, pans, and trays, unexpected movement nearby could make Anna jerk violently, her
heart hammering as if she were in immediate danger. If a male coworker stepped beside her without
warning or she found herself alone with a delivery driver in the back corridor, her whole body went
on alert. She began positioning herself so she could see entrances and keep others in view. She
watched strangers closely, even in ordinary situations, feeling threatened if one approached her on
the street, in a store, or near the restaurant. She noticed where exits were, who was standing too
near, whose footsteps were behind her. Hyperalertness had become part of how she moved through the
day.

At home, this narrowing of safety affected her marriage. She was still living with her husband, but
closeness had become complicated. It was not that she no longer cared for him; rather, emotional
nearness itself could set off the same need to pull back. Conversations that might once have brought
comfort now felt dangerous if they came too close to trust, hurt, or dependence. She kept parts of
herself sealed off because she no longer believed that trusting someone protected you. The betrayal
she felt in the assault remained central: she had trusted, and trust had not kept her safe. That
conviction sat alongside her watchfulness, her fear around men, and her recoil from strangers.

By the time she sought help, Anna was exhausted by living as if danger were always one movement
away. She was still working, but with increasing strain, and the kitchen had become a place where
she was perpetually braced. She dreaded those moments when memory arrived not as a narrative she
could recount, but as jagged pieces and bodily states she could not control or explain. Part of what
finally brought her in was the recognition that she could no longer speak about what had happened
without losing the thread entirely, and that more and more of her life was being organized around
keeping distance from conversations, from men, and from closeness itself. She came to treatment
wanting, above all, to feel less constantly endangered. At the same time, she was deeply afraid that
if she let down her guard or trusted anyone enough to tell the story fully, she would once again
discover too late that trust was exactly what put her at risk.
