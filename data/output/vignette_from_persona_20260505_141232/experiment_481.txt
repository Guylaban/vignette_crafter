ordinary commute on a city bus. What had begun as a familiar ride became, in his mind, the moment
the ordinary world revealed itself as exposed and deceptive. He remembers the abrupt eruption of

passengers into possible threats, and the way shouted commands cut through the chaos. It is those
elements that remain most charged for him now: the violence itself, the barked instructions, the
feeling of being trapped on public transport with no control over what would happen next.

In the months afterward, Thomas found that the attack did not stay in the past as a completed event.
He became acutely attentive to signs that danger might be approaching, especially in places that
should have felt safe. At home, he began listening for small sounds that might mean someone was
entering the house. A shift in the floorboards, a noise at the back door, an unfamiliar sound
outside at night would pull his attention immediately. Even in his own living room he felt on guard,
as if safety could not be assumed and had to be actively monitored. He described this not as caution
but as necessity; in his view, the world had shown him what it really was, and relaxing would be
foolish.

His thinking about other people changed as well. Thomas had come to believe that people are not what

has carried a persistent distrust into everyday encounters. He notices himself studying faces, tones

settings he finds himself wondering who might suddenly become violent, who might be concealing
something, who might issue orders that demand instant obedience. He is certain now that the world is
dangerous, and he says this with the conviction of someone who feels he has learned a hard truth

destroyed by the trauma. He speaks of it not dramatically but flatly, as a settled fact that divides
his life into before and after.

leave him bristling and angry before he has fully registered why. He has become more irritable with
colleagues and staff, quicker to snap, less able to absorb the pressures of the day without
reacting. He is aware that his angry outbursts are affecting how others experience him. At home, his

sign of intrusion.

altogether. He tells himself there is no reason to place himself back into that kind of enclosed
vulnerability, and he has reorganized his routines accordingly. The avoidance is practical on the

situations that would require public transport. The result has been more strain, more rigidity, and
a life that feels progressively smaller than the one he had before.

By the time Thomas sought help, the burden of living in this constant state of vigilance had become
harder to disguise. He was troubled by how often he was scanning for danger in his own home and by

from him the ability to feel safe anywhere and that this is permanent. At the same time, he still
hopes there might be some way to live without always listening for the next threat.
