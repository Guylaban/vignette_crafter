Priya Patel was 44 when she came to treatment, a married South Asian American woman who had spent
years working on the sales floor of a large retail store, where she was known as reliable,
agreeable, and calm with difficult customers. The event she described happened at work. One
afternoon, during a busy shift, a woman near the front of the store suddenly collapsed. Priya saw
the fall, heard the sickening sound of the woman hitting the floor, and watched the confusion that
followed as people shouted for help. Someone began crying loudly; another customer froze; an
employee called 911. Priya stayed nearby long enough to see the woman lying motionless and to
register the fear on the faces around her, but afterward what remained was not a tidy narrative. She
could recall fragments—the woman’s body on the floor, the sound of crying, her own lightheadedness,
the rush of people—but when she tried to tell it straight through, the sequence slipped away from
her. She would stop mid-sentence, unsure what had happened first and what had come after.

In the days that followed, Priya found that certain moments reached back into that afternoon with
startling force. If she heard someone cry, especially in a public place, her chest tightened and she
felt unsteady, as if the floor might tilt under her. When she became dizzy or lightheaded for any
reason, even briefly, it frightened her because it felt too close to how her body had felt while she
was standing near the woman on the ground. Even being asked about the incident could undo her.
Coworkers initially spoke about it in the break room in the practical way people do after something
frightening happens, but as soon as Priya tried to join in, she became overwhelmed. The memories
came back not as a clear story but as broken, emotionally loaded pieces, and with them came a wave
of grief so strong she had to stop talking. What struck her was not just fear but sorrow; she found
herself carrying the image of that woman and feeling a heavy, immediate grief whenever the memory
surfaced.

Over the following months, Priya became noticeably more watchful. At work she scanned the entrances,
the aisles, customers’ faces, and the space near the registers, as if danger might declare itself in
some small sign before anyone else noticed. If a cart rattled unexpectedly behind her or a voice
rose suddenly over the store music, she jumped. She began positioning herself where she could see
more of the floor. When a customer started to look unwell, or when there was any commotion, she felt
herself become intensely alert, almost braced. This constant searching for signs of trouble followed
her outside the store as well, in parking lots, grocery lines, and family outings. She was certain
she needed to stay on guard.

Priya also found herself turning the event over in her mind, asking why it had happened to her, why
she had been the one standing there, why that afternoon had lodged itself so deeply when other
people seemed to move on. She did not arrive at satisfying answers, but the questions returned
repeatedly. In her relationships, particularly at work, she became even more inclined to go along
with what others wanted. If a manager asked her to switch stations or stay in a busier area, she
agreed quickly. If a customer was demanding, she gave in where she could. This made sense to her as
a way to keep things calm and avoid any scene that might escalate into distress around her. Over
time, though, it left her more depleted. Her husband noticed that she came home exhausted and
withdrawn, and that if he gently asked what was wrong, she would sometimes start to speak, then
abruptly stop, tearful and overcome.

By the time she sought help, Priya felt her world had narrowed around anticipation and interruption.
She could still do her job, but every shift involved scanning, bracing, and trying not to be caught
off guard. The store, once familiar, had become a place where crying customers, sudden noise, or her
own momentary dizziness could pull her back into the worst fragments of that day. She was distressed
by how little control she had when the memory returned, and by the fact that she still could not put
the event in order despite having thought about it so often. What finally brought her in was a
recent incident at work when a customer began crying near the registers and Priya immediately felt
overwhelmed and lightheaded, needing to step away before she could continue. She said she was afraid
that if this kept happening she would not be able to function at work or talk to her family about
what she had been carrying. More than anything, she hoped for a way to remember what happened
without being overtaken by grief each time it came back.
