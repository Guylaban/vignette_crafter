Amina is a 47-year-old married architect who came to treatment months after an accident in which she
seriously injured another person. She speaks about the event in a halting way, as if the memory both
presses in on her and slips away when she tries to hold it directly. The accident happened at the
end of a long workday, when she was driving home after leaving a construction meeting. She had been
thinking about unfinished drawings and the next morning’s presentation when, in a moment she still
revisits relentlessly, she failed to respond in time and struck a pedestrian in a crosswalk. What
remains with her most sharply is not a coherent narrative she can tell from beginning to end, but
fragments that arrive with force: the sound of the impact, other people shouting, the high, confused
noise in the street, and the taste in her mouth in those first seconds afterward, metallic and sour,
as if panic itself had a flavor. When the memory returns, she hears those sounds again in her head
with alarming clarity. At the same time, if she tries to deliberately sit down and recount exactly
what happened, she often says her mind goes blank around the crucial moments, which she experiences
as both frightening and incriminating.

In the immediate aftermath, Amina became preoccupied with what she had done wrong. She goes over
tiny details repeatedly—the route she chose, the timing of her departure, whether she should have
stayed later at the office, whether another person would have been more alert, more careful, more
deserving of trust. She has come to believe that somebody else would not have gotten into this
situation. This idea has lodged itself deeply enough that she no longer describes the accident as
something terrible that happened, but as evidence about the kind of person she is. When she looks at
herself now, she feels estranged: the woman who once managed complex projects, coordinated teams,
and took pride in precision feels unrecognisable to her. She says that before the accident she knew
how to move through the world with confidence; now she feels like someone diminished and morally
suspect.

Over the following weeks, her days narrowed around habits that made sense to her in the moment, even
as they consumed more of her attention. She began performing mental rituals, silent phrases and
private sequences of thought, especially before driving, before her husband left the house, or
before she sent colleagues to job sites. If she completed them properly, she felt she was less
likely to let another catastrophe happen. If she was interrupted, she started over. Alongside this,
she tried to steady herself by reminding herself it could have been worse, that the person had
survived, that no one died. But even this relief never lasted; it quickly turned back into long
spells of dwelling on what she should have done differently.

Her work as an architect has become one of the places where the accident feels closest. On
construction sites, ordinary noises make her body react before she can think: a dropped metal tool,
a reversing truck beep, a shouted warning across scaffolding. She jumps violently at sounds that
would once have blended into the workday. More than once she has turned abruptly toward a harmless
noise with the certainty that something terrible is about to happen. In design meetings she loses
track of conversations and finds herself staring at plans without taking in the details, unsettled
by how hard it has become to concentrate in a profession that depends on sustained attention. She
now avoids visiting some sites unless absolutely necessary, delegating tasks she once handled
herself. Colleagues have noticed she is less decisive, and this has embarrassed her, reinforcing her
sense that she is no longer the person they knew.

At home, Amina feels separate even from her husband. She does not describe conflict so much as
distance. Around other people she feels marked by what happened, as if she occupies a different
moral category from everyone else. When friends talk casually about traffic, errands, or being tired
after work, she feels herself withdraw. She assumes they could not understand how a single ordinary
drive could divide a life into before and after. What frightens her most is not only that the
sensory fragments still break through—the sounds in her head, the sudden return of that taste—but
that these moments seem to confirm she is still dangerous, still capable of ruining a life through
one lapse.

She sought help after a recent site visit where a sudden clang of steel made her jolt so hard she
dropped her tablet and had to step away, unable to follow the conversation that came next. Sitting
in her car afterward, she found herself unable to deliberately reconstruct the incident at the site,
while the older accident flooded back in sounds and sensations. It left her shaken and ashamed. By
the time she came to treatment, what she most wanted was not simply to feel calmer, but to
understand whether she could live as herself again. What she fears is that this altered, isolated
version of her is permanent, and that if she lets her guard down, another irreversible mistake will
happen.
