Rafael Moreno is a 70-year-old widowed dentist who came to treatment after years of trying to manage
on his own what happened during a terrorist attack. He describes the event haltingly and only in
fragments, because when he gets close to it he becomes so flooded that he has to stop speaking. Even
in early sessions, he would begin, press his lips together, and lift a hand as if to hold the memory
back. He said more than once that he could not really put it into words. What returns to him is not
a story with a beginning and end so much as a total sensory experience that feels as if it is
happening again: the crack of gunfire, the confusion in the room, the presence of armed men, the
bodily certainty that death was near. The most he has been able to convey consistently is that he
was in a public place when the attack began, that there were men in uniform amid the chaos, and that
the sound of gunshots is what still reaches him first, before any thought does.

In the months after the attack, Rafael did what he had always done in his professional life: he
tried to stay controlled, practical, and prepared. He returned to his dental practice, but his days
began to narrow around signs of danger. The first thing he noticed on entering any building,
including his own office, was where the exits were. He would register them before he noticed the
receptionist’s greeting or the patient schedule. In the waiting room he found himself studying
people closely, not out of curiosity but from a felt need to judge whether anyone might be
dangerous. A patient shifting suddenly in his seat, a stranger lingering near the front desk, a
delivery worker in a uniform stepping into the suite could change the whole atmosphere for him in an
instant. He would feel his body tighten before he had fully taken in why.

Specific things came to carry a charge that was immediately understandable to him, even if he hated
living this way. People in uniform were especially difficult. A police officer walking past the
storefront below his office, a security guard in a medical building, even seeing someone dressed in
a style that reminded him of military clothing would pull his attention hard. Conversations about
the military were almost as bad. If patients or colleagues began talking about deployments, weapons,
wars, or “service,” Rafael felt himself leaving the present and bracing internally, as though
something threatening were already unfolding around him. Gunshot noises were the most potent of all.
Fireworks, a car backfiring, a sharp bang from construction near the office could send him into
immediate alarm. He described being on high alert with any loud or unexpected sound, his body
reacting before his mind caught up.

Over time, Rafael built his life around reducing contact with anything that might bring the attack
too close. He stopped watching news coverage about shootings, bombings, or attacks because even a
brief report could leave him shaken for hours. He began carrying a weapon, telling himself that at
his age, living alone after his wife’s death, it was only sensible to be able to protect himself. He
knew this was not how he had lived before, but the weapon gave him a sense that he was not entirely
exposed. Alongside this, another pattern settled in: he repeatedly blamed himself for what had
happened. He would replay scraps of the day and circle around the same questions—whether he should
have noticed something earlier, moved faster, chosen a different place to stand, done more for other
people. These thoughts did not resolve into answers, but he returned to them again and again.

The strain has become visible in the texture of his daily life. At the practice, he schedules fewer
patients and dislikes being in the office when there is unfamiliar foot traffic in the hallway. He
has become more guarded with staff and more distracted when sudden noises break the routine of the
day. Socially he is narrower too, avoiding situations where current events are likely to come up.
What finally brought him to seek help was a recent incident at work when a sharp bang from nearby
construction made the memory return with such force that he had to stop in the middle of the day and
shut himself in his office. He said it was as if the attack had arrived whole, through sound and
sensation, and for several minutes he was no longer certain he was in his dental practice. That
frightened him not only because of how real it felt, but because he could see how much of his life
had come to revolve around staying ready, staying armed, staying watchful. What he hopes for now is
modest but deeply felt: to be able to walk into his own office without first planning escape, to
hear a loud sound without feeling that he is back there, and to speak about what happened without
being overtaken by it.
