David is a 67-year-old widowed military medic who came to treatment after years of telling himself
he should be able to handle what happened on the road. He had spent much of his adult life in
situations where injury, blood, and sudden death were part of the work, and he had built a sense of
himself around staying steady when others could not. The collision that now returns to him did not
happen in combat but on an ordinary drive, which is part of what still unsettles him. He remembers
the violent interruption of a normal day: the abrupt impact, the crushed metal, the smell that
flooded the vehicle, and the terrifying recognition that he might die there. What stands out most
sharply is not a tidy sequence but fragments that arrive with force—the sharp odor of alcohol on the
other driver, the burnt smell after impact, and the sounds inside the wreck, which he still hears in
his head when the memory breaks through.

When he tries to describe the crash in session, David often begins in a clear, matter-of-fact way
and then loses the thread. He will pause, stare past the therapist, and say that it gets slippery at
exactly the point where he tries to put the whole thing together. He knows there was the hit, the
smell, the noise, the fear, but the account does not stay organized long enough for him to finish
it. At times he speaks about the memory as if it happened to someone else, saying it feels oddly
detached from him, as though he were handed another man’s scene to carry around. Yet the sensory
pieces are immediate. If he passes a crash on the road, even at a distance, he does not just think
about his own collision; he is back in contact with the image of smashed cars and with the sounds he
heard that day. The smell of alcohol on someone’s breath can change him instantly. Burnt odors, even
brief ones in everyday life, make his body react before he has time to tell himself where he is.

In the months after the collision, David began shaping his life around not setting these reactions
off. He stopped driving, and he also stopped being a passenger whenever he could avoid it. For a man
used to competence and mobility, this has narrowed his world considerably. Practical tasks now
require planning around rides, postponement, or simply deciding not to go. In vehicles he feels
trapped and alert, bracing at ordinary traffic sounds and jolting at noises that other people barely
register. Those around him have noticed how quickly he jumps. His body reacts hard when he is
reminded of the crash—his heart surges, his muscles tense, and he feels as though danger has arrived
again without warning.

He also began policing his own mind. Because the memory can feel fragmented and unreal, David has
come to watch his thoughts closely for any sign that he is “going crazy.” He pays attention to
whether an image has come into his head, whether a sound in memory has started up again, whether he
is losing his place. At the same time, he tries hard not to think about the crash at all. He cuts
off reminders internally, pushes the scene away, and attempts to shut down anything connected to it.
This leaves him in a constant bind: scanning for signs in his own mind while trying to force the
subject out of awareness.

What weighs heavily on him is the meaning he has given the collision and his response to it. David
speaks about the crash as something that happened because of the way he acted. Even without offering
a full coherent account, he circles back to what he should have done differently and whether a
better man would have prevented it. He has begun to doubt his judgment more broadly, saying he
cannot trust himself to do the right thing. For someone whose identity was built around making
correct decisions under pressure, this has cut deeply. He does not speak about himself with
sympathy; he says instead that his reaction shows he is weak.

He finally sought help after recognizing how small his life had become and how much effort each day
was taking. The immediate push came from the accumulation of ordinary moments he could no longer
manage easily: the sight of accident-damaged cars on the road, the sudden hit of alcohol on someone
nearby, the burnt smell of something overheating, the jolt of everyday sounds that made his whole
body react. He arrived wanting, in his words, to be able to tell what happened without disappearing
in the middle of it. What frightens him most is the possibility that he can no longer trust his own
mind or himself in a crisis. What he hopes for, more quietly, is to recover some sense that the man
who survived that crash is still recognizably him.
