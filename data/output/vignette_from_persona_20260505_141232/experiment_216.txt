Marisol is a 39-year-old detective who came to treatment after years of telling herself that what
happened in her childhood should not still have such force over her. She grew up in a small
apartment where nights could turn suddenly volatile. The scene she returns to most often is not a
full narrative but a set of sharp images: her mother backed against the kitchen counter, her father
looming close, the muscles in his neck slick with sweat, his voice exploding through the room, a
hand reaching out too fast. Even now, what comes back to Marisol is visual and immediate—the shine
on his skin, the angle of her mother’s face, the blur of movement as he grabbed and shoved. When she
tries to remember the event on purpose, to put it in order and explain exactly what happened from
beginning to end, she finds that she cannot get hold of it that way. Instead there are pictures, and
with them a crushing grief that can leave her feeling winded.

As a child she learned to stay very still and read the room quickly. Years later, she is highly
respected in her police work for noticing exits, positioning, and changes in other people’s bodies
before anyone else does, but in her private life the same vigilance feels relentless. In restaurants
she cannot simply sit down; she scans first and chooses the seat that feels most protected, usually
one with a wall behind her and a clear view of the door. If someone moves unexpectedly near her,
especially from the side, her whole body reacts before she can think, as if danger is already upon
her. At night she wakes abruptly and checks her surroundings to make sure she is safe. She knows she
is in her own home with her partner, yet she still sits up, listens, looks toward the bedroom
doorway, and waits for the jolt in her chest to settle.

Certain moments at work can undo her composure with humiliating speed. When a suspect or witness
raises his voice during an interview, she feels the old alarm flood her body. The smell of sweat in
a cramped interrogation room or hallway can be even worse, bringing back those childhood images with
no warning. She does not describe this as “remembering” in an ordinary sense; it feels more like
being seized by fragments she cannot control. Physical touch is also difficult. If a colleague
brushes past her in a narrow space or places a hand on her shoulder without warning, she stiffens or
recoils. Her partner has learned that even affectionate contact can be met with withdrawal. Marisol
avoids being touched whenever she can, and over time this has changed the texture of the
relationship. She still cares deeply for her partner, but she has narrowed the range of closeness
she can tolerate and says she often feels absent even when they are together.

Over the years she has tried to solve the problem by shutting down feeling altogether. She avoids
not only things that might upset her, but also things that might stir any strong feeling at all,
positive or negative. She keeps herself busy, stays flat, leaves gatherings early, and prefers the
structure of work to the unpredictability of emotional intimacy. On the worst nights, when the
images of her mother’s face and the sound of shouting will not leave her alone, she has hurt herself
physically to stop thinking. She describes this without drama, more as something she discovered
“worked” in the moment by cutting through the internal noise.

What troubles Marisol almost as much as the symptoms themselves is what she has come to believe they
say about her. She is frightened that these reactions mean she is going crazy. She also doubts her
own judgment in ways that are painful for someone in her profession; despite years of competent
investigative work, she can become certain she cannot trust herself to do the right thing. Beneath
that is a more private conviction she has rarely said out loud: that what happened in her home had
something to do with the sort of person she is. The belief is not logical, and she knows that, but
it persists with the same emotional certainty as the nighttime fear.

She finally sought help after a recent case involving a loud domestic disturbance. In the apartment,
a man shouted from another room and moved suddenly toward the doorway while she caught the heavy
smell of body odour in the heat. She reacted instantly, more startled than she felt she should have
been, and later at home she could not settle, waking repeatedly to check that the house was secure.
Her partner, who had been asking her for months to talk to someone, told her he was worried not only
about how on edge she seemed but about how small their life had become around what she was trying
not to feel. Marisol came in saying she wanted to understand why childhood scenes she can barely
deliberately recall still arrive with such force. What frightens her most is the idea that she is
losing her mind; what she hopes for, cautiously, is to be able to sleep through the night, sit
beside the person she loves without bracing, and move through her work without every shout or sudden
movement feeling like the beginning of the same night all over again.
