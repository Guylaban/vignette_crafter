Mateo is a 25-year-old data scientist who came to treatment after more than a year of trying to keep
one period of his life sealed off in his mind. He is single and lives alone, and when he first
described the event that still follows him, he did so in fragments, stopping and starting, as if the
pieces would not stay in order long enough to tell straight through. His younger child cousin, whom
he helped care for during a medical crisis, became suddenly and frighteningly ill. Mateo was one of
the adults present during the emergency, and what remains with him is not a smooth story but a set
of searing moments: the child struggling for life, adults crying, the smell of sweat in a crowded
room and later in the ambulance and hospital, and the sound of sirens cutting through everything.
Even now he says that when he tries to recount what happened, he cannot place what came first and
what came after. The memory feels so extreme to him that part of him still reacts as if it could not
have really happened in his own life. Yet when it comes back, it does not feel distant or doubtful
in his body; it brings back the same terror he felt at the time.

In the weeks after the illness, Mateo tried to function by forcing the whole experience out of
awareness. He worked hard not to think about it, cutting off images as soon as they appeared and
shifting his attention back to coding problems, dashboards, or whatever task was in front of him. He
had also come to believe he could not allow himself anger of any kind. He described clamping down on
it immediately, never letting himself feel or express it, even in ordinary situations. If a
colleague interrupted him in a meeting or a stranger behaved rudely on the train, he would go quiet
and rigid rather than acknowledge irritation. He spoke about this as a rule he had for himself, as
though any strong feeling might break something open that he was barely containing already.

Certain reminders entered his life with painful force. Sirens are the clearest. Working as a data
scientist in a downtown office, he cannot control the sounds outside: ambulances passing during the
day, emergency vehicles cutting through traffic below the windows, a sudden burst of noise on his
walk from the train. When he hears one, his body reacts before he can think. He startles sharply,
his chest tightens, and he feels a rush through his whole body that makes it hard to stay present
with what he is doing. At times he has had to step away from his desk and wait for the reaction to
settle. Crying is another potent trigger. If someone tears up during a conversation, or if he hears
crying in a film or in public, he is immediately brought close to the emotional atmosphere of that
day. The smell of sweat or body odour also affects him in a way that embarrasses him; in crowded
elevators, on public transit after work, or in the gym locker room he can suddenly feel pulled back
toward the emergency, his body responding as if danger is near.

He has gradually organized his life around staying away from medical settings. He will take a longer
route to avoid passing the hospital near his apartment. He looks away or crosses the street when he
sees an ambulance parked outside a building. He postpones routine physical examinations because even
the thought of a clinic feels unbearable. These decisions have accumulated quietly. Friends and
relatives have noticed that he makes excuses when someone suggests visiting an ill family member or
meeting near the medical center. He knows this has narrowed his world, but in the moment avoiding
feels like the only way to keep himself steady.

At night Mateo has particular difficulty settling into sleep. He lies in bed feeling that he needs
to remain alert, as if letting go of vigilance would be unsafe. Ordinary noises in his building can
make him jump. A door closing in the hallway, a car horn outside, something dropped by an upstairs
neighbor—sounds that others would dismiss can jolt him fully awake. He often starts the next workday
tired, already strained before opening his laptop. He is frustrated by how much energy goes into
monitoring his surroundings and trying not to let his mind drift toward the illness and emergency.

What finally brought him to seek help was a recent incident at work: an ambulance siren passed
during a team meeting just as a coworker became tearful over a personal matter. Mateo felt an
immediate, intense bodily surge and had to leave the room abruptly. He was shaken not only by the
reaction itself but by how unreal and disorganized the memory still felt afterward, as though he
could neither fully remember it nor fully escape it. Coming to therapy, he says he wants to be able
to hear a siren or walk past a clinic without feeling overtaken. What frightens him most is the
sense that the terror is still waiting just under the surface, ready to return in full whenever
something in the world resembles that day.
