Linh Nguyen is a 48-year-old Vietnamese American woman who has worked for years as a waitress in a
neighborhood restaurant, the kind of place where regulars expect to be recognized and where being
warm with people is part of the job. She is divorced and lives alone. When she speaks about the
months that changed her life, she does so haltingly, circling the facts rather than telling them
straight through. She says the whole thing is hard to put into words, as if what happened sits in
her mind more as flashes and bodily shock than as a story she can narrate from beginning to end. At
times she describes the memory as feeling strangely separate from her, almost like it happened to
another woman and she is only watching pieces of it from a distance.

The stalking began after a man started coming into the restaurant repeatedly. At first he was only
overly attentive, lingering after paying, trying to make conversation that went beyond ordinary
friendliness. Over time he began appearing outside the restaurant as well, then near her apartment,
making it impossible for Linh to dismiss the pattern as coincidence. The terror for her was in the
repeated crossing of boundaries: finishing a shift and seeing him again when she stepped outside,
turning toward home and feeling that he knew where she lived, hearing movement near her door at
night and wondering if he had followed her there. What made it frightening was not one dramatic
assault but the steady sense that he could enter any part of her life and that nowhere stayed safely
separate from him for long.

In the period that followed, her world became organized around scanning for danger. At home she
began listening constantly for sounds that might mean someone was entering: a footstep in the
hallway, a scrape near the door, a knock from another apartment that made her whole body tense. She
describes a jolt that starts in her chest and spreads through her arms whenever something reminds
her of that time. Even now, an unexpected sound can bring on a surge of physical alarm before she
has time to think. She avoids unfamiliar places because not knowing the exits, the people, or the
layout feels intolerable; if she cannot quickly judge whether a place is safe, she would rather not
go at all. Her life has narrowed accordingly, confined to routes and settings she knows well enough
to monitor.

The memory itself arrives without permission. In the middle of work, carrying plates past the front
windows, she can suddenly be hit by an image of looking over her shoulder after a shift or by the
bodily feeling of knowing she was being watched. She does not experience these moments as orderly
recollections. They intrude abruptly, out of context, and leave her shaken. When asked to describe
the stalking in detail, she falters and says she cannot explain it properly. What remains with her
is not a coherent account but fragments: the dread of stepping outside, the certainty that he might
be there, the feeling of being exposed.

Linh has also changed the way she moves through her own body. She avoids looking at herself and
avoids paying attention to her body more generally. It is as if noticing herself too directly brings
her too close to what happened, to the sense of being visible and vulnerable. She has withdrawn from
people she used to socialize with before the stalking. Former coworkers and acquaintances have
invited her out, but seeing them feels unbearable, tied to a version of herself she no longer
recognizes. At the restaurant she keeps interactions functional and brief. She is especially careful
to avoid any situation that might involve disagreement or confrontation. If a customer is rude, if a
coworker speaks sharply, she backs down immediately, wanting the interaction over before it can
escalate into something she cannot control. This has had consequences at work; others sometimes read
her as overly passive or detached, and she has become more isolated in a setting that once gave her
a sense of connection.

What weighs on her most heavily now is not only fear but the conviction that something essential in
her has gone missing. She says she used to be a happy person and now feels miserable all the time.
Activities that once mattered no longer seem to carry meaning. She talks about feeling dead inside,
not dramatically but flatly, as if she is reporting a fact. The divorce and solitary routine have
made this emptiness feel even more stark. She sought help only after realizing that her life had
become reduced to work, vigilance, and getting back behind a locked door. What frightens her most is
the idea that this is permanent—that she will remain cut off from other people, from pleasure, and
from herself. What she hopes for, though she says it tentatively, is to feel like her life belongs
to her again.
