Miguel Alvarez is a 65-year-old special education teacher who came to treatment after years of
trying to keep his distress managed by controlling his surroundings and narrowing his life. He is
divorced and lives alone, and he described himself as someone who has always prided himself on being
dependable with children who need steadiness. In session, he spoke carefully about his military
trauma, because even talking about it can make his body feel unsteady, as if the room shifts under
him.

During his military service, Miguel was in a combat situation in which the danger became immediate
and disorienting all at once. What has stayed with him most vividly is a moment of confusion after a
nearby blast, when he became dizzy and lightheaded and had to orient himself quickly while trying to
determine where the threat was coming from and how to get out. In the middle of that chaos there was
also a distinct taste in his mouth that he still remembers with disturbing clarity, something that
fused with the fear of not knowing if another attack was coming. He remembers needing to locate an
exit path instantly, scanning for cover, listening for the next sound that might signal danger. Even
now, when he tells the story, he does not describe it as a memory that sits in the past; he braces
himself as if he may need to regain his balance and find a way out again.

In the months and years afterward, Miguel noticed that certain experiences pulled him abruptly back
into that state. If he felt dizzy or lightheaded for any reason, even briefly, he was immediately on
edge. The sensation itself frightened him, not just because it was unpleasant but because it felt
too close to that moment of vulnerability and disorientation. A specific taste could do the same
thing. He found it hard to explain to other people why something so ordinary could change his whole
body, but to him it made perfect sense: that taste belonged to the worst moments of his service, and
when it appeared unexpectedly, his mind and body reacted as though danger might be present again.
Talking about the event also became something he tried to avoid. Once conversation moved in that
direction, he would feel himself tightening, monitoring his body, alert to every sound in the room.

Over time, Miguel organized more and more of his life around preventing that feeling of being
trapped or caught off guard. Whenever he entered a classroom, restaurant, church hall, or waiting
room, he first identified the exits before he could settle at all. He preferred seats near the door
and became visibly uneasy if someone suggested sitting farther inside a room. He scanned
constantly—doorways, windows, people’s movements, anything unusual in the environment. Unexpected or
loud sounds were especially difficult. A slammed locker at school, a dropped metal water bottle in
the hallway, or the sudden shriek of the fire alarm during a drill could send his attention snapping
outward immediately. He described himself as “always checking,” as if relaxing even briefly would be
irresponsible.

His work as a special education teacher has made these habits especially visible. Miguel cares
deeply about his students, but his classroom routine has become shaped by where he can stand and how
quickly he could leave if needed. He stays near the classroom door more than he used to, and when
there is a school assembly or staff meeting he arrives early so he can choose a seat with a clear
exit path. If a situation seems likely to stir strong emotion—a contentious meeting with parents, a
school ceremony with loud sound equipment, even a conversation with a colleague who asks directly
about his military service—he finds ways to limit his exposure or leave quickly. He does not think
of this as avoidance so much as common sense; he is certain that if he lets himself get overwhelmed,
he will lose the control he has worked hard to maintain.

These patterns have narrowed his life outside work as well. Friends have noticed that he is
particular about where he sits and that he sometimes declines invitations without much explanation.
His divorce is not something he linked directly in a simple way to his symptoms, but he acknowledged
that living with him had meant living with his alertness, his need to monitor spaces, and his
reluctance to enter emotionally charged conversations. What frightened him most was not only the
return of old feelings, but the possibility of being somewhere he could not easily leave.

Miguel sought help now after recognizing how exhausting it has become to remain on guard all the
time. A recent episode of lightheadedness at school, followed by a loud noise in the hallway, left
him shaken enough that he had to pause before returning to class. He was embarrassed by how much
effort it took to appear composed in front of his students. He says he wants to keep teaching and
does not want his world to get any smaller. At the same time, he is afraid that talking in detail
about what happened will bring back the same bodily fear he has spent decades trying to outrun.
