Thomas Weber is a 28-year-old financial analyst who came to treatment more than a year after his
young son’s life-threatening illness, saying that life had split into “before and after” and that he
no longer recognized himself in the after. The medical crisis began during a stretch of cold, gray
weather, with hard rain hitting the hospital windows for days. His son had become critically unwell
and deteriorated quickly enough that Thomas experienced those hours as a sequence of searing images
rather than a coherent story: his child in a hospital bed, the color of his skin under fluorescent
lights, monitors and tubing, staff moving quickly, his wife’s face, and his own sense that disaster
was already underway before he could do anything to stop it. Even now, when he gets close to those
memories, they arrive with photographic sharpness and then abruptly cut off because he pushes them
away before he can see the whole scene through. He says he knows exactly what certain moments looked
like, down to small details, but cannot bear to stay with them long enough for them to feel
finished.

In the months after his son survived and returned home, Thomas expected relief. Instead he found
that ordinary parts of daily life had become charged. News stories about children’s illnesses or
medical emergencies could seize his attention instantly. If a headline appeared on his phone while
he was at his desk reviewing spreadsheets, he felt his body react before he had fully read it, as if
something catastrophic were again unfolding nearby. He began avoiding news when he could, but
financial work requires constant monitoring of information, and he could not completely control what
crossed his screen. Being around children also became unexpectedly difficult. At family gatherings,
in grocery stores, or simply passing a playground, he felt a jolt in his chest and a tightening in
his body. It was not that he disliked children; what frightened him was how exposed and fragile they
seemed. Each child could suddenly become, in his mind, a reminder of how close he had come to losing
his own.

Weather became another point of dread. On cold rainy days he noticed himself bracing from the moment
he woke. The dim light, the pressure of clouds, the sound of rain on windows made it feel as though
time had folded back on itself. Those days left him restless at the office and on edge at home, with
the sense that something terrible might happen again simply because the world looked the same as it
had then.

Thomas described reacting repeatedly as if danger were present even when he knew, logically, that
nothing was wrong. In restaurants he scanned for exits before he could settle into conversation. In
office conference rooms he took the seat that gave him the clearest path to the door. If his wife
suggested a movie theater or a crowded children’s birthday party, he found himself calculating how
quickly they could get out if they needed to. At night he struggled to fall asleep because some part
of him felt he needed to remain alert. Lying in bed, he listened for sounds from his son’s room and
for shifts in the house, unable to give himself fully to sleep.

What has distressed him most is the meaning he has drawn about himself. He has come to believe that
when his son needed protection most, he failed. Although he can describe the illness as sudden and
severe, and although others have reminded him that he sought help and stayed present through the
crisis, this has not altered his conviction that a father should have been able to prevent or stop
it. He speaks about himself now as if he were looking at a stranger: more watchful, more brittle,
less spontaneous. He says he used to be steady and practical, someone who could make decisions
cleanly. Now he feels organized at work only by effort, and at home he often feels as though he is
performing the role of himself rather than inhabiting it. “I don’t know who I am anymore,” he told
the therapist, and he said it with equal parts confusion and shame.

His marriage has been affected in concrete ways. His wife has noticed that outings are planned
around where the exits are and whether the weather feels “wrong.” He is physically present with his
family but often mentally scanning, checking, bracing. At work he remains competent, but colleagues
have commented that he seems tense and distracted, especially on days when child-related news is
circulating or when storms roll in.

Thomas finally sought help after a rainy week when a news alert about a child in critical condition
appeared on his phone during the workday. He felt the familiar surge of alarm, left his desk to
compose himself, and later that evening found he could not settle even after checking repeatedly on
his son. What frightened him most was not only the return of the images, but the fear that this
guarded, unrecognizable version of himself was becoming permanent. What he hopes for now is not to
forget what happened to his son, but to be able to remember it without feeling pulled back into the
same moment, and to feel like a husband and father who is present rather than perpetually on watch.
