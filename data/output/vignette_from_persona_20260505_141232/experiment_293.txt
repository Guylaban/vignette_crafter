At 73, Meera Shah had spent decades making other people’s homes feel ordered and beautiful. As an
interior designer, she was still taking selected projects after being widowed, often meeting clients
in apartment lobbies, condominium offices, and newly renovated homes where she would run fabrics
through her fingers and talk easily about light, texture, and proportion. The event that divided her
life into before and after happened on an ordinary workday outside one of those buildings. She had
arrived carrying sample books and upholstery swatches when police officers moved quickly into the
entrance area during some disturbance nearby. What Meera remembers most clearly is not a full
sequence but striking, disconnected moments: the blue of uniforms filling her vision, a command
barked in a tone she still cannot repeat, the feel of a rough synthetic surface scraping hard
against her cheek and hands as she was forced down, her sample case falling open, fabric books
splaying across the pavement. Later, when people asked what exactly happened, she would stop after a
few words. She knew something terrible had occurred and knew she had been handled violently by
police, but the event lived in her mind as broken pieces with blank spaces between them. There were
parts she simply could not reach, no matter how hard she tried. At times she would begin, “They
pushed me and then—” and be unable to find language for what came next.

In the weeks that followed, her daily world became unexpectedly dangerous in ways other people could
not always see. Police cars parked outside a grocery store, officers standing in the lobby of a
residential tower, even a military-style uniform on television could make her body react before she
had any clear thought. She would feel herself stiffen, her breathing turn shallow, her chest
tighten. This frightened her deeply because she had come to believe her body was no longer
trustworthy; it kept behaving as though the assault were happening again even when she was in a
familiar, objectively safe place. Texture, once central to her profession and one of the pleasures
of her work, also became treacherous. Certain rough upholstery fabrics and stiff synthetic materials
pulled her back into the worst sensory fragment she retained from the incident. During client
meetings she sometimes recoiled from a sample without meaning to, then covered awkwardly by saying
the fabric was poor quality. She understood that the reaction did not make rational sense in the
present, but that did little to soften it.

Because so much of her work depended on walking into buildings with security staff, doormen, and
sometimes officers nearby, Meera began changing how she practiced. She asked younger colleagues to
handle site visits in downtown developments where police presence was more common. She shifted to
remote consultations when she could, and when she could not, she arrived late, scanned entrances
before stepping out of her car, and left quickly if she saw a uniform. Clients noticed she had
become harder to schedule. One long-term client told her she seemed distracted and unlike herself.
Meera heard this as confirmation of something she already feared: that she could no longer manage
even minor upsets. If a delivery was delayed or a contractor challenged one of her choices, she felt
immediately overwhelmed, thinking, I can’t deal with this, not even this. Problems she once handled
briskly now seemed to prove that she was fragile and finished.

Over months, her world narrowed. She avoided civic events, busy public buildings, and routes where
she might pass police stationed on corners. She stopped attending a neighborhood cultural fair she
had loved for years after seeing officers there one afternoon and having to leave abruptly. At home,
surrounded by the carefully chosen textiles and colors that had once comforted her, she still felt
little expectation that life could improve. Since her husband’s death she had already learned to
live with loneliness, but after the assault a darker conviction took hold: nothing good was ahead
for her now. She did not say this dramatically; she said it with the flat certainty of someone who
believed she had already seen how quickly dignity and safety could vanish.

What finally brought Meera to seek help was an incident at a client presentation. Reaching into a
bag for material samples, she touched a coarse fabric that matched too closely the texture she
associated with the ground and restraint from that day. She froze in the middle of her sentence,
staring at the swatch while her client waited. Then she noticed, through the conference room glass,
two police officers speaking in the lobby. She left the meeting abruptly, sat in her car shaking,
and could not drive for nearly half an hour. Later, embarrassed and exhausted, she called her
daughter and for the first time said aloud that she was not getting better by simply staying away
from things. Coming to treatment, Meera is most afraid that her body will always live in that moment
and that the missing parts of her memory mean she will never be able to make sense of what happened.
At the same time, she is hoping, quietly and carefully, that she might be able to walk into a
building again, touch a fabric sample again, and remain in the present.
