Daniel is a 79-year-old African American man who came to treatment after years of living around
something he could never fully put into words. He is divorced and still works in a warehouse, a job
he has held onto well past the age when most men around him have retired. He describes himself as
someone who has always needed noise, movement, and other people nearby. Being left alone with his
thoughts has never felt restful to him. It feels dangerous.

When Daniel was a boy, he witnessed severe violence between the adults in his home. He does not tell
the story in a smooth sequence. When he tries, he stalls, goes quiet, and says it is like reaching
into a dark space and coming back with only scraps. He can tell there was drinking. He remembers the
sharp, stale smell of alcohol in the room before anything happened, and he remembers a door being
thrown open hard enough to hit a wall, then later a slam that seemed to shake the whole house. He
knows there was fear in the air and that he was small and trapped in it, listening for what would
happen next. But when he tries to deliberately recall the full scene, the images do not come in
order. Instead there are disconnected fragments: the sound of the door, a bottle smell on someone’s
breath, a burst of shouting, a sense of standing still and waiting. At other times there is no
picture at all, only a sudden wave of dread and helplessness so strong that he feels he is back
inside the same terror without knowing exactly what he is remembering.

Those reactions have followed him across decades and into ordinary moments of life. At work in the
warehouse, he can be loading inventory or checking a shipment when a bay door bangs shut and his
body reacts before he has had time to think. He becomes tense, alert, and unsettled, his attention
pulled away from the task in front of him. If a coworker comes in from lunch smelling of beer, or if
he passes someone on the street with alcohol on their breath before his shift, he feels a jolt of
alarm he cannot easily explain. What bothers him is not only the reaction itself but the fact that
it seems to come from somewhere he cannot fully access. He has come to believe that if he lets
himself slow down enough, whatever sits behind those feelings will overtake him.

The same is true in outdoor places. A patch of trees, an overgrown lot, even a quiet natural area
can unsettle him if it resembles the environment around the home where he was a child. He does not
always have a clear image of what it reminds him of, but the atmosphere feels too close. He becomes
uneasy, scanning, wanting to leave. He says that certain places “feel wrong” immediately, and that
is enough for him to avoid them.

Over the years Daniel has developed ways of staying ahead of these states. He drinks, sometimes
using alcohol and sometimes medication, to flatten the edge of what comes up. He also keeps himself
around people as much as possible. After work he lingers, talks, finds somewhere to go, calls
someone, sits where there is conversation. Solitude is when the emotional force of the memory is
most likely to hit him, and he does not trust what might happen if he is left with it. There are
also times when he picks arguments, especially when he feels cornered internally. In those moments
conflict feels easier to manage than the nameless panic that can rise in him. He would rather be
angry at somebody than feel that old terror with no clear source.

These patterns have shaped his life in cumulative ways. His marriage did not survive years of
drinking, restlessness, and needless conflict. Even now, coworkers know him as social and talkative,
but also as a man who can become sharp or combative with little warning. He knows this has cost him
closeness. Still, from his point of view, the alternatives feel worse: quiet, memory, and the sense
that something unbearable is waiting just out of reach.

What finally brought Daniel in at 79 was the recognition that he is tired. He recently had a moment
at work when a loading door slammed behind him and he felt the old surge so strongly that he had to
stop what he was doing and grip a shelf until it passed. Later that week, at a family gathering, the
smell of alcohol on a relative sent the same feeling through him, and he found himself starting an
argument for no reason he could defend. He does not come asking for a perfect account of the past.
What he wants, more modestly and more painfully, is to stop feeling hijacked by pieces of something
he cannot even clearly remember. What frightens him most is that he will spend the rest of his life
arranging himself around those moments. What he hopes is that he might finally be able to sit in a
quiet room by himself without feeling that he is in danger.
