Daniel Weber was 35 when he was mugged on his way home from school, where he worked as a special
education teacher. It was early evening after a long day, and he had stayed late finishing paperwork
and resetting materials for the next morning. On the walk to his car, he cut through a familiar
stretch of sidewalk near the edge of the school grounds. He remembered hearing shouting before he
fully understood it was directed at him. When he turned, two men were closing in. One demanded his
wallet and phone, loudly, aggressively, in a way that Daniel still described in clipped, visual
fragments rather than as a flowing story. What stayed with him was “like photographs”: the angle of
one man’s face, the open mouth mid-yell, the flash of a hand grabbing at his jacket, the pavement
rushing up as he was shoved. His neck twisted sharply when he hit the ground. He could recall the
pressure there as distinctly as the images. He remembered freezing, fumbling, feeling slow. Even
afterward, what burned in him was not only fear but rage—rage at being cornered, at being yelled at,
at how exposed he had been—and, alongside it, a hard guilt that settled in almost immediately. He
had come to believe somebody else would not have gotten into that situation, would have read the
danger sooner, moved faster, done something more.

In the weeks after the robbery, Daniel returned to work, but the school no longer felt
straightforwardly familiar. As soon as he entered any room or building, he found himself locating
the exits before he could pay attention to anything else. In staff meetings he automatically chose
seats near the door. In classrooms he noted where students, aides, and he himself could move if
something went wrong. He did this even when he knew, logically, that he was safe. He was unable to
relax in ordinary settings that had once been routine. During fire drills, raised voices in the
hall, or moments when one student escalated and shouting broke out nearby, his body reacted before
he could think. Sometimes he suddenly felt as if the robbery were happening again: the same jolt in
his chest, the same narrowed attention, the sense that he was back on the pavement with someone
yelling over him. At those moments he was not merely remembering; he was gripped by the immediacy of
it.

The neck pain became its own kind of reminder. A stiffness at the base of his neck when he turned
too quickly, an ache after a tense day, even the ordinary soreness that came from sleeping badly
could pull him back to the moment of impact. He would feel the pain and instantly see one of those
mental snapshots, vivid and still, followed by a rush of anger. He felt intense rage when the memory
came, but the anger never stayed cleanly directed outward. It folded back into self-accusation. He
was certain he had failed some basic test of competence. He compared himself harshly to an imagined
other man who would have avoided the confrontation, taken another route, noticed the threat in time.
The conclusion he kept reaching was that he was inadequate.

Police presence became difficult in a different way. When officers were visible around the school
after neighborhood incidents or parked near a community event, Daniel stiffened. The sight of a
uniform or patrol car pulled the robbery to mind with the same photographic quality, and with it
came the whole charged mixture of helplessness, fury, and shame. He did not describe police as
dangerous, but their presence marked the world as a place where something violent had already
happened or could happen again. He felt he had to be on guard all the time.

Over months, these habits tightened around his daily life. He avoided lingering alone outside the
school after dark and hurried colleagues along if they were chatting in the parking lot. He became
less spontaneous socially and turned down invitations if they involved unfamiliar venues, partly
because he wanted to know the layout before arriving. At work, where raised voices were not
uncommon, he sometimes lost the thread of what a student or coworker was saying because he was
tracking tone, volume, and doorways instead. He knew this made him seem distracted. For a man who
took pride in being steady with vulnerable students, that stung. It fed the same private verdict:
that he was not handling things the way he should.

By the time he sought help, Daniel was exhausted by the constant watchfulness. What finally pushed
him to call was a recent incident at school when shouting in the hallway made him spin toward the
door and brace as if he were back in the attack. He was shaken by how quickly it happened and by how
visible it felt in front of others. Sitting in therapy, he spoke carefully, often in images rather
than sequences, and rubbed the back of his neck while describing the robbery. What frightened him
most was the sense that even safe places no longer felt safe in his body. What he hoped for was not
to forget what happened, but to walk into his school, hear ordinary noise, see a police car outside,
or feel a twinge in his neck without being thrown back into those frozen pictures of that evening.
