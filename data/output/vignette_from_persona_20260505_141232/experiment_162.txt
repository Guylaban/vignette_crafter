Eleanor is a 76-year-old radiologist who has spent most of her adult life reading images with
disciplined attention, trusting herself to find danger in shadows that other people miss. She is
married, American-born, and had expected the later years of her career to feel orderly and earned.
Instead, the event that now occupies her in fragments took place while she was in a civilian medical
setting in a war zone, where she had gone during a period of conflict to help interpret imaging for
overwhelmed local teams. She remembers the hospital before the attack as overcrowded and strained,
hallways lined with stretchers, staff moving quickly, everyone listening for sounds outside the
building even while trying to keep working. Then there was a moment when the war entered the room
completely: the concussive force, the sense that the structure itself had changed, the rush of
people, the confusion about where the next danger would come from. What she can convey of it comes
in flashes rather than a story. She has said more than once that she cannot seem to put the
frightening event into words, and when she tries deliberately to recall what happened, her mind
seems to go blank around the very parts she feels she should be able to explain.

What remains most accessible is not a sequence but an atmosphere of exposure and humiliation. On
recall she feels an intense shame that she struggles to justify out loud. She does not offer a
polished account of what happened in those minutes; instead she stops, looks away, and says she
should have been more useful, more composed, less frightened. In her mind the event is tied not only
to danger but to a painful sense of herself under that danger. The memory does not come when
invited, yet ordinary moments can make her body act as though the threat is close by.

After returning home to the United States, Eleanor resumed life with her husband and went back to
the radiology department, but daily life narrowed in ways that were at first easy to explain and
then increasingly hard to ignore. In the hospital she became vigilant in a manner that exceeded her
former professional habit of carefulness. She found herself constantly scanning her surroundings for
signs of danger: noting who entered the waiting room, where the exits were, whether a voice in the
corridor sounded agitated, whether someone was moving too quickly toward her. When unfamiliar
technicians, patients’ family members, or delivery workers approached her in ordinary situations,
she felt threatened before she had any reason to be. A stranger coming too close in an elevator or
at the hospital entrance could make her whole body brace. She preferred routes through the building
that she already knew, and if meetings were moved to an unfamiliar office or conference area, she
would often find a reason not to go. Unfamiliar places felt intolerable precisely because she could
not assess the danger there.

At home, the constriction was different but just as marked. Eleanor began avoiding not only distress
but feeling itself. She turned away from anything that might stir her emotionally, whether negative
or positive. If her husband suggested seeing friends, celebrating a birthday, planning a trip, or
even discussing next season’s holidays, she would shut the conversation down. Making plans for the
future felt impossible, as though looking ahead required a confidence she no longer possessed. Long
stretches of the day disappeared into her phone or the internet, not out of pleasure but because the
scrolling and games gave her somewhere to put her attention other than her own mind. Her husband
noticed that evenings that had once involved conversation or shared routines were now spent with
Eleanor silent beside him, eyes fixed on a screen.

Over the months, this altered the shape of her marriage and work. Her husband learned not to ask too
much about what happened overseas because she would go still or leave the room. Colleagues saw her
continue to perform her readings with precision, yet she declined invitations, avoided new settings,
and seemed tense when unfamiliar people came near her workstation. What frightened her most was not
only the possibility of danger in the present, but the sense that the event remained both
unspeakable and morally exposing. She had come to believe that if she let herself feel too
much—grief, fear, even pleasure—something unbearable would break through.

She is seeking help now because the life she and her husband had expected to share has become

plans for the future and she realized she could not tolerate the conversation, then spent the rest
of the night on her phone to avoid thinking. She says she wants some way to live in her own home and
workplace without constantly checking for danger, and she hopes to be able to speak about the war
zone in words rather than fragments. At the same time, she is deeply afraid that if she gets any
closer to the memory, the shame she feels around it will become overwhelming.
