Miguel Alvarez, a 62-year-old surgeon, came to treatment after more than a year of telling himself
that what happened on the highway should have stayed on the highway. He was driving home after a
long hospital shift when a car crossed into his lane and struck his vehicle hard enough to spin it.
He remembered the instant in fragments that felt sharper than the rest of his life: the violent jolt
in his chest and shoulders, the crushing sound of metal folding, the sideways glimpse of another car
caved in from the impact. Most of all, he remembered the feel of the seat belt strap and the torn
upholstery pressing against his neck and forearm as the car settled, those textures fixed in his
mind with a physical immediacy he could still summon. In the aftermath, related people gathered at
the scene and later around his hospital bed and at home, asking for details, touching his shoulder,
telling him how lucky he was. Miguel noticed that even seeing those familiar faces could bring back
the collision with disturbing force, as if their presence belonged to the event itself.

In the weeks that followed, the memory did not become past tense in the way he expected. He could be
scrubbing in for surgery, standing in the surgeons’ lounge, or sitting at home with his wife, and
the accident would force itself into his mind without warning. It was not like ordinary remembering.
He described it as more real than normal memory, so vivid that for a moment the crash seemed to
overtake the present. He would suddenly see again the image of damaged cars, hear the impact, and
feel the texture of the strap and upholstery on his skin. At times he began to recall the sequence
of the accident only to cut it off before it was complete, almost reflexively pushing the image away
when he sensed the next piece coming. He knew he was interrupting it, but he felt he had to;
allowing the memory to unfold all the way seemed intolerable.

Driving through the city soon became full of moments he dreaded. If he passed even a minor fender
bender, or saw a car on the roadside with a crumpled hood, his body tightened and his attention
narrowed. On his commute to the hospital he started rerouting to avoid major intersections and
highways where he might come across wreckage. When colleagues or family members who had been present
after the accident asked how he was doing, he often changed the subject quickly. There was something
about related people—the ones connected to that period, the ones who had seen him immediately
afterward—that made him feel cornered by the memory. He was not only avoiding conversations; he was
protecting himself from the sudden, almost physical return of the crash.

Miguel also began stepping away from things he had once enjoyed. Before the accident he had been
active, taking long walks with his wife and playing recreational tennis with friends from the
hospital. Over time he gave these up. He told himself he was tired, or too busy, but what he could
acknowledge in therapy was that anything that put his body under stress felt dangerous and
unsettling. An elevated heart rate, tight muscles, heavy breathing—sensations that once belonged to
exercise—now felt too close to the bodily intensity of the collision. He narrowed his world
accordingly. His wife noticed that weekends became quieter and smaller. They stayed home more.
Invitations were declined. He spent more evenings sitting in silence, not wanting to think, not
wanting to look directly at what had happened.

At work, his functioning remained outwardly disciplined, but the strain showed in subtle ways.
Walking through the hospital parking structure, he found himself scanning automatically for damaged
vehicles. A dented bumper or shattered glass in a tow-away area could pull him abruptly back into
the crash image. On days when a memory broke through before a procedure, he tried to shut it down
immediately and immerse himself in technical tasks. He remained highly competent, yet more contained
with coworkers, less willing to linger after cases, and less available socially than he had once
been.

What finally brought Miguel to seek help was not one dramatic collapse but the accumulation of these
constrictions. His wife told him she felt he was present in the house but absent from their life. He
recognized that he had organized himself around not thinking, not feeling strain in his body, and
not encountering reminders he could not control. He came in worried that the memories would keep
arriving with the same force forever, and that more of his life would be given over to managing
them. At the same time, he hoped for something simple and deeply important: to see a crashed car
without feeling transported, to be with the people who cared about him without bracing, and to
recover some part of the ordinary enjoyment he felt he had surrendered since the accident.
