Andreas Müller was in his late teens when war reached his town in Europe. He had grown up with
routines that felt solid and ordinary, and then ordinary life was replaced by checkpoints, armed
men, shattered windows, and the constant knowledge that any movement through the streets could
become dangerous without warning. The moment that returns to him most often is from a day when he
was traveling in a vehicle through an area that had already seen shelling and gunfire. What remains
in his mind is not a neat story but a sequence of stark images: the road ahead, the sudden sense of
exposure inside the vehicle, faces turned toward danger, the violent certainty that they might not
make it through. Even now, decades later, he describes these memories as pictures more than words.
At times he can speak about what happened in a flat, detached way, almost as if he is describing
something that occurred to someone else; at other times the images come with such force that he
feels he is back there again, in immediate peril rather than in the present.

In the years after the war, Andreas built a life in the United States. He married, worked, and tried
to become the kind of man who kept going without dwelling on the past. For a long time he believed
that was what strength required. Still, he never fully stopped scanning the world around him. He had
come to believe, not abstractly but as a basic fact of existence, that the world is dangerous and
that safety can vanish in a second. He watches other people closely, especially in public places,
studying their movements, their hands, their tone of voice. He does not think of this as excessive.
To him it is simple common sense, the vigilance a person needs if he wants to survive among people
he cannot trust. Over time he also found that his faith had thinned out and then largely
disappeared. He no longer felt able to rely on other people, on life being meaningful, or on any
higher protection. He could not reconcile what he had lived through with the idea that the world was
ordered or benevolent.

Now 76 and widowed, Andreas works as a cashier. His days are spent in a brightly lit store where he
has to face a steady line of strangers. He notices everything: who seems irritated, who is carrying
themselves in a tense way, who lingers too long, who might be unpredictable. He positions his
attention so that he can keep track of the store entrance and the people nearest his register. When
a customer speaks sharply or moves suddenly, his body reacts before his mind catches up. He does not
describe this as panic so much as instant readiness. What unsettles him most is how quickly the
present can give way to the past. A glimpse of a driver through the front windows of the store, the
sight of people packed into a car outside, even the prospect of getting into a vehicle himself can
bring back those wartime images with vivid immediacy.

For years he has avoided driving whenever he can, and he is deeply uncomfortable even as a
passenger. Being inside a vehicle leaves him feeling trapped and exposed, as though danger could
strike from outside at any moment and leave him with nowhere to go. This has narrowed his world
considerably. He arranges his life around rides, short distances, and excuses. The limitation has
been especially painful because he experiences it as evidence that he is no longer capable of doing
the things that once defined him. He does not say this dramatically; he says it with quiet
bitterness, as a man who remembers being competent and self-reliant and now feels diminished by what
he cannot make himself do.

At home, nights are often the hardest. He has trouble falling asleep because some part of him
insists he needs to stay alert. Lying down feels like surrendering awareness. He listens for sounds,
tracks small disturbances, and remains keyed up long after he wants rest. He also catches himself
thinking over and over about why this happened to him, why he survived what he survived, and what,
if anything, it means. These thoughts do not bring resolution. They circle. At the same time, he has
learned to push away moments of warmth or relief because feeling good can seem unsafe, almost
foolish, as if letting down his guard would expose him to something terrible.

What finally brought Andreas to treatment was not one single crisis but the accumulation of a
smaller life. After his wife’s death, there was less to buffer him from long evenings, poor sleep,
and his own thoughts. At work he could still function, but it took more out of him to remain so
watchful all day. He began to see how much of his life was organized around danger, mistrust, and
avoiding vehicles, and how little room that left for anything else. He is seeking help cautiously.
What frightens him most is the idea of being overtaken by the past or becoming even less capable
than he already feels. What he hopes, though he says it carefully, is to regain some sense that he
can move through the world without having to be on guard every waking moment.
