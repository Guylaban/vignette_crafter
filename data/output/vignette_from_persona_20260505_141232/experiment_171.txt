Sokha is a 66-year-old Cambodian American man who has spent most of his adult life working as a
roofer, a trade he had always taken pride in for its physical demands and the steadiness it gave his
family. He is married, and before the incident he thought of himself as dependable, the kind of man
who could keep going through heat, height, and long days without complaint. The event that now
returns to him happened on a roofing job when he was exposed to toxic chemicals. He remembers the
scene with an unusual sharpness: the smell hitting first, harsh and synthetic, the kind of
industrial odor that seemed to coat the inside of his nose and throat; the confusion on the roof as
he realized something was wrong; the sense that he could not trust his own body to tell him what
would happen next. Even now, the memory comes back with the clarity of a film, replaying in sequence
with details he does not want but cannot seem to blur.

In the weeks afterward, what unsettled Sokha was not only that he could remember the exposure so
vividly, but that at other times the feeling of that moment would come over him with no picture
attached. He could be sitting at home and suddenly feel the same dread and alarm in his body, as if
the danger were present again, without any clear image of the roof or the chemicals. He tried hard
not to think about what had happened. If the memory began to form, he would push it away, tell
himself to stop, get up and busy himself with something else. But the effort left him tense and worn
out. At night, waking in the dark was especially difficult. In those first seconds before orienting
himself, he would feel exposed and unsafe, his body already braced. Darkness itself became charged
for him. Nighttime no longer felt like ordinary rest; it felt like vulnerability.

Certain smells became almost impossible to tolerate. Antiseptic odors, the smell of hospitals, and
other related chemical smells could pull him back immediately. The link made sense to him because
scent had been so central to the original exposure. When he encountered those odors now, it was as
though his mind and body recognized danger before he had time to think. Even routine situations
became complicated. If he had to enter a medical building, he would notice the disinfectant smell
right away and feel himself tighten. On job sites, if there were unfamiliar materials or strong
odors, he became preoccupied and distracted, scanning and second-guessing.

Over time Sokha came to believe that the world was a dangerous place in a way he had not fully
understood before. He was no longer confident in his own judgment. What troubled him most was the
feeling that he could not rely on himself: if he had missed the danger once, how could he trust
himself now? He found it difficult to relax even in places he knew were safe. His attention was
often divided, and he had more trouble concentrating, something that was especially unsettling in
roofing work, where focus matters. He also became reluctant to go to unfamiliar places because he
felt he could not assess what danger might be there. If his wife suggested going somewhere new, he
preferred to stay home or let her go with someone else. The habit narrowed their life together.

At work and at home, Sokha also became markedly less willing to disagree with anyone. He kept quiet,
went along, and avoided asserting himself because conflict felt like one more thing he could not
manage. This had practical consequences. On roofing crews, where men often have to speak up quickly
and clearly, he held back rather than question plans or raise concerns. At home, his wife noticed
that he no longer spoke with the same certainty about ordinary decisions. He spent a great deal of
time dwelling on how he used to be before the exposure—stronger, clearer, more sure of himself.
Those comparisons were painful. He had begun to think in bleak terms about the future, as if there
were no real future left for him beyond enduring day to day with less confidence and less freedom.

By the time he sought help, Sokha was tired of living in constant readiness. What finally brought
him in was the accumulation of small moments: waking in the dark with his heart already clenched,
losing focus on jobs, feeling overcome by a smell in a place other people found ordinary, and seeing
how much of his life had shrunk around avoiding what felt unsafe. He is frightened that this is now
simply who he is, a man who cannot trust himself in a dangerous world. At the same time, he is
hoping, quietly but seriously, that treatment might help him feel steady enough to sleep through the
night, go where he needs to go, and stop measuring himself only against the man he believes he used
to be.
