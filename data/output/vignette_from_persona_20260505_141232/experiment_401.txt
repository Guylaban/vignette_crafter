David is a 58-year-old American dentist who came to treatment after several years of trying to
narrow his life down to what felt manageable. He lives with his partner and still works, but in a
more constricted way than before. The event he returns to began after an ordinary evening drive
home. He had been pulled over by police, and what he remembers most sharply is how quickly the stop
ceased to make sense to him. An officer approached his car, ordered him out, and before David could
make sense of what was happening he was being shouted at, forced down, and struck. In his
recollection, the scene is dominated by sound and impact: the barked commands in his ear, the hard,
overwhelming noise of voices at close range, and the bodily pain where he was pinned and hit. Even
now, when the memory comes back, he sometimes hears those sounds again in his head as if they are
happening in the room with him, and he feels pain in the same places on his body that hurt that
night. At the same time, he often speaks about the event in a detached way, as though it happened to
another man. He can describe it, but the memory carries a strange unreal quality, and he has said
that it feels alien, not fully his.

In the weeks afterward, David found himself preoccupied not only with what had happened but with
what it meant about him. He had come to believe there was something about him that made the event
happen, some quality in the way he looked, moved, or responded that had led the officers to target
him. Alongside that was a fixed sense of violation: what happened had been profoundly unfair, and in
his mind nothing meaningful had been done about it. He speaks about this with bitterness rather than
outrage now, as if outrage would require energy he no longer has. More and more, he has felt that
the life he had before was gone for good. When he says his life has been destroyed by the trauma, he
does not mean that everything literally stopped; he means that the world he moved through with ease
and assumption of ordinary safety no longer exists for him.

That shift has become visible in the smallest parts of his day. In public he monitors the space
around him constantly, scanning entrances, faces, and body movements. What disturbs him most are
strangers who come toward him in routine settings. A person crossing too directly in a parking lot,
someone stepping up behind him at a coffee counter, a man moving toward him on the sidewalk can make
his body tense before he has had time to think. He rarely sits anywhere without first deciding where
the safest seat is. In restaurants he angles himself toward exits or walls; in waiting rooms he
chooses places where no one can approach from behind. At the dental office this has become part of
his working day. He notices who enters the reception area, where they stand, how close they come,
and whether their tone shifts. If a new patient walks quickly toward him in the hallway, his
attention locks onto the approach before he can return to whatever he was doing.

Over time David has tried to manage by shrinking away from anything that might stir him up. He
avoids not only upsetting situations but also experiences that might bring any strong feeling, even
positive ones. He turns down social invitations, skips gatherings with friends, and keeps
celebrations muted because he does not want the lift or drop of emotion that might leave him feeling
exposed. He also avoids stress wherever he can, postponing difficult conversations, cutting back
parts of his schedule, and structuring his days around predictability. These choices make sense to
him; he sees them as the only way to get through the day without becoming overwhelmed. But they have
also narrowed his life with his partner, who has watched him become more absent and unreachable, and
they have changed the atmosphere of his practice, where routines now revolve as much around what
David can tolerate as around the needs of the office.

Sleep has become another battleground. He has trouble falling asleep and often staying asleep, lying
alert to sounds in the house or replaying fragments of the event in the dark. By morning he is tired
but already on guard. He finally sought help after recognizing that this way of living had become
his entire life rather than a temporary adjustment. He was troubled by how automatically he now
searches for danger and by how cut off he has become from other people and from himself. What
frightens him most is the possibility that this is permanent—that the man who was pulled from his
car is the only version of him left. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not to erase what happened
but to feel that the memory belongs to him again without taking over every room he enters.
