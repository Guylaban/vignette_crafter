Anna is a 35-year-old married White American woman who works as a counselor, and when she first
began describing the accident in therapy, what stood out was not a single clear account but a series
of broken images she could not comfortably join together. She could tell there had been an ordinary
day, a drive, a moment when another person was badly hurt because of something she had done, but
when she tried to say it aloud the memory came in jagged pieces: the sudden movement, the impact, a
glimpse of the other person on the ground, sound without sequence, then a blankness she could not
bridge. Each time she circled back to it, she was overcome not only by shock but by grief so intense
that she would stop speaking and stare at the floor, as if continuing would make the scene more
real. What tormented her most was that she felt she should be able to explain exactly what happened
and could not. Even to herself, the story would not hold together.

In the weeks after the accident, Anna became certain that the injury had happened because of the way
she had acted. She did not describe it as bad luck or a tragic mistake. In her mind it was simpler
and harsher: she had failed, and another person had paid for that failure. She had come to believe
that she should have protected others and that she had done the opposite. Those thoughts settled
into something broader and heavier. The work she had once experienced as purposeful—sitting with
clients, helping them make sense of pain—began to feel hollow. She kept going through the motions,
but privately she felt that nothing in her life carried meaning in the same way anymore. She would
come home to her husband and feel the distance between who she had been and who she now believed
herself to be.

At work, these changes showed up in small but noticeable ways. Anna had always been known as warm
and attentive, someone whose steady presence put people at ease. After the accident, she found
herself avoiding eye contact with clients and colleagues. Looking directly at another person felt
unbearable, as if their gaze might contain accusation or expectation she could not meet. In sessions
she sometimes fixed her attention on her notes or on a point over the client’s shoulder. She knew
this made her seem less present, and that awareness brought another layer of shame. She did not
explain the change. She simply tried to get through the day without having to feel seen.

She also began organizing her life around avoiding anything medical. The sight of an ambulance on
the road could send a jolt through her body and leave her gripping the steering wheel, heart racing,
scanning the scene as if something terrible were about to happen again. Hospitals became impossible.
Even routine physical examinations were put off. She told herself she did not need them yet, that
she was too busy, but underneath was a visceral dread of corridors, gurneys, monitors, and the whole
world of injury and emergency care that brought her too close to what she could not bear to
remember. If she heard sirens while driving to the counseling center, she sometimes detoured or sat
in the parking lot longer before going inside, trying to settle herself.

Anna spent a great deal of effort trying not to think about the accident. The harder she pushed the
images away, the more she seemed to live in readiness for their return. Nights became especially
difficult. Sleep no longer came easily; she lay awake braced against thoughts she did not want, or
woke and could not settle again. During the day she felt on guard, watchful in a way that was
exhausting and hard to explain. Her body seemed to anticipate danger even in ordinary settings.
Strong feelings were never far away—fear, horror, anger, guilt, shame—and she moved through them
with the sense that any reminder could tip her back into the rawness of the event.

By the time she sought help, the strain had become difficult to hide at home and at work. Her
husband had noticed that she seemed absent even when she was sitting beside him, and that she
recoiled from conversations that asked for honesty about how she was doing. At the counseling
center, avoiding eye contact and carrying herself in a tense, guarded way had begun to affect how
connected she felt to her clients. What finally brought her in was not a single crisis but the
realization that she could no longer tell herself this would pass if she just kept suppressing it.
She wanted, at minimum, to be able to sleep and to tell a coherent story of what had happened. What
frightened her most was that if she looked directly at the memory—or let other people look directly
at her—she would only confirm what she already believed: that she had failed someone in the worst
possible way, and that nothing meaningful could be built from that.
