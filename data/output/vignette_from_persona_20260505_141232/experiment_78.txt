Omar is a 22-year-old American man of Middle Eastern background who works as an auditor, a job that
suits the part of him that still values order, procedure, and being able to account for things
clearly. It is harder for him to account for what happened to him. He traces the change in his life
to an assault by an ex-partner, someone he had trusted and been alone with in what had once felt
like an ordinary private setting. During the rape, details fixed themselves in him with an intensity
that now feels impossible to control: a particular smell on the other person’s skin and clothes, the
sound of certain phrases his ex used, and the awful sense that the basic rules of how one person
should treat another had been violated in front of him and against him. What stays with him most is
not only fear but degradation. He says, in different ways, that he cannot get past how humiliating
it was to be overpowered by someone he knew, and he has come to measure himself against an imagined
other man who, he is certain, would have seen the situation coming, stopped it, or simply never
ended up there.

In the weeks after the assault, Omar did not describe himself as injured so much as changed. He used
to think of himself as an easygoing, cheerful person, someone who could joke with coworkers and move
through the day without much self-consciousness. Now he experiences himself as constantly miserable,
as if that former version of him belongs to someone else. He is especially shaken by anything that
resembles the ex-partner’s language. If someone says a phrase his ex used to say, even casually and
with no connection to him, he feels his whole attention narrow. Smell can do the same thing. A
related cologne, detergent, or stale mix of sweat and fragrance in a hallway or elevator can stop
him internally even when he keeps his face still. What hits him in those moments is immediate and
bodily; he becomes tense all over, ready to react before he has even fully registered why.

This has become part of his daily life at work. As an auditor, he spends time in conference rooms,
client offices, reception areas, and unfamiliar buildings, and he now manages each setting according
to safety rather than convenience. He chooses seats closest to the exit and notices doors as soon as
he enters. If a meeting room fills up, he calculates whether he can leave quickly. He is wary in
places he does not know well because not being able to assess the danger feels intolerable to him.
Sudden movements by other people—someone standing abruptly, reaching across him, turning sharply in
a narrow corridor—put him immediately on guard. He experiences his body as perpetually braced,
shoulders tight, stomach clenched, as though danger might break in at any moment.

Over months, Omar has altered how he presents himself. He wears concealing clothing, favoring loose
layers that make him feel less exposed and less likely to be noticed. He avoids situations where he
might be evaluated, whether for his appearance or performance. At work, this has meant trying to
stay out of presentations, volunteering less in meetings, and dreading moments when managers review
his work in person. It is not only criticism he fears. Being looked at closely feels unbearable in a
way he struggles to explain, as if attention itself carries the risk of exposure. Outside work, he
avoids unfamiliar restaurants, social gatherings, and invitations that would require entering new
apartments or venues. Being single has come to feel safer than the possibility of hearing certain
intimate phrases again or finding himself in another private space where trust could be misused.

He is also intensely affected by what he sees as moral wrongdoing in other people. Even relatively
ordinary instances of people behaving badly—someone mocking another person, crossing a line, acting
entitled with another’s boundaries—can produce a disproportionate jolt in him. In those moments he
feels both disgust and alarm, as though he is suddenly back in contact with the same kind of
violation. He does not talk much about the assault, but in therapy he keeps returning to the belief
that somebody else would not have let this happen. The comparison is harsh and repetitive. He sees
himself as permanently marked by humiliation, and what frightens him most is that this feeling may
never lift, that he will remain the miserable, watchful version of himself and never recover the
happier person he remembers being.

He finally sought help after noticing how much of his life had narrowed around exits, clothing,
routes, and anticipation. A recent workday crystallized it: entering an unfamiliar client site,
catching a related smell in the lobby, and then hearing a passing remark that echoed something his
ex used to say left him so visibly shaken that he made an excuse to leave. That frightened him, not
because he doubted the reality of what happened, but because he could see how thoroughly the assault
was still present in ordinary moments. He comes to treatment hoping, cautiously, that he might feel
less trapped in vigilance and humiliation, though he is afraid that speaking about it will only
confirm what he already believes about himself.
