was nowhere to go. For several moments she was pinned between luggage barriers and other passengers,
half crouched, half shoved forward, with alarms blaring and people screaming over one another. Smoke
and dust filled the air. She recalls the sickly wave in her stomach, the metallic taste in her
mouth, and the immediate conviction that another explosion could come at any second.

In the days afterward, Claire returned to duty and spoke about the event in the brisk, minimizing
way that had long served her in military life. She told herself she had survived, other people had
not, and that dwelling on it would be self-indulgent. That comparison became a familiar refrain: it
could have been worse, so she should be able to carry on. Yet her reactions did not settle. Loud
noises began to hit her with a force she found difficult to hide. A slammed door in a hangar, an
engine backfire on base, even the sharp clatter of equipment being dropped could make her whole body
jolt before she had time to think. In those moments she felt as if she were back in the terminal at
the instant of the blast, her body already braced for impact.

even busy grocery store aisles felt intolerable because she could feel people closing in around her.

not keep space around herself, she felt trapped. She started avoiding enclosed or confined spaces

lingering near doorways during meetings. As a combat pilot, much of her work required disciplined
focus in restricted, highly controlled spaces, and she began to notice how much effort it took to
manage herself before and after those situations. She was not describing fear of flying; rather, it

to happen.

certain that danger is always about to break open around her.
