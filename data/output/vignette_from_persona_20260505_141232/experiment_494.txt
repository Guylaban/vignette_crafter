Jason is a 39-year-old divorced construction worker who came to treatment after years of trying to
keep his inner life narrowed down to what he could get through a shift. The event he returned to
again and again involved a coworker’s death on a job site several years earlier. Jason had been
working on a commercial build, high up on steel framing, when a man he knew well lost footing and
fell. Jason saw it happen in a sequence of stark, frozen images that still come back to him that way
now: the turn of the man’s body, the sudden emptiness where he had been standing, the unnatural
position after impact. What has stayed with Jason is not a story he tells in a smooth way, but
flashes. When the memory breaks through, it is “like pictures,” abrupt and complete. Along with the
images come sensory details that make the moment feel close rather than past. He can catch the same
metallic, dusty smell of the site mixed with the sharper odor that was present after the fall, and
at times he notices the taste that was in his mouth that day, the grit and bitterness he remembers
as if it were happening again.

In the weeks after the accident, Jason did what seemed to make the most sense to him: he shut
himself down and went back to work. He had come to believe that there was no point in letting
himself feel much of anything, especially anger. Anger seemed dangerous, useless, and close to
something he would not be able to control, so he never allowed himself to feel or express it. When
coworkers complained about safety shortcuts or cursed about deadlines, Jason would go quiet. At
home, after his divorce, the same pattern hardened. He kept his face flat, told people he was fine,
and treated any stirring of rage or protest as something to choke off immediately.

At the same time, his understanding of the world had changed in a way he described as total. Before,
construction had felt risky but governed by rules, skill, and experience. Since the accident, he was
certain those beliefs had been destroyed. In his mind, terrible things did not wait for carelessness
or bad judgment; they could happen in an instant, to anyone, with no warning and no real way to stop
them. He was left with a deep sense of helplessness that extended beyond the site. If a man could
die in front of him in the middle of a normal workday, then bad things were always already on their
way. Alongside that was a quieter but equally painful conviction that he was inadequate. He did not
describe this as guilt in elaborate terms; it came across more as a settled verdict on himself.
Other men would have known what to do, would have prevented it, would have been stronger afterward.
He believed he had failed some test he should have passed.

Over time, Jason developed ways of getting away from himself without calling attention to it. Some
evenings he drove to casinos or placed bets because gambling gave him distraction and a jolt of
excitement that cut through the deadened, constricted state he usually lived in. For a few hours,
the spin of risk narrowed his mind to numbers, cards, and outcomes. At other times, especially after
something at work brought the accident close, he deliberately tried to blank out. He described
moments of making himself “leave” mentally, staring off until he felt far away from his body and
surroundings. On active construction sites this had become particularly troubling. The clang of
metal, the view from elevation, or catching an industrial smell in warm air could bring one of those
visual bursts so sharply that he would mentally pull back from the scene around him. He was still
showing up to work, but not in a way that felt steady or safe to him.

These habits had accumulated consequences. Gambling created financial strain that he kept hidden
after the divorce, and his isolation deepened because he avoided situations where people might ask
how he was doing or notice how checked out he had become. At work he had developed a reputation for
being hard to read and distant. He took jobs and completed tasks, but he no longer trusted himself
in the same easy, practiced way. What frightened him most was not just remembering the death; it was
the sense that the world had become fundamentally unlivable, governed by catastrophe, and that he
himself was not enough for it.

He finally sought help after a recent day on site when a smell carried him back so vividly that he
could taste the grit in his mouth and found himself deliberately going blank while standing in the
middle of a task. Nothing happened, but the fact that it could have was enough to shake him. Jason
came in not because he felt hopeful exactly, but because he was running out of ways to outrun what
he saw in those images. He said he wanted to know whether there was any way to live without feeling
that disaster was always one second away, and without having to disappear from himself to make it
through the day.
