Priya Mehta was 32 when she came for treatment, a single South Asian American woman serving on the
city council in a large urban district. She described the beginning of her problems as the afternoon
a man-made disaster struck during a public community event she was attending in her official role.
The gathering had been routine at first: folding chairs set out in rows, staff moving between
tables, residents waiting to speak with local officials. Then there was a sudden human-caused blast
that shattered the ordinary feel of the room in an instant. People screamed and surged toward the
exits. Chairs scraped and overturned. Priya remembered the terror of not knowing whether there would
be another explosion, whether the person responsible was still nearby, and whether the crowd itself
would become as dangerous as the blast. In those moments, what fixed itself most strongly in her
mind was the necessity of finding a way out immediately and of reading people around her for signs
of danger.

In the weeks that followed, she did not talk about the event as something over. She had come to
believe that it had shown her the truth about the world: that danger could appear without warning in
places that were supposed to be safe, and that anyone who let down their guard was being naive. She
was certain she had to stay alert all the time, because “you never know what can happen next.” This
was not an abstract belief for her. It shaped the way she entered every room. At council meetings,
before she could focus on the agenda, she found herself locating side doors, hallways, stairwells,
and any space behind the dais that might offer a quick exit. If she was invited to speak at a
neighborhood center she had never visited, she often delayed giving an answer or declined outright,
telling herself that in an unfamiliar place she could not properly assess the danger.

Her work made these habits especially visible. As a city council member, much of her day involved
public buildings, evening forums, and meetings with people she did not know. She no longer walked
into these settings with the easy confidence colleagues remembered. She scanned constantly: who was
standing near the door, whose expression looked off, who seemed agitated, who was watching her too
closely. During constituent events she kept track of where security staff were positioned and
whether there was enough open space to move if she needed to. She described not feeling settled
anywhere until she had identified an escape route. Even then, her attention remained divided, one
part of her listening to residents and another sweeping the room for threat.

Night was markedly worse. She said she could not feel safe after dark in the way she once had.
Evening meetings, which were unavoidable in local politics, became ordeals. The darkened parking
garages, street corners outside municipal buildings, and the sense of reduced visibility all left
her more tense and watchful. If an event ran late, she noticed herself becoming increasingly keyed
up, checking who was entering and leaving, gauging distances to exits, and mentally rehearsing how
she would get out if something happened. What frightened her most was the feeling that nighttime
concealed intentions and limited her options.

Over months, her life narrowed around these calculations. She began avoiding unfamiliar venues
whenever she could, steering staff toward city hall or other buildings she knew well. She also
started avoiding situations that put her body under stress. She worried that if her heart was
pounding or her breathing became labored, she would be less able to detect danger or act quickly. As
a result, she cut back on physical activity and passed on events that required rushing between
locations or standing for long periods in dense crowds. The consequence at work was subtle but real:
colleagues noticed she was less willing to appear in new parts of the district, and some outreach
opportunities were lost because she would not agree to attend unless she could first understand the
layout and exits.

By the time she sought help, Priya was exhausted by the constant effort of being on guard. She still
believed the world was dangerous and that vigilance was necessary, but she was beginning to see how
fully that belief had taken over her days and nights. She came in after declining yet another
evening event in an unfamiliar community space and feeling a surge of shame afterward, aware that
her job required presence in exactly the settings she now found hardest to tolerate. She said she
wanted help because she was tired of living as if every room needed an escape plan before she could
breathe normally. At the same time, she was deeply afraid that if she stopped scanning, stopped
watching people, or let herself feel physically off-balance, she would miss the next sign of
catastrophe and be caught unprepared.
