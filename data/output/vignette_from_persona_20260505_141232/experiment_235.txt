Kwame is a 50-year-old African American man who has spent most of his adult life working as a
plumber, a job he once described as proof that he could handle anything practical, messy, or urgent
that came his way. He is married and had been used to moving through other people’s homes, apartment
basements, and commercial buildings with a kind of quiet confidence. That changed after he was
assaulted during a late service call. He had gone alone to a residential building to deal with a
reported leak. In the narrow space near the utility area, another man confronted him and attacked
him at close range. What has stayed with Kwame is not a clear spoken story of the assault so much as
a sequence of hard visual fragments: the sudden movement of a body entering his space, the flash of
an arm, the angle of the corridor, the look of the other man closing in on him. When he tries to
describe what happened from beginning to end, he stalls. He says he sees it, but he cannot get it
into words in a way that feels complete.

In the days that followed, he found himself returning to those images again and again. The memory
came as pictures rather than a narrative, and each time it arrived he was hit by a heavy sense of
guilt. He felt he should have read the situation better, should have reacted differently, should
have done something sooner. Even though he was the one attacked, he kept circling back to the idea
that he had failed some basic test of judgment. Alongside that guilt was a bitter sense that what
happened to him was profoundly unjust. He had been at work, doing an ordinary job, and from his
point of view nothing meaningful had been done about it afterward. He spoke about that with
contained anger, as though the unfairness of it sat in his chest unchanged.

Over the following months, his world narrowed. Kwame began to feel safe only in a very limited set
of places, mainly at home and in a few familiar locations where he believed he knew every entrance,
every sound, every person likely to pass by. Outside those places, he stayed tense. As a plumber, he
is often required to enter cramped, unfamiliar environments and to work while tenants, maintenance
staff, or building owners move around him unexpectedly. Now even a quick motion near his shoulder
could make him jolt as if danger were already there. In one basement job, when a superintendent
stepped suddenly around the corner behind him, Kwame spun around so sharply that he dropped a
wrench. He was embarrassed afterward, but more than that, it confirmed for him that he was no longer
the capable man he had been. He had come to believe that the assault had changed something basic in
him and that he could no longer do the things that once defined him.

At home, the nights became their own struggle. He had trouble falling asleep, and when he did sleep
it was often broken. Lying awake in the dark, he would feel on guard rather than tired, as though
rest itself required a level of safety he could not quite reach. His wife saw that he was worn down
and increasingly restricted in where he would go and what jobs he would accept. He began avoiding
calls in certain buildings or neighborhoods, especially where he would have to work alone in tight
spaces with people moving in and out around him. This had practical consequences. He lost income,
turned down work he once would have taken without hesitation, and became less willing to take the
lead on demanding jobs. For a man whose identity had been tied closely to competence and
reliability, this felt humiliating.

What frightened Kwame most was not only the memory of the assault but his loss of trust in himself.
He was no longer sure he would do the right thing if something happened again. He doubted his
judgment in ordinary moments, second-guessed decisions on job sites, and interpreted his own
hesitation as evidence that he had become diminished. By the time he came for help, his life had
contracted noticeably around a few places that still felt safe, and his work had become a source of
strain rather than pride. He sought treatment after recognizing that this pattern was not easing
with time and after seeing how much of his marriage and livelihood was now organized around avoiding
the sense of danger. What he hopes for most is to feel like himself again at work and at home. What
he fears is that the man who walked confidently into any building to do his job is gone for good.
