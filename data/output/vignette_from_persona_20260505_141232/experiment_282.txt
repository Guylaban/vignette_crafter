Richard is a 70-year-old married American man who has spent most of his adult life working as a
salesperson, a role that once fit the version of himself he recognized easily: sociable, steady
under pressure, comfortable striking up conversation with strangers. The assault happened several
months ago at a stop he had made during the workday, near a commercial strip he used to visit
routinely to see clients. He had gone in expecting an ordinary errand and instead found himself
confronted by a man who was visibly intoxicated. What has stayed with Richard is not only the
suddenness of the attack, but the sensory immediacy of it: the sharp smell of alcohol on the man’s
breath as he came close, the shock of seeing his face at arm’s length, and the disbelief that things
had turned dangerous so quickly. In Richard’s mind, the scene remains tied to that place and to the
offender’s appearance in a way that still feels alarmingly immediate.

In the days afterward, Richard told himself he should be able to put it aside. He tried to carry on
with work and with life at home with his wife, but unwanted thoughts about the assault kept arriving
without warning. They would intrude when he was driving between appointments, when he was setting up
for a sales call, or when the day briefly went quiet. He did not experience them as memories he
chose to revisit; they came as unwelcome mental returns to the assault, and he would tense as soon
as he noticed them beginning. If he caught sight of someone who resembled the offender, even only in
passing, his attention would lock onto the person before he could stop it. The same thing happened
when he encountered the smell of alcohol. Passing a customer who had been drinking at lunch, walking
by a bar entrance, or catching stale liquor on someone’s clothes could pull him abruptly back into
the sense that the assault was happening again in some essential way.

Because so much of his work depends on moving through the community, these moments began to shape
his days. Richard started avoiding the location where the assault occurred, first by taking a
different route, then by declining to stop in that area altogether. He told himself there was no
good reason to go near it if he could help it. Over weeks and months, this avoidance became part of
how he organized his sales territory. Accounts in that part of town were postponed, reassigned when
possible, or visited only after prolonged hesitation. The change affected his work rhythm and
narrowed the ease with which he had once moved through his job. Places that used to feel neutral now
carried the possibility of seeing the offender again, and he remained alert to that possibility
whenever he was out.

At home, Richard put equal effort into trying not to think about the assault. When thoughts
surfaced, he pushed them away hard, changing the subject in his own mind or getting up to busy
himself with something practical. He found that he was also numbing his feelings more generally. His
wife noticed he seemed less emotionally present, as though he had gone flat in situations where he
would once have shown warmth or irritation or humor. Richard recognized this too, and it unsettled
him. He had come to feel that the person he had become was unrecognizable to him. He did not
describe himself simply as shaken; he felt altered in a more fundamental way, as though something
about the assault had exposed a defect in him. There was, he believed, something wrong with him as a
person if he could not return to normal, if a smell or a glimpse of a man on the street could still
throw him off balance.

he should have read the situation differently, said something else, moved sooner, handled himself in
another way. These thoughts did not resolve anything for him, but they remained persistent and
personal, folded into a broader conviction that the assault said something shameful about him.

By the time he sought help, Richard was still working, but with increasing strain. The practical

brought him in was not one dramatic worsening, but the accumulation of these changes and his growing
fear that this diminished, guarded version of himself might be permanent. What he wants most is to
feel recognizable to himself again; what frightens him most is the possibility that he cannot.
