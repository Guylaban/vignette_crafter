Kwame is a 45-year-old African American man who has spent most of his adult life doing agricultural
work, moving through long days of irrigation lines, machinery noise, and the exposed distances of
open fields. When he speaks about why he has come for help now, he does not begin with the present.
He goes back to a house from his childhood, to nights when he would hear his mother and the man who
lived with them fighting behind a closed door. The worst memory is one he returns to unwillingly but
in sharp fragments: his mother being thrown against the kitchen wall, a chair scraping hard across
the floor, the sound of her crying out, and himself standing nearby, frozen and useless. He
remembers the yellow kitchen light, the tightness in his chest, and the certainty that something
terrible was happening while he did nothing that mattered. What remains most unbearable for him is
not only what he saw, but the conviction he carries that he failed her. He says, in different ways,
that he should have done more, should have stepped in, should have stopped it somehow, even though
he was only a child.

In the years afterward, Kwame did not speak much about those nights. He tried to force the memories
away whenever they came up, pushing his mind toward work, chores, anything else. If an image broke
through anyway, he would clamp down harder, telling himself not to think about it. But the effort
never gave him peace. He found himself turning over the same thoughts for hours, especially at
night, imagining ways he might have gotten even with the man who hurt his mother. Even now, decades
later, his mind can get pulled into those revenge fantasies with a grim, repetitive logic, as if he
is still trying to settle something unfinished. He does not describe these thoughts with
satisfaction. They leave him tense and bitter, and afterward he feels even further from the person
he believes he used to be.

Kwame says he was once a happy person, or at least he remembers there being an easier version of
himself. Now he experiences himself as permanently miserable, a man who has lost something central
and cannot get it back. He has also come to believe that faith itself has gone out of his life. He
does not mean only religious faith, though that too has dimmed. More broadly, he no longer trusts
people, life, or reassurances of any kind. If someone tells him he is safe, he feels almost
irritated by the statement, because inside it does not register as real. He is certain that safety
can disappear too quickly to be believed in.

That sense is with him every day in the fields. Agricultural work often requires men to move in and
out of trucks, storage sheds, and irrigation areas together, and Kwame has developed a firm rule for
himself: he tries never to be alone with another person. He does not announce this rule, but he
organizes himself around it. If a coworker asks him to check equipment in a barn or walk a fence
line without others nearby, he finds a reason to delay, brings someone else along, or avoids the
task if he can. In his own mind this is simply prudent. Being alone with one other person feels
exposed, unpredictable, too much like being trapped close to someone whose intentions he cannot
fully know. This has made him seem distant and difficult at work. Supervisors have noticed that he
hesitates with certain assignments, and coworkers sometimes read his behavior as unfriendly. At
home, his partner has also felt the strain. Kwame keeps watch rather than relaxing, especially after
dark, scanning sounds outside the house, checking locks again, staying alert deep into the night.
Nighttime feels distinctly less safe to him than daytime; darkness sharpens his expectation that
something could happen without warning.

By the time he sought help, the pattern had become harder to contain. His partner had told him that
he never seems at ease, that even in ordinary evenings he looks like a man waiting for danger. Kwame
recognized the truth of that. He is tired of living on guard and tired of being pulled into old
scenes he tries so hard not to think about. What finally brought him in was not a single crisis but
the accumulating weight of misery, distrust, and guilt. He is afraid that this is simply who he is
now: a man who cannot feel safe, cannot trust anyone’s reassurance, and cannot forgive himself for
freezing when his mother needed him. At the same time, he is hoping, cautiously, that talking about
it might loosen the grip of those nights enough for him to stop living as if they are still
happening.
