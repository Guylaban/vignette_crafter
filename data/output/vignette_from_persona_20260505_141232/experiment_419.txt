Sophie is a 19-year-old dancer who came to treatment months after surviving a man-made disaster at a
crowded performance venue where she had gone with other young dancers. What she can say about that
night comes out in fragments. She knows what happened, but when she tries to put it into words she
often stops, presses her hands over her face, and goes quiet, as if language disappears just as the
scene returns in full. In her mind it does not feel like a story from the past. It arrives all at
once, completely real, with the crush of bodies, the sensation of being pinned and unable to move,
and the smell from that night coming back so vividly that for a moment she is back there rather than
in the therapy room. What has stayed with her most sharply is the terror of being trapped in a crowd
during an intentional act of human violence, feeling herself shoved, held, and unable to get free
while people around her surged in panic.

In the weeks afterward, Sophie found that ordinary states of her body had become loaded with danger.
When she was very tired after rehearsals, the heaviness in her limbs and the fogginess in her head
felt too close to the helplessness she had felt that night. Exhaustion no longer meant she had
worked hard; it meant she was vulnerable. She began watching for danger around her constantly. If
someone moved suddenly near her, even another dancer crossing behind her in the studio, she reacted
as if something terrible were about to happen. At night she woke abruptly and checked her room,
listening and orienting herself until she could convince herself she was safe enough to lie back
down. Even then, sleep stayed light and easily broken.

Being out in public became especially fraught. Walking to class or to the subway, Sophie often
became certain someone was behind her or tracking her. She would glance over her shoulder
repeatedly, change pace, or hold herself tense until she reached somewhere she could see an exit. In
crowded sidewalks or hallways, if someone brushed past or a stranger came too close, her body
reacted to the contact as though she were being restrained again. She sometimes lifted both hands to
cover her face in those moments, partly to shut everything out and partly because she felt exposed
and unbearably visible. The gesture had become automatic enough that she sometimes did it in the
studio when other dancers noticed she had gone pale.

Before the disaster, dancing had been the place where Sophie felt most at home. Over time that
changed in concrete ways. The studio mirrors, the physical closeness of partnering, and the fatigue
after long rehearsals all became situations she dreaded rather than welcomed. She started declining
invitations to go out with other company members after practice, then skipping nonessential outings
altogether. On days when she did not absolutely have to leave home, she stayed in. She also gave up
things she used to enjoy without quite announcing it to anyone: no more lingering after class, no
more social evenings, no more pleasure in movement for its own sake. Friends saw less of her, and
Sophie felt increasingly separate from other people her age. Watching classmates make plans and talk
easily about performances, she had come to believe that she no longer belonged anywhere—not fully
with dancers, not with carefree friends, not even with people who had known her before.

What troubles her deeply is not only that the disaster happened, but that it feels profoundly unfair
and unresolved. She returns again and again to the fact that people did this, that the danger came
from other human beings, and that “nothing has been done about it” is how it feels inside her when
she thinks about the aftermath. At the same time, she tries hard not to think about it directly. She
is frightened that if she lets herself remember on purpose, she will not be able to handle what
comes back. So she organizes her life around not setting it off, even as reminders break through
anyway in smell, touch, and sudden bodily states she cannot control.

By the time Sophie sought help, the strain was showing in every part of daily life. In rehearsal she
struggled to concentrate on choreography and corrections, losing track midway through combinations
she would once have learned quickly. She was sleeping badly, socializing less, and feeling
increasingly alone. What finally brought her in was a recent moment in the studio when another
dancer moved unexpectedly behind her and Sophie recoiled so violently that rehearsal had to stop.
She was embarrassed afterward and shaken by how real the danger had felt. Sitting in treatment now,
she is afraid that the memory will swallow her if she gets too close to it, and just as afraid that
if nothing changes she will lose dancing, the one place she used to feel she belonged.
