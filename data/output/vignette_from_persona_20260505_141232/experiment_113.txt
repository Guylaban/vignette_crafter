Tavita is a 46-year-old widowed man of Pacific Islander background who works as an auditor, a job
that has long suited his preference for order, clear procedures, and being able to account for what
happened and why. He traces the beginning of his current difficulties to the night a major natural
disaster struck while he was at home with his wife. What remains most vivid to him is not a single
clean sequence but a set of sensory fragments that still arrive with disturbing force. He remembers
waking in darkness and confusion, the house suddenly no longer feeling stable or safe, and the
strange stillness just before the full force of what was happening registered. When he speaks about
that night, he sometimes stops mid-sentence, looking disoriented, as if he is back inside it. He
says that when the memory comes, he can smell the same odors from that night and sometimes even
taste what was in his mouth then. At other times he says the whole thing feels impossible, “too
extreme to be real,” and he has difficulty connecting the fact of his survival with the man sitting
in the room now.

In the weeks after the disaster, Tavita found that ordinary moments could unsettle him in ways he
did not expect. Night-time became especially difficult. Waking in the dark would bring an immediate
jolt of fear before he had even fully opened his eyes, as though something terrible were already
underway. Physical locations also took on an uneasy charge. Certain places did not just remind him
of the event abstractly; they felt contaminated by it, as if being there meant he was again exposed
to forces no person could control. Even moments that others would call positive began to feel
unsafe. If he noticed himself relaxing at home after work, or laughing briefly with someone, he
would tense and pull back. He had come to believe that letting his guard down was dangerous, that
bad things happened when people were comfortable enough to stop watching.

Over time, this shaped the way he moved through daily life. Tavita began avoiding unfamiliar places
whenever possible because he felt he could not properly assess the danger there. He preferred known
routes, known buildings, known routines. As an auditor, he is sometimes expected to visit different
offices or unfamiliar sites, and he increasingly found reasons to postpone, reschedule, or ask
whether meetings could be handled remotely. When something ran behind schedule—a late email, a
delayed arrival, a routine hold-up in a document review—his mind jumped quickly to the assumption
that something bad was happening. Silence was particularly hard for him. In a quiet office corridor
after hours, or in the hush of a conference room before others arrived, he would feel his body
brace. Unusual silence did not register as neutral; it felt like a warning.

He does not talk much about the disaster or what followed. Since his wife died, people have
sometimes tried to ask how he is managing, but he tends to shut those conversations down quickly or
give spare, factual answers. He says little because words feel inadequate and because he does not
want to be pulled back into images and sensations he already struggles to contain. Smoking has
become one of the few things he reaches for regularly. He smokes outside his office building, in the
parking lot before driving home, and sometimes late at night when he cannot settle. Relaxation
itself has become something he avoids on purpose. He keeps the television on, checks the time, stays
mentally occupied, and resists the point where his body might soften, because that vulnerable
feeling frightens him.

He describes a painful shift in how he experiences himself. There are long stretches when he feels
emotionally flat, “dead inside,” as if his inner life has gone distant and muted. Alongside this
numbness is a fixed sense that the world is fundamentally dangerous and that, when terrible things
begin, he cannot stop them. He does not present this as a theory so much as a fact learned too
thoroughly. In session, what stands out is how little room he feels there is for safety, surprise,
or ease.

What finally brought Tavita to seek help was not one dramatic collapse but the accumulation of
strain. The restrictions in his life had become obvious even to him: shrinking his movements,
avoiding assignments, refusing invitations, enduring nights rather than sleeping through them. He
was also troubled by how unreal the memory could feel at one moment and how physically immediate it
could feel the next, with smell and taste arriving so sharply that he questioned whether he had ever
truly left that night behind. He comes to treatment frightened that this is now permanent—that he
will spend the rest of his life braced against danger and empty of feeling—but also hoping,
carefully, that he might learn how to be in the dark, in silence, or even briefly at peace without
expecting disaster to follow.
