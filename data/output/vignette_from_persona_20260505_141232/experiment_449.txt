Mika is a 27-year-old non-binary special education teacher who came to treatment after months of
feeling as if ordinary movement through the world had become charged with danger. They live with
their partner and, until the past year, had been proud of how steady and practical they were,
especially in the demanding rhythm of school life. The event they return to when describing the
start of all this was not a single dramatic assault but a period of stalking that left them feeling
repeatedly located, watched, and approached without warning. What remains most disturbing to Mika is
not one complete story they can tell from beginning to end, but a set of moments that come back
unevenly. Some parts are sharply preserved: walking to their car after work and hearing footsteps
behind them that seemed to match their pace, turning toward the sound and finding someone just far
enough back to make them question themselves, catching the sensation of being observed before they
could identify from where. Other stretches are missing altogether. They say they know something
happened over weeks that made the threat unmistakable, but when they try to recall it, they get
disconnected pieces that do not fit together, as if they are holding a few vivid frames from a film
with the rest cut out.

In the period afterward, Mika became preoccupied with how the stalking might have been prevented.
They go back over small decisions—whether they should have left school earlier, parked somewhere
else, taken another route, noticed sooner when they felt watched. These thoughts do not feel
abstract to them; they are insistent attempts to pin down the moment where they might have changed
the outcome. Yet the harder they think, the less complete the memory feels. They can describe one
afternoon with striking clarity—the sound of footsteps behind them on the sidewalk, the immediate
tightening in their body, the certainty that someone was there for them—but then they lose the
sequence of what happened next. That incompleteness troubles them deeply. They have come to believe
that the missing parts matter, that if they cannot assemble the whole picture they will stay
vulnerable.

At work, the school environment has become one of the places where this is most visible. Mika spends
their day moving through hallways, doorways, and busy classrooms where children, aides, and teachers
are constantly in motion. They notice themselves checking corners, windows, and the space behind
them without fully deciding to do it. If they hear footsteps in the corridor outside their
classroom, their attention snaps away from whatever they are doing. When a colleague comes up beside
them quickly or a student moves suddenly near the door, they go immediately on guard. They know, on
one level, that schools are noisy and active places, but that knowledge does little to settle the
jolt they feel in their body. What frightens Mika most in these moments is the feeling that danger
can arrive just outside their field of vision, exactly as it once did.

Over time, they have stopped allowing themselves to relax, even at home with their partner. Rest has
come to feel risky, as though letting their guard down would make them miss something important. If
they are sitting on the couch in the evening and begin to soften, they find themselves suddenly
listening for movement outside, checking the windows, or thinking about whether anyone could see in.
Walking from the parking lot, going out to run errands, or leaving school after dismissal all become
occasions for scanning the environment. They look over their shoulder, track who is behind them, and
pay close attention to whether anyone’s pace changes. The specific sound of footsteps nearby is
especially potent; it can pull them into an instant sense that they are being followed before they
have even turned around.

These habits have begun to cost them. At school, the constant watchfulness makes it harder to stay
fully present with students, and they dislike how often they are monitoring the hallway instead of
focusing on their work. With their partner, they have become harder to reassure, not because they do
not want closeness, but because calm itself no longer feels safe. Mika sought help after realizing
that the sense of being watched was no longer confined to obviously vulnerable moments; it had
spread into routine parts of the day, and they were exhausted by living in that state. Sitting in
the intake session, they spoke carefully, as if trying to assemble a record from scattered pieces.
What they want most is to feel that they can walk through the world, and through their school,
without bracing at every sound behind them. What they fear most is that if they stop watching, they
will once again fail to see danger until it is already too close.
