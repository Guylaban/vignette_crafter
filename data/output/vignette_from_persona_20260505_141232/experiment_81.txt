Liam is a 21-year-old single white American who now works as a bus driver, though the event that
still organizes so much of his life happened during his military service. When he tries to describe
it, he stalls out quickly. He says he knows what happened, but the words disappear when he reaches
for them, and what comes instead are broken pieces: heat, noise, the smell of something burnt, the
chopping sound of aircraft overhead, a blast that seemed to split time in half. The memory does not
come to him as a story with a beginning and end. It comes as disconnected moments and as a replay
that runs like a film in his mind, sudden and vivid, as if he is back inside it rather than
remembering it from a distance.

From the fragments he has been able to convey, Liam was deployed in a setting where helicopter and
aircraft noise was part of daily life until, on one day, those sounds fused with terror. There was
an explosion nearby, abrupt and disorienting, followed by the smell of burning that he still cannot
name more precisely without shutting down. What remains with him is not a coherent account but a
series of sensory impressions that feel dangerously alive: the bang, the air changing, the burnt
smell that seemed to coat the inside of his nose and throat, the sense that nowhere was safe. Even
now, when he tries to put the event into language, he grows frustrated and ashamed, as if failing to
tell it properly means he has not really grasped it himself.

In the months after he returned, Liam found that ordinary life did not feel ordinary. He had come to
believe that the world is a dangerous place and that staying alert at all times was simply
necessary. He scans automatically, checking sounds, movements, exits, and people’s behavior. On his
bus route he watches mirrors, passengers’ hands, the sidewalk ahead, and every sudden noise around
the vehicle. If a truck backfires nearby or there is any sharp bang in traffic, his body reacts
before he can think. The same thing happens when a helicopter passes overhead. Even when he knows he
is in an American city and not where he was deployed, part of him is certain that letting his guard
down would be a mistake.

The smell of something burnt is especially potent. A scorched odor from an overheated engine, burnt
food drifting from a takeout bag, or a faint electrical smell on the bus can pull him abruptly into
the replay. In those moments the images come back in jagged, repeating clips rather than as a full
memory he can sort through. He can continue functioning outwardly, but inside he feels overtaken and
tense, bracing as if something else is about to happen. He does not experience these reactions as
exaggerated. To Liam, they feel like proof that danger can erupt anywhere.

Over time he began arranging his life around not setting these reactions off. He spends long hours
on his phone, gaming, or scrolling online because the steady stream of screens gives him somewhere
to put his mind besides the fragments of the deployment event. If he is alone in his apartment after
work and not looking at a screen, the replay is more likely to start, so he keeps himself occupied
late into the night. He also avoids crowded places such as shopping centers or public events. Crowds
make him feel exposed and hemmed in, with too many variables to track at once. Being single has
become easier to maintain than explaining why he refuses concerts, festivals, or even busy
restaurants.

He avoids places of worship and religious gatherings as well. He does not elaborate much on this
except to say that he cannot tolerate being there. The avoidance has narrowed his life in practical
ways; he turns down invitations, keeps interactions brief, and lives in a small loop of work, home,
and screen time. At work, route assignments that pass busy downtown stops are a source of dread, and
he has become more withdrawn with coworkers, preferring not to linger or socialize after a shift.

What frightened Liam most by the time he sought help was not only the persistence of the memories
but what he had started to think they meant about the future. He said he was no longer sure his life
was worth living anymore, and he reported this in a flat, worn way that suggested how long he had
been carrying it. He was not coming in because he had suddenly found language for the trauma; if
anything, that still felt impossible. He came because he was exhausted from being on guard all the
time, from structuring every day around avoiding bangs, aircraft sounds, burnt smells, crowds, and
quiet moments with his own mind. What he hopes for is simple and tentative: to be able to drive his
route, go home, and live in the world without feeling as though the next sound in the air could
return him to the worst moments of his life.
