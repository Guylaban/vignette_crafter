his adult life working as an HVAC technician. He described the event that still sat in his mind not
as a single dramatic accident but as a moment when an ordinary service call turned abruptly and
unmistakably dangerous. He had been sent to inspect a commercial cooling system in a storage area

ventilation, the kind of room he had worked in countless times before, and noticing a sharp chemical
smell that did not fit with the usual odors of machinery, dust, and refrigerant. Within moments the
air felt wrong. He recalled the sting in his nose and throat, the sense that his chest had tightened
before he had fully understood what was happening, and the terrible realization that staying where
he was might mean collapsing where no one could get to him quickly. What remained with him most
strongly was not only the exposure itself but the feeling of being trapped in an enclosed room with
something toxic around him that he could not see and could not control.

In the weeks afterward, Vinh found that the world no longer felt reliably safe, especially indoors.
When people reassured him that a building had been cleared, or that a smell was harmless, or that he
was overreacting, he could not make himself believe them. He would nod, but internally he stayed
unconvinced, certain that safety was something other people claimed too easily. What frightened him
most was the idea that danger could already be in the air while everyone around him acted as though
nothing was wrong. He began to carry himself as if he needed to be ready at all times. He sat with
his shoulders tight, jaw clenched, noticing every shift in air quality and every sensation in his
body. He described himself as never fully unwinding, as though relaxing would mean letting down his
guard at exactly the wrong moment.

That belief followed him into his routines at home. Before bed he checked doors and windows
repeatedly, moving from one part of the house to another and then circling back because certainty
never lasted. His wife would tell him the locks were set and the house was secure, but reassurance
did not settle him. He needed to see the latch, test the knob, look again at the window frame. Even
after returning to bed, he often felt compelled to get up once more. He did not describe a specific
fear of burglary so much as a broader conviction that if he stopped monitoring the environment,
something dangerous could get in or be missed. He knew the checking frustrated his wife and
stretched out their evenings, but he felt unable to stop.

He also became increasingly unwilling to be alone in a room or building. If he arrived early to a
job site and found himself by himself in a mechanical room, warehouse, or empty office suite, he
felt a surge of alarm. The image of being trapped again with toxic air nearby came back to him with
force. Over time he changed how he worked. He delayed entering spaces until someone else was
present, called coworkers to stay on the line, or found reasons to wait outside. As months passed,
he also began avoiding anything he thought might be stressful, not just dangerous jobs. He turned
down difficult service calls, postponed tasks, and steered away from tense conversations because any
increase in stress felt like too much for a system already stretched tight. At home he avoided quiet
downtime as well. Sitting still in the evening, watching television, or trying to rest made him
uneasy; he had come to feel that relaxation itself was dangerous, as though letting his body soften
would leave him unprotected.

These habits narrowed his life. On the job, coworkers noticed he was slower to enter enclosed work
areas and less willing to take assignments independently. At home, his wife saw how rarely he
allowed himself to settle, and how often ordinary evenings became organized around his need to check
and recheck the house. Vinh did not present these behaviors as irrational. From his point of view,
they were what a careful person did after learning firsthand that a normal workday could turn
hazardous in seconds.

By the time he sought help, he was exhausted by the constant tension in his body and by how much
effort it took to feel even minimally prepared. What finally brought him in was the recognition that
he was structuring more and more of his life around not being alone, not relaxing, and not trusting
anyone else’s judgment about safety. He said he was afraid that if he ever truly let his guard down,
he would miss the next danger. At the same time, he hoped for some way to be in a room by himself
again—at work, in his own house, anywhere—without feeling as if invisible threat was already closing
in.
