Matiu Tevaga, a 65-year-old widowed project manager, spoke about the worst period of his life as
beginning in hospital corridors, under fluorescent lights, when his child became critically ill and
it was no longer clear whether they would survive. He remembered the sound of hurried footsteps, the
clipped voices of medical staff, the repeated movement in and out of examination rooms, and above
all his child struggling for breath. What stayed with him most sharply was the sight of their chest
working hard for air while adults around them moved quickly, made decisions, and used words he
barely understood in the moment. He had experienced those hours as a series of suspended waits:
waiting for test results, waiting for a doctor to return, waiting to be told whether the treatment
was working. Each delay had felt unbearable, as if time itself meant danger. He could still recall
standing beside the bed thinking that if he had acted faster, spoken up sooner, insisted on
something different, he might have protected his child better.

In the months that followed, those scenes did not settle into the past for Matiu. Encounters with
people connected to that period could unsettle him immediately. If he saw a relative who had been
present during the illness, or even someone who reminded him of those days, he felt his body tense
before he had fully registered why. Medical settings were particularly difficult. He avoided
hospitals whenever he could, would drive a longer route to avoid passing the one where his child had
been treated, and postponed his own physical examinations. He described a recent instance when his
primary care physician recommended a routine check-up; Matiu agreed on the phone, then canceled the
appointment the day before because he could not tolerate the thought of being in an exam room again.
Even hearing about someone else’s procedure was enough to leave him unsettled for the rest of the
day.

What frightened him just as much were sensations in his own body. Any episode of breathlessness,
even mild shortness of breath after climbing stairs or carrying materials at work, could bring him
straight back to the hospital. In those moments he was no longer simply winded; he felt a wave of
fear and a powerful bodily jolt, his chest tightening as if something terrible were already
underway. He became watchful whenever he noticed changes in breathing, and if something in daily
life took longer than expected—a family member not answering the phone, a delayed text reply, a
meeting running over schedule—his mind moved quickly to the sense that something bad must be
happening. He knew other people saw these reactions as excessive, but to him the wait itself felt
dangerous.

Matiu tried hard not to think about what happened. He said that if he let his mind go near those
memories, everything in him reacted. So he kept himself occupied almost constantly. At work he
filled every gap in the day with tasks, checking timelines, revising schedules, answering emails the
moment they came in. As a project manager, he could always find one more detail to monitor, one more
problem to solve, and he used that structure to keep his mind from drifting. Colleagues knew him as
dependable but increasingly rigid about delays; if a contractor was late or a decision was held up,
he became visibly distressed and short with people, certain that holdups meant serious trouble.
Outside work, he kept the television on, took on errands that did not need doing, and stayed busy
late into the evening because quiet gave his thoughts too much room.

Underneath that constant activity was a painful set of conclusions he had drawn about himself. Matiu
had come to believe that the crisis happened because of the way he acted, though he struggled to say
exactly what he should have done differently. He returned again and again to the idea that he had
failed in the most basic duty of a parent: he should have protected his child, and he had not. That
belief had spread into the way he saw himself more generally. Faced with decisions, even ordinary
ones, he second-guessed himself, wary that his judgment could not be trusted. He often asked others
to confirm plans he had already made, then still worried he was getting things wrong.

By the time he sought help, these patterns had narrowed his life considerably. He was avoiding
medical contact despite his age and his doctor’s advice, and his family had begun to notice how
tense he became when anyone mentioned illness, appointments, or relatives from that period. What
finally brought him in was a recent episode of shortness of breath before a routine medical visit he
had been trying to force himself to attend; he became so overwhelmed that he turned around in the
parking lot and drove home shaking. He said he was tired of living as though another catastrophe
were always seconds away. At the same time, he was afraid that talking about what happened would
prove what he already believed—that when it mattered most, he had not done the right thing.
