When he first describes what happened, Arun does not tell it as a single clean story. He is a
51-year-old married tax inspector, a careful, orderly man who is used to evidence and sequence, yet
when he speaks about the abuse by a former romantic partner, the account comes in jagged sections.
He remembers her face changing in an instant during arguments, the atmosphere in the apartment
becoming charged and unreadable, and the particular terror of not knowing when a conversation would
turn. What stands out most clearly is the sense of unpredictability: a normal evening could become
dangerous without warning. He recalls being shoved hard enough to lose balance, struck during
confrontations, and cornered in rooms where he felt trapped and watched for the next movement. Other
parts will not come. He says there are stretches he has tried repeatedly to recover but cannot, as
if pieces of the event were cut out altogether. At other times fragments force themselves in without
invitation—a flash of her arm moving toward him, the pressure of bracing himself, the shock of
realizing that someone he loved had become someone he feared.

In the weeks after leaving that relationship, Arun tried to carry on as if the matter were over. He
went to work, reviewed filings, met with taxpayers, and told himself that because he was no longer
living with her he should be able to move on. Instead, the memories began to intrude in ways that
felt humiliating and beyond his control. A fragment would break into his mind while he was sitting
at his desk, or while waiting for an elevator, and then vanish before he could make sense of it. He
found this especially unnerving because he could not summon the memory deliberately in a complete
form, no matter how hard he tried. The pieces came when they wanted. He came to dread that loss of
control almost as much as the original violence.

Certain states now unsettle him immediately. When he is very tired at the end of a long workday, he
notices himself becoming sharply uneasy, as if exhaustion leaves him exposed. Ordinary physical
discomfort can do the same. If his collar feels tight, if a chair is awkward, if he is stuck too
long in a warm conference room, his body seems to register danger before his mind has caught up.
Unpredictable situations are particularly difficult. At work he can tolerate routine audits and
scheduled meetings, but if a taxpayer arrives angry without an appointment, or a supervisor changes
plans suddenly and asks him to join an unscripted discussion, he feels a jolt of alarm that seems
wildly out of proportion to what is happening. He knows, even in the moment, that nothing is
actually wrong, yet he reacts as if he is in immediate danger.

That sense of danger has crept into the physical way he moves through the world. In restaurants,
training rooms, and waiting areas, Arun looks first for a seat where his back can rest against a
wall and he can see the room. If none is available, he remains tense and distracted, unable to
follow conversation fully. In the office he has shifted how he arranges himself during meetings,
choosing chairs at the perimeter when he can do so without drawing attention. Walking from the
parking garage into the government building, he often checks behind him more than once, scanning to
see whether anyone is following. He feels embarrassed by this, especially because his work depends
on appearing composed and authoritative, but the checking has become automatic enough that he
sometimes notices it only after he has already turned around.

What distresses Arun most is what the abuse has come to mean about him and about other people. He
had trusted this partner intimately, and he is certain he was betrayed. He speaks of that betrayal
not abstractly but with injury and disbelief: the person who should have cared for him instead used
his closeness to hurt him. Since then, he has had periods when he feels less like a person than like
something handled and positioned by someone else, as if his own subjectivity had been stripped away.
He also speaks bleakly about the future. Nothing good can happen to me anymore, he says flatly, not
as melodrama but as a conclusion he has repeated to himself often enough that it now feels factual.

His marriage has been affected not by any lack of love for his wife, but by the narrowing of his
world. He avoids many spontaneous outings because he cannot tolerate not knowing what the setting
will be like. When he is exhausted, he becomes withdrawn rather than talkative because he knows how
easily he startles and how quickly his mind fills with fragments he does not want. Over time his
wife has noticed that he studies rooms on entry, chooses the same protected seat, and seems to
disappear into himself when physically uncomfortable or overtired.

He sought help after a recent week at work in which several unexpected schedule changes left him
feeling constantly on edge. By the time he got home each evening he was exhausted, and with the
exhaustion came more intrusive fragments and more scanning. What finally frightened him was not one
dramatic incident but the realization that he was organizing more and more of his life around
feeling unsafe. Sitting in the intake session, with his chair angled so he could see the door, Arun
said he wants some possibility that life might feel human again. What he fears most is that this is
permanent: that he will always be the man who cannot trust, cannot relax, and cannot believe
anything good is still ahead.
