Miguel is a 67-year-old physician who came to treatment months after his adult son’s life-
threatening illness, speaking about it in fragments and then apologizing for “not saying it right.”
He lives alone and has spent most of his life in medicine, a setting that once gave him a sense of
competence and steadiness. The event that now intrudes on him happened when his son was admitted in
critical condition. Miguel remembers the speed of it first: the phone call, the rush to the
hospital, the abrupt transition from being a doctor who understood emergencies to being a father
watching his child’s life hang in the balance. What stays with him are not smooth memories but
disconnected pieces that arrive with force—the sight of his son in the hospital bed, the machinery
around him, the atmosphere of urgency in the room, the sense that he might lose him. When he tries
to describe the worst part, he stalls. He says he knows exactly what happened and at the same time
cannot find words for it. He can give isolated images, but not a full account. Even privately, he
cannot make it into a story that runs from beginning to end.

In the weeks after the hospitalization, Miguel returned to work, but his days changed in ways that
were obvious to him even if he tried to conceal them from colleagues. He kept himself constantly
occupied, filling every empty moment with charting, extra reading, calls, errands, anything that
would stop his mind from drifting back to the hospital. At home, he left the television on, sorted
papers he did not need to sort, and found reasons to stay busy late into the night. He tried hard
not to think about what had happened to his son. If an image began to come up, he would interrupt it
immediately, turning his attention to something concrete in front of him. He had come to dread the
internal shift that happened when his mind slowed down, because that was when the memory fragments
pressed in.

At the hospital where he works, the strain is sharpest. A sudden deterioration in a patient, the
sound of urgent movement in a corridor, or seeing a worried parent at a bedside can bring on a
moment in which he no longer feels fully anchored in the present. For a few seconds he feels as if
he is back in that earlier crisis, not as a physician but as the terrified father who could do
nothing except watch. During these moments his body reacts before he can reason with himself. He
becomes acutely watchful, scanning faces, monitors, doorways, tone of voice. He notices every shift
in the room and cannot relax his attention. What frightens him most is how suddenly this happens. He
knows where he is, yet part of him feels pulled into the past so vividly that he later struggles to
explain it.

Miguel is also burdened by intense emotions that do not settle. Fear remains close to the surface,
especially when his son does not answer a call right away or when any family medical issue is
mentioned. Alongside the fear is guilt and shame that he finds particularly hard to bear as a
doctor. He does not elaborate these feelings easily, but they appear in the way he judges himself
for not having been able to protect his son and in how harshly he speaks to himself for still
reacting so strongly. He is embarrassed by the fact that he cannot simply “put it in order,”
especially given his profession and age. He believes he should be able to speak about a medical
crisis clearly and calmly, yet when this event is his own, the words disappear.

Over time, his efforts to push the experience away have narrowed his life. Conversations with family
stay on practical topics because he avoids any discussion of the illness. If someone begins to
revisit that period, he redirects or excuses himself. At work, he remains capable, but he has become
more guarded and tense, and there are moments when he lingers outside certain rooms before entering,
collecting himself. Colleagues have noticed that he seems perpetually on alert. Miguel himself knows
that he is exhausted from maintaining that level of watchfulness and from trying to outrun his own
mind.

He finally sought help after a recent episode at the hospital in which a child’s medical emergency
brought back the sense, all at once, that his son was again in danger. For the rest of the day he
felt shaken and ashamed, unable to explain to himself why the memory still arrived in pieces and
with such force. In coming to treatment, Miguel says he wants to be able to remember what happened
without feeling as if he is inside it again. What he fears most is that if he stops holding the
memory back, it will overwhelm him completely; what he hopes is that he might one day be able to
speak about his son’s illness in a way that feels whole, and survive the telling.
