Anika Patel is a 30-year-old software engineer who came to treatment after years of trying to keep
her distress contained within the routines of adult life. She is married and lives with her husband
in an apartment she has worked hard to make feel orderly and secure, but the sense of safety she
wants from it never quite settles in. When she speaks about her childhood, she does not offer a
single clear story so much as fragments that stop and start. She was physically abused by a
caregiver when she was young, and even in the first sessions she struggled to tell it in sequence.
She would begin with an image of being trapped in a room at home, then lose the thread, correct
herself, and go quiet. It was clear that the memories did not arrive for her as a narrative she
could walk through from beginning to end. They came in pieces, and once they came she was quickly
overtaken by feeling and had to stop.

What she could describe made the atmosphere of those experiences vivid. She remembers being close
enough to the caregiver to smell sweat and stale alcohol on their breath and clothes. There was also
a particular taste that she still associates with those moments, something so specific that she
notices it immediately in the present and feels her body tense before she has fully registered why.
In her account, the sensory details are more available than the order of events. She knows there
were repeated episodes of being hurt, but when she tries to place one moment before another, the
timeline collapses. She has come to dread the memories not only because of what happened, but
because recalling them feels like standing too close to something unbearable. When a fragment
returns, she becomes overwhelmed so quickly that she cuts herself off, changes the subject, or tells
herself not to think about it any further.

Over time these reactions became woven into ordinary adult situations. In the office, where she
works on a software team in long stretches of close collaboration, she is acutely aware of smells in
a way that feels disproportionate even to her. If a coworker comes back from lunch with alcohol on
their breath after a happy hour, or if she catches the sour smell of sweat in a crowded conference
room after people have rushed in from outside, she feels a jolt of alarm and has to concentrate on
appearing normal. She may excuse herself to the restroom, stand at the sink, and wait for the wave
of emotion to pass. She does not explain this to colleagues; from the outside, it can look as though
she is simply private or easily overstimulated. At home, certain foods or flavors can bring the same
abrupt reaction. A taste will hit her and she is no longer just eating dinner with her husband; she
is fighting the urge to push the whole memory system away before anything fuller emerges.

Sleep has become one of the clearest places where her distress shows itself. At night she has
trouble falling asleep, and if she wakes she often cannot settle again. The apartment is objectively
secure, and she knows this, but knowledge does not translate into ease. She checks the front door
lock, then checks it again, then moves to the windows. If her husband reassures her that everything
is locked, she may believe him for a moment and still feel compelled to verify it herself. She
describes lying in bed with her body braced, unable to relax even when nothing is wrong. What
frightens her most in those moments is not a specific expectation that someone is about to enter,
but the feeling that letting down her guard would be a mistake. Her husband has grown concerned and
sometimes frustrated, especially when the checking stretches late into the night and leaves them
both tired the next morning.

The strain has begun to touch her work as well. On days after poor sleep, she finds it harder to
focus on complex coding tasks and more difficult to tolerate the bustle of the office. She has
started timing her office attendance to avoid social events where alcohol may be present, and when
team gatherings happen she often leaves early with a practical excuse. She knows these choices
narrow her life, but they also make sense to her: they reduce the chances of being hit by a smell or
taste that will leave her flooded and struggling to regain composure.

What brought Anika to seek help now was not a new trauma, but the accumulating realization that she
is organizing more and more of her life around not being caught off guard. She is exhausted by
broken sleep, embarrassed by the repeated checking, and discouraged by how quickly she becomes
overwhelmed whenever she tries to remember what happened to her as a child. She says she wants, more
than anything, to be able to be at home with her husband and feel that she is actually there, rather
than on alert. At the same time, she is afraid that if she lets herself remember more fully, the
feelings will be too much to bear.
