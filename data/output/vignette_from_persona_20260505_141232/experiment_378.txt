Martin Weber is a 66-year-old software engineer who came to treatment after years of living around
reactions he rarely spoke about, even to his wife. He grew up in the Midwest in a family that prized
self-control and privacy, and when he describes his childhood he does so sparingly, as if each
detail has to be handled with care. The central trauma he returns to is repeated physical abuse by a
caregiver in the home. What stands out in his account is not only being hit, but the way it arrived
without warning. He remembers being approached from behind or having a hand suddenly clamp onto his
arm or shoulder before he had even turned around. He remembers the coldness of a room or the feel of
temperature on his skin just before contact, and the immediate pounding in his chest as he tried to
gauge what was coming. In his memory, ordinary objects in the house were never just ordinary;
anything within reach could become dangerous. Even now, when the memory flashes up suddenly, it
comes with that same sense that harm can materialize out of whatever is nearby.

For decades Martin built a life that, from the outside, looked stable. He married, developed a long
career in software engineering, and became known at work as precise, reliable, and somewhat private.
But he had come to believe that safety depended on being especially careful because “you never know
what can happen next.” He scans rooms automatically for objects that could be used as weapons or for
anything hard, sharp, or heavy enough to do damage. In conference rooms he notices the letter
opener, the metal water bottle, the heavy stapler. In restaurants he clocks the steak knives before
he has settled into his seat. He does not describe this as a choice so much as something his body
does first, before he has had time to think. If a colleague brushes past him unexpectedly in a
crowded hallway, he jolts violently, heart hammering, ashamed to find himself reacting before he
knows why.

His body has become, in his mind, an unsafe place to pay attention to. A sudden pounding heart is
one of the most potent cues. If he notices it after climbing stairs, during an argument, or even
after rushing to make a meeting, he can feel himself pulled toward the old scenes. The memory does
not unfold as a calm recollection; it intrudes abruptly, and with it comes the same certainty of
danger. Certain temperatures on his skin can do the same. A chill in an over-air-conditioned office,
stepping into a cold room, or the particular cool touch of air on his forearms can stop him in place
for a moment, as if he has been dropped back into childhood before his mind catches up.

Over time Martin narrowed his life in ways that made sense to him. He stopped paying attention to
his body whenever possible. He avoids looking at himself closely, does not linger over bodily
sensations, and turns away from anything that would make him too aware of his physical self. He also
avoids touch. His wife has learned, painfully, that even affectionate contact can unsettle him if it
is not expected. He pulls away from casual pats on the back, stiffens when someone reaches for his
shoulder, and prefers to position himself where no one can come up behind him. At work he has long
favored remote communication, framing it as efficiency, but in person he keeps distance and avoids
the loose social contact that others take for granted.

He also withdrew from people he had socialized with before the abuse, or who belonged to that
earlier world. Over the years reunions, old family gatherings, and contact with longstanding
acquaintances came to feel intolerable. He could not shake the sense that other people were
fundamentally untrustworthy and that he himself was somehow permanently set apart from them, an
outsider who would never move through relationships with the same ease others seemed to have. Those
beliefs have made his world lonelier, though he speaks of the loneliness almost as a cost of staying
safe.

What finally brought him in was not a single crisis but an accumulation. Recently, an unexpected
touch at work triggered such a strong alarm response that a younger coworker asked if he was all
right, and Martin found himself unable to answer. Around the same time, his wife told him she felt
she had to “announce” every gesture in their home, as if she were living around a perimeter. He was
shaken by how normal that had come to seem. Now, seeking help in his mid-sixties, Martin says he is
tired of living as though danger is always one second away, but he is equally afraid that lowering
his guard would be naive. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not to become a different person, but to
have his own body, his marriage, and ordinary rooms feel less like places where anything might
suddenly turn.
