Arjun Patel was 29 when he came to treatment, a married man who worked as a house painter, spending
most days moving between apartment buildings, renovation sites, and private homes with drop cloths,
ladders, and paint-splattered work pants. The robbery had happened eight months earlier, at the end
of a long workday. He had been carrying his tools and the week’s cash payment as he walked past a
row of storefronts on a route he had taken many times. He still spoke about that block as if it held
a charge. Even before he could describe the whole event, he could describe the location with
precision—the narrow stretch of sidewalk, the recessed doorway, the alley opening beside it. Those
physical places had become potent for him, not as abstract reminders but as exact spaces his body
reacted to.

He remembered a man stepping into his path and another coming from the side. After that, the memory
became jagged. Arjun would say he knew there had been shouting, a hand grabbing at him, the
sickening realization that he was trapped between them, but he could not put the sequence together
in a way that felt complete. When he tried to recount it deliberately for his wife or later in
therapy, he would stall and look confused, as if the memory broke apart the harder he reached for
it. What came most sharply were fragments: the scrape of his shoes against the pavement, the feel of
his bag being yanked, the sense of being watched and overpowered. Alongside those flashes came a
rush of guilt that made his face tighten. He had come to believe he should have done something
differently—crossed the street sooner, noticed them earlier, protected the money, fought back
harder. Even though he knew he had been outnumbered, when the memory surged up he felt accused by
it.

In the weeks after the robbery, the event returned to him suddenly, without warning. He could be
rolling paint onto a wall and, for a second, be right back on that sidewalk. Sometimes it happened
when he passed similar storefronts for work; sometimes the trigger was waking in the dark from a
nightmare with his heart pounding before he was fully awake. On those mornings he lay rigid in bed,
already braced, listening for sounds in the apartment. His wife would ask what was wrong, and he
often told her it was nothing, but he would get up feeling as if danger had started before sunrise.
He described his body as never really settling. His shoulders stayed tight, his jaw clenched, and
even routine errands felt like situations he had to prepare for physically.

That constant readiness followed him into his work. Because painters spend time entering unfamiliar
buildings and moving through hallways, stairwells, and back entrances, Arjun found himself checking
every corner and doorway before he focused on the job. He watched people’s hands, their distance
from him, the ways they approached. If he heard footsteps behind him while carrying supplies, he
turned immediately. Coworkers had begun to notice that he startled into anger. If someone came up
too quickly or joked about him being jumpy, he snapped back hard. At home, too, his wife had seen
him become irritable in ways that were new for him—short-tempered over small interruptions, sharp in
tone, quick to assume bad intentions. He did not experience this as overreaction. From his point of
view, being on guard was what made sense now. He was certain that people could not be trusted, and
the robbery had settled into him as proof that anyone could turn on you if they saw an opportunity.

Over the following months, Arjun quietly narrowed his world. He took longer routes to avoid the
block where he had been robbed and tried to decline jobs in that neighborhood when he could, which
was not always possible in his line of work. When he had to go near similar locations, he arrived
already tense and distracted, scanning constantly rather than talking with clients or coworkers. He
shared less with friends and family because he felt set apart from them, as if other people moved
through the city with an ease that no longer belonged to him. Their reassurances did not land. He
had come to feel that relying on others was naïve; even asking for help seemed foolish if, in the
end, no one could really keep him safe.

What finally brought him to seek treatment was not one dramatic crisis but the accumulation of
strain. His wife told him she felt shut out and was worried by how angry and distant he had become.
At work, a supervisor commented on his temper after he barked at a coworker who had approached him
from behind on a job site. Arjun arrived for help exhausted by nights broken by nightmares and by
days spent keyed up and watchful. What frightened him most was the sense that the robbery still
lived in his body and in certain places, and that he no longer knew how to be around people without
expecting something from them. What he hoped for, cautiously, was not to erase what happened but to
stop feeling as if every sidewalk, every doorway, and every waking from sleep might lead him back
there again.
