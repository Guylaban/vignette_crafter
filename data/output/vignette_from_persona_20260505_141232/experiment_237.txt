Linh Tran is a 61-year-old Vietnamese American sculptor who came to treatment after years of trying
to live around an experience she still cannot put into a clear sequence. She is married and works
out of a studio attached to her home, where long solitary hours with plaster, clay, welding tools,
and deliveries had once felt containing. The event she witnessed happened in the late afternoon
several years ago, when she was leaving an arts building after dropping off work for a group
exhibition. As she stepped onto the sidewalk, a man walking a few yards ahead of her was suddenly
set upon from behind. What stayed with Linh was not a clean narrative but sharp, unbearable
fragments: the quick approach from the rear, the sound of footsteps changing pace, the smell of a
heavy cologne as one of the men rushed past her, the startled half-turn of the victim, and then the
confusion of bodies, shouting, and the terrible sense that violence could erupt without warning in
an ordinary public moment. She remembers freezing and then backing away, her heart pounding so hard
she thought she might faint. When she tries to tell it now, she gets caught between images and loses
the thread. She says she knows she was there and saw it, but the memory comes as flashes that feel
more real than normal memory, almost as if she is standing on that sidewalk again rather than
remembering it from a distance.

In the months afterward, Linh found that what frightened her most was the unpredictability of it.
She had come to believe that danger announces itself too late, if at all, and that the world is
fundamentally unsafe. She noticed herself scanning for what might come from behind. Footsteps
nearby, especially if she could not immediately see who they belonged to, made her body jolt before
she could think. If someone came up behind her at the supply store or in a parking lot, her chest
tightened and her arms went cold. A specific kind of sharp men’s cologne could undo her for the rest
of the day; once, when a gallery client leaned over one of her pieces wearing a scent close to the
one she remembers from the assault, she felt nauseated and shaky and had to excuse herself to the
bathroom, where she stood gripping the sink until the dizziness passed.

These moments left her feeling ashamed as well as frightened. Linh had always thought of herself as
disciplined, observant, and steady with her hands. Now she was certain there was something wrong
with her as a person if she could not simply “get over” something she had only witnessed. She
compared herself unfavorably to other people who seemed able to move through crowded spaces without
constantly monitoring who was near them. She became more mistrustful in daily life, watching
strangers’ movements closely and assuming that people were not to be relied upon. Even minor
unpredictability—an unexpected knock at the studio door, a delivery arriving earlier than planned,
hearing someone’s steps outside when she thought she was alone—could make her heart race and her
whole body brace.

Night became particularly difficult. After dark she found it much harder to feel safe than during
the day. In the studio, where sounds echo differently at night, she would stop working if she heard
movement outside or footsteps on the side path and wait in silence, listening. At home, her husband
noticed she checked locks repeatedly and startled intensely at ordinary noises. She was not able to
explain to him in a way that felt complete why night felt so exposed; when she tried, she would
begin with one sensory detail and then lose the sequence, becoming frustrated and tearful because
there was no coherent story she could hand over, only vivid pieces.

Over time these habits narrowed her life. She scheduled pickups and client visits only in daylight
when she could, avoided crowded openings, and sometimes asked her husband to handle errands that
involved public parking lots or unfamiliar buildings. Professionally, she became more reluctant to
attend exhibition events, especially those with milling guests moving unpredictably behind her. This
had practical consequences: fewer appearances, less contact with buyers, and a shrinking sense of
herself in the art community she had spent decades building.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was an incident in her own studio. An assistant curator
visiting to discuss a commissioned piece stepped behind her unexpectedly while wearing a strong
perfume. Linh spun around, trembling so hard she had to sit down, humiliated by how visible her
reaction was. Afterward she told her husband she could not keep arranging her work and her life
around the fear that someone might come up behind her. She arrives in treatment afraid that she has
become permanently altered, that the world is too dangerous to relax in and that people cannot be
trusted enough for her to lower her guard. At the same time, she is hoping for some way to remember
what she witnessed without feeling as if it is happening all over again, and to return to her studio
at night without every sound turning the room into that sidewalk.
