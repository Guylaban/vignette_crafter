Ama came to treatment at 20, already established in demanding work as a pharmacist, and spoke about
the reason for coming in haltingly, as if language itself kept failing at the edge of what had
happened to her. She was a young African woman living in the United States with her partner,
outwardly composed and highly functional at work, but carrying a private sense that she had to stay
on guard at all times. The traumatic event she referred to was a period of torture that she survived
while others did not. When she tried to describe it, her account broke apart into fragments: a room,
the force of being held down, the terror of being made to do things against her will, water used in
a way that made her believe she might die. She could give pieces, but not a full story. She said she
remembered only parts and that the rest seemed missing, and when she pushed herself to say more, she
often stopped and shook her head, as though there were no words available for the worst moments.

What remained especially vivid was not a coherent narrative but an immediate physical knowledge.
When memories came up, her body seemed to return before her mind did. She would stiffen, draw
inward, and feel herself again in the same position she had been in during the torture, pinned by
fear and bracing for what would come next. Water had become one of the most potent reminders. The
sight of a flooded street after heavy rain, a wide river glimpsed from a car window, even water
pooling in a parking lot could bring a jolt of dread that felt wildly out of proportion to the
present but absolutely convincing in the moment. Arguments had a similar effect. Raised voices,
sharp tones, the feeling that conflict was escalating could make her feel as though control was
about to be taken from her. More than anything, being forced into something or even feeling
pressured could bring a wave of panic and anger. What frightened her most was the sense of losing
autonomy again.

In the months after the torture, Ama’s life became organized around preventing that feeling from
returning. She had come to believe that if she let herself think directly about what happened, she
would not be able to handle it. So she learned to turn away from anything that might bring it
closer. She avoided times of day and situations that seemed to usher in nightmares, staying up later
than she wanted or keeping herself occupied until she was exhausted. Sleep itself felt unsafe. At
night she found it hard to fall asleep because she felt she needed to remain alert, listening for
sounds in the apartment, noticing every shift in light or movement. During the day she constantly
scanned her surroundings for signs of danger. She could not settle anywhere until she had identified
how to get out. In restaurants, waiting rooms, and even at her pharmacy job, she checked exits
almost automatically. When she started a shift, part of her attention went not only to medication
orders and customer questions but to the door behind the counter, the stockroom passage, who was
entering, and whether she could leave quickly if she had to.

That vigilance narrowed her world. She avoided people who resembled those involved in what had
happened or whom she associated with them, sometimes leaving aisles in stores or asking a coworker
to handle a customer because something about the person’s appearance made her body tense. Arguments
with her partner were especially difficult. Even ordinary disagreements could feel dangerous once
voices sharpened, and she might shut down, leave the room, or insist on ending the conversation
immediately. To her, this was not overreacting; it felt necessary to prevent something catastrophic.
Alongside this was a painful guilt she struggled to shake. She told herself she deserved to suffer
because she had lived when others had not. At times this belief sat with her heavily and silently,
less as a thought she examined than as a conclusion she felt she had no right to challenge.

By the time she sought help, the strain of living this way had become harder to contain. Her partner
had noticed how little she slept and how often she seemed elsewhere even when she was physically
present. At work, staying so alert to every entrance and every possible exit was exhausting, and she
was increasingly aware that much of her day was spent bracing rather than resting. What finally
brought her in was not a single crisis but the accumulation of too many moments in which water on
the street, a customer’s angry tone, or the sensation of being cornered by someone else’s demands
could pull her out of the present. She wanted help finding a way to live without feeling that danger
was always one step away. At the same time, she was afraid that if she came too close to the memory,
it would overwhelm her completely, and afraid as well of what it might mean to loosen the suffering
she felt she owed.
