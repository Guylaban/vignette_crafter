At 42, Meera Patel came to treatment exhausted by the effort of getting through ordinary days. She
is a married South Asian American woman who works as a maid, cleaning private homes and a small
office building on weekends. When she first tried to describe what had happened to her, her account
came in broken pieces. She would begin, stop, shake her head, and say that the whole thing
disappeared just when she got close to it, as if her mind pushed it away before it could become
complete. She knew the facts in outline: for months, a man began following her during her commute
and near the apartment complex where she worked. But when she tried to tell it straight through, she
could not hold it in order. Certain moments came back sharply and bodily, while the rest felt
blocked off.

What she could describe most clearly was the atmosphere of those encounters. The man would appear
after she left work, often in the late afternoon, after she had finished cleaning apartments. He
would stand too close, stare at her body in a way that made her feel stripped and cornered, and
speak to her as if she owed him attention. On more than one occasion she smelled alcohol on him
before she fully registered his face. She remembered the stale, sour smell of liquor mixing with the
chemical lemon scent of cleaning products still on her hands. One day, after hurrying away from him
with her jaw clenched and her mouth dry, she became aware of the metallic, bitter taste in her mouth
so strongly that even now, when the memory breaks through, she can taste it again. Over time the
stalking became less ambiguous to her. He showed up outside places where he should not have known to
find her, watched her in silence, and made comments that turned her body into something for him to
inspect. What frightened her most was not only that he was there, but the certainty that he had been
paying attention to her routines.

In the weeks and months that followed, Meera did not feel safe even when the man was not present. At
home with her husband, she began checking the front door, then the windows, then returning again a
few minutes later to make sure she had really locked them. She said silence in the apartment had
become unbearable. If the hallway outside went quiet or the street noise dropped away, she felt a
jolt in her chest, as if danger had moved closer. Even inside her own home she stayed on guard,
listening for steps, a knock, a voice outside the door. She had come to believe that being relaxed
was risky, that something bad could happen in the exact moment she let herself settle.

Her work kept putting her into situations that touched the worst parts of the experience. In
clients’ homes she often cleaned bedrooms and bathrooms alone, and if a male resident looked at her
in a way that felt lingering or appraising, her whole body tightened. She would keep her eyes down,
finish as fast as possible, and later replay the interaction repeatedly. Before speaking to clients,
building staff, or even neighbors, she rehearsed what she would say: how to sound brief, polite, not
inviting, not rude, not noticeable. She wanted every interaction controlled in advance. Physical
pain could also set her off. If her shoulder or lower back ached after scrubbing tubs or carrying
supplies, the sensation could pull her toward the memory before she fully understood why, and then
she would fight to shut it down before it unfolded.

Outside work, her life narrowed. She stopped seeing friends because being out felt exposed and
because she could not bear casual questions about how she was doing. She spent much of her energy
worrying about the safety of people she loved. If her husband was late answering his phone, her mind
rushed immediately to images of something terrible having happened. If a relative was traveling, she
felt unable to rest until she heard they were home. To her, this vigilance seemed like
responsibility, the only way to be prepared.

By the time she sought help, Meera was worn down by living as if danger were always about to
announce itself from behind a quiet hallway or an unopened door. What finally brought her in was a
recent moment at work when a client’s guest, smelling of alcohol, thanked her while looking her up
and down; afterward she locked herself in the bathroom, shaking, with that old bitter taste suddenly
in her mouth. She told the therapist she was tired of arranging her life around fear and tired of
not being able to tell her own story from beginning to end. At the same time, she was afraid that if
she let herself remember it fully, it would become overwhelming and she would never feel safe again.
