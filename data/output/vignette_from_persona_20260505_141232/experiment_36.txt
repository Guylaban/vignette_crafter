At 65, Elaine had spent most of her adult life as a military medic, a role that had taught her how
to move toward crisis with practiced steadiness. She was widowed several years before the accident,
lived alone, and had been proud of remaining capable and useful in a profession built around
emergency response. The accident happened on an ordinary drive, the kind of trip she had made
countless times without thinking. Another car struck with sudden force, and what stayed with her was
not a neat sequence but fragments: the violent impact, the sight of damaged cars locked together,
the noise, and the immediate sense that she might not survive what was happening. In the aftermath,
she could recall enough to know the facts, but when she tried to put it into words, she often
stalled. She would begin, then stop, looking frustrated and shaken, saying she knew what happened
but could not seem to describe it properly.

In the first weeks afterward, Elaine expected herself to recover through discipline. Instead,
memories of the crash began arriving in a way that felt less like remembering than reliving. If an
image of a wrecked car came on television or she passed a roadway accident, she did not experience
it as a distant reminder. For a moment she felt as if she were back inside the original collision,
with her body reacting before she had time to orient herself. Similar cars could do it too. She
would notice a make or color close to the one involved and feel her stomach drop, her muscles
tighten, and her attention narrow around the vehicle. What frightened her most was how quickly the
world around her could vanish behind the sense that the crash was happening again.

Because of her work, she encountered these reminders in concrete and difficult ways. As a military
medic, she still moved through environments where vehicles, road incidents, and sudden commotion
were part of daily life. Seeing damaged cars on base or hearing others discuss accidents could leave
her deeply uncomfortable, but she rarely said much about it. She had come to believe that if she let
herself think directly about the crash, the feelings would become unmanageable. So she tried hard
not to think about it. She redirected herself into tasks, paperwork, television, anything that might
pull her mind elsewhere. When that failed and the distress lingered into the evening, she ate
excessively, not because she was hungry but because the act of eating dulled things for a while and
gave her something immediate to focus on.

Over the months, her body never seemed to stand down. Unexpected or loud sounds made her snap to
attention at once, as if danger were already present. A slammed door, a dropped piece of equipment,
a sudden bang in a corridor could leave her rigid and braced before she knew why. She described
herself as constantly tense, ready to react, unable to settle fully even in familiar places. This
was especially unsettling to her because she knew the difference between ordinary alertness and the
kind that took over her whole body. She did not experience it as caution; it felt like being held in
a state of readiness she could not switch off.

Talking about the accident in any sustained way was difficult. When memory came back, it came with
so much force that she felt flooded and had to stop. In session she might start with a plain factual
sentence, then go quiet, eyes fixed somewhere far away, her breathing shallow. Sometimes she seemed
to lose her place in the room for a few seconds, caught in the experience rather than describing it.
She found this humiliating. After a career spent helping other people in emergencies, she was
distressed by how little control she felt over her own reactions. She told herself to be practical,
to get on with things, but the harder she pushed thoughts away, the more abruptly they seemed to
return.

Her habits narrowed her life without her intending them to. She spent more time alone at home with
food and television, keeping herself occupied so there was less room for memory. At work, she stayed
functional, but colleagues likely saw a more withdrawn version of her. She kept conversations on the
surface and steered away from anything that might touch on the accident or damaged vehicles. The gap
between how competent she appeared and how shaken she felt became harder to carry.

What finally brought Elaine to seek help was not one dramatic change but the accumulation of these
moments: feeling as if she was back in the crash when she saw accident imagery, jumping violently at
sudden sounds, and finding that even trying to explain what happened left her overwhelmed and
wordless. She came in wanting relief from the constant sense of being on alert and from the moments
when the past no longer felt past. At the same time, she was afraid that speaking about the accident
too directly would pull her fully back into it. What she hoped for, quietly but clearly, was to be
able to remember what happened without feeling that she was living through it again.
