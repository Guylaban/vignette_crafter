she once valued for its routine, timing, and constant movement through crowded stations. She is
married and lives with her husband, but when she speaks about the event that brought her to

without warning and with such force that she feels she is back there again.

the pain and fear, but the sensory details surrounding them. She remembers moments when everything
suddenly went quiet, and that silence felt dangerous rather than peaceful, as if something terrible
was about to happen. She remembers smoke in the air, close enough and thick enough that even now the
faint smell of fire on someone’s clothes can make her body stiffen. She also remembers seeing
wounds—her own and those of others—with a vividness that has never dulled. Even in session, she does
not offer a neat account of sequence or duration. Instead she describes still images: a burned
smell, a room gone still, an injured body in front of her. When these memories come, she does not
experience them as distant recollections. She feels as though the event is happening in the present,
her body reacting before she can remind herself where she is.

In the months and years afterward, Amina returned to work and tried to keep life moving. On the
trains, however, the world offered too many moments that matched what her mind had stored. If a
carriage suddenly fell quiet between stations, she felt herself bracing, listening, waiting for
something to happen. If she passed a passenger with a bandaged hand, a split lip, or fresh stitches,
her attention locked onto the injury and the old images surged forward. The smell of smoke was
especially potent. A commuter coming in from a cigarette break, the faint burnt odor from overheated
equipment, even smoke drifting from the street near the platform could pull her abruptly out of the
present. In those moments she was no longer simply at work checking tickets and monitoring doors;
she was caught in vivid visual fragments that felt immediate and real.

can, describing it less as comfort than as something that gives her a concrete action when she feels
wound tight. Much of her thinking is spent rehearsing disaster. Before a shift, on the way home,
lying in bed, she runs through worst-case scenarios so she will not be caught off guard. She
imagines what she would do if someone approached suddenly, if something happened in the house, if
there were an attack, if she were trapped. To her, this feels like preparation, the only responsible
way to live.

At home, her husband has seen how little rest she gets from any environment. Amina listens for

can be as alarming to her as noise. If the house goes completely still, she finds herself straining
to hear, alert and rigid. Sudden movements from other people put her on guard immediately; even an
ordinary quick gesture can make her body jerk into readiness before she has time to think. She says
she cannot relax even when she knows, logically, that she is safe. That distinction matters to her:
she is aware of where she is, aware that she is with her husband or at work among colleagues, but
her body does not follow that knowledge.

What finally brought Amina to seek help was not a single collapse but the accumulation of living
this way. At work, she has found herself increasingly preoccupied with scanning, with smoke, with

overtake the present in an instant. What she hopes for, cautiously, is not to erase what happened,
but to be able to stand in a quiet room, or smell smoke in passing, or see someone’s bandaged arm on
the train and remain where she actually is.
