Tane is a 67-year-old widowed interior designer who came to treatment after years of living around
an experience of captivity that still intrudes into ordinary moments with startling force. He is a
Pacific Islander man, soft-spoken and careful in how he presents himself, and even in the first
sessions he appeared to prepare himself before speaking, pausing at the doorway and silently
rehearsing what he meant to say. He described this as something he now does before nearly any
interaction. If he has to meet a client, speak to a contractor, or even answer a direct question, he
runs through possible exchanges in advance, trying to avoid being caught off guard.

When he speaks about the traumatic event itself, the account comes in fragments. He was held against
his will for a period of time in a confined space by armed men, and what remains most sharply with
him is not a smooth narrative but sensory pieces: the cramped enclosure, the helpless waiting, and
above all the sound of men shouting in Arabic nearby. At points he can state the facts in a flat,
almost detached way, as if he is describing something that happened to someone else. Then, when he
tries to continue, he loses the thread completely. He stops mid-sentence, apologizes, presses his
lips together, and says he cannot explain it clearly. What returns most vividly are the sounds. When
memories come back, he does not only remember that there was shouting; he hears it in his head again
with an immediacy that makes the present room recede for a moment.

In the months after he was released, he found that the memory arrived suddenly and without
invitation. He could be driving, making coffee, or looking over fabric samples, and the event would
come to mind with no warning. Sometimes what accompanied it was not even an image but the internal
replay of raised voices in Arabic, and his body would react before he had time to orient himself. He
described a jolt through his chest and shoulders, as if danger had already been established before
his mind had caught up. He did not experience these moments as simple remembrance. To him they felt
like signs that he was still not safe enough, still too exposed.

Over time, his world narrowed in practical ways. As an interior designer, much of his work involves
entering unfamiliar homes, meeting clients in offices, and visiting cramped elevators, storage
rooms, and partially finished interiors. These became increasingly difficult for him. He started
refusing projects in high-rise buildings because he did not want to use enclosed elevators. If a
client asked to meet in a compact conference room, he would try to change the venue or cancel
altogether. He became selective about site visits and eventually began avoiding work more broadly,
telling himself he was scaling back with age, though he knew that fear was guiding many of those
decisions. The consequences accumulated. He lost contracts, responded slowly to longstanding
clients, and took on fewer collaborative jobs than before.

In any room, Tane scans for where he can sit with his back to the wall and a full view of the
entrance. He does this in waiting rooms, restaurants, design presentations, and even in friends’
homes. If the only open chair leaves him exposed, he feels unsafe in a way that is immediate and
physical. He notices exits automatically and dislikes anyone approaching from behind. Emotional
vulnerability is potent in much the same way. If a conversation begins to feel personal, or if he
senses himself becoming moved, the same alarm rises in him. He then becomes more deliberate, more
scripted, as though carefully chosen words might keep him protected.

He lives alone now after being widowed, and he described how much easier it has become to stay in
controlled spaces and limit contact. Yet the strong negative feelings remain close to the surface:
fear most obviously, but also anger, shame, and guilt that he struggles to put into words. At times
he talks about the captivity with a striking numbness, then later in the same session appears shaken
by a brief sound in the hallway that resembles a barked command. What unsettles him is the
unpredictability of it all—the way he can feel detached one moment and then overtaken the next.

He finally sought help after turning down another substantial project because the client’s office
was in a building with a small elevator and windowless meeting rooms. He recognized that his life
had become organized around not feeling trapped, not being surprised, not having to speak without
preparation. What he fears most is that he will remain governed by these reactions for the rest of
his life, shrinking further from work and from other people. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not
to erase what happened, but to be able to enter a room, take a meeting, and speak as himself without
first having to defend against the past.
