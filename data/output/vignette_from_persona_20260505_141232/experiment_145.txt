At 49, Linh Tran came to treatment after years of trying to contain her life by narrowing it. She
was a divorced Vietnamese American woman who worked in IT support for a large office complex, and
she spoke about her military deployment sparingly, as if even naming parts of it might bring it too
close. When she did describe the event that stayed with her, she did so in clipped, practical
detail. She had been in a combat zone on a day when aircraft were moving overhead and the air had
already carried the acrid residue of burning fuel and scorched material. What remained most vivid to
her was not a broad sequence but a set of sensory moments: the low chopping sound of helicopters
crossing close enough to feel in her chest, and a burnt smell that seemed to coat the back of her
throat. In her memory, those sensations meant immediate danger. Even now, she spoke as someone who
had learned that if she smelled burning or heard aircraft overhead, something terrible could be
about to happen.

In the months after she returned home, Linh did not talk much about the deployment or what followed.
She had come to believe that talking about it would open something she would not be able to shut
again. When people asked broad questions about her service, she gave flat, efficient answers and
redirected the conversation. Over time, this became less a choice than a rule she lived by. She also
began avoiding situations likely to stir strong emotion. She skipped films, family arguments, tense
conversations, and even ordinary gatherings if she thought she might feel too much. To her, strong
feeling itself seemed dangerous; once she started to get upset, she was not sure where it would
stop.

Her daily work in IT support exposed her to reminders she could not fully control. The office sat
beneath a flight path, and on some days aircraft noise pressed through the building unexpectedly.
When that happened, Linh’s body reacted before she had time to think. Her heart slammed, her muscles
tightened, and she would go cold and then hot in quick succession. If a burnt smell drifted in from
overheated wiring, a break room toaster, or construction outside, she could feel herself pulled
instantly into alarm. She would stop what she was doing and scan the area, looking for the source,
orienting herself toward exits, checking corridors and doorways. Coworkers noticed that she startled
easily and often turned to look behind her in the middle of routine interactions. Linh did this
constantly in hallways, parking lots, and even at her desk if someone approached from the rear. She
was certain that failing to check was how people got caught off guard.

This vigilance followed her everywhere. In ordinary public places, if a stranger came toward her too
directly, she felt threatened, as though an approach that others would dismiss as harmless might
conceal danger. She altered where she stood in lines, chose seats with a clear view of entrances,
and monitored anyone moving into her peripheral vision. She knew other people did not understand why
she kept looking over her shoulder, but for Linh it felt irresponsible not to. The world seemed full
of moments when someone could come up behind her if she let her attention drift.

When reminders broke through despite all this, Linh sometimes used pain to interrupt her own mind.
She described hurting herself physically not as a wish to die, but as a way to stop the flood of
images and bodily panic when they rose too sharply. She spoke about this with visible shame and
little elaboration, as though even admitting it violated the strict control she tried to maintain.
The pattern left her increasingly isolated. At work she kept interactions brief and technical,
avoiding any personal conversation that might lead to questions. Outside work, she had grown distant
from others because so much of her effort went into staying composed and steering clear of anything
emotionally charged.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was not one dramatic collapse but the accumulation of these
moments in the place where she spent most of her days. After a burnt electrical smell spread through
the office during a routine equipment problem, just as aircraft noise passed overhead, she had such
an intense physical reaction that she had to leave her station abruptly. Afterward she felt exposed
and deeply tired of living in a state of readiness. She presented for treatment still reluctant to
discuss the combat event itself and wary of what therapy might stir up. What frightened her most was
losing control of the memories and reactions she had spent years containing. At the same time, she
said she wanted “one place” where she would not have to keep scanning, bracing, and hiding how much
effort ordinary days required.
