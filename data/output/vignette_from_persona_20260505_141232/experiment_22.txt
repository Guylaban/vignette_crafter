Nadia is a 50-year-old Middle Eastern American woman who came to treatment after years of living as
if danger might break through at any moment. She works as a social worker, a role that fits with how
carefully she watches over other people, but it also places her every day in hallways, waiting
rooms, apartment buildings, and unfamiliar neighborhoods where she finds herself automatically
surveying exits, voices, and sudden movements. She is divorced and lives alone, and by the time she
sought help she described herself as exhausted by how much of her life was organized around staying
unnoticed, staying composed, and staying ready.

Nadia came to the United States as a refugee after fleeing political violence in her country of
origin. When she speaks about that period, what stands out is not a single abstract idea of danger
but the felt reality of being visible and therefore vulnerable. She remembers moving through streets
where people watched one another closely, where being identified, questioned, or singled out could
carry terrible consequences. During the period when she was trying to leave, she learned to keep her
head down, cover herself in loose, concealing clothing, and make herself as unremarkable as
possible. She recalls the bodily certainty that attention from the wrong person could mean that
safety was over. In crowded public places she learned to track doorways, faces, and shifts in tone
around her, staying alert for the first sign that something was wrong. Even after she reached the
United States, those habits did not remain in the past as memories of what she once had to do. They
continued as the way she moved through the world.

Over the years, Nadia built a life that from the outside looked stable and deeply competent. She
trained as a social worker, raised herself into a profession centered on helping vulnerable people,
and maintained the disciplined, self-contained manner that colleagues often read as calm
professionalism. Inside, she did not experience it as calm. She said that she was almost always
monitoring. When she entered her office building in the morning, she noticed who was near the
entrance, who was standing too still, who seemed agitated in the waiting room, whether the stairwell
door was propped open, and how quickly she could leave if she needed to. If a voice rose sharply in
the hallway or someone approached her unexpectedly from behind, her body reacted before she had time
to think, with a jolt of alarm as if something dangerous were already underway. She knew, even in
the moment, that many of these situations were ordinary and that nothing had actually happened, but
that knowledge did not stop the surge.

Nadia also described arranging herself physically to avoid being noticed. She routinely chose dark,
loose, concealing clothes and avoided anything that felt bright, fitted, or attention-drawing. She
did not present this as fashion or modesty alone; to her it felt prudent, nearly self-evident.
Blending in reduced the chance that eyes would land on her. Being less visible meant being less
exposed. On difficult days at work, especially when she knew she would be visiting an unfamiliar
home or meeting with a volatile client, she was even more deliberate about this, checking her
reflection not for appearance but for whether she could pass through a space without attracting
notice.

Just as striking was how carefully she tried to avoid situations that might bring up strong emotion.
She had become skilled at steering away from anything that could open her up too much: certain
conversations, news from the Middle East, public commemorations, emotional films, even moments in
which clients’ stories echoed too closely with displacement or fear. She would redirect sessions,
keep herself task-focused, and later tell herself she was simply staying professional. In her
personal life this narrowing had consequences. Friends experienced her as guarded. Her adult
relationships felt constrained by how quickly she pulled back from conversations that might leave
her overwhelmed. After her divorce, she became more solitary, telling herself that solitude was
easier than being emotionally exposed.

Sleep offered little relief. Nadia said she often woke abruptly in the night and, before she was
fully conscious, was already checking whether she was safe. She listened for sounds in the
apartment, checked locks, looked toward windows, and only then tried to settle again. Even on quiet
nights, in a familiar home, she could not truly relax. She described lying in bed with her body held
tight, aware that she was in America, in her own apartment, with no immediate threat present, yet
unable to feel what she intellectually knew. Safety remained something she verified repeatedly
rather than something she inhabited.

What finally brought her to seek help was not a single crisis but the accumulation of strain. She
had begun to notice how often she startled at harmless events during work visits, how much energy
went into scanning every setting, and how little room remained in her life for ease, pleasure, or
closeness. A recent moment in a clinic waiting area, when a sudden noise sent her heart racing and
left her shaken in front of coworkers, embarrassed her enough that she could no longer dismiss it as
simple vigilance. She came in saying she was tired of living “ready all the time.” What frightened
her most was the sense that she might never be able to let her guard down, even in places that were
objectively safe. What she hoped for, cautiously, was not to forget what happened, but to stop
feeling that every ordinary moment might turn dangerous without warning.
