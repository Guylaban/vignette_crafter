for being precise, fast, and composed under pressure. She lived alone, was single, and had returned
to the United States after spending time in a civilian area that became an active war zone. When she
tried to describe what happened there, her speech would stall. She would say she knew what happened,
that she had not forgotten it, but that the words would not come in any orderly way. What she
carried instead were a handful of sharply bounded images, as if the whole experience had been cut
into still photographs: the flash and noise of an explosion, a bicycle thrown at an angle she still
could not make sense of, and the sight of human body parts in the street. Between those images,
whole stretches of time felt absent to her. She was left with the unsettling sense that the worst
thing that had ever happened to her existed as disconnected pieces, with the rest missing.

In the moment itself, what terrified Aisha most was the abruptness with which ordinary life turned
unrecognizable. Civilian movement had still been continuing around her. A bicycle nearby had been
part of the everyday scene just seconds before it became linked in her mind with the blast. After
the explosion, she registered the sound first as a violent bang that seemed to split the world open,
and then the visual fragments fixed themselves: the bicycle, the exposed body parts, the ruined
street. Later she could never tell the sequence cleanly. She knew there had been fear, confusion,
and the immediate understanding that people had been torn apart, but when she tried to narrate it
she felt as though she was reaching toward blank space around those pictures.

In the months after she returned home, those same elements appeared in her daily life with a force
that seemed disproportionate to anyone who had not been there. A bicycle chained to a rack outside
an office building could stop her in place. She would catch sight of the wheel or handlebars and
feel pulled back toward those images before she even fully knew what she was reacting to. Sudden
bang sounds were especially difficult. In New York streets and office corridors, a dropped metal
cart, construction noise, or a door slamming could make her body go rigid while her mind filled with
that single moment of explosion. Visual reminders involving specific body parts were also potent. A
glimpse of an injured hand, a bare leg at an odd angle on a subway platform, even an anatomy image
on a screen at work could bring on the same sick, jolting recognition, as if her mind were matching
the present to the fragments she already carried.

At work, the changes became harder to hide. Auditing had once depended on exactly the capacities
Aisha valued in herself: sustained concentration, clear language, and the ability to hold complex
information together. Now she had come to believe she was no longer capable of doing the things that
had defined her. In meetings she could still produce competent work, but privately she felt
diminished and mechanical, as though she were only performing the outline of her former self. When a
delivery bike flashed past the office windows or a loud bang echoed from nearby construction, she
would lose the thread of what she was reviewing and sit motionless over spreadsheets she used to
move through quickly. She did not explain much to coworkers. It felt easier to become impersonal, to
keep interactions strictly functional. She described herself as feeling like an object rather than a
person, as if she were something to be moved through the day, not someone fully living it.

Outside work, the numbness frightened her almost as much as the memories. She did not describe
herself as constantly overwhelmed; instead she often felt dead inside. Friends noticed she was

social life, but lately it meant she could return home and pass whole evenings in silence without
having to pretend. She found herself avoiding the effort of explanation because she could not form a
coherent account anyway. The event remained unspeakable not because it was absent, but because it
was present in images that would not become language.

frozen on the sidewalk, staring at the messenger’s legs with a sense of horror she could not shake.
She later sat in the restroom at work, unable to return to her desk for nearly an hour, ashamed and
certain that something essential in her had been lost. Coming to treatment, Aisha said she wanted to
know whether she could ever feel like a person again. What she feared most was that these fragments
were all that remained of her and that the capable, fully alive version of herself was gone for
