Omar Haddad was 30 when he was referred for treatment, a divorced Middle Eastern American man who
worked as a tax inspector and had begun to organize his life around not being inside a moving
vehicle. The collision had happened at night after he left a work-related dinner where several
people had been drinking. Omar had not been drinking himself, but he remembered the sharp smell of
alcohol inside the other car and on the breath of the man who staggered on the roadside afterward.
What stayed with him most was the instant before impact: headlights veering where they should not
have been, the shape of a car like the one that struck him filling his windshield, and the
conviction that there was no time left to do anything useful. In recounting it, he returned to the
image rather than a full narrative, as if the event existed in flashes: the car coming at him, metal
folding, a loud cracking sound, and that heavy alcohol smell mixed with airbag dust.

In the weeks after the crash, Omar found that certain moments seemed to pull that scene directly
into the present. Passing a damaged car on the road could stop him cold; he said it was not simply
that he remembered the crash, but that the image arrived all at once, vivid and close, as if he were
back in front of the windshield waiting for impact. If he caught the smell of alcohol on someone in
a grocery line or outside a restaurant, his body tightened before he had time to think. Similar cars
were especially difficult. When he noticed one in traffic or parked near his apartment complex, he
would stare at it, already braced, scanning its dents, its angle, the person getting in. He had
become convinced that ordinary attention was no longer enough, that he had to stay on guard because
danger could appear in an instant and because, in his view, he had already learned what happened
when he failed to anticipate it.

That sense of threat spread into daily life. Unexpected or loud sounds made him jump hard enough to
embarrass him at work. A dropped file in the office, a door slamming in the hallway, a car horn
outside the county building where he conducted inspections could send a jolt through him. He
described himself as superalert all day, always listening for something sharp or sudden. Colleagues
noticed that he startled easily and laughed it off as stress, but Omar experienced it as proof that
something in him had changed. He had come to believe he could no longer rely on himself. Before the
collision he had thought of himself as competent, careful, and steady, someone who managed details
for a living and took pride in being dependable. Now he was certain those qualities no longer
protected him, and he questioned whether he was capable of doing the things that had defined him as
an adult man living independently after his divorce.

Because driving or even riding in a car felt intolerable, Omar began arranging his days around
avoidance. He delayed field visits, traded assignments when he could, and made excuses about
scheduling conflicts rather than explain that being a passenger was nearly as bad as driving
himself. When in-office duties were available, he clung to them. On days when travel was
unavoidable, he became tense hours beforehand and sometimes cancelled at the last minute. This
started to affect how he was seen at work; a job that had once rested on mobility and routine site
visits became harder to perform, and Omar privately took that as more evidence that he was no longer
capable.

Outside work, he spent long stretches on his phone, gaming or scrolling late into the night because
silence left too much room for the crash imagery to return. He described it less as enjoyment than
as escape, a way to keep his mind occupied until he was exhausted enough to sleep. He also began
eating less. Sometimes he skipped meals entirely, saying that restricting food gave him a strange
sense of control on days when he otherwise felt detached and ineffective. His routines narrowed.
Friends invited him out, but he often declined if it meant riding with someone else, and even when
he did go, the smell of alcohol in bars or on people passing by made him want to leave immediately.

What distressed him most was not only fear, but a deadened quality he felt in himself. He said he no
longer trusted his own reactions and was afraid he would never feel normal emotions again. Even
moments that should have brought relief or pleasure seemed flattened. By the time he sought help,
the strain had become difficult to hide: work travel was a recurring problem, his eating had become
irregular enough that he felt run down, and whole evenings disappeared into his phone while his life
seemed to shrink around him. He came in after realizing that a routine sight on the road—a wrecked
car being towed, a smell of beer from a man walking past, the glimpse of a car like the one from the
crash—could still overtake him so completely. What he wanted, cautiously, was to feel like himself
again. What frightened him was the possibility that the man he had been before the collision was
gone for good.
