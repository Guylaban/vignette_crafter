routines that made him feel adult: taking the same train into the city, wearing his badge at a
startup office with glass meeting rooms, staying late to refine mockups before a product sprint. The
stalking began in a way that, to him at first, felt too ambiguous to call anything. He noticed the
same man more than once outside his apartment building and again near the coffee shop where he
stopped before work. Then the sightings spread into the shape of his week. The man was across the
street when Kofi left the office after dark, seated at the far end of a train car, standing near the
entrance of the grocery store he used on Sundays. What made it frightening was not only recognition
but the accumulating certainty of it. Kofi would look up and feel the jolt of seeing that familiar
face already watching him, as if parts of his day were no longer his own.

Over time he began to move through ordinary spaces with the sense that anything could happen without
warning. On evenings when he stayed late at the office to finish interface revisions, he found
himself checking the reflections in the lobby glass before stepping outside. If someone paused too
long near the entrance or seemed to be on the same block for more than a minute, his whole body
tightened. He had come to believe that the world was fundamentally unsafe, not in an abstract way
but in the practical geography of sidewalks, train platforms, apartment hallways, and office doors.
The places that had once felt predictable now seemed full of openings through which danger could
enter.

What stayed with him most from that period was the feeling of being studied. He remembered trying to
convince himself he was overreacting, then seeing the man again in a setting that should have had
nothing to do with the earlier encounters and feeling his stomach drop. In his mind, that repetition
meant something: nowhere was protected simply because it should have been. Since then, Kofi had
become certain that he had to stay especially alert because you never know what can happen next. He
described scanning before unlocking his apartment door, checking behind him on the walk from the
station, and choosing seats in public places where he could watch entrances. At work, during design
reviews, part of his attention drifted toward the corridor outside the conference room whenever
footsteps slowed near the glass. Even while discussing fonts or user flows, he was tracking
movement, exits, and the possibility of being followed again.

Alongside that vigilance was a steady current of self-blame that he found hard to put down. He had
come to believe the stalking had happened because of the way he acted. He replayed his own behavior
repeatedly, wondering whether he had been too visible, too predictable, too polite, too unaware. If
he had changed his route earlier, if he had not kept such regular hours, if he had noticed sooner
what was happening, maybe none of it would have continued. He did not present these thoughts as
guesses; he carried them like conclusions. When friends told him it was not his fault, he nodded
without conviction. To him, their reassurance missed the point that his choices had made him easy to
track.

In the months that followed, that logic narrowed his life. He varied his commute compulsively and
sometimes left work early or lingered long after others had gone so he would not be outside at the
“wrong” time. He stopped going to the same café near the office because being a regular there now
felt reckless. At home he became cautious about routines, delaying errands or taking longer routes
for no reason other than the fear of being predictable. The cost of this was visible. He was often
tired at work after spending so much energy monitoring his surroundings, and his concentration
slipped during tasks that once absorbed him. Colleagues noticed he was quieter and less spontaneous.
He was single, and where he had once imagined city life opening outward for him, his days now felt
organized around not being caught off guard.

By the time he sought help, Kofi was exhausted by the effort of trying to stay one step ahead of a
threat that, in his mind, had proved itself capable of appearing anywhere. What finally brought him
in was not one dramatic incident but the recognition that he no longer moved through his own life
with any sense of ease. Even on uneventful days, he was braced. He wanted help because he was tired
of feeling that every street and doorway required calculation, but what frightened him most was the
idea of letting his guard down and being wrong. At the same time, he still hoped it might be
possible to work, commute, and live like other people his age without feeling that danger was
waiting just outside the frame.
