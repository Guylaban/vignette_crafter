Marisol Alvarez is a 39-year-old Army soldier who came to treatment after months of insisting to
herself that she should be able to handle this on her own. She is married, disciplined, and used to
functioning under pressure, which made it harder for her to make sense of how much she had changed
since living through a natural disaster several years earlier. The event occurred during a period of
severe storm conditions, when she was in a coastal area affected by sudden, destructive flooding.
She still describes the weather that day with unusual precision: the thick, heavy air, the sky
turning a bruised color by late afternoon, the sense that the rain was no longer ordinary rain but
something advancing. What stays with her most is the combination of noise and helplessness—the
pounding wind, crashing debris, sirens, shouted orders, and the sound of structures giving way while
water rose around her.

During the disaster, Marisol found herself trapped in rapidly changing conditions where every
decision seemed to be overtaken by something larger than she could control. Emergency responders and
police were present in the chaos, moving people, blocking roads, shouting directions, deciding where
civilians could and could not go. She remembers seeing uniforms and flashing lights while feeling
reduced to a body to be moved, counted, and directed. That memory has remained intensely personal
for her. When she speaks about it now, what comes through is not only fear but humiliation. She had
always thought of herself as capable and effective, yet in those hours she felt stripped of that
identity, as if she were no longer a person with judgment or agency but simply an object in the path
of danger.

In the months afterward, the world no longer felt neutral. Similar weather conditions were
especially potent. If the air grew heavy before a storm, if rain came down in sheets, or if the
light outside took on the same dim cast she remembered from that day, her body reacted before she
could reason with it. Loud noises also hit her hard: a slammed door, a metal crash in the barracks,
sudden thunder, even ordinary commotion that others barely noticed. She would jump sharply and then
feel ashamed that she had done so. The presence of police could affect her in a different but
equally immediate way. Seeing patrol cars near a road closure, hearing a police officer raise a
voice in a crowd, or noticing officers directing people at a public event brought back that same
trapped feeling from the disaster, when movement and safety had seemed to belong to other people’s
decisions.

As time went on, Marisol adapted her life around a constant sense that danger might break through at
any moment. Whenever she entered a room—whether a briefing space on base, a restaurant with her
husband, or a waiting area—she had to identify exits before she could settle. She would choose seats
that left her with a clear path out and kept the room in view. In unfamiliar places she found
herself tracking doors, windows, stairwells, and bottlenecks almost automatically. Even on duty,
where vigilance was valued, she knew her attention had taken on a different quality. She was not
just alert; she was continuously scanning, reading people’s movements, listening for shifts in
sound, and checking her surroundings for signs that something was about to go wrong. Ordinary sounds
made her jerk upright or turn suddenly, and the quick surge in her body left her tense and
embarrassed.

She had come to believe that these reactions meant something deeply personal and damning. More than
once she said, in essence, that if she were stronger, this would have passed by now. Being a soldier
sharpened that judgment. She measured herself against an internal standard of steadiness and found
herself wanting. She saw her own startle, her need to map exits, and her inability to relax in bad
weather as proof that she was “a lousy coper.” Alongside that self-criticism was a broader, bleaker
conclusion: bad things could happen without warning and she could not stop them. When storms rolled
in or authority figures began directing movement, what frightened her most was the sense that events
could once again overtake her completely. She also struggled with a painful feeling of
depersonalization, describing moments when she felt less like a full human being than something to
be managed or acted upon.

These patterns gradually affected her marriage and work. Her husband noticed that she was never
fully at ease in public and that she seemed preoccupied with where to go if something happened.
Small outings became tense negotiations around seating, parking, and leaving quickly if the weather
changed. At work, she kept functioning, but the effort of constantly monitoring her surroundings
left her exhausted and irritable by the end of the day. She finally sought help after a recent spell
of stormy weather coincided with a loud metallic crash during a routine workday. She startled so
violently that several people turned to look, and later, passing police near the installation
entrance, she felt the same old wave of dread and powerlessness. What pushed her into treatment was
not a single new symptom but the fear that this was who she had become: someone always bracing,
always scanning, never fully in command of herself. What she hopes for now is modest but urgent—to
feel like a person again, not just a body preparing for the next bad thing.
