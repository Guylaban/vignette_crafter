Nadia Haddad is a 38-year-old divorced school principal who came to treatment carrying herself with
the controlled vigilance of someone who does not expect any place to stay safe for long. Before she
could settle into the therapist’s office, she noted the door, the second exit through the adjoining
reception area, and the windows. She said she always does this now, needing to know how she would
get out before she can pay attention to anything else. Even at home she moves through her house this
way, checking locks, noticing sightlines, listening for sounds in the yard, feeling on guard in
rooms that used to feel ordinary.

Her trauma occurred during combat deployment several years earlier. Nadia does not tell the story in
a smooth sequence. When she tries, the account breaks apart and she becomes frustrated, saying she
knows the pieces are there but cannot put them in the right order. She remembers being in a convoy
and then, in the next breath, being outside the vehicle; she is unsure what came first, the blast or
the shouting. What is most immediate is not a clear timeline but sound. When the memory comes back,
she hears it again in her head: the violent crack, metal striking metal, someone yelling commands,
someone else screaming. Those sounds arrive with such force that for a moment they seem closer than
the room she is actually in. She also remembers the hot surge of anger that followed—anger at the
attack, at the chaos, at people around her, at how exposed and breakable everyone suddenly was. Even
now, when the memory surfaces, rage is what rises fastest, sharp enough that she has to clench her
jaw and grip the arms of her chair.

In the months after she returned home, Nadia found that the world no longer arranged itself into
safe and unsafe places in any predictable way. The combat zone had made total sense to her in one
respect: danger was real, vigilance was useful, and trust could be costly. Back in the United
States, she had expected to resume civilian life, but she found herself unable to relax into it. She
had come to believe that people could not be trusted, not strangers and not even people who
presented themselves as helpful. She watched others carefully for inconsistency, hidden motives,
carelessness. In meetings at school she sat where she could see the door and scanned the room while
teachers were talking. At assemblies she found herself calculating where students would be moved if
something happened and which hallway would clear fastest. These habits fit easily enough with some
parts of being a principal—being responsible, being prepared—but for Nadia they were not just
professional diligence. They were the only way she could feel barely comfortable.

Her daily work repeatedly brushes against what happened to her. Sudden noises in a school
building—the bang of a heavy door, a dropped stack of books over the intercom static, children
shouting down a hallway—can make her body tense before she has time to think. On those days the
auditory fragments of the deployment return, and with them fear, horror, anger, guilt, and shame,
all felt intensely and often all at once. She is ashamed of how quickly she reacts internally and of
how much effort it takes to appear composed in front of staff and students. She has come to see
these reactions as proof that she is, in her words, “a lousy coper,” someone who should have been
able to handle what happened better and should certainly be able to live normally by now.

Over time this belief has narrowed her life. At work she keeps interactions efficient and guarded,
delegating more than she used to and avoiding the kind of informal trust-building with teachers and
parents that the role once required naturally of her. At home, being alone has not brought relief;
it has simply meant there is no one else to share the burden of watching. After her divorce, the
house became even quieter, but not more peaceful. She moves through it alert, checking routes out,
unable to feel fully off duty. What troubles her almost as much as the fear is the deadened sense
that nothing in her life feels meaningful anymore. She still does what needs to be done and is
regarded as competent, but the work that once mattered to her deeply now often feels hollow, as if
she is performing the outline of a life rather than living one.

She sought help after recognizing how little of herself remained available to her work and
relationships. A recent incident at school, when a loud crash in the hallway instantly filled her
head with the sounds of the convoy attack, left her shaken and furious for hours, long after the
building had settled. What finally frightened her was not only the intensity of the reaction but the
thought that this might be all her life would ever be: scanning exits, mistrusting people, and
moving through days that no longer feel meaningful. What she hopes for is modest but urgent—to be in
a room, at school or at home, without first planning how to escape it, and to believe that her
reactions do not define her as weak.
