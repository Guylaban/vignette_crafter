acutely alert to men around him, especially if he might end up alone with one. What frightened him
most was not just remembering what had happened, but the sense that it could happen again if he
failed to notice danger quickly enough. He had come to believe that unfamiliar places were
especially unsafe because he could not judge exits, other people, or what might happen behind a

This has shaped how he moves through daily life. At work, he is expected to meet clients and consult
with colleagues in offices and conference rooms, but certain ordinary moments are hard for him to

shock through his body before he has registered that it is only someone arriving late to a meeting.
If a male coworker asks to speak with him alone, Kwame feels his chest tighten and his attention
narrow to where the man is standing, how close he is, and where the door is. He tries to keep
interactions brief or position himself so he is not trapped in the room. When new assignments
require him to go somewhere unfamiliar, he hesitates or looks for reasons to delay. To him this
feels sensible rather than excessive; if he does not know the place, he cannot assess the danger,
and that uncertainty feels intolerable.

other people do not seem to understand. A man stepping toward him to ask a question on the sidewalk

person means harm. He avoids unfamiliar neighborhoods and social situations, especially if he cannot

needs to go, then comes home.

hallway at work, a door banging, or being alone with a man makes it feel as though his body already

thrown back into the assault.
