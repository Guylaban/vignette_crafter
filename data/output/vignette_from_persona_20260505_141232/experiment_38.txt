Mereana Taito is a 66-year-old American woman of Pacific Islander background who came to treatment
after years of trying to live around memories she still could not fully tell. Divorced and living
alone, she works as a curriculum designer, a job she has kept partly because much of it can be done
from home. When she first spoke about the event, she did not begin with a smooth account. She would
stop, go quiet, and say she knew what happened but could not find words that matched it. What she
did manage came in pieces.

She had been deployed in a combat setting many years ago. The parts she returns to most abruptly are
sensory and positional rather than orderly: the chopping sound of helicopters overhead, the pressure
of command around her, the felt certainty that other people held power over where she moved and
whether she was safe. In one fragment she is being directed sharply by someone above her in rank,
with no room to question or slow down; in another there is aircraft noise so loud it seems to fill
her chest; in another she is aware of herself moving because she is told to move, not because she
has chosen it. When she tries to place these moments into a single sequence, the story slips. She
says the whole thing feels so extreme that it is hard to believe it actually happened to her, as if
she is describing scenes from someone else’s life and then being shocked to remember they are hers.

In the years after returning home, the memory did not stay in the past in any settled way. It came
to mind suddenly, without warning, in the middle of ordinary tasks. A helicopter passing overhead
could stop her in place before she even consciously knew why. The sound would pull up those
disconnected pieces so quickly that for a moment the present thinned out. She also found herself
reacting strongly in situations that involved hierarchy or control. Meetings with forceful
administrators or clients at work were especially difficult if someone spoke in a way that suggested
they had total authority and expected immediate compliance. As a curriculum designer, she is often
in conference rooms with school leaders and district staff, and she has developed a habit of
choosing a chair nearest the door before anyone else sits down. She tells herself it is practical,
but she knows she is scanning exits the moment she enters.

Over time, her world became smaller. She began avoiding going out unless she had to. She ordered
what she could online, turned down invitations, and preferred workdays that allowed her to remain at
home with her materials rather than travel to schools or district offices. Even when she did go out,
she arranged herself carefully in space, staying near an exit in cafés, workshops, and waiting
rooms. The habit has shaped her relationships as much as her routines. Friends from earlier parts of
her life gradually stopped asking her to join them when she so often declined. Mereana feels this as
a painful separateness. She looks at other people moving easily through the world and feels set
apart from them, as if they share some ordinary trust she does not have.

She is also careful not to let herself feel too much. Strong emotion of any kind—fear, grief, anger,
even excitement—seems risky to her, something that could break open those pieces she struggles to
contain. So she flattens things when she can, changes the subject in her own mind, and keeps herself
focused on tasks. Yet this control has left her unsure of herself. She has come to doubt whether she
can trust herself to do the right thing when pressure rises. In work settings, if a conflict
escalates or a senior person becomes directive, she can feel a familiar uncertainty take over: she
must stay alert, must keep watch, must not relax. She describes living as though she has to be on
guard all the time.

What finally brought her to seek help was a recent incident during an in-person curriculum meeting
downtown. A helicopter passed low overhead while a district official was pressing the group for
immediate changes. Mereana felt the memory surge up in jagged pieces, and although she stayed in the
room, she could barely follow what was being said. Afterward she sat in her car for a long time,
shaken by how quickly she had lost her footing. She told herself she could not keep organizing her
life around doorways, weather, and who might take command of a room. Still, what frightens her most
about treatment is being asked to say plainly what happened when words have always failed her. What
she hopes, quietly, is not to become someone else, but to be able to remain in the life she has
without feeling that any aircraft sound or any powerful voice can abruptly take it away.
