Laura is a 48-year-old single woman who works in social ministry, much of her week spent moving
between church offices, food pantry spaces, hospital rooms, and community meetings where she helps
people in crisis find practical support. The event she returns to in session is a life-threatening
car accident that happened while she was being transported by someone else. What remains most vivid
to her is not only the violence of the impact but the fact that she had no control over what was
happening. She describes the sickening moment of realizing the car was committed to the collision
and that she could not stop it, get out, or make her body do anything useful. When she speaks about
it, the image that comes back most sharply is of cars smashed together at impossible angles, the
kind of scene she still cannot pass on the road without feeling herself go rigid.

In the weeks afterward, Laura found that reminders were not abstract. Seeing wrecked cars, even in
ordinary traffic after a minor fender-bender, could pull her immediately into the sense that
catastrophe was right there again. Feeling physically unwell was another powerful cue. If she became
dizzy, nauseated, weak, or even vaguely “off,” her mind went straight to danger. She had come to
feel that her body could not be counted on anymore, that physical sensations were warnings she had
to obey. Situations that put her body under stress began to feel especially risky, so she started
pulling back from exertion and anything that left her heart pounding or her muscles strained. Rest
did not bring relief either. Relaxing felt dangerous to her, as if letting her guard down would
leave her exposed to whatever might happen next. She stayed busy, alert, and braced.

Her work repeatedly places her in exactly the kinds of settings where these reactions show
themselves. Entering a hospital room, a parish hall, or a crowded service office, Laura
automatically looks for exits first. She notices doorways, stairwells, and the clearest route out
before she can focus on the conversation she is there to have. In meetings, she prefers seats near
the door and feels unsettled if someone else chooses for her or if she is expected to remain
somewhere without an easy way to leave. Being forced into anything now carries a particular charge.
Even routine pressures from work—being told to ride along to an outreach site, being assigned
somewhere without much notice, being urged to “just sit and take a breath”—can stir an immediate
inner resistance that feels larger than the moment. Other people’s reassurance does little. She says
that when someone tells her she is safe, the words do not land; she cannot make herself believe
them.

Over months, Laura’s world narrowed around transportation. She stopped driving whenever she could
and avoided being a passenger as well. This created practical problems in a job that often depends
on movement from place to place. She began rearranging her schedule, declining visits, and relying
on others to cover parts of her work that once would have been routine for her. The consequences
have been concrete and embarrassing to her. She worries that colleagues see her as difficult or
unreliable, though she rarely explains more than that travel is “hard right now.” Ordinary sounds
also catch her off guard. A dropped object in a church kitchen, a slammed car door outside the
ministry office, a sudden noise in a hospital corridor can make her jump sharply, leaving her
flushed and ashamed in front of others.

Alongside this, Laura carries a fixed sense of wrongness about what happened. She returns often to
the unfairness of it—that her life was upended in an instant and, in her view, nothing meaningful
has been done about it. She speaks about the accident with the conviction that bad things can happen
without warning and that she has no real way to stop them. What frightens her most is not only
another accident, but the idea that she could once again find herself trapped, dependent, and
physically vulnerable, with her own body proving unreliable at the very moment she needs it.

She sought help after recognizing how much of her life had become organized around preventing
exposure to the feelings she dreads. The immediate prompt was increasing difficulty carrying out her
ministry work, especially when travel or unfamiliar buildings were involved. She is tired of
planning every day around exits, transport, and whether her body feels steady enough. At the same
time, she is afraid that treatment will ask her to surrender the vigilance that feels necessary to
survive. What she hopes for, cautiously, is not to forget the accident, but to move through her days
without feeling that danger is waiting in every room, every bodily sensation, and every car on the
road.
