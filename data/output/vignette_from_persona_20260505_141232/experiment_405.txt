a busy county court. He described the disaster in fragments that arrived as pictures more than
words, and even in session he tended to speak around it at first, as if saying too much might pull
him back into it. The event had happened during severe storm conditions while he was visiting family
in a coastal area. He remembered the day less as a sequence than as a set of frozen images: the sky
turning an odd color, rain driven sideways, water rising where it should not have been, the sudden
confusion of people shouting over wind and sirens. What remained with him most strongly were a few
visual moments that seemed cut out of time—standing in floodwater that had climbed too fast, seeing
emergency vehicles trying to reach stranded residents, and the bright, clinical interior of a
hospital area where survivors were being assessed after the storm. Even now, when the memory
returned, it did not feel like recollection. He said it came back “all at once,” as if he were there
again, with the sights, sounds, bodily sensations, and smell of antiseptic becoming completely real
for a moment.

In the weeks afterward, Arun tried to carry on with the discipline that had always defined him. He
returned to work, put on his robe, heard cases, and told himself to stay focused on facts and
procedure. He was determined not to think about the disaster. If an image began to intrude—a flash
of dark water, emergency lights reflecting on wet pavement, the sharp hospital smell—he would force
his attention elsewhere, press forward with the next task, and speak even more precisely from the
bench. From the outside he appeared controlled. Internally, he was working constantly to push the
pictures back down.

Certain moments in daily life quickly became charged. Ambulance sirens were especially difficult. In
his courthouse, emergency vehicles regularly passed the building on the street below, and the sound
could cut through proceedings without warning. When that happened, Arun felt his body tense before
he had time to think. The courtroom around him could seem to drop away and be replaced by those
storm scenes: flashing lights through heavy rain, urgency, people trying to get somewhere impossible
to reach. He also became acutely aware of smell. Passing near the courthouse clinic, accompanying
someone to a hospital, or catching the clean chemical scent of antiseptic on a hallway or elevator
triggered an immediate sense that the disaster was present again. He did not experience these as
memories in the ordinary sense. They were vivid, sensory episodes, dominated by images, as if the
mind had stored the event in pictures and then projected them straight back into the room.

Weather was another problem. On days when the air felt like it had before the disaster—thick, storm-
heavy, with the particular dimness that comes before severe rain—Arun became watchful and restless.
He found it hard to settle at home, hard to sit on the couch with his wife, hard to do anything that
resembled letting his guard down. He had come to feel that relaxation itself was unsafe. If he
loosened his attention, something terrible could happen again and catch him unprepared. So even in
quiet evenings he remained mentally braced, checking forecasts, looking out the window, staying
upright when his wife urged him to rest.

Over time, he also began limiting how much he went out. He kept his routines narrow: home, court,
essential errands only. He declined social invitations and cut short family outings, especially when
bad weather threatened or if the destination involved hospitals or dense downtown traffic where
ambulances were common. He told himself he was being practical, but the pattern gradually shrank his
life. His wife noticed that he seemed absent even when physically present, always managing his
surroundings, always ready to leave. At work, the strain showed in quieter ways. He still performed
competently, but colleagues saw him becoming more rigid about breaks and less willing to step into
informal conversations in the hallway if sirens or commotion outside might interrupt.

By the time Arun sought help, he was exhausted by the effort of holding the disaster out of mind.
What finally pushed him to come was a recent storm warning during a court day: thunder rolled
outside, an ambulance passed, and for a few seconds he was no longer in his courtroom but back
inside those visual snapshots, with the smell and panic so real that he had to pause the
proceedings. He was ashamed that this had happened in public, but what frightened him more was how
fully real it had felt. He said he wanted help because he could no longer keep living “on alert”
every day. At the same time, he was afraid that if he stopped suppressing the memories, they would
overtake him completely. What he hoped for, carefully stated, was not to erase what happened but to
be able to hear a siren, smell a hospital corridor, or watch storm clouds gather without feeling as
though the disaster had returned.
