David is a 48-year-old married shopowner who came to treatment after several years of living around
a loss he still experiences as if it has not fully ended. He speaks about the event carefully at
first, circling it, then with sudden flashes of detail that make it clear how sharply it remains
with him. The trauma was the violent death of a loved one, and what stays with him is not a general
sense of grief but a set of moments that return with disturbing clarity. He can describe the
victim’s face, the setting, and the sequence of what he learned and saw with a precision that feels
to him less like remembering than being taken back into it. At times he says it is “like a film”
that starts playing without his permission. When that happens, he does not feel as though he is
thinking about something from years ago; it feels immediate, as if it is unfolding again in the
present.

In the first weeks after the death, David found himself unable to tolerate ordinary conversations
that touched on loss. If customers in his shop began talking about someone who had died, even in the
abstract or in the familiar way people exchange condolences about a neighbor or relative, he felt
his body tighten before he had fully registered why. Stories about people losing loved ones did not
stay other people’s stories for long. They pulled him toward the images he has tried so hard not to
revisit. He began stepping away from the counter under the pretense of checking inventory or taking
a phone call. He also learned to avoid places and occasions that were overtly organized around
grief. Funerals, cemeteries, and memorial gatherings came to feel unbearable. He would tell his wife
he simply could not do it, and over time there were services he did not attend and families he did
not visit because he knew the atmosphere, the flowers, the condolences, even the sight of mourners,
would bring everything up too forcefully.

What is striking is how specifically reminders of victims affect him. It is not only his own loved
one he reacts to. News stories, photographs, even passing references to someone who was harmed can
stop him cold. He feels pulled into the scene in a way that is hard to interrupt. What frightens him
most is the suddenness of it: one second he is standing in his store, ringing up a customer or
straightening a display, and the next he is caught in a vivid replay. He has come to dread that
sense of helpless transport, so he has organized more and more of his life around not thinking about
the event and not encountering anything that might stir grief. If a conversation starts going in
that direction, he shuts it down or leaves. If his wife brings up the person they lost, he becomes
distant, lights a cigarette, and moves outside. Smoking has become one of the few things he feels he
can do in the moment, something concrete to occupy his hands and mouth when he is trying to push
away what is coming up.

These habits have narrowed his life. As a shopowner, he cannot fully control what enters the store,
and this has made his workday feel unpredictable. A customer’s offhand story about a funeral, a
sympathy card on display nearby, or a local conversation about a death in town can alter the rest of
his day. He becomes tense, watchful, and emotionally flooded. He describes strong waves of fear,
anger, guilt, and shame that can come over him quickly, sometimes all mixed together. Ordinary
sounds in the shop can make him jump hard enough to embarrass him. The clatter of something dropped,
a sudden knock, the quick scrape of a chair, or unexpected movement in his peripheral vision can
make him react as though something dangerous is happening. He knows the reaction is bigger than the
situation calls for, but in the moment his body does not seem to know that. He has started
positioning himself where he can see people approach, and when someone moves suddenly near him he
recoils before he can think.

His marriage has felt the strain of these patterns. His wife has wanted to remember, talk, and
sometimes attend grief-related events that matter to their family and community; David has
increasingly refused. From his perspective, he is not being cold. He is certain that if he lets
himself go near these reminders, the memory will take over and he will lose his footing. Still, he
sees the cost. He has become more cut off, more irritable, more absent even when he is physically
present.

He finally sought help after a recent grief-related event he tried to avoid became impossible to
ignore, and he found himself overtaken again by the same immediate, vivid replay. What brought him
in was not only how painful the memory remains, but the realization that he has built his days
around escaping it and still does not feel safe from it. He says he wants “some way to keep it in
the past,” and what he fears most is that the next reminder—someone’s story in the shop, a funeral
he cannot avoid, the sight of another victim on a screen—will pull him back just as completely.
