begun to divide sharply into before and after. She is a forensic analyst, a detail-oriented woman

against the cabinet, her own hand slipping as she tried to reach him, the phone in her grip as she
called for help. What came back with those images was sound. Even now she could hear, in her head,
the emergency dispatcher’s voice asking questions she could barely answer, her own voice sounding
strange and thin, and the terrible absence of any reply from her husband as she called his name.

narrative. She would get to one of those moments—the tile under her knees, the dispatcher saying,
“Stay with me,” the silence after she shouted for him—and then stop abruptly, flooded and unable to
continue. She described the memory as if it attacked in pictures. A few seconds would appear in her
mind with unnerving clarity, and the emotion that came with them was so intense that she had to shut
down the conversation, look away, or grip the sides of the chair until it passed. She was not
remembering it as something over and finished. She was colliding with fragments she could hear and
see all over again.

language, and sudden requests for urgent review all echoed too closely the sounds and pressure of
that day in her kitchen. Some mornings she would sit in her car outside the building rehearsing the

humiliating, but going in felt unbearable.

alternative was lying in the dark with those images ready to return. Eating also became something

husband was gone. Coffee replaced breakfast, dinner was skipped, and her body thinned as her days
narrowed.
