the words came out in fragments—snatches of a moment she could not fully hold. The explosion had
happened in a crowded market while she was visiting extended family in a region she had never
expected to become a war zone. She remembered the light first, then the sound that seemed to rip the
air apart, and then the unbearable stillness that followed. What stayed with her most vividly was a
single image: a severed hand lying on the pavement next to an overturned bicycle, fingers splayed as
if still reaching for something. She had been knocked to the ground, dust and debris raining down,
but she had no clear memory of how she got back up or who helped her. The event existed for her as a
series of disconnected snapshots—the bicycle wheel still spinning, the hand, a woman’s scream that
seemed to come from far away—and the rest was a blank. She knew what had happened, could recite the
facts if pressed, but when she tried to describe it she felt her throat close, the right words
dissolving before she could shape them.

Back in the United States, Priya returned to her job at a mid-sized accounting firm. The work had
always given her a sense of competence—she was precise, thorough, someone who caught discrepancies
others missed. But in the months after the explosion, that feeling evaporated. She found herself
staring at spreadsheets, the numbers blurring, a hollow sensation settling in her chest. She had
come to believe that the person she used to be—capable, alert, fully present—was gone. What
frightened her most was the growing sense that she was no longer a person at all, but something like
an object: she felt dead inside, numb to things that once mattered, as though the explosion had
hollowed her out and left only a shell that went through the motions. This feeling of being less
than human made her withdraw from colleagues, because interacting with them only reminded her that
she could not connect the way she used to.

The triggers were unpredictable but relentless. A colleague rolling up her sleeve to show a new
watch sent Priya’s attention immediately to the exposed forearm, and in that split second the
snapshot of the severed hand flashed before her eyes. The memory was so sharp it felt more real than
the office around her, and with it came a fresh wave of numbness—an emotional shutdown that
confirmed again that she was broken. She learned to avoid looking at bare arms or hands, but the
vigilance itself exhausted her. Worse was the bicycle. Every morning she had to walk past the bike
rack near the building’s entrance, and each time she saw a handlebar or a wheel, the incomplete
picture in her mind surged forward: the bicycle on its side, the hand beside it, the rest of the
scene missing. She began arriving early to avoid seeing anyone locking up a bike, and when a
coworker mentioned a weekend ride, Priya felt her stomach drop. The memory, fragmentary as it was,
attached itself to that word—bicycle—and the sound of a bike bell in the parking lot sent her heart
pounding and her mind straight back to the market, making the hollow feeling even deeper.

The numbness itself fed the cycle. Feeling dead inside, Priya found herself scanning her environment
for potential threats, hyperaware of any sound that might be an explosion—a car backfiring, a door
slamming, even a dropped file. Each time, she braced, and each time she was left feeling more like
an object, less capable of functioning. Her manager noticed her slipping: missed details, a distant
look during meetings. Priya knew she was no longer the auditor she had been, and the certainty of
that failure made her avoid even simple interactions, because they might reveal her incompetence.
She stopped joining colleagues for lunch, stayed late to avoid the bike rack at rush hour, and spent
evenings on her couch, staring at nothing, the snapshots recurring in her mind like a film she could
not turn off.

What finally brought her to therapy was a panic attack in the office when a delivery truck backfired
outside her window. She had frozen at her desk, unable to breathe, the image of the bicycle and the
hand overwhelming her, and afterward she had to leave early. Her supervisor suggested she take some
time off, but Priya was terrified that if she stopped working she would entirely disappear into the
numb, object-like state. She came to the session hoping, though not believing, that someone could
help her find the words she had lost—and maybe, somewhere in that process, recover a sense of being
a person again.
