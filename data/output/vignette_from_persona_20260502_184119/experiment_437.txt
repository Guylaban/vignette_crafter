Harold was seventy-seven years old and still working as a delivery driver when he was assaulted
outside a customer’s garage. The man had argued with him over a misdelivered package, and then
suddenly shoved him to the concrete, then kicked him twice before his wife screamed from the doorway
and the man ran. Harold lay there for what felt like a long time, not because he was hurt badly—he
had a bruised hip and a scraped palm—but because something in him had stopped. He had always prided
himself on reading people, on knowing how to calm a tense situation. That had been his job for forty
years. Now he could not stop replaying the moment, the shock of being wrong, the way his body had
not moved fast enough, the certainty that he had missed some sign.

In the weeks afterward, Harold began to notice a new voice inside him, one that whispered *you can't
trust yourself anymore*. He had been the one who got out of the truck, after all. He had chosen the
route. He had nodded at the man. The thought that he might have misjudged everything—that his
instincts, honed over decades, were worthless—felt like the ground shifting under his feet. And it
was not just about that one incident. He found himself looking at the whole world differently: a
place where a stranger could turn violent without warning, where rules he had believed
in—politeness, respect, the basic decency of people—no longer held. His wife noticed he had stopped
talking about the news. He could not stand to hear about random attacks. It confirmed what he
already knew: the world was not the way he had thought it was, and he had no reliable compass left.

The worst part was that whenever the memory surfaced—the scrape of concrete on his hip, the man’s
voice rising—he felt a wave of panic so thick he could not breathe. He became convinced that if he
let himself think about it fully, if he really allowed the images to come, he would fall apart
entirely. He would not be able to get up. So he developed a strict rule: do not think about it. When
the memory tried to intrude, he would snap his attention to something else—the radio, his route
list, the temperature gauge on the dashboard. He kept himself busy from the moment he woke until the
moment he fell into bed, exhausted. He took extra shifts, volunteered to cover for sick coworkers,
drove longer routes. At home he cleaned out the garage, organized the pantry, reorganized his tool
chest twice. The moment he sat still, his mind began to creep back toward that garage, and he jumped
up to find another task.

There was another strategy, too, one he did not talk about. He started eating more than he used
to—not because he was hungry, but because the act of eating, of chewing and swallowing and focusing
on the taste, seemed to fill the space that the memories were trying to claim. He would come home
after a long shift and make himself a large sandwich, then another one later in the evening, then a
bowl of ice cream while he watched television with the volume turned up. His wife commented that he
had put on weight. He shrugged. He did not know how to tell her that the food was a kind of wall,
that every bite was a way of saying *I am here, in this moment, not back there*.

Over time, the strategies became his life. He worked so much that he rarely talked with his wife
about anything deeper than what was for dinner. He avoided any road that took him past the
customer’s house, even if it added twenty minutes to his day. He never told anyone at work what had
happened, because that would mean thinking about it, which he could not do. But the weight of the
suppression was wearing him down. He slept badly, woke early, and felt a constant low-level dread
that he could not name.

What finally brought him to seek help was a health scare: his doctor told him his blood pressure was
dangerously high and that his weight gain was putting strain on his heart. Harold sat in the exam
room, and something cracked. He thought about all the hours he had spent running from a single
memory, and how the running itself was now hurting him. He agreed to a referral to a therapist,
though he was not sure talking could undo what had happened. What he was most afraid of was that the
therapist would ask him to describe the assault in detail, and that he would break down right there
on the couch and never stop. But he was also afraid of what would happen to his body if he kept
going the way he was. So he went, carrying both fears like stones in his pockets, hoping that maybe,
at his age, it was not too late to find a different way.
