Leilani was sixty-seven when she finally sat in a therapist’s office, her hands folded in her lap,
her gaze fixed on a spot just above the clinician’s shoulder. She had come because her daughter,
worried by a phone call at three in the morning, had insisted. But the truth was, Leilani had been
living this way for decades.

The abuse had started in the third year of her marriage. Her husband, a respected community leader
in their Samoan-American neighborhood, would come home from a late meeting and find fault with
dinner, or the way she had arranged the living room. At first it was a shove, a slap, then a fist.
She remembered the night he had grabbed her by the hair and slammed her head against the kitchen
counter—the tile cold and hard, the blood warm on her cheek. She had stayed. She had told herself he
was stressed, that she could manage things better, that a good wife did not leave. He died of a
heart attack twelve years ago, and Leilani had felt relief and then a guilt that never quite lifted.

In the months after his death, she had come to believe that her reactions during and after the
abuse—the shaking, the compliance, the hours of silent crying—proved something fundamental. She was
a lousy coper, she decided. Other women would have left, would have fought back, would have rebuilt
their lives. She, on the other hand, had stayed, and afterward had found herself unable to feel like
the person she used to be. There was something wrong with her as a person—a defect that must have
been there all along, or the abuse wouldn't have happened, or she would have recovered by now. These
thoughts settled into her bones.

Because she felt defective, she began to avoid eye contact with everyone. At work, where she managed
the hospital's patient database, she kept her eyes on her monitor during meetings, pretending to
review data. If a colleague approached her desk, she would glance up briefly and then look away,
afraid they would see the shame in her eyes, afraid they would know she was not whole. Similarly,
she avoided any physical contact. When a coworker once touched her arm to get her attention, Leilani
flinched so visibly that the woman apologized and never did it again. That reaction only confirmed
to Leilani that she was broken, that her body could not tolerate normal human connection, and that
she was not the warm, generous person her children remembered.

The most relentless strategy was the sleep delay. She would stay up until two or three in the
morning, watching old cooking shows or scrolling through archived emails, because going to bed meant
lying in the dark with the thoughts she could not outrun. In those quiet hours, the negative
appraisals grew louder. See, you can't even handle a normal bedtime—what kind of person are you now?
Not the one you used to be. And the cycle tightened: the later she stayed awake, the more exhausted
she became, and exhaustion made the defective-self belief feel even more like an objective fact. She
began to tell herself that she no longer recognized the woman in the mirror. Identity loss became a
daily certainty.

At work, the consequences accumulated. Leilani had always been meticulous, but now she double-
checked entries obsessively, afraid of making a mistake that would reveal her incompetence. She
avoided the break room because it meant small talk and the risk of accidental touch. Her supervisor
noticed she was pulling away from team projects, but attributed it to her age and impending
retirement. Leilani herself did not connect her avoidance of touch and eye contact to her belief
that she was defective; she simply felt that she was being careful, that she was protecting herself
and others from the uncomfortable truth of who she had become.

What finally brought her to seek help was not insight but a concrete event: a younger colleague had
reached out to shake her hand after a successful database migration, and Leilani had pulled back so
quickly that she knocked over a stack of papers. The colleague's face registered hurt and confusion,
and Leilani could not stop replaying that expression. She came to therapy afraid that she was
becoming impossible to be around, but even more afraid that the therapy itself would confirm what
she already knew—that she was a bad coper, that her identity was gone, that there was indeed
something fundamentally wrong with her. She sat in the chair, still not making eye contact, and
waited to be told she was broken.
