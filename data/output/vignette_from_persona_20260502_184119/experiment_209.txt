Leilani, a 66-year-old shopowner of Pacific Islander descent, still remembers the late afternoon two
years ago when a protest escalated outside her small gift shop. Police officers stormed in,
shouting, and she was shoved to the concrete floor, handcuffed, and beaten. The concussion left her
with gaps in her memory—she cannot recall how long she lay there or who finally helped her up. What
she does remember feels so extreme that it hardly seems real, and she struggles to put any of it
into words, as if the event exists only in images and sensations she cannot name.

Now the same time of day—the golden light slanting through her shop windows—triggers a wave of
dizziness that leaves her breathless. That dizziness, in turn, brings back a fragment of the memory:
the feel of the cold floor, the weight on her back, but nothing coherent. The more she feels watched
by customers or passersby, the more the memory feels like a story that happened to someone else.
That sense of unreality makes her certain she is going crazy. She has come to believe she is a lousy
coper, inadequate for normal life, because she cannot even trust her own mind.

To manage these experiences, Leilani has developed a set of strategies. She closes her shop early
when the time-of-day trigger approaches, avoiding being in the dark. She avoids the anniversary of
the protest completely—she spends that entire week at home, drawing the blinds. And she spends money
impulsively online, buying things she does not need, to distract herself from the looping fragments
of memory. This compulsive spending leaves her financially anxious, which makes her dizzy more
often, turning the somatic trigger into a daily occurrence. The avoidance also prevents her from
elaborating the memory, so the gaps remain sealed, and the story stays nonverbal and unreal.

Her belief that she is inadequate feeds directly into how she experiences the memory: she tells
herself that a normal person would have filled in the blanks by now, and that her inability to do so
proves she is going crazy. That appraisal makes her even more fearful of the memory, so she avoids
it further, deepening the gaps. When the memory does intrude—unbidden, in flashes—it reinforces her
sense of being a bad coper. She thinks, “If I were stronger, I wouldn’t be stuck on this.”

The gaps also make her hypervigilant. She scans every customer’s face for signs of threat, feeling
watched even when alone. That vigilance primes her to notice the time of day and any hint of
dizziness, turning ordinary moments into trigger events. Meanwhile, avoiding the anniversary makes
the date loom larger in her mind, heightening her sensitivity to all triggers around that time.

Leilani has started to notice that her strategies are not working. She is spending more than she can
afford, and the shame of that only confirms her negative appraisals. She has withdrawn from her
community, afraid that someone will ask about the protest or see her dizzy spells. Her daughter,
worried, finally persuaded her to come to a clinic. What frightens Leilani most is that she might be
beyond help—that these reactions mean she has already lost her mind. She hopes someone can tell her
she is not crazy, that the gaps can be filled, and that she can return to the shop without fear.
