Kwame is a 77-year-old school principal who was assaulted two years ago outside the middle school
where he had worked for nearly forty years. A former student, intoxicated and belligerent, cornered
him in the parking lot, forced him into a storage closet, and demanded money. Kwame complied, but
the man hit him anyway, leaving him bleeding on the concrete floor. What stayed with him most was
the sickly-sweet smell of cheap whiskey on the man's breath—a scent that, even now, could turn his
world upside down.

The assault left Kwame changed in ways he did not fully understand. He began scanning for exits as
soon as he entered any room—his office, a friend's living room, even his own kitchen. The slightest
unexpected movement, a hand reaching for a wallet, made him flinch. But the most troubling change
was the way the smell of alcohol could instantly transport him back: a whiff of bourbon at a
colleague's retirement party, and suddenly he was in that closet again, hearing the man's voice,
feeling the old grief that sometimes overwhelmed him. At other moments, when he recalled the event,
he felt nothing at all—numb, as if it had happened to a stranger.

Kwame had come to believe the assault was his own fault. "I should have seen it coming," he would
think. "A principal should command respect. I lost control." This self-blame morphed into a broader
conviction: he could not rely on himself to stay safe, and he could not rely on others either—the
police arrived too late, the school's security was a joke. That belief kept him on edge. At staff
meetings, he sat near the door, scanning, certain that someone might corner him again. It also
intensified the memories: whenever he thought "I can't trust anyone," the auditory recall grew
louder, the man's voice more distinct. And his sensitivity to being forced or losing control meant
that even a simple request—"Can you sign these papers now?"—could set his heart racing and bring the
alcohol smell back.

The memories themselves kept the cycle going. During a quiet evening at home, a sudden recollection
of the assault would make him jump at his wife's approach, startling her. And those memories
sometimes seemed to conjure the trigger: he would be sitting at dinner and smell whiskey, even
though no one was drinking. His constant vigilance, in turn, fed the memories: scanning the room for
danger often led him to replay the moment he realized he could not escape. And the hypervigilance
reinforced his worst appraisals—"See? I can never be safe"—deepening the sense that he was broken.

His work as principal suffered. He began avoiding school events where alcohol might be served,
delegating attendance to his vice principal. He insisted on leaving his office door open so he could
see the hallway. He felt trapped in meetings, his hand gripping the armrest, ready to leave. At
home, his wife noticed he had stopped going to the store on weekends; the smell of the liquor aisle
was impossible.

What finally brought Kwame to therapy was a panic attack at a restaurant. A waiter walked by, and
the cologne on his shirt smelled like whiskey. Kwame froze, then fled to the bathroom, shaking. That
night, he told his wife he was afraid he would never feel safe again. He came to the clinic hoping
to regain some sense of control—but part of him believed it was already too late.
