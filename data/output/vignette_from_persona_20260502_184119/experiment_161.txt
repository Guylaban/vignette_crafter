David is a 54-year-old civil servant who lives with his girlfriend, Elena. Three years ago, he left
a long-term relationship with a man who, over the final year, subjected him to repeated physical
assaults. The worst episode happened in their bedroom: an argument over finances escalated, and his
partner pinned him to the bed, forearm across his throat. David could not breathe; he tasted
copper—his own blood from a split lip—and the ceiling above him blurred into a series of still
images, each one frozen and precise, like photographs clicked in sequence. He stopped fighting and
went limp, and afterwards the memory of that night did not come back as a continuous story. When he
deliberately tried to recall it, only fragments emerged, but the taste would return
unbidden—metallic, sharp—and with it came the photographic images he could not control.

Now, David avoids being alone with men. At work, when a male colleague closes his office door for a
private discussion, David’s chest tightens. He keeps his own door open and schedules meetings in
open-plan areas or conference rooms with glass walls. The loss of control he experienced during the
abuse has become a generalised dread: any situation where he feels trapped or powerless, even a
crowded elevator, sends his mind reeling back to that bedroom. Sleeping alone—if Elena works late or
travels—is unbearable; the empty bed triggers the taste memory and the photographic flash of the
ceiling, and he will call friends or stay up until dawn rather than lie still.

To cope, David has developed a set of strategies that keep him moving. He detaches from the
memories: when a flash surfaces, he mentally narrates his current surroundings in a flat, clinical
tone, refusing to let the images feel real. He also keeps himself constantly surrounded by people.
After work he volunteers for committees, attends every social event, and often invites neighbours
over in the evening. Being alone in a room, even for a few minutes, feels dangerous—the taste might
come, and then the photographs. The constant socialising is exhausting, but it works in the moment.

Yet the strategies themselves create new problems. The more he detaches from his memories, the
harder it becomes to access them deliberately—he now relies on fragmentary flashes that arrive
without warning, and the deliberate gaps make the unexpected intrusions feel more alien and
threatening. And the constant socialising means he is almost never alone with his thoughts, but it
also means he is frequently in the presence of men—colleagues, neighbours, friends’ partners—and
each encounter heightens his hypervigilance. When a male coworker claps him on the shoulder, David’s
stomach drops; he feels the loss of control all over again, and the taste floods his mouth.

The cycle tightens. At home, Elena has noticed that David cannot sit through a quiet evening. He
fills the silence with podcasts or invites people over, and when she asks why he seems afraid of
their own apartment, he deflects. He has come to believe that if he stops moving, if he lets himself
be alone, the memories will crush him—that the taste and the photographs will become so vivid he
will relive the choking. What finally drove him to seek help was a panic attack during a performance
review: his male boss praised his work, and David, feeling pinned by the attention and the closed
door, lost all sense of where he was. He excused himself, trembling, and the taste of blood stayed
in his mouth for the rest of the day. Now he sits in a therapist’s office, hoping to find a way to
let the memories exist without running from them, but terrified that slowing down will only bring
the photographs back for good.
