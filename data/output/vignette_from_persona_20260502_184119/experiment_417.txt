The assault occurred on a Tuesday evening in late October. Margaret had stayed late at the
courthouse, reviewing discovery for a felony trial set to begin the following week. As she walked
through the basement parking garage toward her car, a man came up from behind her, grabbed her arm,
and pressed something sharp against her ribs. He took her briefcase — the one her husband had given
her for their fortieth anniversary — and shoved her hard against a concrete pillar before running
off. She remembered the smell of stale cigarette smoke on his jacket and the way her knees buckled
as she slid down the pillar. Security found her twenty minutes later, still sitting on the damp
concrete, unable to stand. The man was never identified. The briefcase was never recovered.

Margaret has been a public defender for over forty years. She has represented clients accused of
violent crimes, sat across tables from men convicted of assault, and walked through that same
parking garage a thousand times. At seventy-eight, she had always thought of herself as someone who
could handle whatever came. Her husband, Edward, retired now for a decade, often told her she was
tougher than anyone he had ever met.

She frequently looks behind her to check if someone is following her. This is most pronounced in
parking garages and courthouse hallways, but it has begun to extend to the grocery store and the
sidewalk near her home. She watches other people closely for signs that they might harm her. In the
courtroom, her eyes move constantly to the gallery, scanning the faces of family members, observers,
and defendants waiting for their cases to be called. She is easily startled. A door slamming, a
voice raised in the hallway outside chambers, a hand placed on her shoulder without warning — these
cause a physical reaction that leaves her feeling exposed and, she has come to believe, visible in a
way that undermines the authority she has spent a lifetime building.

What brought Margaret to seek help was not the symptoms themselves, but a moment in court three
weeks ago. She was cross-examining a witness when the bailiff closed a door more firmly than usual.
Margaret flinched. The judge paused. The opposing counsel looked up. For the first time in her
career, she lost her train of thought in front of a jury. She sat down and could not remember what
she had been about to ask. That night, she told Edward she was afraid she was losing her edge. He
said nothing for a long time, and then asked if she had ever thought about talking to someone.

Margaret is not certain that talking will help. She has spent her life arguing, questioning,
analyzing — she is not sure what it means to sit on the other side of the table. But she is tired of
looking over her shoulder. She is tired of the way her heart pounds when a colleague approaches from
behind. She is tired of feeling, at seventy-eight, like a woman who cannot trust the world she has
navigated for decades. What she fears most is that this will cost her the work she loves. What she
hopes, though she would not say it aloud, is that someone can tell her why she cannot shake the
sense that someone is always there, just behind her.
