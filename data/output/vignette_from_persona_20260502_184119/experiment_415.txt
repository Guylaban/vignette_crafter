Marcus was fifty-four and had spent thirty years in the cockpit of a fighter jet—first for his home
nation in Oceania, then as a contractor for the U.S. Air Force. The accident happened on a clear
afternoon during a routine training sortie over the Nevada desert. A catastrophic engine failure at
fifteen thousand feet. He managed to eject seconds before the aircraft became a fireball, but the
chute deployment was violent and he came down hard on a rocky ridge, fracturing three vertebrae and
rupturing a disc in his lower back. Physically, he recovered over eighteen months. What did not
heal, he came to believe, was something inside him.

In the months that followed, Marcus felt as though a vital part of himself had been sheared away. He
could no longer recall what it felt like to be moved by anything—a sunset, his wife’s laughter, the
roar of afterburners. “I will never feel normal emotions again,” he told himself, and the certainty
of it lodged in his chest like shrapnel. He stared at his face in the mirror and barely recognized
the man who had once been decisive, fearless, quick to grin. *I don’t know myself anymore.* And
worse: *I have permanently changed for the worse.* These were not passing thoughts; they became the
bedrock of his inner life, the filter through which every experience passed.

Because he believed he was fundamentally broken, Marcus began to treat the world as a place that
could break him further. He started scanning every room for exits before he could sit down—the mess
hall, the briefing room, even his own living room. At the base, he found himself walking the
perimeter of the hangar during preflight checks, noting every emergency egress door, every fire
extinguisher. He told himself it was professionalism, but the compulsion was relentless. At home, he
could not sit with his back to a doorway. His wife, Lani, watched him jerk his head toward every
unexpected sound—a car door, a creaking floorboard. He felt perpetually on guard, even inside his
own four walls, as though the accident had left him with a radar that could never be switched off.
*Being superalert, watchful, on guard*—that was his constant state. And the more he stayed on guard,
the more it confirmed that he was indeed a changed, damaged man.

The connection tightened into a loop. Because he believed he had lost his core self and could not
trust his own reactions, he had to monitor every potential threat. And because he was always
monitoring, he never gave himself the chance to feel safe, which only deepened his conviction that
his emotions were gone for good. At work, his escape-route planning became so obsessive that a
younger pilot joked, “Marcus, you check the canopy release like it’s gonna save your life.” He did
not laugh. What frightened him most was the certainty that this vigilance was necessary—that without
it, he would be caught off guard, and that the next accident would finish what the first one
started.

Months became years. Lani told him she felt as though she were living with a stranger who was always
waiting for something bad to happen. He had withdrawn from their Saturday hikes, from barbecues with
friends, from any situation where he could not mentally map an exit. She finally insisted he see a
psychologist. He came to the appointment reluctantly, but he came. What he was hoping for, he
admitted in the first session, was to feel less like a ghost. What he was most afraid of was that
nothing could bring his old self back—that the man who had once flown into danger without a second
thought was gone, and that all that remained was this watchful, hollow shell.
