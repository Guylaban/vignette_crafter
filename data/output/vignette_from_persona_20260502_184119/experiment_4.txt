Amir was seven the first time his father’s open hand caught him across the face hard enough to knock
him to the kitchen floor. There was no warning—just a flash of heat and a ringing in his ear that
lasted through dinner. The beatings continued for years, always unannounced, always over something
Amir could not predict: a dish left unwashed, a word spoken too softly, a silence his father read as
defiance. By the time he left home at eighteen, Amir had learned that safety meant never relaxing,
never turning his back, never assuming that a quiet room would stay quiet.

Now, at nineteen and newly married, Amir works as an environmental scientist for a small consulting
firm. His job requires him to spend hours in a shared office or out in the field with colleagues,
and every setting feels layered with unseen threat. When voices rise in a meeting—even a friendly
debate over sampling methods—Amir’s stomach knots and his hands go cold. The sound of a coworker
crying in the breakroom after a personal call sends a spike of adrenaline through him, and he will
leave the floor rather than pass near the door. Most intrusively, being touched unexpectedly, even a
tap on the shoulder from a teammate asking for a file, makes him flinch and step back, heart
pounding as if the touch were a blow.

Amir has come to believe that the world is filled with hidden danger, and that only constant
vigilance can keep him safe. He studies every face that enters a room, scanning for the narrowing of
eyes or the tightening of a jaw that might signal anger or rejection. In meetings he chooses a seat
with his back to the wall, and at lunch he positions himself so he can see the door and everyone
around him. This hypervigilance narrows his attention: he is so focused on detecting threat that he
often misreads neutral expressions as hostile, and the very act of scanning makes him notice
potential triggers—a colleague’s raised eyebrow, a sudden silence—that others would ignore.

To manage the constant sense of danger, Amir has developed a tight set of rules. He avoids any
situation that might involve disagreement or confrontation, so he never speaks up in meetings even
when his data is questioned, and he defers to whoever seems most assertive. He avoids being alone in
a room or building, because when he is by himself there is no one to watch for him, and the silence
feels like waiting. Most of all, he avoids people: he eats lunch at his desk with his back to the
wall, he takes the stairs instead of the elevator to minimize chance encounters, and he has stopped
accepting invitations to colleague gatherings. But these strategies backfire. His silence frustrates
coworkers, who interpret it as disinterest or incompetence, and their irritation leads to sharper
words that Amir reads as signs of imminent attack—which only deepens his avoidance. When he comes
home to his wife, he is so wound up that a simple question about his day feels like an
interrogation, and he lashes out or withdraws, sparking arguments that he then must escape from by
leaving the room. His wife’s tears, once a source of comfort, now feel like a trigger, and he cannot
bear to see her cry without his pulse racing.

Amir has not slept through a night in months. He has started asking for solo field assignments to
avoid people, but the isolation makes him jump at every rustle of wind. He sought help only after
his supervisor pulled him aside and said his evasiveness was affecting team morale and his
performance review was at risk. What Amir does not say is that he is terrified of losing his job,
which would mean returning home to his wife full-time, where her presence would be constant and he
would have no escape from the vigilance that exhausts him. He hopes, vaguely, that therapy can give
him a few hours of peace—a room where his back is not to the wall, and where no one will cry or
touch him without warning.
