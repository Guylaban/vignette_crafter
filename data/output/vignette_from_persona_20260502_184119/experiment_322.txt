Carlos Valdez is a fifty-eight-year-old forensic analyst who spent more than three decades
reconstructing the aftermath of industrial accidents. He was one of the first responders called to a
chemical plant explosion on the outskirts of Houston two years ago—a man-made disaster triggered by
neglected safety protocols. The scene was chaos: twisted metal, a toxic cloud, the smell of burning
solvents, and the bodies of workers he recognised from previous site visits. Carlos spent ten hours
in full protective gear, documenting evidence, stepping around debris, and watching emergency crews
carry out the wounded and the dead. One of his junior colleagues, a man he had trained, died in the
blast before rescue teams could reach him. Carlos remembers the moment clearly—the sound of another
explosion, the ground shaking, and the feeling that he should have done more to get everyone out.

In the months that followed, Carlos noticed a deep restlessness that would not settle. He began to
avoid unfamiliar places entirely. He drove the same route to his office, ate at the same two
restaurants, and declined invitations to new restaurants or unfamiliar neighbourhoods. At work, his
supervisor understood when he asked to stop taking field assignments; Carlos now insists on handling
only lab-based casework, never setting foot in a new building or an unknown site. He is constantly
alert—his body tense, shoulders tight, jaw clenched—even when sitting in his own living room. Any
sudden noise, a car backfiring or a door slammed in the hallway, makes him flinch so hard he nearly
drops whatever he is holding. His wife has grown used to him jumping at the sound of the microwave
timer.

Separately, Carlos has developed a set of harsh beliefs about himself and his world. He has come to
see his reactions since the explosion as proof that he is a lousy coper. He tells himself that real
forensic analysts—the kind he used to be—would have moved on by now, would not flinch at a loud
noise or feel their heart race when a colleague suggests a new meeting location. He feels he is no
longer capable of doing the things that once defined him: leading a team, making split-second
decisions under pressure, staying calm in uncertainty. To Carlos, the world now feels like a place
where danger hides in every unfamiliar street, every unplanned event, every new face. He scans
parking lots for suspicious vehicles and watches the exits in every room. He is certain that if he
lets his guard down for one moment, something terrible will happen again.

whenever his wife suggests a day out to a new park. His marriage has become a quiet negotiation
around his avoidance—she has stopped asking him to run errands in unfamiliar parts of town, and she
does the grocery shopping alone. At work, his productivity has slipped. He processes evidence
meticulously, but he no longer volunteers for new assignments and has turned down a promotion that
involved supervising field teams. His colleagues have noticed his withdrawal, though they attribute
it to stress and age.

What finally brought Carlos to a clinic was his wife’s plea after he refused to attend a family
gathering at a cousin’s new home. He realised he could not keep shrinking his world. He fears he is
too broken to return to his old role, too fragile to face uncertainty. Yet he still hopes—against
the weight of his own conviction—that he might find a way to feel capable again, to trust that the
world is not always waiting to harm him.
