grocery for nearly twenty years. Five years ago, a tornado ripped through his neighborhood while he
was alone in the back storeroom, counting inventory. The lights went, the metal shelves buckled, and
the concrete walls groaned as though the whole building would collapse on him. He lay flat on his
belly, hands over his head, praying—and for twenty minutes he was certain he would die. When the
storm passed, he walked out into shattered glass and a sky that looked wrong, and something in him
never really walked out again.

In the months that followed, Kwame’s sleep unraveled. He lay rigid in bed, listening for the low
rumble of wind, convinced that if he let his guard down another storm would catch him unaware. His
wife, Grace, would touch his shoulder and he’d flinch. The world, he came to believe, was a
dangerous place—one that could turn violent without warning—and that conviction seeped into
everything. He began scanning the ceiling of his bedroom for structural weaknesses; he checked
weather alerts hourly. The belief that he had permanently changed for the worse made the memories of
the tornado feel more immediate, as though they were happening right now rather than years ago. And
each time a memory surged—the sound of shelving twisting, the smell of wet concrete—it only deepened
his certainty that the world was no longer safe.

At work, Kwame started positioning himself so that he had a clear line of sight to the front doors,
the side exit, and the emergency door near the dairy coolers. He scanned for exits as soon as he
entered any room, his eyes tracing the fire escape route even while he rang up a customer’s
groceries. The hypervigilance became automatic: he was watchful, on guard, never fully present. This
constant state of alert kept his body keyed up, and when his heart raced for no obvious reason, the
memory of the tornado would burst into his mind—the shudder of the floor, the pressure in his
ears—so that he felt he was reliving it all over again. In turn, each replay reinforced his sense
that the world was inherently hostile, that he had to be on guard every second.

He developed small routines to manage the fear. He always stayed close to an exit, even at home; he
refused to sit in a restaurant booth unless he could see the door. When a memory threatened to take
hold, he distracted himself—counting ceiling tiles, calculating the total of a grocery order,
anything to push the image away. But the distraction itself left him feeling weak, as though he
could no longer trust his own mind, which confirmed his belief that he had been permanently damaged.
And the habit of staying near exits, of never fully relaxing, only made his hypervigilance worse: by
never letting himself feel safe, he kept his guard up constantly.

he woke her by sitting up in the dark to scan the window. She stopped touching his shoulder. Kwame
knew he was pushing her away, but he could not stop. The world was dangerous, and he had lost the
part of himself that had ever felt otherwise. What finally brought him to a therapist’s office was a

slamming, convinced the ceiling was falling. His manager found him crouching, panting, and asked if
he needed to go home early. Kwame said yes, and he knew then that the loop—the memory driving the

good. He came to the appointment hoping for something, though he could not name it; he was most
afraid that nothing would change, that he would spend whatever years he had left scanning for an
exit that never came.
