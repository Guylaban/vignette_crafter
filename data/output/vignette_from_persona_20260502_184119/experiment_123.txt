Ravi, a thirty-two-year-old housekeeper from a South Indian family, had been married for four years
and lived with his wife in a small apartment in the Chicago suburbs. The mugging happened on a cool
November evening, just after he finished cleaning a downtown office building. He was walking toward
the bus stop when a man in a dark hoodie came up from behind, pressed something hard into his ribs,
and demanded his wallet. Ravi froze, handed over his wallet and phone, and stood still while the man
shoved him against a brick wall, his head cracking against the rough surface. The man ran. Ravi slid
to the ground, his hands pressed to the back of his skull, and for a long moment he could not move.
The police arrived, but the man was never caught.

Over the following weeks, Ravi found that the memory of that night would return without warning, and
when it did, he felt a sharp, physical pain in the same spot on the back of his head—a dull ache
that spread down his neck. The images were startlingly clear: the man’s height, the gravelly voice,
the cold metal against his ribs. He could describe the scene to his wife only in fragments; whenever
he tried to put the whole thing into words, the sentences stalled, and he would shake his head and
say he could not find the words.

What began to trouble Ravi most was a creeping certainty that he could no longer trust anyone he did
not already know. Every unfamiliar face on the street seemed to hold a potential threat. The world,
he came to believe, was simply a dangerous place, and people were not what they seemed. This
conviction colored everything. When he walked to work now, he scanned the sidewalks for hooded
figures, and every time he spotted someone who matched the attacker’s build, the memory surged
back—the sharp pain, the vivid image of the brick wall, the helplessness. He would feel his breath
shorten and a pulse of heat in his chest.

Because the memory felt so real and so painful, Ravi’s belief that the world was dangerous only
deepened. It seemed to him that the attack had revealed a fundamental truth he had been blind to. He
began to avoid the block where it happened, then the bus stop, then any street that felt too quiet
or too dark. At work, cleaning rooms in a hotel, he started to lock the door from the inside while
he worked, and he flinched whenever a guest knocked unexpectedly. His supervisor noticed he had
become jumpy and withdrawn, but Ravi could not explain it; he still could not find the right words.

The loop tightened across months. Each vivid memory confirmed his distrust, and each new cautionary
thought—don’t make eye contact, keep your back to the wall—made the memory more likely to surface.
His wife grew worried. He had stopped wanting to go out to dinner, stopped meeting her friends, and
once, when a stranger brushed past him in a grocery aisle, he dropped a jar and stood paralyzed. She
urged him to see a doctor.

What finally brought Ravi to the clinic was a morning at work when a guest, a large man in a dark
hoodie, came to the door of a room Ravi was cleaning. The man simply smiled and said hello, but Ravi
felt the pain in his head, saw the brick wall, and could not speak. He left the hotel without
clocking out. That evening, sitting across from his wife, he told her he could not keep living like
this—always scanning, always afraid. The memory would not stop coming, and each time it did, the
world became more dangerous. He was afraid of what might happen if he could not find a way out.
