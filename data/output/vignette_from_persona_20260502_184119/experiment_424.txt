Roberto Garcia is a 54-year-old business owner who came to therapy at his wife’s insistence, though
he himself would say he is fine—just tired, a little on edge. He still owns the small grocery store
in the East Los Angeles neighborhood he built from nothing twenty years ago, but he no longer enjoys
the work. He describes himself as a man who “got too close to something he shouldn’t have seen.”
That something happened during his twenties, when he volunteered with an aid organization in a
Central American war zone. One afternoon at a checkpoint, a truck backfired two feet from him, and
the soldier beside him was shot through the chest before the echo faded. Roberto was sprayed with
blood and knocked to the ground by the man’s body. He still remembers the heat of the bullet
fragment that grazed his arm, and the way the soldier’s eyes went slack.

For years after, Roberto threw himself into work and family, telling himself he had been lucky. But
the old chaos started seeping in about three years ago, not as a memory but as a feeling that
something was wrong. He began carrying a compact pistol in his store apron—just in case—and found
himself scanning every customer who stepped too close to the counter. At first it felt like
vigilance; now it feels like dread. A single loud noise—a car backfire, a dropped crate—stops his
breath and sends his hand to the grip hidden under his apron. The instant his fingers wrap around
the weapon, his mind goes into overdrive: he replays the checkpoint, the shot, the soldier’s blood,
and he starts asking himself why he survived when that man did not. He goes over that question again
and again, sometimes for hours after closing, sitting alone in the back office with the pistol on
the desk in front of him.

The weapon itself has become a trigger. Because he carries it, Roberto is hyperaware of every
potential threat: a customer reaching into a jacket, a teenager laughing too loud in the candy
aisle. That laugh especially rattles him—he feels confused by the sound, almost angry that anyone
can be happy in a world where such things happen. Positive emotions in others strike him as naïve or
dangerous, and he has started snapping at his wife when she tells a joke at dinner. He picks
arguments with her, with customers, with the delivery driver—anything to feel the sharp spike of
adrenaline that drowns out the rumination for a few minutes. “I start a fight,” he said flatly,
“just to stop thinking.” But the fights always circle back. After a shouting match with a customer
about a mistaken price, Roberto stood trembling in the aisle, the gun heavy against his ribs, and he
understood that he had created the very proximity—the heated face-to-face closeness—that now
triggered his panic. He had walked right into the zone.

The cycle has cost him. His wife now sleeps in the guest room; two of his regular customers have
stopped coming. Last month, during a small celebration for his employee’s birthday, someone popped a
balloon and Roberto dropped to the floor, knocking over a display. The shame afterwards was
unbearable, and he locked himself in the office and pulled out his pistol, not to hurt himself, but
just to hold it—a habit that now feels like the only reliable thing in his life.

The last straw came when his twelve-year-old nephew ran up to hug him in the store parking lot and
Roberto shoved him away before he realized what he was doing. He saw the boy’s face crumple, and
that night he told his wife he would see someone. What Roberto is most afraid of now is that he will
push everyone away and be left alone with the question he cannot stop asking: “Why did I survive?”
But part of him, the part that still picks the lock on the gun safe each morning, is not sure he
wants to find out.
