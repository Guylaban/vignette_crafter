Mana is a 27-year-old special education teacher who works in a elementary school in a midwestern
suburb. They present with a PCL-5 score of 34, reflecting moderate-severe PTSD symptoms following a
prolonged stalking experience that began about eight months ago. The stalker was a former coworker—a
teaching assistant who had been let go—and for three months he would appear outside Mana’s
apartment, follow their car to the grocery store, and once stood outside their classroom door during
parent-teacher night, watching through the small window. Mana never reported it because they doubted
anyone would believe them, and when he finally stopped coming around, they told themselves it was
over.

But the feeling of being watched never left. Now, every day at school, Mana finds themselves
scanning the parking lot before walking to their car, heart rate spiking when they hear footsteps
behind them in the hallway. These triggers—being followed, being watched, the sound of approaching
steps—are immediate and visceral. The moment they hear footsteps echoing in the empty corridor after
the final bell, Mana’s mind yanks them back to the memories: but not as a coherent story. Instead,
they get fragments—a glimpse of his face in the rearview mirror, the prickling sensation on their
neck, a wordless sense of being cornered—but the sequence is jumbled and some moments are
terrifyingly clear while others are blank. The incomplete picture torments them.

As a result of these triggered fragments, Mana has developed a set of maladaptive strategies that
they recognize as problematic but cannot stop. The most consuming is rumination about how the
stalking could have been prevented—they replay the weeks before, searching for a moment they might
have acted differently. They have also stopped relaxing entirely. Even sitting on the couch after
work feels dangerous; if their guard drops, something bad will happen. So instead, Mana spends their
evenings checking the locks multiple times and scanning the street from the window. At school, this
translates into constantly scanning the room for signs of threat: a student’s sudden movement, the
door opening unexpectedly, any change in the environment.

The hypervigilance itself has a corrosive effect on Mana’s memory. Because they are always on alert,
they cannot let their mind settle into a full recollection of the event; the fragments remain
fragmented, and the gaps grow larger. They know this, and the gaps drive further rumination—if only
they could remember the whole thing, they could figure out what they missed and prevent it from
happening again. This loop tightens: the more Mana scans for danger, the more triggers they notice
(a stranger lingering near their car, a coworker walking too close behind them), and the more they
ruminate and scan.

The consequence has been relational and occupational. Mana’s partner has noticed their constant
scanning and the way they startle at footsteps in their own apartment. Mana no longer goes on
evening walks together, claiming they prefer to stay home, but at home they cannot relax anyway. At
work, their principal has gently asked why they seem so “on edge” during classroom observations.
Mana is terrified that the stalker will return, or that someone new is now watching them. They
finally sought help because their partner said something had to change—and because Mana themselves
is exhausted by the endless vigilance, yet terrified that letting go of it will make the danger
real.
