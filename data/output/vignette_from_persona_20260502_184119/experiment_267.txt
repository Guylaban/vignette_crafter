came back in fragments: the way his hand shook, the silence that followed, the slow realization that
nothing would ever be the same. He had always believed the world was fundamentally fair, that hard
work and love kept people safe. That belief had been destroyed in a single sentence over the phone.
Now, six months later, he described himself as someone who used to be happy but was now permanently
miserable, and he could not shake the conviction that the world was a dangerous place where any
ordinary moment could turn catastrophic.

The danger felt real, not abstract. Ravi found himself unable to relax even in his own apartment, a
space he had once considered his sanctuary. Ordinary sounds—the hum of the refrigerator, the creak
of floorboards, the clatter of a neighbor’s keys—made him jump, his heart lurching as if a car were
about to crash through the wall. Sleeping had become a nightly ordeal; he lay awake for hours, ears
straining into the dark, waiting for the phone to ring again. These threat reactions, he came to
believe, were necessary vigilance. If he let his guard down, something terrible would happen again.
His negative appraisals of the world as unsafe directly fed his constant sense of threat, and that
threat, in turn, reinforced his view that the world could never be trusted.

To manage this overwhelming state, Ravi had developed a set of strategies that seemed protective but
only deepened his isolation. He stopped making any plans beyond the next few hours—no weekend trips,
no freelance deadlines, no thoughts about his future as a writer. When his friends invited him to a
café where he and Arjun used to write together, he refused without explanation, avoiding any
reminder of his brother. He also caught himself repeatedly asking his mother, “Am I going to be
okay?” and “Is it normal to feel this way?”—seeking reassurance that he was not losing his mind.
These maladaptive strategies were directly shaped by his shattered beliefs: if the world was
dangerous and his happiness was gone forever, then planning ahead felt pointless and reminders were
unbearable. His avoidance of grief reminders and future plans reinforced his negative appraisals
because every time he turned down an invitation or refused to think about tomorrow, he confirmed to
himself that his life was permanently broken. The cycle tightened when his avoidance also fed his
threat system: by retreating from any potential triggers, he never learned that he could survive
them, so his startle response and hypervigilance only grew worse.

Ravi’s work as a writer suffered. He used to draft short stories in coffee shops, but now he could
not sit still in any public space; the scrape of a chair or the sizzle of an espresso machine sent
him scanning the room for danger. He worked only from his bedroom, and even there the page stayed
blank. His editor had stopped asking about deadlines. His relationships also frayed—his friends,
tired of his repeated requests for reassurance and his refusal to meet, began calling less often. He
noticed the distance but told himself it was just another proof that the world was cold and that he
was unworthy of connection.

still existed. In the waiting room of the clinic, he held a folded paper with Arjun’s name on it and
