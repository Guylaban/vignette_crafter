Marco is a twenty-two-year-old HVAC technician living in a small apartment in Phoenix. Eight months
ago, while working on a rooftop unit at a strip mall, he misjudged a pressure valve on an aging
refrigerant line. The line burst, and a shard of metal struck a fellow technician in the neck. The
man survived but suffered a spinal injury that left him partially paralyzed. Marco remembers the jet
of gas, the smell of compressor oil and ozone, the way the other man’s eyes went wide before he
crumpled. The image comes back without warning—sometimes when he’s driving, sometimes in the middle
of a quiet job—and when it does, he can smell that oil again, sharp and chemical, as if it’s right
there in the air. But the memory doesn’t feel like his own. He has described it to his therapist as
watching a scene from a movie, or something that happened to someone else, even though he knows it
was his hands on that valve.

Marco has come to believe that his body is fundamentally unreliable. He can no longer trust his
reflexes, his judgment, his own hands. The thought that he could have done something different—could
have checked the gauge one more time, could have yelled a warning—echoes through his mind daily, and
he is certain he will never forgive himself for not stopping what happened. That guilt drives him to
replay the event over and over, analyzing each second for where he went wrong, as though he might
find a version of the past in which he did enough. In the weeks after the accident he stopped going
out with friends, stopped answering calls from his supervisor, and began timing his work schedule to
avoid any job that reminded him of that rooftop. He refuses to accept assignments that involve
refrigeration lines, even though that means lost income and tense conversations with his boss. The
anniversaries—the date of the accident, the date the other man was discharged from the hospital—loom
like deadlines he cannot escape, and he dreads them months in advance.

What frightens Marco most is silence. Unusual quiet—an empty parking lot, a lunch break in a dead
hallway—makes him feel that something dangerous is about to happen. His body reacts before his mind
can catch up: his heart pounds, his palms sweat, and he has to leave. As a result, he has shrunk his
world to a few places where he feels safe: his apartment with the television always on, his cousin’s
garage, one specific aisle of a grocery store where the background noise is constant. He is aware
that this is unsustainable, but the moment he steps into a quieter space, the intrusive memory of
the accident rises again—the hiss of the valve, the silence that followed before the other man
screamed—and the threat feeling spikes. The memory and the threat feed each other; the more he
avoids, the fewer places feel safe, and the more the memory forces its way in during the rare
moments of quiet he cannot control.

Marco’s avoidance and overanalyzing have only deepened his sense that he is no longer the person he
used to be. He used to be easygoing, quick to laugh, confident in his work. Now he cannot trust
himself to do a simple repair without checking and rechecking. Each time he avoids a stressor—a job
site, a social invitation—he tells himself he did the right thing, but underneath there is a quiet
voice that says, *See? You can’t be trusted around anyone.* That self-appraisal tightens the loop,
and the urge to ruminate grows stronger. He has stopped seeing his family because they ask how he is
doing, and the question brings the memory back with its alien quality, as though the accident
belongs to a stranger whose life he is now living.

He sought help after his supervisor gave him a final warning about missed assignments. Marco sat in
the quiet of his apartment, the television off for once, and felt the familiar dread creep in. He
realized he had not left that apartment in four days, and that the person he had become was someone
he barely recognized. What he hopes for—though he can barely admit it—is to feel that his own body
is his again, and to find a way to live with what his hands did without letting it consume
everything else.
