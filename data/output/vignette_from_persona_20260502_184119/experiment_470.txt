Priya Sharma was thirty-two years old, a chaplain who had once found comfort in the quiet rituals of
her hospital chapel. That had been before the kidnapping. For three days she had been held in a
basement room, her wrists bound behind her back with coarse rope, the smell of damp concrete and
cigarette smoke filling her lungs. Soldiers in camouflage fatigues had taken turns questioning her,
their boots echoing on the floor, the crack of a leather belt against the table beside her ear. When
she tried to speak she was gagged; when she struggled she was shoved face-down and held there until
she stopped. The physical sensations—the pressure of a forearm across her neck, the jingle of a belt
buckle, the heavy tread of boots—lodged in her body like splinters she could not extract.

In the months afterward, Priya found that the memory of those hours refused to take verbal shape.
She knew what had happened, could see the cracked ceiling tiles and the dust motes in the single
bulb's light, but when a colleague asked gently about her recovery, her throat closed. She would
start a sentence—“They kept me in a room …”—and then the thread would vanish, the words dissolving
into a static that left her staring blankly. The harder she tried to explain, the more the images
pressed in without language. This inability to put the event into words began to drive her behavior.
To stop the frustrated circling in her head, she took to pressing the tip of a kitchen knife into
her forearm late at night—the sharp sting cut through the wordless panic, and for a few minutes she
could breathe. She also stopped accepting invitations from friends from her old life; conversations
about ordinary things felt impossible when she could not say what was wrong, and their confusion
only made her feel more isolated.

The memory’s nonverbal quality also made certain sounds and sensations act as sudden triggers,
because she had no narrative buffer between the image and the feeling. A car door slamming in the
parking lot would yank her back to the basement; she would feel the rope around her wrists before
she could even name the sound. Once, during a hospital fire drill, the echoing footsteps in the
hallway sent her into a full-body freeze, her hands curling into fists, her breath shallow. The
sight of a police uniform at a traffic stop left her trembling for hours afterward. She had come to
believe that these triggers were random attacks, not understanding that they were fragments of a
memory she could not contain in words.

To manage the unpredictability, Priya began avoiding anything that felt exposed. She stopped walking
through the hospital’s main atrium, a wide, open space with glass walls, because she felt visible
and vulnerable there. She avoided the chapel’s side door that opened onto a courtyard. Week by week,
her world narrowed. The avoidance itself, however, seemed to keep the memory locked in its raw,
unprocessed form. She no longer went to the weekly chaplain peer group where she might have had to
try to describe her experience; without that practice, the images stayed silent and fragmented. The
self-harm gave her a momentary reprieve, but afterward she felt ashamed and even less able to form
sentences about what had happened. The more she avoided and distracted, the more elusive the words
became.

Priya’s divorce had happened quietly, two years after the trauma. Her husband had grown tired of her
flinching from his touch, of finding bloodied tissues in the bathroom sink, of the emptiness when he
asked what she was thinking and she could only shake her head. Now she lived alone. At work, she had
transferred to administrative duties, processing paperwork in a small, windowless office, because
she could no longer conduct bedside visits without scanning every male doctor’s uniform. What
finally brought her to seek help was a recent incident: a patient’s family member at the information
desk asked her for directions, and the man’s heavy accent—similar to one of her captors’—sent her
into such a dissociative episode that she found herself on the floor of a supply closet, not knowing
how she had gotten there. She was afraid of what she might do to herself the next time the wordless
images came, and even more afraid that she would never find the words to make them stop.
