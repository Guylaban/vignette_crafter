Mark is a 56-year-old professional dancer who has performed with a regional company for over three
decades. He lives with his wife in a quiet suburb, and until six months ago, he considered himself
lucky—a man who had built a career doing what he loved. Then, one evening on the way to a rehearsal,
a familiar song came on the radio, and he glanced down to adjust the volume. In that split second,
his car drifted across the lane and struck a cyclist at the edge of the road. The man was airlifted
to the hospital with a traumatic brain injury and multiple fractures. Mark was not charged, but he
has not been the same since.

Certain songs now stop him cold. When that same melody drifts from a passing car or a coffee shop
speaker, his chest tightens and he is immediately back in the moment—the jolt, the thud, the silence
afterward. He has come to believe that he will never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what
happened. He should have been paying attention; he should have swerved differently, hit the brakes
sooner. The guilt is so sharp that it reshapes everything: he sees the accident as proof that he
failed to protect someone who needed protecting, and that he has failed in his most important role
in life—the role of a person who causes no harm. Those appraisals amplify the triggers. Now he is
hyperaware of any sound that could be a crash, any sudden movement near the road, and he interprets
these as fresh reminders that he is a person who hurt another.

To manage the unbearable feelings, Mark tries not to think about the trauma. He changes the station
when certain songs play, and he refuses to drive past the intersection where it happened. He also
dwells on how he used to be before the event—the dancer who moved with confidence, who could relax
after a show, who trusted his own reactions. This dwelling creates a hollow ache, and the effort of
suppression keeps his nervous system on edge. He has difficulty falling asleep because he needs to
stay alert, listening for sounds he might have caused, even at home where he knows he is safe. His
wife has noticed he cannot relax, and his focus in rehearsals has all but vanished; he misses
counts, loses his balance, and the director has started giving him simpler roles.

The constant threat monitoring feeds back into the triggers. Because Mark is always scanning for
danger, he is more likely to notice crowded spaces and interpret them as suffocating, more likely to
flinch at a car braking too hard, more likely to be flooded by guilt when that song inevitably comes
on. The cycle tightens: a trigger sparks the appraisal that he failed; the appraisal drives him to
avoid and suppress; avoidance maintains his hypervigilance; hypervigilance makes him more sensitive
to triggers. He feels trapped.

Last month, after he left a dress rehearsal early because a dancer backstage played a playlist that
included the song, his wife insisted he see someone. Mark is afraid that if he stops suppressing, he
will fall apart. But he is also terrified of losing his career—dancing is all he has known—and of
the widening distance between him and the man he used to be. He wants to feel safe in his own body
again, and to believe he deserves to move forward.
