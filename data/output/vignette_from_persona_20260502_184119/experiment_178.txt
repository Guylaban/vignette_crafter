Kai was twenty-four when the abuse began, two years into a relationship that had once felt like a
sanctuary. His partner, a man he trusted completely, turned violent during an argument over a missed
dinner. The first hit was a slap; the second a fist to his ribs. Kai froze, unable to fight back or
flee, and in the weeks that followed, the pattern repeated—bruises hidden under long sleeves,
excuses made to friends, a growing certainty that he must have caused it. The relationship ended
only when his partner moved out, but by then the damage was done. As a journalist covering local
politics, Kai had always prided himself on objectivity and composure; now, every assignment became a
minefield. He began to notice that simply seeing a news report about domestic violence could send
him reeling, the memory surging back so vividly that he felt the old ache in his ribs and the
suffocating heat of shame. When that happened, he would find himself mentally reviewing the fights,
asking what he had done wrong, replaying his own words until he was convinced of his inadequacy. The
self-blame was automatic, a loop he could not stop.

The triggers multiplied. Riding the bus—something he did daily to get to the newsroom—became
unbearable. Every time the bus was late, Kai’s stomach tightened; he was certain something terrible
was happening, that the delay meant danger. He began waking up at 3 a.m., heart pounding, scanning
the dark room for intruders, even though he lived alone. His girlfriend, who stayed over on
weekends, noticed he flinched whenever she touched him unexpectedly, and he could not explain why.
He had come to believe that people could not be trusted, that you could never know who would harm
you, and this conviction colored every interaction. When he had to interview a male source one-on-
one, he felt a cold dread that made his voice shake; he started requesting that a colleague join
him, or he would avoid the interview altogether. His editor noticed him pulling back from
assignments that required evening meetings, but Kai could not bring himself to explain.

The struggle to suppress his anger compounded the problem. After the abuse, Kai had sworn never to
lose control, so he walled off any flash of irritation, swallowing it until his jaw ached. But the
suppression only deepened his sense of inadequacy—he saw his own passivity as weakness, proof that
he was not good enough, that he had deserved what happened. Meanwhile, he began restricting his
food, skipping lunch or dinner as a way to regain some control, telling himself he was not hungry.
The undereating left him drained, but it also felt like a punishment he deserved.

The memory itself refused to stay in the past. When it returned—triggered by a bus ride, a loud
voice, a man standing too close—it felt as if it were happening in the present moment, the same
fear, the same physical pain in his ribs and jaw. The overwhelm was so intense he would have to stop
whatever he was doing, sometimes leaving an interview mid-sentence or stepping off the bus at a
random stop. Each recurrence reinforced his belief that the world was unsafe, that his own judgment
could not be trusted. He began to interpret any delay—a slow elevator, a long line at the grocery
store—as a sign that something bad was about to happen. Even when his girlfriend assured him he was
safe, he found it impossible to believe her. The threat felt ever-present, and that constant
vigilance only confirmed his negative appraisals: he was inadequate, people were dangerous, harm was
inevitable.

The cycle tightened. His avoidance of buses and male colleagues made his job harder; he started
working from home, missing deadlines, losing the edge that had once defined him. His girlfriend grew
frustrated with his emotional distance, and Kai, unable to articulate his terror, withdrew further.
What finally brought him to seek help was a panic attack on the bus last week—a delay of three
minutes, and he was gasping, convinced he was about to be hit again. He sat in the therapist’s
office the next day, hands clasped, afraid that talking about it would make it real all over again,
but more afraid of what he was becoming. What he most wanted was to stop feeling like a stranger in
his own life, to trust that the bus would arrive and that he would be okay.
