Amir was twenty-seven when he saw a man die on the tracks. It was a late October evening, the rails
slick with rain, and he had just stepped down from the cab to check a signal issue near the yard
crossing. A transient had been walking the ballast, unsteady, and by the time Amir saw him it was
far too late. The man's leg caught between the points of a switching mechanism moments before a
maintenance car rolled through. There was blood—more than Amir had ever seen, dark and impossibly
warm-looking against the cold steel—and a wound that exposed bone and tissue in a way that made his
own body go numb. He remembers the paramedics shouting at him to step back, and the sensation of
warm spray on his forearm that he later scrubbed raw in the station bathroom, unable to stop feeling
it.

In the weeks that followed, Amir found that the world had become a place of hidden threats. The
sight of a child with a scraped knee on the platform sent his heart racing. A coworker showing off a
fresh cut from a loose cable caused him to turn away so abruptly that he nearly stumbled. But most
troubling was the temperature thing. He had come to associate the particular clammy warmth of blood
on his skin with mortal danger, and now any sudden sensation of heat or humidity against his arm—the
blast of vent air from a subway grate, the steam rising from his coffee cup—triggered a wave of
alertness that felt impossible to override. Each time this happened, he felt his certainty that he
was not safe deepen. Even when his supervisor told him the yard had been inspected and cleared, even
when his mother reminded him that he had been nowhere near the incident, even when he logically knew
the tracks were empty—he could not believe it. The vigilance remained.

A year later, Amir still worked the same route, but the job had changed. He had become hyperalert in
ways that exhausted him. He scanned the tracks obsessively, checked the same signal twice and then a
third time, and had started arriving at the depot two hours early just to walk the line before his
shift began. He had come to believe that if he let his guard down—if he stopped watching, just for a
moment—something would happen again. A passenger spilling hot coffee near the conductor's booth was
enough to make him break out in a sweat. The sight of a cyclist's bloody elbow beside the crossing
gate could ruin his entire afternoon. He was certain that the world was full of violence he might
miss, and that missing it would mean being caught off guard, and being caught off guard meant
catastrophe.

Sleep had become difficult. Amir found himself lying awake in his studio apartment long after
midnight, listening to the building's pipes and the distant rumble of freight trains, unable to let
his mind go quiet. He needed to stay alert. He told himself he was being careful, but the truth was
that he had begun to dread the moment his eyes closed, because in the dark the sensations came
back—the warmth on his arm, the smell of the rail yard, the sound of the brakes too late. He would
get maybe four hours of restless, broken sleep before the alarm went off, and he would start the
whole cycle again.

What finally brought Amir to a clinic was a moment of public shame. He had been walking through the
station concourse when a maintenance worker near a turnstile dropped a wrench on his own hand,
drawing blood. Amir froze. A security guard asked if he was okay, and he could not answer—his throat
had closed, his vision had narrowed, and he had to sit down on a bench for twenty minutes before he
could stand again. A colleague from the yard saw him and later asked gently if he was all right.
Amir said yes. But he was not all right.

Now, in the therapist's office, he is afraid of what is happening to him. He does not want to lose
his job, and he cannot imagine continuing like this for another year. What he wants most is to feel
the way he used to—to step onto a train without scanning every shadow, to drink coffee without
flinching at the warmth, to believe someone when they tell him he is safe. But he has no idea how to
get there.
