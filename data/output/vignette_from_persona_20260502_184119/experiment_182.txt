Tran was forty-two, a farmer in California’s Central Valley, married for fifteen years to Mai, and
father to five-year-old Leo. Three years ago, Leo contracted a severe bacterial infection that
spiraled into sepsis within hours. Tran remembered that night with a clarity that felt as sharp and
immediate as the moment itself: the frantic drive to the emergency room, the fluorescent lights of
the waiting area, a sad country song playing over the speakers, and the sight of Leo’s tiny body
limp on the gurney, his arms thin and pale with the IV line taped to his wrist. Tran had stood
frozen, watching the nurses work, and in the dark of the hospital room later, when the machines
beeped and the only light came from a monitor, he had stared at the outline of Leo’s hand—the same
hand he had held a hundred times—and thought it looked lifeless. Leo survived, but Tran did not.

In the months that followed, Tran found himself unable to go to bed at a normal hour. He would stay
up until two or three in the morning, watching television with the sound low, delaying the moment he
had to lie down in the dark. He had come to believe that if he closed his eyes in the darkness, he
would see that hospital room again, and the memory would feel less like a memory and more like
something happening all over again. He was right: when he finally did fall asleep, fragments of that
night would appear without warning—a sudden flash of the IV line, the shape of Leo’s collarbone
under the hospital gown—and he would jerk awake, heart pounding, convinced for a moment that his son
was still sick. The memory did not feel like the past; it felt present, as if Tran was still
standing in that bright waiting room, hearing the song.

Tran began to avoid other people as well. On the farm, he worked alone as much as possible, weeding
rows of tomatoes and peppers without saying a word to the occasional neighbor who drove by. He was
certain that if someone asked about Leo’s health—or worse, if they mentioned that he looked healthy
now—the question would drag him back into the reliving. Even his wife Mai became someone he stayed
away from in the evenings, claiming he was too tired to talk. But his tiredness was a lie: he was
delaying sleep, sitting in the living room while Mai slept, and the longer he stayed awake in the
dim light of the house, the more his eyes would catch on ordinary things—the curve of a lampshade,
the shadow of a chair—and his mind would turn them into the shape of a child’s arm. The triggers
were everywhere: a specific song on the radio, and he would freeze; the dark of the bedroom, and he
would refuse to go in; the sight of his own son’s hand holding a spoon, and Tran would feel his
throat tighten.

The worst part, Tran told the therapist, was that the more he tried to avoid the triggers, the more
they seemed to find him. Delaying sleep meant he was awake in the darkness, which itself was a
trigger. Avoiding people meant he spent long hours alone on the farm, where his mind wandered and
the memory pieces surfaced unbidden. And when a memory came—a sudden fragment of Leo’s thin wrist—it
made him hyperaware of every body part he saw, amplifying the triggers until he could not look at
his own child without a wave of nausea. The cycle was relentless: a memory would trigger a new
sensitivity, and the sensitivity would trigger another memory, and the only response he knew was to
stay up later, work harder, and retreat further from everyone who cared about him.

By the time he walked into the clinic, his marriage was strained—Mai had told him he was “not
himself”—and he had lost weight from the sleepless nights. What frightened him most was the fear
that he would never regain the simple ability to lie down in the dark and feel safe, or to hear a
song and not be thrown back into that hospital corridor. He wanted to be free of the past, but the
past felt like the present, and he did not know how to step out of it.
