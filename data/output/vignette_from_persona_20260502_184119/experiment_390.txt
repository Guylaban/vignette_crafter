He had been a young man in 1968, a recent immigrant from a small village in Punjab, when the draft
letter arrived. Within months he was in the Central Highlands of Vietnam, a rifleman in a company
that walked through elephant grass and rubber plantations. He remembered a particular afternoon in
March, the heat so thick it felt like walking through wet cloth, when his squad entered a village
that was supposed to be empty. They had been told to clear it. What Raj remembered most vividly was
not the firing, not the shouting, but the moment afterward: standing in the middle of a dirt road,
the thatched huts burning on either side, and seeing the body of a woman holding a child who was no
longer moving. He had stood there, frozen, as a sergeant yelled at him to move. He did not fire that
day – he had been on flank security and arrived after the worst was over – but he had seen. And what
he saw had settled into him like a stone.

After the war, Raj returned to the United States, found work at a loading dock, married a woman from
his own community, had children. He did not speak about Vietnam. When his wife asked about the
nightmares, he said they were nothing. When he jerked awake at three in the morning, sweating, he
went to the bathroom and stared at his face in the mirror until his heart slowed. He learned to keep
the memories in a locked room inside his head. For decades, that worked. He raised his children,
paid his bills, retired at sixty-five from the warehouse, and then, because he could not sit still,
went back to work part-time as a greeter and stock clerk. The physical exhaustion helped him sleep.

stomach turn and his throat tighten. The sound of a helicopter on the news, even a traffic
helicopter, sent a chill across his scalp. He had stopped visiting his children because one of them
lived near a small airport, and the distant drone of engines would leave him restless for hours. He
never told them why. They assumed he was becoming difficult in old age.

lunch alone in a supply closet, the door shut, the lights off. He had come to believe that if he let
himself feel the fear fully, it would never stop – that he would be pulled back into that afternoon
in the village and never return. What frightened him most was not the memory itself, but the
certainty that he could not survive it again.

once told him, in a rare moment of honesty, that he had been "gone" since before their first son was
born. Raj had not understood what she meant then, but now he thought he did. He had been standing in
a burning village for fifty years. The rest of his life had happened around that standing.
