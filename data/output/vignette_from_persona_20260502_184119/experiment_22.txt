Layla was fifty years old, a social worker in a community clinic, and she had spent the past six
years trying to outrun a night that never ended. The trauma that fractured her life had begun as a
journey—a desperate flight from her hometown in a country that had become a war zone. She and her
two young children had crossed a border at night, crouching in the back of a smuggler’s truck while
gunfire echoed through the dark. She could still feel the cold metal floor against her knees, the
weight of her son’s hand clamped over her mouth so he would not cry out. When they finally reached
safety, she had told herself the danger was behind them. But danger, Layla discovered, had a way of
living inside her.

In the months after resettlement, she noticed that her body refused to believe she was safe. She
would be standing at the grocery store, reaching for a can of beans, and suddenly her heart would
pound as if gunfire were cracking the air. These false alarms—reactions to nothing at all—left her
shaky and ashamed. She began scanning every room she entered, checking exits, noting faces that
lingered too long. It made sense to her: if she could see the threat before it arrived, she could
protect herself. At night, she woke two or three times to walk through her apartment, checking
locks, peering out the window, making sure no one had breached her fragile perimeter. She told
herself this was vigilance, not illness, but she could not relax. Even in her own living room, a
place she knew was safe, her muscles stayed tight.

These strategies gave her a fleeting sense of control, but they also fed the very fear they were
meant to manage. The more she scanned, the more she spotted ambiguous cues—a man in a heavy coat, a
car idling too long—and each tiny anomaly triggered another false alarm. The more she avoided strong
emotions, the more the world felt charged. At work, Layla was a gifted advocate for refugee
families, but she had developed a quiet habit of steering conversations away from feelings. When a
client began to cry, recounting a sleepless night like the ones Layla knew, she would nod and offer
a practical resource, then hurry to the next meeting. She wore loose, concealing clothing—long
cardigans and scarves—not because of the weather, but because she believed that if she made herself
smaller, less visible, she would be overlooked. She never said this aloud, but the belief was
absolute.

The cycle tightened over years. Layla’s children were now adults, and she lived alone, which meant
no one challenged her routines. She arrived at the clinic early, parked under a streetlamp even in
daylight, and took the same route home every evening. Her colleagues noticed she was always
watchful, always braced, and they assumed it was professional compassion fatigue. They did not know
that she had not sat through a full movie or dinner with friends in years—emotional situations felt
dangerous, as if feeling too much would unlock something she could not contain. She had come to
believe that her vigilance was the only thing standing between her and catastrophe.

What finally brought her to a therapist was a moment at her desk. A client’s adult son had come to
the clinic asking for help with a housing application, and the young man’s resemblance to a soldier
from that long-ago night—the same sharp jaw, the same tired eyes—sent Layla into a full alarm. She
excused herself, locked the office bathroom, and stood trembling with her hand over her mouth for
fifteen minutes. That evening she realized she could not keep running. She was exhausted, and the
danger she was scanning for was not in the room. She needed to understand why her own mind had
become a second exile.
