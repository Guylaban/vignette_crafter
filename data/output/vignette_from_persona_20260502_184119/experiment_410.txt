Klara was thirty-nine when the infection nearly killed her. A routine dental procedure had
introduced bacteria into her bloodstream, and within days she was septic, lying in an ICU with tubes
in her arms and a ventilator forcing air into her lungs. For two weeks she lay trapped under the
fluorescent lights, unable to move, unable to speak, surrounded by the sharp, sweet smell of
antiseptic and the beeping of monitors. In the worst moments, she truly believed she was dying, and
that there was nothing she could do about it. When she finally recovered and went home, her body
mended, but something in her mind did not.

Months later, the world still felt dangerous. Klara returned to her dental practice, but the
familiar smell of disinfectant in her own operatory made her stomach clench. Each time she caught a
whiff of antiseptic, the thought that she was weak returned, along with the certainty that her body
had betrayed her and could never be trusted again. She began to notice old photos of herself from
before the illness—snapshots of a woman who had not known helplessness—and she felt a sharp gap
between that person and the one she was now. That person had been whole; this person felt
permanently damaged, changed for the worse. The feeling of entrapment, of having no options,
resurfaced whenever she was in a crowded room or even in her small clinic, and it whispered that she
was too fragile to handle even ordinary life.

Because Klara believed she was weak and that her body was unreliable, she started avoiding anything
that might confirm those beliefs. She stopped looking at her own body in the mirror, turning away
when she undressed, afraid she would see evidence of damage. She avoided talking to her partner
about the illness, refusing to say the word “sepsis” even when he asked, because speaking about it
made the weakness feel real. She also began to avoid other people, declining invitations and leaving
social gatherings early, certain that they could see she was not the same person—that she was
broken. Every time she avoided, the conviction grew stronger. Her silence about the illness deepened
her sense of permanent change; by not talking, she confirmed to herself that there was something
unspeakable wrong.

The cycle tightened. Her view of herself as damaged made her more sensitive to triggers. She began
to scan her environment for hospital smells, and when she found them—even in a hand sanitizer at the
grocery store—the sense of entrapment flooded back, and she felt weak all over again. At work, she
started avoiding certain dental procedures that required strong antiseptics, delegating them to her
assistant or rescheduling patients. This avoidance felt like a compromise, but it also felt like
defeat. Her partner noticed her distance and grew frustrated; she could not explain why she could
not talk, and his irritation only confirmed to her that she had become a different, lesser person.
She began to feel trapped not just in her memories but in her own life, with no options to escape
the loop.

What finally brought Klara to therapy was a panic attack in her dental operatory. A patient came in
with a mouth infection, and the combination of the odor, the feeling of being enclosed in the small
room, and the thought that she had no control over her own safety sent her heart racing. She excused
herself to the bathroom, where she could not stop shaking. That night she told her partner she
needed help. She is afraid that therapy will only confirm what she already believes—that she is
broken, that the damage is permanent—but she is also desperate to feel normal again, to trust her
body, to not flinch at a smell. She wants to believe there is a way out, even though she cannot see
it yet.
