Amina is a thirty-eight-year-old commercial fisherman who works out of a small Gulf Coast harbor, a
job that demands early mornings and long hours on the water. Eighteen months ago, her younger
brother Kwame was killed in a car accident while she was driving him back to the port after a visit.
A truck ran a red light and T-boned the driver’s side; Kwame died at the scene. In the months that
followed, Amina could not stop replaying the moment—the screech, the impact, the terrible stillness.
She would dwell on how she could have prevented it, going over every decision she made that day,
certain that she should have checked the intersection before proceeding, should have seen the truck
coming. This self-blame was relentless: she told herself that the accident happened because of the
way she acted, that she had failed to protect her brother, and that she would never forgive herself
for not doing more.

Every year on the anniversary of the crash, Amina is overwhelmed by a wave of intense fear and
anger, and her body reacts with a pounding heart and trembling hands. To escape these feelings, she
started staying up very late, delaying sleep so she wouldn’t have to lie in the dark replaying the
crash. She also began spending money impulsively—buying clothes she didn’t need, ordering takeout
she barely touched—to distract herself from the guilt. But these strategies only deepened her sense
of failure. The more she spent, the more she blamed herself for being weak and irresponsible. The
nights she stayed up, she would ruminate on prevention, and that rumination would bring the image of
the car crash back into her mind, as vivid as the day it happened. Even without an external
reminder, her own thoughts became a trigger.

Her daily life is saturated with reminders. She drives to the harbor each morning, and that commute
takes her past the intersection where Kwame died. Her heart races and she feels nauseous every time
she approaches it. At the dock, other fishermen sometimes talk about workplace accidents, and the
word “accident” alone sends a shock through her chest, her face flushing with anger and grief. When
a customer mentioned a recent car collision on the news, Amina had to walk away to keep from
snapping. Her irritability has grown; she has had angry outbursts at her husband for minor things,
and afterward she feels even more guilty. That guilt then drives her back to rumination or to a
compulsive online shopping spree, which in turn leaves her exhausted and more irritable the next
day.

Her husband finally urged her to seek help after she spent a whole paycheck on items she didn’t need
and then stayed up until three in the morning replaying the crash. What frightened Amina most was
that she could no longer trust herself—she felt that if she had failed to protect Kwame, she might
fail again, and she could not bear that. She came to therapy hoping to stop the cycle, afraid that
if she didn’t, she would lose her marriage and her livelihood, and that the guilt would never let
her go.
