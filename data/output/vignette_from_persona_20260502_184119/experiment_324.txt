hall behind him. He froze, then ducked behind a pillar, unable to move toward the attacker or to
guide the panicked crowd. He remembers the smells of cordite and spilt coffee, the wet thud of
bodies, and the long, stretched quiet after the final shot. But when he tries to describe what
happened next, the sequence blurs: he can picture a shoe, a shattered glass case, a woman’s hand on
the floor, but he cannot arrange them in time. Sometimes, when he forces himself to talk about it,
his voice goes flat and the images feel distant, as if they belong to someone else—a sensation that
leaves him both haunted and oddly disconnected from his own past.

Right after the attack, Robert could not shake the feeling that he should have acted differently. He
became convinced that someone else in his position would have charged the gunman or at least shouted
a warning, and that he had failed in his most important duty: protecting others. These appraisals
cut into him daily. He began to believe he could not handle even minor stressors—a loud noise, a
raised voice—without falling apart. To cope, he adopted a rigid set of habits. At his security post,
he now positions his chair against the far wall, scanning the entire room with his back protected.

rehearsing how he would get even with the assailant, imagining confrontations and acts of vengeance
that loop endlessly but never soothe him. And he remains hypervigilant toward every person who
enters his line of sight, cataloging their hands, their jackets, their pace.

replay and resentment. He sought help only after she threatened to leave, and he now sits in the
clinic’s waiting room with his back pressed against the wall, unsure whether he wants to get better
or just to stop feeling so ashamed.
