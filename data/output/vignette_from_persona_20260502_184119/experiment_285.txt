Three years ago, during a break between rehearsals, Naomi was waiting on a subway platform when she
witnessed a police officer shoot a man during a struggle. The sound was deafening; she saw the man
fall and the officer stand over him. She could not move, could not scream—she was a bystander,
frozen, while others fled. In the days after, her memories came only in fragments: the officer’s
raised gun, the man’s face slack, the smell of metal. She could not put the event into words; when
she tried to tell a friend, the story would not form. The officer was cleared, and no one was held
accountable, and Naomi came to believe that the people responsible had faced no consequences at all.
That injustice lodged itself in her chest, and she began to feel permanently set apart from
others—an outsider who could never rejoin the safety of ordinary life.

Because she was certain that violence could go unpunished, Naomi found it impossible to trust that
she was safe, even when others told her she was fine. The world felt openly hostile. Her conviction
that the shooter faced no consequences kept the memory alive: the fragments played more often, more
vividly, as if the injustice itself forced the scene to replay. Each time a piece of the memory
surfaced—the flash of the gun, the crowd’s screams—her heart would race, and she would be flooded
with fear, then anger, then the familiar shame of having done nothing. Her persistent fear convinced
her she was permanently vulnerable, which deepened her belief that she was isolated and beyond
protection.

Naomi began to choose where she sat based on how safe she felt—always with her back to a wall,
always near an exit. At auditions and rehearsals, she scanned the room for anything that reminded
her of that day. Military and police uniforms, even weapons used as props, triggered a visceral
panic. She stopped taking the subway entirely, convinced the next violence was waiting. When casting
calls involved police gear or location shoots near public transit, she declined—each refusal costing
her work but reinforcing that she was no longer part of the normal world. To distract herself from
the crushing isolation, she started spending impulsively: new clothes, expensive dinners, anything
to fill the hours when the memories threatened. But the spending only made her feel more
disconnected from her savings and from people who did not understand why a stable actress would
sabotage her career.

The more she avoided the subway and uniforms, the more threatening they became—she never learned
that public transport could be safe. Her avoidance also left her feeling even more cut off from
colleagues who still took the train to the theater, and their casual conversations about commutes
became reminders of her difference. The isolation deepened her sense of being an outsider, which in
turn made the fragmented memories feel more vivid and inescapable. The fear kept the event alive,
and the memory kept the fear fresh—a loop that tightened every week.

Last month, Naomi turned down a role in a film that required a scene at a train station. Her agent
told her she was limiting herself, and the director asked why she kept passing up good work. She had
no words. That night, she found herself online, searching for therapists who specialized in trauma.
What frightened her most was the thought that she might never get back on the subway—that the
outsider she had become was permanent. She wanted to act again, to go to a rehearsal without
scanning for exits. But she also feared that if she tried to talk about the shooting, the fragments
would turn into something even worse, something she could not control.
