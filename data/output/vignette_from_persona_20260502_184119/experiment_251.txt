Ricardo, a 62-year-old Latin American surgeon, had spent decades in the operating room controlling
what could be controlled. That changed on a rainy Tuesday night when he was driving home from a
twelve-hour emergent case. A sedan ran a red light at sixty miles per hour and T-boned his car,
sending it spinning into oncoming traffic. He remembers the sound of metal folding against metal,
the jolt that threw his body against the door, and then the wet, gritty feel of shattered safety
glass grinding into his cheek as he tried to push himself upright. Blood from a forehead laceration
ran into his mouth. He was pinned for nearly twenty minutes. The driver who hit him turned out to be
a distant cousin he had not seen in years—a fact that made the entire event feel almost personal.

In the weeks after his physical recovery, Ricardo noticed that the memory of the crash felt
disturbingly different from ordinary recollection. The sensation of glass against his skin, the roar
of the impact, and the sight of twisted cars seemed more real than actual conversation or work—more
vivid, as if the accident were still happening. These memories forced their way into his mind at
unpredictable moments, often without warning: while scrubbing in, while driving to the hospital,
while sitting down to dinner. He found himself pushing the memory away before he could let it fully
unfold, because following it to the end meant reliving the terror of being trapped.

These intrusive, hyper-real memory fragments began to make ordinary things feel dangerous. Every
time Ricardo saw a relative—his wife, his daughter, even a familiar face in the hospital hallway—his
mind instantly summoned the image of his cousin’s car crumpling against his own. His brain had
learned to associate known, trusted people with the crash. That association became so strong that
simply hearing a family member’s voice could trigger a cascade of accident imagery.

In response, Ricardo began to avoid situations that put his body under stress, because his body had
become the unreliable container of that awful memory. He stopped jogging, which he had done every
morning for twenty years. He gave up his weekend golf game, a source of genuine pleasure, because
the physical exertion and the open road to the course made him feel vulnerable. He also avoided
thinking about the event altogether: when unwanted images surfaced, he turned on the news, stepped
outside, or threw himself into surgical planning—anything to push the memory back.

Yet these strategies only made things worse. By staying away from physical activity and pleasant
routines, Ricardo had more idle time during which his mind could wander toward the crash. And by
avoiding relatives—cutting phone calls short, declining family dinners—he inadvertently made them
strangers, which heightened his startled recognition every time he did see them. His avoidance
created the very triggers it was meant to suppress.

Months passed. Ricardo’s wife noticed he no longer laughed at her jokes, that he came home from work
and sat in the garage for twenty minutes before entering the house. At the hospital, his colleagues
saw a once-steady surgeon flinch when a nurse dropped a metal tray. He knew he was not the same.
What finally brought him to seek help was a moment in the operating room: a trauma case involving a
car accident victim, and the wet sound of blood on his gloves. The texture—rubbery and slick—sent
him into a flashback so vivid that he had to step away from the table. After that, he could no
longer tell himself he had it under control. He came to therapy afraid that if he let himself
remember fully, he would break entirely—and also afraid that if he did not, he might lose his
family, his career, and the life he had built before the glass and the blood and the cousin he never
called anymore.
