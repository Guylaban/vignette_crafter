Lena was nineteen, a dancer on the cusp of a professional company, when a fire tore through the
converted warehouse where she rehearsed. The panic hit first—a surge of people, and she was knocked
to the floor, pinned beneath a fallen lighting rig that pressed across her chest and arms. She could
not move, could only gasp against the weight, her face pressed into the gritty floor while the smell
of burning plastic and scorched wood filled her nose. Behind her she heard someone shouting, felt
the crowd surge again, and in that moment she was certain someone was right at her back, pressing
closer, that she would be trampled. She clawed at the metal, finally slipped out with bleeding
hands, and escaped into the cold night air.

In the weeks that followed, the memory returned not as a story but as a full, suffocating reliving.
She would smell that acrid plastic, feel the weight on her body, and the shouting behind her would
seem real. Lena could not find words to describe what happened—the few times she tried, the
sentences broke apart in her throat. She came to believe that if she let herself think about the
event, she would not be able to handle it, would fall apart completely. That thought avoidance
hardened into a conviction: what happened was deeply unfair. The rig had been poorly maintained,
nobody was held accountable, and she was left with this inescapable knowledge. She felt she no
longer belonged anywhere—not in the dance world, which now seemed dangerous and untrustworthy, not
in any other life she could imagine.

Fatigue settled into her bones from nights spent waking to check she was safe. When she felt very
tired, the memory surfaced more easily: the exhaustion itself seemed to lower her guard, and the
smell would return (Triggers → Memory). When she walked down a street and felt that someone was
behind her—a stranger walking the same pace, a car idling too long—the full sensory reliving crashed
over her again (Triggers → Memory). And any tight space, a crowded elevator or a fitted jacket,
triggered the feeling of being restrained, and her chest tightened as if the rig were back. Lena
began to avoid going out at all. The sensation of being followed, she reasoned, would not happen if
she stayed home (Triggers → Maladaptive Strategies). She also started covering her face with her
hands whenever an unexpected movement nearby startled her—a car door slamming, a person stepping
suddenly into her peripheral vision—because the startle reaction itself felt like the moment the
crowd surged (Threat → Maladaptive Strategies). Her hypervigilance made her interpret ordinary
sounds and motions as potential danger: a quick footstep behind her meant being followed, a sudden
pressure on her shoulder in a queue felt like being pinned (Threat → Triggers).

Her belief that she could not cope with the memory drove her to avoid any conversation about the
fire, any article, any location that might remind her, and that meant she stopped going to dance
class or even meeting friends who might ask (Negative Appraisals → Maladaptive Strategies). When a
full reliving episode did break through—the smell, the weight, the voice at her back—she would
immediately retreat, hiding her face in her hands until it passed, reinforcing the pattern (Memory →
Maladaptive Strategies). But the avoidance itself worsened her triggers: staying home for days made
her feel even more exhausted, and when she finally forced herself outside, the contrast made every
movement feel threatening, and she was hypersensitive to the sensation of being watched (Maladaptive
Strategies → Triggers). Covering her face also limited her vision, so she flinched more readily at
sudden movements, feeding her constant alertness (Maladaptive Strategies → Threat).

She gave up dancing entirely, along with every other pleasant activity—dinner out, shopping, even
walks in the park—because each one carried the risk of a trigger or a memory (Maladaptive
Strategies: Stop Enjoyment). Her concentration during any reading or task was fractured; she
constantly scanned for potential threats. She woke several times a night to check that the door was
locked, that no one was in the room. At nineteen, her life had narrowed to a small apartment she
barely left. It was her mother who finally insisted she see someone, after Lena spent a third
evening huddled on the bathroom floor, hands covering her face, smelling smoke that was not there.
What Lena fears now is that she will never again feel safe enough to dance, that she will remain
untethered forever—someone who saw injustice and could not process it, someone who belongs nowhere.
But she has come, reluctantly, because she is desperate to find a way back.
