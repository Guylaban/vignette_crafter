Erik was twenty-seven when it happened, walking back to his car after a late shift at the Coast
Guard station in Seattle. He had stopped at an ATM near the waterfront, a route he had taken a
hundred times. Two men came up behind him—he never saw their faces clearly, only the glint of a
knife and the weight of a hand shoving his shoulder. They took his wallet, his phone, his watch, and
told him not to look back. He complied. He walked to his car with his hands shaking, drove home, and
told his girlfriend he had been robbed. That night he tried to describe it and found there were no
words that felt right. He could still see the blade, feel the push, but the story came out flat, as
though he were reciting someone else's experience.

Over the following weeks, Erik noticed that his memory of the mugging came in fragments. A flash of
the concrete wall beside the ATM. The sound of gravel under a shoe. The cold metal of the car door
handle afterward. These pieces did not fit together into a single coherent scene. Sometimes when he
tried to tell the story to a coworker at the station, the words would stall and he would fall
silent. It was not that he was upset—he felt nothing. Completely numb, as if the man on that
sidewalk had been a stranger. This confused him more than the fear would have. He sat in the ready
room one morning after a night patrol, staring at his coffee, and realized he could replay the
entire event in his head and not feel a thing. It was like watching a movie of a character he did
not know.

At work, the Coast Guard routine continued. Erik stood watch, ran drills, checked equipment. But
certain moments triggered the fragments without warning. The fluorescent hum of the station's
hallway—same pitch as the light above the ATM. The metallic click of a locker closing. Each time, a
piece would surface: the edge of the blade, the pressure on his shoulder. They came unbidden,
disconnected, and left as quickly as they arrived. He did not tell anyone about them. He did not
avoid the ATM or the waterfront—he simply found no language for what was happening. His girlfriend
noticed he had grown quieter. When she asked about the robbery, he said, "I'm fine," and believed
it, because the numbness felt like being fine.

Months passed. The fragments did not stop, but they became familiar, like a bad radio station he
could not turn off. Erik continued his duties without error. No one at the station knew he still saw
the knife in his peripheral vision during night watch, or that the cold weight of a life jacket
sometimes felt like the hand on his shoulder. What finally brought him to a therapist was a moment
of confusion on a training exercise. He was supervising a rescue drill and a crewmate shouted a
command—the same tone, the same urgency—and Erik froze. Just for a second. Then he shook it off. But
that second stayed with him. He told his girlfriend he wanted to talk to someone, and she found a
civilian psychologist who worked with first responders.

In the first session, Erik sat forward on the couch and said, "I don't even know what to say about
it. My memory doesn't work right." He wanted to understand why the pieces would not connect, why he
could recall the event and feel nothing, why the fragments kept surfacing at odd moments. He was not
afraid of the mugging anymore, he explained—he was afraid of his own mind not making sense. And he
was worried that next time, the freeze might last longer than a second.
