Mai, a 51-year-old dentist in a small suburban practice, still remembers the precise hum of the song
that used to play from her stalker’s phone. It had been five years since the former patient—a quiet
man she’d treated for a routine filling—began leaving notes on her car windshield, then waiting
outside her clinic, then following her home in evenings. She called the police twice, but he always
disappeared before they arrived, and without a direct threat they could only file a report. One
night he stood beneath her bedroom window, the song looping softly through the dark. That was the
night something shifted permanently inside Mai. She finally obtained a restraining order after he
left a voicemail whispering the lyrics, but by then the damage was done. Even after his arrest and a
brief jail sentence, Mai could never shake the certainty that she was still being watched.

In the months that followed, the world itself became a map of hazards. A familiar melody from a
passing car, a television report about another stalking case, even the sight of a police
cruiser—each triggered an immediate and visceral flooding of dread. What frightened Mai most was
that these moments did not pass. They left a residue: she would find herself scanning every room for
exits, choosing a seat only if it offered a clear view of the door and a wall at her back. She came
to believe that safety was an illusion, even when her husband reassured her that the man was gone.
“They say he’s in another state,” she would whisper, “but they said that before.”

Every day at her dental practice, Mai enacted these rules unconsciously. She positioned her office
chair so her back faced the corner, her eyes trained on the hallway. When the receptionist played
soft pop in the waiting area and an old song from that period came on, Mai excused herself to the
break room until it finished. She avoided all true-crime media, but even a brief newspaper headline
about harassment could leave her jittery for the rest of the afternoon. And police—the very people
who initially could not prevent the stalker—made her feel paradoxically more exposed; seeing a squad
car parked near the clinic would bring back the image of the officer shrugging, saying there wasn’t
enough evidence.

These triggers did not merely remind Mai of the past; they actively eroded her capacity to feel safe
in the present. She began arriving home only after dark, always checking her rearview mirror, and
she insisted that they sit in the corner booth at restaurants, her back solid against the wall. Her
husband grew frustrated—he wanted to go to concerts, to parties, to simply walk the block—but Mai
could not explain why her body refused to believe any reassurance. What she felt was not a memory;
it was a living threat that flickered back to life whenever the wrong sound or image appeared.

Mai finally sought help because her husband, with gentle desperation, told her he could not watch
her shrink away from life any longer. She came to the first session with her hands clasped tight,
unable to fully commit to the idea that talking could help. “I know he’s gone,” she said, her voice
barely above a whisper, “but something inside me still believes he’s out there, ready to start that
song again.” What she hoped—what she was afraid to hope for—was that she might one day walk into a
room without first plotting her escape.
