Chidi was thirty-three, a social media manager in Chicago, married for five years to a woman who
still looked at him with concern he couldn’t meet. Two years earlier he had been a relief worker in
a war zone, distributing supplies in a town that changed hands three times in six weeks. The day
that undid him began like the others—a line of families, dust and diesel, the high whine of
generators—until a mortar round hit the distribution point fifty yards away. He was knocked flat by
the shockwave; when he sat up, there was a boy’s shoe on the ground beside him, still laced, and a
woman nearby whose face he could not remember afterward except as a sound. He helped carry wounded,
but the people who fired the mortar were never identified, let alone held accountable. That absence
of consequence lodged in him like a splinter: no one had answered for it, and the more he turned it
over, the more he felt the world was a place where terrible things happened and nothing was set
right.

Back home, the injustice stayed with him. He believed the attack had been deeply unfair, and the
fact that no one faced consequences made him certain that danger could come at any moment, from any
direction, without warning. He started scanning every environment before he entered it—checking
corners, watching hands, noticing exits. His hypervigilance became automatic; he would catch himself
flinching at a car backfiring, or freezing when a delivery truck growled too close to the curb. At
work, managing a brand’s social feed meant scrolling through thousands of pieces of content daily. A
single thumbnail of a dusty street or a blurry explosion would lock his concentration until he was
aware only of his heart and the small black edge of panic. He found it almost impossible to
concentrate on simple captions or analytics because his attention kept drifting to ambient sounds—a
dropped keyboard, a colleague’s laughter—any of which could be the first note of something worse.

He tried to manage the vigilance by pushing the memories away. When a thought of the boy’s shoe
surfaced, he forced it down; when he felt the startle rising at a sudden noise, he told himself not
to think about it. He detached from the memories as if they belonged to someone else, a stranger he
was responsible for but did not want to know. For a few months this seemed to work. But the strategy
backfired: the more he avoided thinking about the event, the more his threat system stayed on high
alert, as if the unprocessed material leaked into his surroundings. His scanning intensified—he
checked the rearview mirror repeatedly while driving, and at night he would get up to walk the
apartment, listening. His wife would wake and ask what he was doing, and he would say he was
checking the locks, but really he was checking for the shape of something he could not name.

The cycle tightened. Because he could not rely on himself to stay calm—he felt he might lose
control, might scream or run in a meeting—he relied even more on scanning, on avoidance, on the
hollow ritual of detachment. Each time he suppressed a memory, his hypervigilance spiked; each spike
made him more certain that he could not trust his own mind, which fed the sense that the world was
still unfair and unaccountable, and that he was alone in his response to it. His startle response
became so pronounced that a colleague touching his shoulder sent him nearly out of his chair. He
started wearing headphones at his desk, partly to concentrate but mostly to control what he heard.

He came to therapy because his wife, gently, told him she could not live with someone who was always
braced for an explosion that might never come. He was afraid, though, that she was wrong—that the
explosion would come, and he would be the only one who saw it, frozen by the memory of a boy’s shoe
and a face he could not fully remember.
