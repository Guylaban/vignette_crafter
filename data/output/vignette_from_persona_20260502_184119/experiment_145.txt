Lin was forty-nine, divorced, and working the IT help desk at a community college in the San Gabriel
Valley. She had served a single tour in Afghanistan fifteen years ago as a communications
specialist. The forward operating base where she was stationed had been hit by a mortar attack on a
hot, dusty afternoon. She remembered the shriek of the incoming round, the sudden pressure, then the
fire that caught a fuel truck parked near the comms tent. The smell of burning diesel and plastic
filled the air, thick and acrid, as she helped drag a wounded soldier toward the landing zone where
a medevac helicopter was descending, rotors thudding low and loud. That sound—the rhythmic chop of
blades—and that burnt smell had never left her.

Now, back in civilian life, those same triggers found her in ordinary places. A faint whiff of
something burning in the office kitchen—someone's burnt toast, a malfunctioning server fan—could
make her hands shake and her heart pound before she even knew what was happening. When a maintenance
helicopter flew low over the campus, she would freeze at her desk, her body tensing as though the
walls might collapse. The workplace itself had become a trigger: the hum of computers, the
fluorescent lights, the way colleagues would suddenly appear at her cubicle. Each surprise approach
felt like a potential threat. She had begun to feel threatened by strangers even in neutral
situations—a student asking for directions, a delivery person at the front desk. Her physical
reactions were immediate: rapid breathing, a hot flush, the urge to bolt. She found herself checking
behind her constantly, scanning the hallway before she left the office.

The feeling of being threatened drove her into rigid patterns. To escape that raw, jumpy state, she
started avoiding any situation that might stir strong emotions—she stopped watching the news,
declined invitations to crowded gatherings, and kept conversations at work surface-level. She also
refused to talk about the deployment with anyone; when a well-meaning coworker once asked about her
military service, she changed the subject and then excused herself to the restroom. That same
hypervigilance, however, also sharpened her awareness of triggers: the more on edge she felt, the
more she noticed every helicopter sound in the distance, every odd smell, every unexpected footstep
behind her. Her threat system was constantly feeding on itself.

The avoidance and self-silencing only made things worse. By not talking about the event, she never
gave her memories a chance to soften; the burnt smell and helicopter sound stayed as vivid and
terrifying as they had been that afternoon. At times the intrusive thoughts became so unbearable
that she hurt herself physically—digging her nails into her palm or pressing the sharp edge of a
paperclip into her forearm—because the pain was something she could control, a momentary focus that
drowned out the flashback. But that very act of self-harm brought the trauma back to the forefront
of her mind, looping her into a fresh wave of threat perception. After she hurt herself, she would
feel even more exposed and unsafe, scanning the parking lot on her way home as if someone were
watching.

What finally brought Lin to a clinic was a specific incident two weeks ago. A student had left a bag
of burnt popcorn in the office microwave, and the smell had filled the entire IT suite. She had a
full panic attack—hyperventilating, disoriented, convinced she smelled diesel—and ended up hiding in
the supply closet for twenty minutes. Her supervisor, concerned, suggested she take leave. Lin now
sat in the therapist’s office because she was afraid of losing her job, and she was even more afraid
that the images from that base would never leave her. She hoped, hesitantly, that talking about it
would not make it worse. She was tired of checking behind her, tired of the pain in her hands, tired
of feeling like a stranger in her own life.
