T‑boned his sedan. He spent two weeks in the trauma unit with a fractured pelvis and internal
bleeding, then months in rehabilitation. Physically he healed enough to return to work, but
something in him never quite settled. He had come to believe that his body was now permanently
damaged—a conviction that surfaced every time he felt a twinge in his lower back or had to brace
himself against a heavy patient transfer. “You can never know who will harm you,” he would think,
scanning the faces of drivers in the parking lot. The accident had taught him that safety was an
illusion.

the floor in the gym, his whole body jerked as if the collision were happening again. The startle

concentration dissolve, leaving him staring at the treatment table unable to remember the patient’s
exercise plan. These loud noises—the crash of a dropped weight, the sudden bang of a door—triggered

the dark, the residue of the dream pressing him into a certainty that he was still vulnerable, still
at risk.

To manage this dread, Somsak developed a set of quiet rituals. At the start of every shift he

against the chaos the accident had introduced. He also began mentally rehearsing worst‑case
scenarios for each patient, imagining what could go wrong during a transfer or an exercise and how

anything that might serve as a weapon or a hazard: the scissors on the counter, the fire
