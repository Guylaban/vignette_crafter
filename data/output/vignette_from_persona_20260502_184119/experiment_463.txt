Leila had been a judge for thirty-seven years—weighted sentences, parsing evidence, delivering
verdicts she believed in. But nothing in the courtroom had prepared her for the morning she received
the call about her son, Tarek. He had collapsed at his desk at forty-two: a massive coronary, no
warning, no second chance. By the time Leila reached the hospital, he was gone. She had been
presiding over a custody hearing when her clerk slipped her the note; she had read it, excused
herself, and walked out of the chamber. In the months that followed, she could not stop returning to
that walk—measured, deliberate, composed. She should have run. She should have known. What kind of
mother exits a courtroom while her son is dying?

Alone in her chambers afterward, she began to turn the event over and over, searching for the moment
she could have changed things. She remembered, with painful clarity, that she had noticed Tarek
seemed tired the last time they spoke. He had mentioned chest tightness, briefly, and she had told
him to take an antacid. That piece of memory became unbearable. She became convinced that the event
happened because of the way she acted—because she had dismissed his symptom, because she had been
too absorbed in a docket to press him further. In her own mind, she had failed to protect him, and
the word *failed* took on a judge's finality: there was no appeal.

She had always taken pride in her composure under pressure. Now she saw that composure as evidence
of something ugly. Her reactions since the event—the way she could still sit through a hearing
without crying, the way she could drive home and prepare dinner—convinced her that she was a lousy
coper. A good mother would have fallen apart. A good mother would have sensed the danger. Leila had
sensed nothing, and her calm felt like a betrayal of love itself.

At home, her husband, Karim, tried to draw her out. He would mention Tarek's name gently, hoping she
might share a memory, but Leila would shake her head and return to the kitchen or her study. She had
come to believe that talking about him was a kind of performance—that if she grieved "wrongly," she
would only confirm her own inadequacy. So she said very little. Karim began to eat dinner alone more
often. Their conversations narrowed to logistics: who would pick up the groceries, whether the car
needed servicing. Leila knew she was pulling away, but she also knew she deserved to be alone.

At work, she continued to hear cases, but she noticed a change in herself. She had less patience for
defendants who made excuses. She found herself delivering harsher sentences to parents who had
neglected their children, and once she caught herself thinking, *You should have protected them,*
before she caught the thought and recognized it as her own voice. The courtroom had become a mirror
she could not look away from.

What finally brought her to seek help was a small thing. Karim had left a framed photograph of Tarek
on the kitchen table—a candid shot from a family barbecue, Tarek laughing with a piece of watermelon
in his hand. Leila had picked it up, held it for a moment, and placed it face down on the counter.
Then she had called her doctor the next morning. She was terrified that she had already become
someone she did not recognize: a mother who could not look at her son's face, a judge who could not
trust her own fairness, a woman seventy-five years old who still believed, deep in the hollow of her
chest, that if she had only been better, he would still be alive.
