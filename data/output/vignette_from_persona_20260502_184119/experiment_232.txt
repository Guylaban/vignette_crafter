Marco, a twenty-eight-year-old prison guard, had been working the evening shift when a riot erupted
in C-block. He and his closest colleague, Diego, moved to contain it. A knot of inmates surged, and
in the crush Marco lost sight of Diego. By the time he fought through, Diego was on the floor, a
shank buried in his chest. Marco pressed his hands over the wound, but the blood kept coming. Diego
died before the medical team arrived. In the weeks that followed, Marco replayed that moment
hundreds of times: the feel of warm blood, the weight of Diego’s body going slack, the certainty
that if he had only moved faster or been louder, Diego would still be alive.

Now, eight months later, Marco cannot escape the conviction that he failed not just Diego but
everyone under his watch. “I used to be happy,” he told his wife, Ana, one night after a nightmare.
“Now I’m always miserable. There’s no future for me.” That belief has taken root: he sees himself as
a man who ruins everything he touches.

The triggers are everywhere. A news report about prison violence makes Marco’s hands tremble; his
mind goes fuzzy and he feels as though he is watching himself from outside his own body, detached
and unreal. If a coworker mentions a family member who died—even in a different context, like a
grandmother passing—Marco’s stomach clenches and he has to press his palms over his face, blocking
out the world until the feeling passes. Those moments of unreality confirm his hopelessness: he is
broken, unable to function like a normal person. And the hopelessness, in turn, makes him
hypervigilant to any hint of loss. A throwaway line about someone losing a loved one now hits him
like a punch; he scans every conversation for it, sure it will drag him under again.

To escape, Marco has developed a set of strategies that only make things worse. When the
dissociative fog settles, he covers his face with his hands, hiding from the trigger. At work he
picks arguments with guards who weren’t there, accusing them of not understanding, of being soft—he
knows it pushes people away, but the conflict distracts him from the images. He avoids anything that
reminds him of Diego: the locker room, certain radio stations they listened to, even the Mexican
restaurant they visited every Friday. The more he avoids, though, the more isolated he becomes, and
the more his belief that he is a miserable failure hardens. He no longer sees the point in trying.
The same hopelessness that makes him scan for triggers also drives him to start fights—he figures if
he is already lost, he might as well burn the rest down.

But the arguments create their own triggers. After shouting at Ana for asking how his shift went,
she will bring up his grief—“You never talk about Diego, it’s eating you alive”—and that talk of
loss sends him straight back into the dissociative fog, his hands reaching for his face. He cannot
win. The loop tightens: a news report (trigger) leads him to cover his face (maladaptive strategy)
and then to berate a coworker (another strategy), which makes the coworker bring up the riot
(trigger), which reinforces the appraisal that Marco is a danger to everyone around him (negative
appraisal). The appraisal then drives him to avoid even more, cutting off the few human connections
he has left.

For a while, Marco’s strategies allowed him to keep working, but the cost is mounting. He has been
written up twice for aggressive conduct with inmates and once for yelling at a lieutenant. His
marriage is strained; Ana sleeps in the guest room most nights. What finally brought him to a
therapist was a moment two weeks ago when he came home, saw a photo of Diego on the fridge—Ana had
put it there to help him remember good times—and he felt nothing. No sadness, no anger, just a
hollow detachment that terrified him more than the pain ever had. He is afraid that if he does not
change, he will lose his job, lose Ana, and lose whatever small part of himself still hopes. But
when he thinks about letting the feelings in, he is certain he will drown.
