Tama is a 53-year-old photographer who, three years ago, was sent to cover a political uprising in a
small Pacific island nation. While driving between villages, his vehicle was stopped by armed
militia. They blindfolded him, bound his hands, and held him for seventeen days in a concrete room.
He never saw his captors’ faces, but he learned to read their moods through the pitch of their
voices, the speed of their footsteps, the way the door would slam or creak open. He was certain at
any moment they would kill him. When he finally escaped during a chaotic firefight, he ran for hours
through the jungle, not stopping until he reached a coastal town.

Back home in Honolulu, Tama tried to resume his life as a wedding and event photographer. But the
world felt different. Every venue he entered for a shoot, he would first scan for exits—where the
back door was, whether a window could open, whether a hallway led to a dead end. He always chose a
spot to stand that gave him a clear view of the room and kept his back to a wall. If a guest moved
suddenly toward him—raising a hand to wave, stepping quickly to catch a falling champagne
flute—Tama’s chest would seize, his muscles would harden, and for a split second he would forget he
was holding a camera, not running for his life. His body was constantly tense, coiled like an animal
that had learned never to relax. He began to ask his wife, Maya, every morning: “Do I look okay? Are
we safe? Is this normal?” He would text her multiple times during a job just to hear her say
everything was fine.

The more he watched for danger, the more danger he found. A man standing too still near the bar. A
woman laughing too loudly. The way a door swung shut. Each time his body went on alert, Tama felt a
fresh wave of certainty that danger was real—and then he would check the exits again, shift his
position, text Maya for reassurance. The reassurance worked for a few minutes, then the doubt crept
back. He had come to believe that his safety depended on never letting his guard down, that if he
stopped monitoring the room, something terrible would happen. But the monitoring itself exhausted
him. At the last wedding he photographed, he spent so much time planning escape routes and watching
guests’ movements that he missed the couple’s first kiss. The bride’s mother complained. Maya, after
months of comforting him, finally said she couldn’t keep telling him he was safe when he never
seemed to believe her.

That was what brought Tama to seek help. He was afraid of losing his wife, afraid of losing his
career, but most of all afraid that the captivity had changed something permanent in him—that he
would spend the rest of his life watching shadows, never able to lower his shoulders or trust that
the door would stay closed. He hoped, quietly, that a therapist could teach him how to unlearn the
vigilance that had kept him alive in that room, but was now keeping him from living.
