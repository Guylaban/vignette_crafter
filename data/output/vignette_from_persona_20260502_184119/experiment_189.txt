Mariama is a 72-year-old forester, a profession she chose decades after arriving in the United
States as a refugee from Liberia’s civil war. She still carries the night the rebels came. It was
past midnight when the first mortar round hit the edge of her village, a sound so vast and
percussive it seemed to lift the earth beneath her feet. In the chaos that followed—explosions
tearing through the darkness, the sharp crack of small-arms fire—she grabbed her two youngest
children and ran into the forest. Her husband, who had gone to investigate the first blast, never
made it back to the house. She learned later that he had been killed by shrapnel. For three weeks
she and the children hid among the trees, surviving on cassava and rainwater, every rustle of leaves
and distant bang signifying armed men drawing closer. That period forged an association she has
never been able to break: darkness means danger, sudden sounds mean death, and people who move with
purpose through the shadows mean betrayal.

Now, more than thirty years later, Mariama lives alone in a small house at the edge of a national
forest where she works managing trails and monitoring wildlife. The quiet she once craved has become
unbearable. Every night, as dusk settles, she turns on the television at full volume or plays loud
gospel music from a portable speaker in the kitchen. She tells herself the noise keeps her company,
but the truth is that silence leaves space for sounds she cannot control—a creaking floorboard, a
distant truck backfiring, the wind rattling a window latch. What she has not recognized is that the
loud music itself creates a new vulnerability. Because she cannot hear approaching footsteps or
ambient noises, any unexpected bang—a door slamming, a branch breaking outside—hits her with full,
unfiltered force, sending her heart racing and her mind back to that first mortar round. The
strategy she uses to feel safe makes the triggers she fears more likely to catch her off guard and
strike harder when they come.

During the day, Mariama’s hypervigilance takes a different form. Out on the trails, she constantly
scans her surroundings, checking between trees and behind rocks with the practiced attention of
someone who once had to spot armed men before they spotted her. She is particularly watchful of
other people. Hikers who pause too long on a ridge, men who carry backpacks and move with deliberate
steps, anyone who seems to be watching rather than walking—her gaze locks onto them, parsing their
behavior for signs of threat. In the small town where she shops for supplies, she has come to
interpret ordinary interactions as suspicious: a man standing near his car in the parking lot, two
people talking in low voices outside the post office. Each perceived suspicion confirms the need for
vigilance, and the vigilance ensures she will keep noticing ambiguous behaviors and reading danger
into them. The scanning, meant to protect her, steadily populates her world with potential threats.

Her strategies have reshaped her life in concrete ways. She no longer visits her adult daughter in
the city because the drive requires stopping at a gas station after dark, and she cannot tolerate
being outside at night. She has stopped attending her church’s evening prayer meetings. Her
supervisor has noticed she avoids certain trail sections and finishes her field work earlier each
season to be home before sunset. She knows she is withdrawing, but the reasoning feels airtight: if
she stays hypervigilant, if she keeps the noise on, she is doing what it takes to survive. What
finally brought Mariama to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when she heard a car backfire on
the road behind her house, and before she could think, she was on the floor behind the sofa, her
hands over her head, trembling, unable to move for nearly an hour. She is afraid of what she might
become if she lets the strategies go, but she is more afraid that she has been making an already
unbearable situation worse. She wants to know if there is a way to feel safe without having to fight
the silence so hard, and whether a woman her age can still learn to trust the dark.
