Mark, a 58-year-old dentist in a long-term relationship, still flinched every time a patient’s
shadow fell across the operatory doorway—a reflex that had begun three years earlier, after a
traffic stop turned into what he could only describe as a beating. He had been driving home from a
late appointment when two officers mistook his sedan for a stolen vehicle, pulled him out, and spent
several minutes on his back and ribs while he pleaded. The memory returned not as a coherent story
but as pieces that belonged to someone else: the metallic crunch of a boot against his spine, the
scrape of asphalt across his cheek, the officer’s voice ordering him to stay down. He knew it had
happened to him, yet the scenes felt like a film he had stumbled into by mistake. That sense of
dislocation was the only way his mind seemed able to hold what his body had endured.

Over the following months, Mark became convinced that some flaw in him had drawn the violence. He
replayed the moment the patrol car’s lights came on and asked himself what he could have said, how
he could have looked, to signal *different*—as if his own face or posture had been an invitation.
This belief that his intrinsic fault was to blame made the memories feel even more alien; they
belonged to a version of himself who had been weak or wrong, a person he no longer recognized. When
the auditory flashbacks came—a sharp command, a crack of radio static—his ribs would ache as if
freshly struck, and he would wonder whether the man in those sounds was even him.

To keep the memories at bay, Mark began steering his life into a narrow channel. He avoided anything
that might stir emotion, positive or negative, because feeling anything seemed to crack the seal on
the pain recall. He cancelled a dinner with old friends because the restaurant would be crowded and
unpredictable. At his dental practice, he outfitted the waiting room with partitions so he could not
see who was approaching the front desk, and he arranged his own operatory chair so his back faced a
solid wall. He checked every new patient’s name, then scanned the hallway before leaving a room.
Still, the threat of strangers felt constant: a man walking too close on the sidewalk, a delivery
driver who lingered at the door—each one triggered a spike of auditory recall, the officer’s voice
overlapping with the present moment, and a fresh wave of pain in his lower back.

That sense of threat also worsened his sleep. He lay awake listening for footsteps in the hall, his
body braced, and when he finally drifted off, the dream version of the stop always ended with a blow
he could not avoid. Waking from these nightmares, he would lie rigid and replay the injustice: no
charges were filed, no apology came, the officers’ names were never released. That feeling of being
crushed by an unfair system reinforced his belief that his life had been destroyed—not just
physically but morally, chronologically. Every avoidance, every checking of the room, every refusal
to let a stranger stand behind him at a checkout line only confirmed that he was now a person who
had to live this way.

The patterns tightened into a loop he could not see. Because he avoided all stress, he never went to
the small park where he used to walk; because he never walked there, he never gave himself the
chance to learn that strangers on a path were not officers with badges. Because he checked his
surroundings compulsively, he stayed hypervigilant, and hypervigilance kept the auditory recall
present. The alien quality of the memory—the sense that the whole event had happened to a different
man—meant he could not integrate it into his story, so it stayed raw and easily triggered. And each
triggered memory reaffirmed that he was fundamentally marked, that the injustice was permanent, that
his life was over.

What finally brought Mark to a therapist was his partner’s quiet observation that he had not touched
her in months—not out of anger but because he had shut down all feeling, even affection. He came not
hoping to erase the memory, but terrified that he might never again feel like the person he had been
before the lights came on in his rearview mirror.
