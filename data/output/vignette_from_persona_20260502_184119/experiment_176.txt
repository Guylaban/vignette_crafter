without warning. A whiff of industrial cleaner, the hum of an old ventilation fan, even the sight of
a glass of water can instantly bring back the full sensory experience, and when she tries to explain
what happened to a colleague, she loses the thread midsentence, unable to finish. The taste itself
is so vivid that she sometimes spits involuntarily, which mortifies her, and she has come to believe
that if she talks about the event too much, it will somehow make it real again. So she tells herself
it was not that bad, that she overreacted, even though her body knows otherwise.

Priya’s body feels constantly tense, as if coiled to react to the next invisible threat. She scans
every room she enters for signs of danger—a strange hissing sound, an unfamiliar odor, a vent that
looks poorly sealed. This hypervigilance began soon after the exposure; she noticed she was snapping
at junior staff for trivial mistakes, her irritability erupting without warning. What frightens her
most is that the memory itself seems to generate this physical alertness: the instant the taste or
image surfaces, her shoulders hitch, her jaw clenches, and she is suddenly scanning again. She has
also noticed that the reverse is true—when she is already on guard, say after a tense meeting, a
minor trigger is more likely to flood her with the full memory, the taste rising unbidden.

avoids anything she anticipates could be stressful—she turns down assignments involving conflict

conditioning unit. She also avoids thinking about the event altogether; when a related thought

she finds herself repeating a private mantra: *It didn’t really happen. It wasn’t that bad.* Yet

ambush her—often in quiet moments, when she lets her guard down. The avoidance also keeps her threat

that the danger is over, so she remains perpetually tense, scanning, ready.

mind, that the memory is eroding her competence, and she hopes—though she can barely admit it—that
someone can help her find a way to live without the constant expectation of danger.
