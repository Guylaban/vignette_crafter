Layla was nineteen when the mortar round hit her forward operating base in Helmand Province. She had
been a communications specialist, assigned to a reinforced bunker that doubled as a relay station.
The first explosion deafened her; the second collapsed a section of the sandbag wall and pinned a
soldier beneath a beam. For the next three hours she crouched beside him, pressing a field dressing
to his thigh while he faded in and out of consciousness. She remembers the sound of his breathing,
the weight of debris against her back, and the certainty that they would not make it. They did make
it—he was evacuated, she was pulled out after the all-clear—but something in her changed that night.
She finished her deployment mechanically and left the service two months later, at twenty, with a
recommendation for a database administration program.

Now, at twenty-one, Layla works as a DBA for a mid-sized logistics company. She lives alone in a
one-bedroom apartment in a suburb that feels safe enough. She has built her life around a set of
rules she never consciously decided on.

Layla avoids any situation that puts her body under physical stress. She takes the elevator even for
a single flight of stairs. In the data center where she sometimes works, she refuses to carry server
racks or climb ladders, always asking a colleague to handle the lifting. She drives to the grocery
store two blocks away because walking makes her heart race, and the feeling of being out of breath
reminds her of the dust and pressure in the bunker. She canceled her gym membership the week she
moved into the apartment and has not exercised since.

At home and at work, Layla cleans and organizes things repeatedly to feel in control. Her desk at
the office is spotless—keyboard aligned with the monitor, cables tied with Velcro straps, a single
pen exactly parallel to the notepad. She wipes down the surfaces three times a day with disinfectant
wipes. At home, she vacuums the living room every evening, folds her clothes into precise
rectangles, and rearranges the items on her bathroom counter if they shift by even an inch. If
someone moves one of her possessions, she becomes restless until she restores the order. The
cleaning gives her a quiet sense of certainty that nothing else provides.

Layla never goes out at night. She leaves work by four-thirty, before the winter sun sets, and has
her groceries delivered rather than shop after dark. She declined a promotion that would have
required occasional after-hours support calls, and she has not seen her friends for dinner in over a
year because they tend to meet at seven or eight. She does not answer the door after dusk and keeps
all her blinds drawn once the streetlights come on. When she needs milk or bread on a late
afternoon, she plans the trip carefully so she is home before the last light fades.

These rules have made her life narrow but manageable. She gets by. She notices that her colleagues
find her habits odd—the cleaning, the early departures, the refusal to help with physical tasks—and
she has stopped trying to explain. What brought her to seek help was the growing loneliness: a
birthday she spent alone, a sister’s wedding she left before the reception started. Layla is not
sure she can give up the routines, but she is beginning to fear what will happen if she keeps them.
