Carlos is a 55-year-old Latin American man who serves as a Navy officer, a role he has held for the
past two decades. He lives with his partner on a naval base, and his days are structured around
command responsibilities aboard a destroyer. But the present is haunted by a past he cannot escape.
Twenty-three years ago, before he ever wore a uniform, Carlos was a civilian in his home country
during a brutal civil war. He and his younger sister were caught in a militia raid on their
neighborhood. While he hid in a cupboard, pressing his hand over her mouth to keep her quiet, she
was found and dragged away. He did nothing—froze, unable to move or speak. She was never seen again.
That moment has become the fixed point around which his inner life revolves.

The memory of that afternoon does not arrive as a story he tells; it arrives as a total immersion.
At any moment—during a briefing, while he inspects equipment, in the dead silence of his
quarters—the scene returns with full sensory force: the smell of dust and cordite, the sound of
boots on tile, the weight of his sister’s body against his chest, the wet heat of her breath on his
palm. In these intrusions he is not an officer in his fifties; he is a terrified young man in a
closet, and the guilt is immediate, crushing. He feels the inaction like a physical weight, and the
thought that follows is always the same: *I am weak. I should have protected her. I will never
forgive myself.*

That belief—that he is fundamentally weak and that he failed—does not stay confined to the memory.
It bleeds into his everyday life. When he gives orders, he sometimes hesitates, and the hesitation
triggers a flash of self-contempt: *A weak man cannot lead.* And that thought, in turn, seems to
summon the full reliving of the closet, the dust, the shame. The causal path runs both ways, a loop
that tightens with each pass. The memory feeds the appraisal—*I am worthless because I did
nothing*—and the appraisal primes the memory, making it more vivid, more probable, more real. He has
come to believe that he is a coward, and every sensory reliving confirms it.

Sometimes the memory comes but the feeling does not. Carlos can recount the events of that day with
clinical detachment, numb to the point where it feels as though it happened to a stranger. These
moments of emotional flatness are almost more disturbing than the full reliving, because they
convince him that he has no right to feel numb—that numbness is another sign of weakness, another
failure of proper response. He cannot win.

In his work environment, the triggers are unavoidable. A sudden loud noise on deck—a hatch slamming,
a horn—can catapult him back into the closet. The sight of a female subordinate who reminds him of
his sister is enough to initiate a spiral that lasts hours. He has learned to keep his face neutral,
but his partner has noticed the distance, the irritability, the way he flinches at unexpected
sounds. Their relationship has grown strained, and Carlos knows that his inability to talk about the
event is part of the damage.

He came to therapy after a near-miss on the ship last month. During a drill, he froze for three
seconds—a lifetime in a command position. His second-in-command had to give the order. No one was
hurt, but the incident terrified Carlos. He realized that the memory and the guilt were not just
mental ghosts; they were actively eroding his competence. What he fears most is that one day he will
freeze again and someone will die because of it—and that then he will have truly failed to protect,
just as he did before. He hopes that by speaking the story aloud, he might break the loop, though he
is not yet sure he deserves to.
