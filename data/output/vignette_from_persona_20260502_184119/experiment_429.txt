Three years ago, while attending a weekend market in his hometown, a man drove a truck into the
crowd. Tama was thrown against a concrete planter, his leg pinned under debris. He lay there for
what felt like an hour, hearing screams, feeling the heat from a burning stall, and watching a woman
beside him go still. When he finally crawled free, he could not stop his hands from shaking. He
still feels them shake now, even in his studio.

In the months that followed, the memory of that day forced its way into his mind without warning.
The images were brutally clear—the twisted metal, the woman’s face, the sound of his own breath.
When the memory came, his body would crouch involuntarily, his shoulders hunching, his knees
bending, as if he were still pressed against that planter. He would find himself frozen, unable to
move until the reenactment passed. These episodes left him feeling that the world was a dangerous
place, and that he could not rely on himself or anyone else to keep him safe. Because the world felt
so threatening, he began to avoid going out at all. He cancelled his weekly trips to the supply
store and stopped walking to the seafront to sketch. He had come to believe that leaving home
invited disaster.

To manage the fear, Tama tried hard to control his emotions. He would grit his teeth when tears
came, force his breathing to stay shallow, and tell himself not to feel anything. But this effort
only seemed to make the intrusive memories more vivid. The more he clamped down, the more his body
reenacted the crouch, and the more the images burst through. He also started asking his niece, the
only person he still spoke with, to reassure him that he was okay. He would call her late at night,
apologizing, but needing to hear her say he was safe, that he had not changed. Yet after each call
he felt more convinced that he could not rely on her—or anyone—because the reassurance never lasted.
This fed his belief that the world was still dangerous and that he was incapable of handling it on
his own.

Tama’s painting, once his life’s anchor, became another battleground. He would stand at his easel,
brush in hand, and the memory would force in: the reek of gasoline, the weight of the planter, the
sound of the woman’s silence. His body would lock into the crouch, and he would drop the brush. He
tried to avoid those moments by painting only abstract shapes, but the memories did not care. His
studio, the one place he thought he could control, became the place where he felt most helpless. He
stopped answering his niece’s calls, ashamed of needing her, and yet he still dialed her number,
desperate for her voice. Each time he hung up, he felt the world close in a little more.

Now, Tama has barely left his house in six months. He has not painted in three weeks. What finally
brought him to the clinic was the realization that he could no longer even face the blank canvas—the
one thing that had always made him feel like himself. He is afraid that if he goes out, something
terrible will happen again, and he is equally afraid that staying inside will erase who he used to
be. He hopes, though he struggles to say it aloud, that someone might help him trust himself again.
