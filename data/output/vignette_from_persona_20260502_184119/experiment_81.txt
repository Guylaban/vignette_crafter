was the last thing he remembered clearly; everything after came in disconnected pieces—the sound of
the explosion reverberating inside his skull, the burnt smell clinging to his uniform, the
helicopter’s blades, but never a coherent story he could put into words. He knew what had happened,
but when he tried to describe it, the words simply would not come.

Back home, Ryan’s world had shrunk. He had come to believe that every street corner, every passing
car, every closed space could hold another threat. The world no longer felt neutral—it felt actively
dangerous, and he had to be on guard all the time. That hypervigilance bled into everything. Because
he was certain that a crowd could mask a bomber or a sudden noise could signal an ambush, he avoided
shopping centres and public events entirely. What frightened him most was the thought that he might
let his guard down. He also stopped going to church, a place he had once found comfort; now it felt
hollow, and he was not sure his life was worth living anymore anyway. To keep the thoughts at bay,
he spent long hours on his phone, scrolling through social media or playing endless rounds of a
mobile game, sometimes until three in the morning. If he was not driving or sleeping, he was
escaping into the screen.

distraction. But the more he used his phone to push the memories aside, the more those memories
seemed to return in disconnected pieces, as if his avoidance had shattered them further. And each
intrusive replay reinforced his conviction that the world was a dangerous place designed to take him
by surprise.

of rotors. He knew he could not keep driving away from it. He wanted the memories to stop, but deep
down he was terrified that nothing would ever make them quiet.
