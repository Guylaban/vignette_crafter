eight years. He had been standing near the electronics counter when the man walked in with a rifle
and began firing. The event lasted no more than two minutes, but for Mark it collapsed into a

industrial carpet, and the texture of that rough weave—sticky with spilled soda and dust—burned into
his senses. He could still feel it, days later, as if the fabric had transferred itself onto his
palms.

not as ordinary recollections but as moments that felt more real than the present: he would be
stocking shelves and suddenly See the flash, Hear the pop, Taste the metallic tang of fear. What
confused him most was the shame. He had done nothing wrong, yet when he remembered lying there while
others died—or, worse, when he recalled the man who had kept firing, the moral violation of it, the
complete disregard for human life—Mark felt a hot, private humiliation. He had not helped anyone. He
had not even been brave. He had just stayed flat on the carpet, and the carpet was still with him.

His daily environment as a retail worker became a minefield. Physical proximity to other
customers—crowds jostling in the aisles—made his chest tighten and brought the snapshots roaring
back. Even worse were the moments when he saw people misbehaving: a shopper yelling at a cashier, a
teenager stealing a candy bar, a small moral violation that felt to Mark like a re-enactment of the
shooter’s disregard. And then there was the texture. When he touched anything rough and fibrous—the
cardboard of a shipping box, a coworker’s wool sweater, the lining of his own jacket—the carpet came
alive under his fingers, and he would freeze, lost in the memory.

been that bad, that he was overreacting, that it must have been a different store, a different day.

come. He hoped the therapist could help him make the snapshots ordinary again. He was terrified that
