the sink until the wave passed. Blood became a threat—not simply a bodily cue, but a promise that
she was about to feel unwell again. And whenever she did feel physically unwell—a headache, nausea,

she agreed with whatever the charge nurse suggested, nodded along when patients’ families demanded

does that? She felt like she no longer knew herself. The woman who had once laughed through twelve-
hour shifts, who had argued with doctors and advocated for terrified mothers, was gone. Nothing in
her life felt meaningful anymore. The birth of a grandchild last spring should have been a joy, but

smear in the hospital confirmed that she was not the person she had been, and that sense of loss
made her more alert to the next trigger, more afraid of feeling unwell.

room, hands folded in her lap, certain that she is too broken to fix, but terrified of staying like
