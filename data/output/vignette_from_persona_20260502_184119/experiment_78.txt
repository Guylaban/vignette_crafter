Amir was twenty years old when the assault happened, a night that had started with the familiar
scent of jasmine perfume and ended with him walking home in the dark, unable to make sense of what
had just occurred. He had been seeing a woman from his university for several months, someone he
trusted, and when she invited him to her apartment that evening, he had no reason to suspect what
would follow. The smell of jasmine became the central thread of that memory—it was on her skin, in
the sheets, and it lingered in his hair and clothes long after. But it was not just the smell. What
happened that night felt like a profound moral violation: she had taken something from him that he
had not consented to give, and in the weeks afterward he began to see the same casual disregard for
boundaries everywhere—people cutting in line, coworkers taking credit, strangers shouting at service
workers—and each small transgression seemed to confirm that the world was a place where people
simply took what they wanted. The humiliation was the worst part. He would replay the moment he had
said yes because he was too scared to say no, and he would think: somebody else would not have
gotten into this situation. Somebody smarter, somebody stronger. He came to believe that he used to
be a happy person, but that version of himself was gone, replaced by someone who felt permanently
marked, permanently less than.

In the months that followed, Amir found himself reacting to things he had never noticed before. When
a woman on the train wore jasmine perfume, his throat would close and he would have to get off at
the next stop. When a colleague said "you know what I mean" in exactly the intonation his ex had
used, Amir would feel a flash of heat and a need to be somewhere else. These triggers pushed him
into habits that felt necessary for survival. He began to wear concealing clothing—oversized jackets
and hoodies even in warm weather—hoping to make himself invisible, to avoid being seen or noticed or
evaluated. He started every day by checking the layout of whatever building he was in, locating the
exits before he could focus on anything else. He avoided performance reviews and one-on-one meetings
with his manager, volunteering for audit tasks that kept him in the back office rather than in
client-facing roles. But each avoidance came with its own cost: when he chose the hoodie, when he
declined a meeting, when he sat with his back to the wall at lunch, he told himself that this was
proof of what he had become. The strategies confirmed the very appraisals that had driven him to
adopt them—a loop that tightened with every repetition.

His body was never at rest. Amir felt constantly tense, his shoulders locked, his jaw clenched, his
hands ready to grip something. When a coworker reached across his desk without warning, he would
flinch and feel his heart hammer before his mind had registered the movement. He avoided unfamiliar
places entirely—new client sites, restaurants he had not been to before—because he could not assess
the danger in advance, could not know where the exits were or who might be watching him. The threat
itself sharpened the triggers: the more vigilant he was, the more he noticed the smells, the more he
caught people misbehaving, the more his ears snatched at phrases that echoed her voice. And his
strategies kept him in a constant state of hyperawareness. By always scanning for exits, by always
positioning himself defensively, by always monitoring his own visibility, he was priming his nervous
system to expect danger at every turn.

By the time he sought help, Amir was twenty-two and had been living this way for nearly two years.

of evaluation he could not tolerate. His work had narrowed to the same few audit files he could
complete without going to new places. What finally brought him to a therapist was a moment in the

not quite the same but close enough. He had felt the smell of jasmine from nowhere—probably just

had worn for months, and he thought: I cannot keep doing this. He wanted to feel like himself again,
but he was not sure that person still existed, or whether there was anything left to find.
