the weight of her betrayal more clearly than any single moment; the memory arrives in disconnected
pieces: the smell of damp concrete, the metal taste of blood, the rotors overhead, and then the
suffocating silence after she left.

dim hallway at the community center where he worked, the way the shadows fell would instantaneously
return the full recollection—the helplessness, the sound of her voice, the helicopter drone. He
began to avoid the block where the apartment stood, taking two buses out of his way to run a
meeting. The darkness became a constant, menacing presence; he came to believe that dark spaces were
dangerous because he had been beaten in one, and that belief made him scan every shadow for a
threat. His shattered sense of the world—he had trusted that a partner would never harm him—now
colored every environment. A flickering streetlight or an alley at dusk felt like a trap.

These appraisals of betrayal and self-blame deepened his memories. He was certain, for example, that

would replay the humiliation of finding himself pinned against the wall, his own character exposed.
The memories, in turn, kept his negative beliefs alive. Each flashback confirmed that he had been
weak, that the world was treacherous, and that his judgment could never be trusted again.

strategy, however, made him feel invisible and powerless—another piece of evidence that he was a
person who deserved what happened. His concealment also made him more alert to any potential danger;
the very act of hiding forced him to monitor who might be looking at him, amplifying the sense that
threats were everywhere. The helicopter sounds became unbearable. When a news chopper passed over
his neighborhood, the engine noise triggered a disoriented, fragmented recall—the blades, the
blows—and he would freeze, certain he was back in that basement.

Over months, Amir’s avoidance became a pattern. He stopped walking through the part of town where
the apartment was located, which meant he lost the direct bus route to work and arrived late twice a
week. He turned down a speaking engagement at a public plaza because the evening lighting would be
dim. His hypervigilance toward people strained his relationships with colleagues; they mistook his
scanning for standoffishness. His wife, a patient woman, noticed he flinched when she reached for

sudden shadow—reinforced his conviction that he was fundamentally broken, and that belief, in turn,
made him more alert to the next trigger.

What finally brought him to therapy was a moment at the community center when a colleague turned off

day he called a clinic. Sitting in the intake, he described the helicopters, the basement, the sense
that his life had been reduced to a series of ambushes. He was most afraid that acknowledging the
abuse meant admitting his partner had won, but he was also desperate to stop living inside the loop
of memory and fear.
