Lukas, a 26-year-old fisherman from a small coastal town in Maine, still carries the three days he
was held captive two years ago. A territorial dispute with another crew escalated into violence—he
was taken at knifepoint, blindfolded, and locked in a shipping container behind a warehouse. For
hours each day, they beat him with a rubber hose, burned his chest with a cigarette lighter, and
wrenched his arm until his shoulder dislocated. Through the metal walls he could hear a baby crying
from a nearby house, high and insistent, threading into his own screams. When they finally dumped
him on a rural road, a bus roared past, its headlights and the faces at the windows flooding him
with the certainty that they were coming back for him.

Now, married to Emily and working the same waters, every crying baby on the dock or in a passing car
seizes his body. The sound of a baby’s wail instantly drags him back into that container: his arm
locks at his side, he feels the burn on his ribs, and a wave of rage surges through him—he wants to
hit something, break something. The memory is not just an image; it is his body repeating the
position, his shoulder aching with the old dislocation, his chest stinging as if the burn were
fresh.

That rage and physical reenactment have reshaped how Lukas moves through the world. He cannot sit
with his back to a door; at home he positions his chair against a wall, facing the room, and he
listens for footsteps or the creak of a loose floorboard. On his fishing boat he stations himself
with his back to the cabin wall, scanning the horizon for any unfamiliar vessel. He notices
potential weapons everywhere—a gaff hook, a filleting knife, a coil of rope—and catalogues them
without meaning to.

The vigilance, in turn, feeds the memories. When he hears a bus outside his house—the low rumble
through the walls—his hyperawareness kicks in and the container memory returns at full force, his
body locking, pain flaring. The more he scans for threat, the more easily he is triggered. A baby
crying on the dock sends him into a flashback; a crowded bus makes him ditch his trip and walk three
miles home. Even feeling emotionally vulnerable—after a heated argument with Emily, when her hurt
face made him feel exposed—that internal state itself has become a trigger. He now avoids
conversations about his past, stays silent when she asks how he is, and keeps his back to her as he
sleeps.

The cycle has tightened over months. Lukas avoids buses completely, walking wherever he can or
relying on Emily to drive. He refuses invitations to friends’ homes where babies might be present.
On the boat, his crewmates have noticed he flinches at sudden engine noises and positions himself
defensively. His avoidance has cost him: he missed a lucrative charter because the only transport
was a bus, and his relationship with Emily is strained by his emotional distance. He sought help
only after she told him she could not live with his silence and his rage any longer. What frightens
Lukas most is the anger—the intensity of it, the feeling that if he let go, he might hurt someone.
He hopes therapy can help him find a way out of the loop without having to talk about everything,
but he is certain that if he opens up, the memories will drown him.
