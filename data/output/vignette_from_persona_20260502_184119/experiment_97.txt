Karim, a 52-year-old actor of Middle Eastern heritage, has not set foot in a theatre since the night
his wife Layla was killed. It was a spring gala for the local arts community when a gunman opened
fire. Layla died instantly; the shooter vanished and was never caught. For Karim, that failure—of
the police, of the security, of every institution that should have protected them—became the lens
through which he now sees the world. He also cannot forgive himself for not doing more; the inaction
guilt is a constant ache.

The memory itself is fractured. He recalls the flash of the muzzle, the sound of Layla's gasp, but
these pieces do not form a coherent sequence. Some moments are frozen like photographs, painfully
vivid. Whenever he remembers, the guilt surges, and with it the feeling that he is not safe
anywhere.

In the months that followed, triggers began to seize him without warning. The sound of a child
crying—his daughter Maya's sobs, or a stranger's child at the grocery store—would instantly bring
back those photographic images and the guilt. Media coverage of other shootings, even fictional
crime dramas he might once have auditioned for, would fracture his concentration and fill him with
fear, horror, and anger. Each trigger reinforced his belief that institutions had failed and that
the shooter would never face justice.

To cope with the constant sense of threat, Karim started avoiding being alone. He filled his
schedule with people, agreeing to whatever they wanted because being with others felt safer. But
crowds terrified him, so he avoided shopping centers and public events. His people-pleasing,
however, sometimes led him into environments that revived the memories: when a friend asked him to
watch a crime drama, the sound of gunfire on screen sent his mind reeling back to the gala. Each
time he avoided a crowd, he silently repeated that he should have done more, deepening the inaction
guilt and the conviction that no one was held accountable.

The hypervigilance he developed—always scanning for exits, flinching at sudden noises—made him
acutely sensitive to possible triggers; a child's cry that he once would have ignored now commanded
his full attention. And because he avoided being alone, he was often in public spaces where triggers
were more likely to occur, such as a shopping mall where a news report of a shooting blared from a
store television. The guilt-laden memories drove him to cling even more tightly to others: he would
agree to any plan just to keep someone nearby, even if that plan meant facing a crowded venue he
dreaded. But his constant social scheduling, while temporarily soothing, prevented him from ever
learning that he could be alone without danger, so his sense of threat never abated.

Feeling perpetually unsafe, Karim became more convinced that the system had failed him—after all, if
he could not trust his safety, it must be because no one had been brought to justice. And the belief
that the shooter was still out there kept his threat system on high alert, making every unfamiliar
face a potential danger. When a trigger like a child crying or a news segment appeared, the sudden
emotional surge would cause his concentration to dissolve and negative emotions to overwhelm him.
That same trigger would also instantly revive the fragmented, photographic memories. And just as
instantly, he would think, “Nothing has changed, no one is safe, no one is held accountable.” To
regain a sliver of safety, he would then seek out any friendly face, going along with their
suggestions without question.

Karim’s career as an actor has suffered. He cannot concentrate on scripts, and the thought of
performing in front of a crowd is unbearable. The final push came when his ten-year-old daughter
Maya began having nightmares about the shooting, asking him why they couldn't catch the bad man. He
realized he could not help her while drowning himself. He sought therapy, hoping to find a way to be
present for his daughter—but terrified that talking about the night of the gala would shatter the
fragile equilibrium he has built.
