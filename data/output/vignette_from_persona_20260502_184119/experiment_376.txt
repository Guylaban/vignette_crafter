skin, the smell of oil and garlic, the way his knuckles whitened. But the memory comes in
disconnected pieces—the knife, then the floor, then her own voice saying “please” without knowing if
she actually spoke. The terror of that moment returns with photographic clarity, as if she is still
pinned there, yet the sequences between images are missing. In the years since, she left that man,
eventually married a kind accountant, and became a tutor, working one-on-one with students in their
homes. But she has never shaken the belief that she has permanently changed for the worse—that the
woman before that kitchen no longer exists. She feels like an outsider everywhere, even in her own
marriage, certain that no one can ever understand what she carries, and that you can never know who
will harm you.

Mai does not realize how much this belief shapes her memory until she is driving to a tutoring
session. The sight of a man in a loose jacket on the sidewalk makes her stomach drop, and suddenly
she is back in the kitchen, the knife-edge at her throat. The threat triggers the memory
directly—her mind leaps from the stranger’s silhouette to the blade, as if the two are connected by
a wire. Because she already believes she is permanently damaged, she interprets these intrusions not
as ordinary flashbacks but as proof that she is broken beyond repair, that the event has rewritten
her. That appraisal, in turn, makes the memories more frightening and more frequent; she expects
them, dreads them, and so they come.

To cope, Mai has developed strategies that feel necessary but only tighten the cycle. Before
entering a student’s home, she scans the front porch for anything that could be used as a weapon—a
loose brick, a garden tool. She checks her rearview mirror obsessively while driving, even on empty
streets, certain someone might be approaching. She is hypervigilant toward any person who seems
aggressive or unpredictable, especially men who raise their voices. During a session, if a father
walks past the room too quickly, Mai’s body reacts before her mind does—her shoulders tense, her
breath shortens—and she begins scanning the room for potential weapons all over again. This constant
monitoring ensures she notices every dangerous object: the letter opener on the desk, the kitchen
knife visible through a half-open door. Each discovery reinforces her sense of threat, and that
sense of threat sends her right back into the memory of the knife at her throat, as if the present
and the past have fused.

Over the months, the cycle has worn her down. She has started canceling sessions with families who
have male partners at home during the day, telling herself it is safer. Her husband has noticed she
flinches when he reaches for a kitchen utensil, and she cannot explain why. What finally brought her
to seek help was a moment last week: she was tutoring a fourteen-year-old boy, and he accidentally
dropped a textbook with a loud slap on the table. Mai screamed, then apologized, then excused

is afraid that the permanent damage is real—that she will never be able to stop seeing knives in
every room. But she has come, because a small part of her still hopes that the woman she was before
the kitchen exists somewhere, waiting to be found.
