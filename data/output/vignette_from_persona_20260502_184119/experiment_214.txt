Minh is a 72-year-old nanny, a job he took after retiring from a decades-long career in restaurant
work. He is married and lives with his wife in a small apartment, but his days are spent caring for
the toddler of a young professional couple. For most of his life, Minh has carried a secret he never
speaks of: the physical abuse he suffered at the hands of his former romantic partner. The beatings
occurred over several years, always in the dark of their shared bedroom after he had expressed any
frustration or anger. He learned that showing emotion only made the violence worse, so he taught
himself to suppress all anger completely, to avoid any circumstance that might feel like that dark
room, and to steer clear of anything he considered stressful. Now, nearly five decades later, these
strategies have become automatic, but they no longer keep the past at bay.

When memories of the abuse surface—often triggered by a sudden darkening of a room or the unexpected
movement of a child running past him—Minh feels the same terror he felt then. His body stiffens, his
arms draw in as if he is bracing for a blow. The memory feels alien, as though it belongs to someone
else, yet the physical reenactment is undeniable: his legs lock, his shoulders hunch, and for a
moment he is back in that bedroom. This terror directly fuels his hypervigilance. He startles at the
slightest noise—a dropped spoon, a creaking door—and cannot relax even when he knows he is safe. At
work, the toddler’s sudden grabs or the mother walking into the room unexpectedly send his heart
racing. Each startle reinforces the memory, because his body remembers the position he was in when
the abuse happened, and he finds himself recreating that posture even as he tries to calm down.

The threat response, in turn, strengthens his maladaptive strategies. After a startle episode, Minh
becomes even more determined to suppress any flicker of irritation or annoyance. He avoids going
into the child’s dark room at night, asking the mother to handle bedtime instead. He declines to
take the child to the park if the weather is overcast, because dim light reminds him of the bedroom.
He tells himself that if he can just control his environment perfectly, the memories will stay away.
But the opposite is true: the more he avoids, the more the memories intrude. When he deliberately
swallows his anger at a misbehaving child, the effort of suppression itself triggers a vivid
flashback—his jaw clenches, his vision narrows, and he feels the cold floor beneath his back. The
memory returns stronger, feeding the cycle.

Over the months, this loop has tightened. Minh has become increasingly isolated at work,
communicating only in short sentences, avoiding any interaction that might feel confrontational. His
wife has noticed that he jumps at her touch and spends his evenings sitting stiffly in the living
room, unable to relax. She urged him to see a therapist after he broke down crying during a
thunderstorm, unable to explain why the sound of rain against the window terrified him. What finally
brought Minh to the clinic was a single moment: the toddler accidentally knocked over a lamp,
plunging the room into darkness. Minh dropped to his knees, his arms wrapped around his head, and
stayed that way for several minutes. The mother found him there. He could not bring himself to
explain. He is most afraid that if he lets himself talk about the abuse, the anger he has buried for
so long will explode and destroy everything he has built. But he is also exhausted, and he hopes
that maybe, at last, he can learn to live without the constant vigilance.
