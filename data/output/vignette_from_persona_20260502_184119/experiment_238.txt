Michael was forty-nine when he finally walked into a therapist’s office, a year after retiring from
the city council. He had served two terms, known for his steady voice in budget debates and his
reluctance to attend community meet-and-greets. His wife, Diane, had been after him for months. “You
flinch every time I touch you,” she said. “You haven’t slept through the night in years. Something’s
wrong.” He knew she was right, but he couldn’t bring himself to explain why.

The trouble started in Afghanistan, during his second deployment. He was a logistics officer, not
infantry, but that didn’t matter the day the convoy hit an IED. He remembered the blast — the
pressure, the ringing, the smoke — but after that, the memory broke apart. He could piece together
fragments: the medic’s hands on his arm, a soldier shouting, the taste of dust and copper. But the
middle was gone, a blank that left him dizzy when he tried to fill it. When he recounted the event
to Diane or to his VA doctor, he would lose his thread mid-sentence, stammer, and give up. The
terror, though, never faded. Every time the memory surfaced — triggered by a car backfiring or a
helicopter overhead — it hit him with the same visceral force, his heart hammering, his chest tight.

Back home, Michael began to see the world differently. The bombing had taught him that danger could
be anywhere, and he became certain that he had to stay on guard at all times. He scanned crowds for
suspicious packages, noted every exit in a room, and sat with his back to the wall at restaurants.
His own body betrayed him: his palms would sweat, his muscles would tense, and he would feel a surge
of adrenaline for no reason he could name. He came to believe that his body was his enemy, reacting
as if the IED were still detonating. This sense of constant threat made it impossible to trust
safety even when Diane assured him they were fine. At night, he lay awake, listening for footsteps,
and when he finally fell asleep, a sudden sound would jolt him awake, gasping.

The threat itself spiraled back into his memory. The more hypervigilant he became, the more he
noticed gaps in his recall — he would catch himself flinching at a touch and then wonder what he had
been remembering, the fragments seeming to dissolve further. Sleep deprivation eroded his
concentration, making the blank spots in his story even larger.

To cope, Michael developed strategies that he thought would keep him functional. He blasted loud
music or talk radio whenever he was home alone, using the noise to drown out intrusive images. He
constantly monitored his own thoughts, scanning for any sign that he was “losing it” — a flash of
anger, a memory that came on too strong. And he avoided being touched by anyone: no handshakes at
council events, no hugs from friends, no sudden pats on the back. When Diane reached for him in bed,
he would stiffen and pull away.

These strategies, however, only made things worse. The sensory distraction kept him from sitting
with his memories, so they never integrated; he remained stuck with the same incomplete, terror-
laden fragments. His thought monitoring convinced him that something was wrong with his mind,
reinforcing the belief that his body and brain were untrustworthy. Avoiding touch meant he never had
a corrective experience — nobody’s gentle touch ever taught him that contact could be safe. Instead,
any accidental brush from a constituent at a town hall sent his heart racing, and he would retreat
into his office, certain the world was dangerous.

The cycle tightened. Because he could not trust his own memory, he felt more vulnerable, which
deepened his hypervigilance. Because he was always on guard, his memory stayed fragmented and
frightening. His negative appraisals — the world is dangerous, my body is against me — fed directly
into his sense of threat, making the startle response quicker and the sleeplessness worse. In turn,
every startle, every sleepless night, confirmed that he was right to see danger everywhere.

What finally brought Michael to the clinic was a moment at a council retirement party. A colleague
clapped him on the back, and he spun around, fist raised, before he could stop himself. The room
went quiet. Diane had tears in her eyes. He realized then that his strategies were failing him as a
husband and as a public servant. He still cannot explain what happened in that convoy without losing
the thread, and the terror is as fresh as ever. He is afraid that therapy will mean reliving it, but
he is more afraid of losing Diane — and himself — to the silence he has built around the missing
parts of his story.
