Tai, a 67-year-old widowed interior designer of Samoan descent, has not entered his studio in over a
year. The trouble began two years ago, when two men broke into his home, bound him to a chair in a
small bathroom, and ransacked the house while shouting in Arabic. For several hours he sat in that
confined space, convinced he would be killed. The memory of that afternoon has never left him.

Intrusive memories strike without warning—a sudden image of the bathroom tiles, or more often the
sound of shouting in Arabic from a passing car or a neighbor's television. These triggers instantly
flood him with strong negative feelings: fear, horror, anger, and a deep shame that he did not fight
back. His body reacts before his mind can catch up—his heart pounds, his breathing turns shallow,
and he feels an urgent need to press his back against a wall where he can see the entire room. That
state of hypervigilance, in turn, tends to pull forward more memories—the captors' voices, the smell
of the space. When he tries to describe what happened to a friend, he often loses the thread and
cannot finish, and at other times he recalls the events but feels completely numb, as if they
happened to a stranger; yet even that numb recall triggers the same visceral threat response, as if
his own detachment fools no part of him.

To manage the constant sense of danger, Tai has developed rigid strategies. He rehearses what to say
before any social interaction, scripting each line so he can avoid feeling emotionally vulnerable.
He avoids enclosed or confined spaces entirely—elevators, small restrooms, tight hallways. Most
consequentially, he has stopped going to work. As an interior designer, he used to visit clients'
homes and new construction sites; now the thought of entering an unfamiliar enclosed room is
unbearable. These strategies, however, have a paradoxical effect. Rehearsing conversations keeps his
mind fixed on potential threats, amplifying his sense of danger and keeping his body on constant
alert. Avoiding work and confinement leaves him isolated at home, where the silence often gives way
to auditory memories—the shouting in Arabic echoes in his head, and the memory becomes more vivid
and intrusive the more he tries to shut it out.

Since the attack, Tai has lived in a state of near-constant hypervigilance. He always chooses a seat
with his back to a wall, scanning the room for exits. This level of alertness makes him acutely
sensitive to any sound that could be shouting, especially in a language he does not understand. A
raised voice at a grocery store, a neighbor's television, a car horn—each can trigger a vivid
intrusive memory, because his body is already braced for danger before his mind has even identified
the source.

Tai finally sought help after a trip to the grocery store ended in a panic attack when a customer
shouted to a friend across the aisle. He is terrified he will become completely housebound. He is
hoping to regain the independence he lost after his wife’s death, but he is also afraid that talking
about the captivity will make the memories worse—and that he might lose the only thread of control
he has left.
