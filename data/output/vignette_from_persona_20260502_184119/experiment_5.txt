Kai was twenty years old when he joined a humanitarian convoy delivering medical supplies across a
contested border region in Southeast Asia. He had believed in the work—neutral, lifesaving, above
the politics. But on the third week, their convoy was stopped at a checkpoint by armed men in
mismatched military fatigues. One of the soldiers pointed a rifle at Kai’s chest and demanded he
kneel. Kai complied, hands raised, while the soldier’s eyes moved slowly over his face, studying
him. The entire time, Kai felt watched not just by that soldier but by everyone in the compound—men
on rooftops, a woman peering through a curtain, a child holding a phone that might have been
recording. His team was let through eventually, but the feeling never left. A week later, a nearby
hospital was shelled. Kai had argued to stay and help with the triage, but his supervisor ordered
the team to evacuate. They left before the second shell hit. In the months that followed, the memory
of those eyes—the soldier’s deliberate stare, the watchers from the windows—became fixed in Kai’s
mind as the moment his role as a protector had failed. He should have done something, stayed, argued
harder. Instead his body had frozen, then fled.

Back in the United States, Kai enrolled in a university to finish his degree. He told himself he was
moving on. But on campus, certain sights began to feel like landmines. A campus police cruiser
idling by the library made his chest tighten. A student in a blue ROTC uniform walking past the quad
sent a jolt through him—he had to look down, clench his fists, and walk the other way. Being watched
became unbearable. In the dining hall, if a stranger’s gaze lingered too long, even in passing, Kai
would set down his tray and leave. He started wearing a baseball cap pulled low and avoided the main
routes that passed the police station two blocks from his apartment. He stopped going to the student
lounge where he used to meet friends; it felt too exposed, too many faces turned toward the door.
When forced to interact, he kept his eyes averted, worried that if someone saw his fear, they might
see something else—a guilty man, a failed man.

Kai’s body felt like a traitor. His heart would race at the sound of a car door slamming, his palms
sweat when he saw a security guard’s uniform in a grocery store aisle. He believed his body was
lying to him—overreacting, proof that he was still weak, still the man who couldn’t hold his ground.
Each physical reaction reinforced the idea that he had failed in his most important role: to
protect, to be someone others could rely on. And if he couldn’t trust his own body, how could he
trust that he would ever feel safe? This thought looped back into his vigilance. Because he felt
helpless—certain that bad things would happen and he could not stop them—he began scanning every
environment for the next threat. A man standing too still on the subway platform. A police officer
who glanced in his direction. Every time he saw a uniform or felt eyes on him, the helplessness
deepened, and every time the helplessness deepened, he felt more watched.

Over the year, Kai’s world narrowed. He stopped accepting invitations from classmates. He avoided
the student union entirely. He switched to online grocery orders to bypass the store with the
security guard. His grades slipped—not because he could not do the work, but because he could not
sit in a lecture hall without checking every face, every exit, every patch of dark fabric that might
be a uniform. He had not told anyone about the convoy or the watching. He was certain they would see
him as broken, or worse, as someone who had failed and deserved his fear.

What finally brought Kai to the clinic was a persistent, grinding exhaustion. He had spent so many
months avoiding and tensing that he no longer remembered what it felt like to relax. One morning, he
saw a campus officer standing near the bus stop and instead of walking away, he stood still and
wept. He could not keep running. He was afraid—afraid that talking would make the memories more
real, afraid that nothing would change. But he was more afraid of another year spent shrinking from
every eye that met his.
