Carmen was sixty-six now, but the memory of that afternoon in San Salvador had not dimmed. She had
been walking home from the market when the first mortar round hit two blocks away, then a second,
closer. She threw herself behind a low wall as the air filled with dust and screams. When she lifted
her head, she saw a soldier in camouflage sprint past, rifle raised, and then a man crumple onto the
cobblestones. The soldier kept running. Carmen had never learned who the man was, but the image—the
green uniform, the sharp crack of the explosion, the sight of that soldier’s face in profile—had
lodged itself somewhere deep. She made it home, and weeks later she arranged passage to the United
States. She never spoke of it. She built a life: a job as an editor at a small academic press, a
marriage that eventually ended amicably, an apartment in a quiet part of the city. She thought she
had left the war behind.

She had not. The first sign came years ago, during a Fourth of July barbecue. A teenager lit a
string of firecrackers, and Carmen’s hands went cold. Her heart pounded so hard she had to sit down.
She told herself it was just a sound, but her body refused to believe it. Over time, the triggers
multiplied. A car backfiring on her street sent her heart racing for hours. A police officer walking
into her favorite coffee shop made her leave without ordering. And then there was the night a siren
wailed past her building and she found herself crouching behind her couch, certain she could hear
boots on the stairs. What frightened her most was that the feeling of danger never truly left—it
only changed in intensity. She had come to believe that safety was a lie people told themselves; she
knew better.

Now, as an editor working from home, Carmen had arranged her life around an invisible perimeter. She
kept her blinds closed after sunset. She slept with the television on—low volume, a news
channel—because absolute silence made her strain for sounds that might not be there. Several times a
night she would wake, heart already thudding, and walk through her apartment checking the locks.
Even when a friend assured her the neighborhood was safe, even when her own daughter told her she
was being irrational, Carmen could not shake the conviction that somewhere, just out of sight, a
uniformed figure was watching. The worst episodes happened at night. During the day she could
manage: she distracted herself with manuscripts, edited carefully, answered emails. But dusk brought
a tightening in her chest that she could not talk away.

The causal loop was invisible to her but relentless. A few weeks ago, she was working late on a
manuscript about Central American history—she had edited many such books without trouble—when a
chapter mentioned the Salvadoran civil war. She did not read it; she simply saw the word “mortar”
and closed the file. That night she lay awake, certain she heard distant booms. The more she tried
to convince herself she was safe, the more her mind supplied reasons she was not: the creak of the
building, the shadow of a tree branch, a car door slamming. Each sound became a potential trigger,
and each trigger renewed her vigilance, which made her listen harder, which brought more triggers.
She had taken to sleeping in the living room because her bedroom window faced the street. Even
there, she would sit upright at 3 a.m., watching the window, unable to believe the quiet.

What finally brought Carmen to a therapist was not the fear itself but its cost. She had missed
three deadlines at work because she could not concentrate after broken nights. Her daughter,
visiting from out of state, had found her circling the apartment at 2 a.m. and had insisted she talk
to someone. Carmen agreed, partly to placate her daughter, partly because she was exhausted. She
still could not say she believed she was safe. But she could say she was tired of being afraid.
Sitting in the waiting room, she watched the door, half-expecting a police officer to walk in. She
did not know how to stop that expectation. She only knew she wanted to try.
