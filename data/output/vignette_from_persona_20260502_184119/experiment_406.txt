Layla was forty-six years old, a pharmacist who had spent nearly two decades behind the counter of a
bustling chain pharmacy, but the memory that governed her life now was not one of pills or
prescriptions—it was the morning her eight-year-old son, Omar, had stopped breathing. An allergic
reaction, sudden and terrifying. She had called 911, ridden in the ambulance, watched him be rushed
into the emergency room, and for three days he had lain in the pediatric ICU while machines breathed
for him. He recovered fully, but Layla never did.

In the months that followed, she began to notice a hollow certainty settling inside her: she was a
weak person, fundamentally defective, someone who had not been able to protect her own child. She
told herself that nothing good could ever happen again—how could it, when she had failed at the most
basic task of motherhood? Because she saw herself as weak and flawed, she started to avoid anything
that reminded her of that day. She stopped taking the route that passed the hospital, even if it
added twenty minutes to her drive. She asked a colleague to handle any prescription that came with
an emergency label. At home, she changed the channel the moment a medical drama appeared. She tried
very hard not to think about the trauma, pushing the images away whenever they surfaced, but the
effort left her drained and hollow. And late at night, long after Omar was asleep, she would lie in
bed and ask herself over and over why this had happened to her—as if an answer might somehow undo
what she believed about herself.

The avoidance and the constant suppression did not bring peace. Instead, they seemed to feed a
growing sense of vigilance. Layla found she could not relax even when she knew she was safe—sitting
in her own living room, she would feel her shoulders tense, her breath shallow. Ordinary sounds—a
car door slamming, a delivery truck rumbling past—made her jump as if she had been shocked. Sleep
became a battleground: she would lie awake for hours, her mind cycling through the same images
despite her best efforts to block them, and when she finally drifted off, she woke again before
dawn. The hypervigilance only deepened her belief that something was fundamentally wrong with her—a
normal person would not react this way, she thought; a normal person would have moved on. And that
conviction, in turn, made her even more alert to any possible sign of danger, scanning her
surroundings for triggers, waiting for the next thing to startle her.

At work, the cycle tightened. Layla worked in a busy pharmacy where ambulances passed outside
several times a day. The first time she heard a siren after the trauma, she had ducked behind the
counter, her heart pounding, and from then on she kept earbuds in during her shifts, music playing
low to dull the sound. She saw her own fear as further proof of her weakness—why could she not just
cope like everyone else?—and that judgment made her avoid even more: she stopped attending team
meetings held near the break room window that faced the street, she asked to be scheduled only for
morning shifts when emergency arrivals were less frequent, and she never, ever spoke about what had
happened. But the suppression did not calm her; it only made her more jumpy. When a customer
mentioned in passing that her grandson had been rushed to the ER with an allergic reaction, Layla
felt her chest tighten and her vision narrow. She excused herself to the back, hands shaking, and
spent ten minutes trying to breathe. That evening, she lay in bed once again unable to sleep, the
old question pressing: *Why did this happen to me?* And the old answer returning: *Because you are
weak. Because you are broken.*

Weeks passed. Her manager had begun to notice her flinch at the sound of the pharmacy’s own
intercom, her avoidance of certain tasks, the way she never joined her coworkers for lunch in the
break room. A colleague gently asked if she was all right; Layla said she was fine, but the question
stayed with her, and that night she found herself searching online for therapists who specialized in
trauma. She was not sure what she hoped for—she had come to believe that nothing good could ever
happen again, that she was too defective to be fixed—but the episode at work had frightened her. She
was tired of the sleeplessness, tired of the jumpiness, tired of the constant effort it took to keep
the memories at bay. What scared her most was the thought that she was losing control of herself
entirely, that the person she had been before—the confident pharmacist, the capable mother—had
disappeared and left only this vigilant, avoidant shell in her place. She made the call to a
psychologist’s office the next morning, her voice barely steady, and as she waited for the
appointment date, she tried not to think about whether it would matter.
