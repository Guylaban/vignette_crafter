of language, and the physical layout of the hospital building itself became permanent landmarks in
her inner geography.

In the months afterward, Leilani found that any reminder of that space could send her heart racing.
Driving past a hospital, even from a block away, made her palms sweat. She began to avoid the entire
district where the medical center stood, taking long detours through the industrial zone. When a
colleague in her architecture firm used medical terminology during a presentation, Leilani felt her
chest tighten and excused herself, walking briskly around the block until her breathing steadied.
That was when she started running—compulsively, at first a few miles, then ten, then fifteen, until
her knees ached. She told herself it was for health, but really it was to outrun the images.

her loss was written on her skin like a scar. This conviction made her cancel plans with friends

it. The isolation fed the belief that life had lost all meaning, which in turn made her more
vigilant for hospital smells and garbled chatter—she was constantly scanning, bracing for the next
assault.

Her days became a cycle of scanning and retreating. She would wake and check the air for antiseptic;

traveled, but which inadvertently brought her closer to the trigger. She never acknowledged the
