slammed her head against the kitchen table. She remembered the dull crack, the metallic taste in her
mouth, and the way her body went rigid—locked in that exact position, arms pinned, face pressed

she never told anyone, because she lacked the language for what happened, and because speaking felt
impossible. That memory never faded, but it never took a narrative shape. Instead it lived in her
body: when she recalled the event, she felt her shoulders hunch, her breath shallow, her neck
stiffen, as if she were still pinned to that table, trapped and furious.

Over the decades, the memory began to drive her every move without her fully knowing why. At work as
a social ministry worker, when a client raised their voice, Nadia’s body would tighten and she would
feel the same rigid posture return. She had come to believe that she could not be alone with another
person—not in her office, not in a waiting room. She always chose the seat nearest the exit at
meetings, and she avoided one-on-one sessions whenever possible, delegating them to colleagues and
fabricating reasons. The bodily reenactment itself, the sensation of being pinned, triggered her
need to keep an exit clear, to never be cornered; that was how the memory turned into a strategy for
survival.

The memories also ignited an anger that was out of proportion to the moment. When she closed her
office door, even briefly, the flash of rage would surge, and she would snap at the next person who
walked in. She frequently reacted as if something was wrong when nothing was—her heart racing, her
fists clenching—and she often looked behind her to check if someone was following, though she knew
rationally no one was there. These false alarms and checking behaviors were, for her, direct
consequences of the memory intruding. The threat felt real because the memory felt present.

That constant sense of danger, in turn, reinforced her avoidance. The more she checked behind her,
the more convinced she became that she had to stay near the exit, that she could not be alone with
anyone. She started to avoid going to work altogether on some days, calling in sick or arriving late
to avoid the morning handshake with the receptionist. The perceived threat also seemed to pull the
memory closer; when she was hypervigilant, scanning the room, she would suddenly feel the pressure
of her father’s hand on her head again—the threat monitoring brought the past into the present.

Her avoidance made the memory worse. By never allowing herself to be alone with another person, she
never had the chance to learn that it could be safe. The bodily reenactment persisted because she
could not put the event into words, and talking would have required not avoiding. The rage on recall
only intensified each time she fled a situation, leaving her feeling more trapped and more furious,
a cycle she could not name but felt tightening.

the way she insisted on sleeping with the bedroom door open and the light on. More recently, at
work, she erupted at a colleague who touched her shoulder unexpectedly, shouting and then refusing

would lose the job that had given her purpose. She finally came to the clinic because she could no
longer pretend the past stayed in the past. She hoped—though she barely dared to—that talking might
finally give the memory words.
