Leo is a 49-year-old fashion designer living in Brooklyn with his wife of twenty-two years, Elena.
The trauma that shattered their life happened on a Sunday morning in early autumn. They were cycling
along the Hudson River Greenway when a driver ran a red light and struck Elena from the side. Leo
saw the entire sequence: the flash of red metal, the sickening thud, her body lifting and then
landing motionless. But what he cannot recall—no matter how hard he tries—is the sound of her voice
afterward, or how he got off his own bike, or the face of the person who called 911. Those moments
are blank, wiped clean. What stays instead are fragments: the exact shade of the car, the angle of
her arm, the smell of hot asphalt. And the guilt—whenever the memory returns, it hits him like a
wave that buckles his knees. He feels he should have shouted, pulled her back, done something. The
overwhelm is so intense that he has to stop whatever he is doing, sometimes leaning against a wall
until his breathing steadies.

In the weeks that followed, Leo began to notice that his own body betrayed him. The mere sight of a
red car—or any red, really, even a bolt of crimson silk in his studio—made his heart slam against
his ribs and his palms go slick. At night, lying beside Elena, who is now home and recovering
slowly, he cannot fall asleep without the images clawing back: the flash, the thud, the blankness.
He lies rigid, flooded with guilt and a raw, shapeless anger that has no target. The physical
reactivity and the sleeplessness are so draining that he has started to dread the evenings, which
only makes the memory intrude more forcefully. He has come to believe that were he to let himself
fully remember the blank parts, he might lose control entirely.

To defend against that terror, Leo has built an elaborate scaffold of avoidance. He tries not to
think about the accident at all, and whenever a memory surfaces he immediately redirects his mind—he
turns on music, opens a sketchbook, calls a colleague. Keeping his mind occupied all the time has
become his primary survival strategy. At work, he now deliberately avoids any project that involves
future deadlines more than two weeks out; making any plan feels like tempting fate, as if the future
were a trap that could spring another unbearable moment. He has told his assistant to stop
scheduling showroom appointments for next season. Yet the very act of suppression seems to
strengthen the memory. When he forces his thoughts away, the fragments return sharper, more vivid,
and even more laden with guilt. The more he tries to control the memory, the more it controls him.

The cycle tightens on itself. On a recent Tuesday, while selecting fabrics for a client, Leo’s hand
brushed against a bolt of deep burgundy velvet. The color was close enough—that red—and his chest
seized, his vision tunneled. He dropped the bolt and walked out of the showroom without explanation.
Later, sitting on a bench, he could not stop the memory from crashing in: that exact shade, the way
it had reflected the morning light, and the blank spot where his own reaction should have been. The
emotional overwhelm was so severe that he spent the rest of the day in his studio with the lights
off, trying to think of anything else. He knows these strategies are not working, but they are the
only thing that makes the days bearable.

What finally brought Leo to seek help was a quiet moment at dinner when Elena said, “You’re not here
anymore, Leo. You’re always fighting something I can’t see.” She was right. He had stopped making
any plans with her—no weekend trips, no dinner reservations, not even a movie next week. The
avoidance had seeped into their marriage, and the guilt from the memory now mingled with a new,
present-day guilt: he was failing the person he had failed to protect. He is terrified that if he
lets the memories come, they will drown him. But he is also terrified of the life he is building
around their absence. He wants to learn how to live in the present again, even if the present
includes that red car and the blank spaces he cannot fill.
