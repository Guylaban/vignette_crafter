At twenty years old, Mai had been a soldier for just over a year when a fellow soldier assaulted her
during a late-night watch. He had been a friend, someone she trusted; the attack came without
warning. She froze, unable to scream or push him off, and afterward she told no one, afraid of how
it would look. She was quietly transferred to a different unit, but the experience followed her in
images that arrived without warning—flashframes of the barracks floor, his hands, the weight of him.
The memories felt like they belonged to another woman, not to her. They were too extreme to believe
they had really happened, and yet they kept playing.

In the weeks that followed, Mai began to cope in the only ways she knew. She found herself eating
more than usual, often late at night, using the fullness in her stomach to drown out the pictures in
her mind. She would deliberately redirect her thoughts to anything else—her husband’s face, the next
day’s drill schedule—but the images always returned. And she spent hours going over the details of
the assault, trying to understand why she hadn’t fought back, trying to find a moment where she
could have changed the outcome. These strategies gave her momentary relief, but they also kept the
memories alive. The more she analysed, the more vivid the images became; the more she ate, the more
disconnected she felt from the soldier she used to be.

Mai’s sense of herself began to hollow out. She grew certain that someone else—a stronger soldier, a
better soldier—would have stopped the assault before it happened. That belief settled into her like
a stone: she was weak, and it was her fault. She also felt she had lost her place in the community
of soldiers who had once felt like family, and even in her own culture, which taught her to be
resilient; she no longer recognised the woman in the mirror. The more alien her memories seemed, the
more she told herself that woman was gone. And the more she engaged in her coping strategies—the
overeating, the redirection, the obsessive replaying of the event—the more she confirmed that she
was no longer the person she had been.

Her daily life as a soldier became a minefield. She avoided the quartermaster’s building where she
had first met him. She gave up night duties altogether, claiming exhaustion, and began walking long
routes to the mess hall to avoid passing the barracks where the assault had happened. At work, she
was quiet, withdrawn; the easy camaraderie she had once felt with her squad had vanished. She told
herself they would never understand, that they would judge her for freezing, and so she kept her
distance. At home, her husband noticed she was eating more and sleeping less, that she flinched when
he touched her in the dark, but she could not bring herself to explain.

It was after a routine training exercise—when a male soldier grabbed her arm unexpectedly and she
froze again, unable to move or speak—that her command referred her for a mental health evaluation.
Sitting in the intake office, Mai spoke in a low voice about the assault and about the stranger she
had become. She was terrified that she would never feel like herself again, but she was also, for
the first time, beginning to hope that someone might help her make sense of the story she could not
believe had happened to her.
