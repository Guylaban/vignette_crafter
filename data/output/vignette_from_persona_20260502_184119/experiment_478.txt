Marco was twenty-three when the festival stage collapsed. He had gone with three friends from work,
a rare Friday night off from his QA engineering job, where he spent his days scanning code for bugs
that could crash systems. That night, the crash was real. The metal scaffolding groaned, then gave
way in a single, sickening second. He was near the edge of the crowd and managed to run clear, but
two of his friends were closer. One was pulled out with a broken leg. The other didn’t make it. In
the chaos that followed—sirens, shouting, emergency lights—Marco saw first responders lifting
bodies, and a police officer’s arm blocking him from going back in. He stood there, frozen, watching
the news helicopters circle overhead. For months afterward, he could not stop replaying that moment,
the moment he believed he should have done something more. He should have yelled a warning, pulled
them back, shielded them. He had failed to protect them, and that thought burrowed into him like a
splinter he could not remove.

Now, a year later, Marco lives in a smaller apartment closer to his office. He stopped going out on
weekends. He avoids the neighborhood where the festival was held, but the triggers find him
everywhere. A coworker casually mentions a highway pileup during lunch, and Marco’s stomach
clenches. He excuses himself to the bathroom, where the fluorescent lights hum and flicker, and he
feels the world pull away, a dissociative feeling of unreality that leaves him watching his own
hands from a great distance. When he turns on the news and sees coverage of a warehouse fire, he
shuts it off immediately, his heart pounding. He has built a careful system: no television in the
living room, a browser extension that blocks news sites, and a strict policy of steering
conversations away from anything involving accidents or disasters. He has deleted all social media
apps from his phone.

The triggers do more than make him avoid; they also reinforce the beliefs that have taken root since
the collapse. Every time he hears a crash discussion, the thought returns: he should have protected
his friends, and he failed. He has come to believe that danger is invisible and arbitrary—you can
never know who will harm you, or when. The stage might have been built by people who cut corners;
the whole world felt rigged that way. And if nothing is safe, then nothing good can happen anymore.
Marco has not applied for a promotion he wanted. He stopped dating. He tells himself there is no
point planning for a future that feels already hollowed out.

His avoidance strategies are driven by these appraisals. Because he believes he failed to protect,
he cannot bear to see police officers—they remind him of the scene, of his own helplessness, and of
the official report that concluded no one could have prevented the collapse. He crosses the street
when he sees a patrol car. Because he believes nothing good can happen, he avoids any media that
might confirm that belief, even positive stories feel like a lie. The counterfactual thinking is the
most exhausting: he spends hours imagining how life would have been different if the event had not
occurred, if he had insisted they stand farther back, if he had been faster. It is a mental loop
that gives him a sense of control he never actually had, yet it leaves him more drained each time.

The consequences have accumulated quietly. His boss noticed his disengagement; Marco was moved off a
high-visibility project. He rarely sees his surviving friend from that night, because their
conversations inevitably circle back to the disaster, and Marco cannot tolerate it. He spends his
evenings alone, running through alternative timelines in his head. What finally brought him to seek
help was a morning when he could not bring himself to go to work at all. He called in sick, sat on
his couch, and stared at the wall for four hours. He realized he was afraid of what he might do if
this went on—not that he would hurt himself, but that he would simply stop existing as a person,
fade into the routine of avoidance until there was nothing left to avoid. He is most afraid that
therapy will confirm what he already suspects: that he is beyond repair, that nothing good can
happen to him anymore. But he came anyway, because some part of him, buried beneath the debris,
still believes he might be wrong.
