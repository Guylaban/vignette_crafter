Robert is a sixty-three-year-old married physician, a hospital internist who had always taken pride
in his steady hand and calm demeanor under pressure. That changed five years ago on a Tuesday
evening when he was riding the city bus home after a double shift. Two men boarded, shouting orders
in clipped, furious voices: “Everyone down! Don’t move!” He dropped to the floor as gunfire erupted,
his face pressed against the grimy rubber mat, smelling diesel and blood. He stayed frozen while
screams and footsteps moved past him. He survived without physical injury, but the sound of those
commands—the authority in the voices—etched itself into him.

In the months that followed, Robert found himself unable to shake the feeling that the world was
fundamentally unsafe. His life, he came to believe, had been destroyed—not in a single moment, but
slowly, as the confidence he had in people and in his own safety eroded. He started to distrust even
familiar colleagues and neighbors, convinced that anyone could hide dangerous intentions. That
distrust fed a constant vigilance: at home, he would lie awake listening for the creak of a
floorboard or the click of a door latch, certain someone was entering. Even inside his own house he
felt on guard, his muscles tight, his attention split. His wife noticed his irritability—sharp
outbursts over small things, a new and unsettling edge to his voice.

Work became a minefield. Robert’s hospital, like any, ran on orders and instructions. A senior
physician telling him to review a chart, a nurse relaying a protocol change—each command, however
routine, triggered a visceral replay of the bus. In those moments, his old belief that the world was
a dangerous place felt freshly confirmed, and his distrust of others deepened. That distrust, in
turn, made him more alert to authority figures, so he began to notice commands everywhere,
anticipating them as threats. The bus itself he simply stopped using; he drove or took expensive
taxis, which strained the family budget and added to his wife’s quiet frustration.

The cycle tightened. Every hypervigilant scan of his home—listening for sounds of an intruder—fed
his conviction that danger was imminent and that his old life was irretrievable. That conviction, in
turn, kept his guard up, so he never felt safe enough to let down his monitoring. He became short-
tempered at the hospital, snapping when flustered, and his colleagues learned to give him wide
berth. His wife told him she felt she was living with a stranger. A few weeks ago, after a
particularly harsh argument about a forgotten errand, she said she could not keep going this way.
That was what finally brought Robert to my office.

being afraid in his own home, tired of the anger that surges without warning. He hopes therapy can
help him reclaim some of the steadiness he lost on that bus floor. But underneath that hope, what
frightens him most is the suspicion that the damage might be permanent—that his life really has been
destroyed, and that no amount of talking will put it back together.
