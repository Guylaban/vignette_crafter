Amir is a 41-year-old fast food worker who lives alone in a small one-bedroom apartment in a
suburban strip mall complex. Two years ago, while working the closing shift at a different
restaurant, he witnessed a customer pull a knife on his coworker during a dispute over an order. The
altercation ended when a security guard tackled the man, but not before Amir saw the blade glint
under the fluorescent lights and heard the coworker scream. He has not worked the closing shift at a
new job since, but the memory itself is not what troubles him—it is the ordinary moments that
suddenly feel dangerous.

The same time of day the incident occurred, around 10:45 p.m., Amir’s body tenses without his
permission. When he is standing at the fry station and the digital clock hits that hour, his hands
start to tremble. He learned to request earlier shifts, but the schedule occasionally rotates, and
on those nights he becomes hypervigilant, scanning the dining area for any sign of conflict. He has
come to believe that something terrible is always about to happen when the evening drags past that
threshold. This sense of impending threat carries over to other triggers. Being criticised or
corrected by his manager, even gently, sets off a cascade of alarm. A simple, “Can you wrap that
burger a little tighter?” makes Amir feel as though he is being singled out, that the criticism is a
prelude to escalation. He now avoids eye contact with his supervisors and braces himself before each
shift, certain that any correction could spiral into confrontation.

Loud noises—the slam of a metal tray, the hiss of the steam cleaner, a dropped plate—make him flinch
and search for exits. These sounds, he is convinced, signal immediate danger. The threat does not
dissipate quickly; his heart races, his breathing quickens, and he feels a pressing need to retreat
to the back storage room, which he has designated as the only place he can calm down. He only feels
safe in that narrow, windowless space, where the lighting is dim and nobody can surprise him.
Outside of work, his apartment has become a fortress. He rarely leaves except for his shifts, and
even then he hurries home directly.

Delays of any kind now feel catastrophic. When a customer order takes too long, or when his bus is
late, Amir’s mind immediately jumps to the worst scenario. A staggered delivery of supplies means
someone is trying to cause trouble. A longer-than-usual shift means management is plotting against
him. He cannot tolerate waiting, because waiting feels like the moment before violence. This
hypervigilance has cost him friendships and made dating nearly impossible. He turned down
invitations from coworkers because the thought of being outside his safe zone—his apartment or the
storage room—is too overwhelming.

Amir finally sought help after a particularly bad night. His schedule had been pushed to the evening
again, and a loud argument between customers at the counter triggered a full-blown panic. He hid in
the storage room for nearly half an hour, trembling, convinced that the argument would turn physical
and that he would be drawn in. His manager found him and threatened to fire him if he could not
manage his “nerves.” That ultimatum, combined with his own exhaustion, pushed Amir to call a clinic
the next morning. What he fears most is that he cannot keep working, but he does not know how to
stop the cycle—each criticism, each loud noise, each clock hand reaching ten forty-five renews the
feeling that he is no longer safe anywhere.
