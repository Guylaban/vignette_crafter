Kwame Osei was nineteen years old and had been a detective for barely a year when the undercover
operation collapsed. He had been planted in a small trafficking ring, and on the third night,
someone recognized him. He was dragged into a windowless basement room, his hands bound behind his
back, and held there for four days. The only light came from a single bulb that clicked on and off
at random intervals, plunging him into total blackness for minutes at a time. He learned to dread
the silence that followed each click, because it meant the voices of the men holding him would get
closer. They never hurt him badly—just enough to keep him terrified—but what stayed with him was the
helplessness, the way the darkness made time vanish.

After he was rescued, Kwame tried to go back to his regular duties. He told himself it was over and
he could move on. But when he was asked about the experience for the required debrief, his mind went
blank. He knew exactly what had happened—the click of the light, the shuffle of shoes on concrete,
the taste of dust—but he could not find the words to describe any of it. Other times, without
warning, he would hear those same muffled voices in his head, as if they were just outside his
apartment door. Once, during a quiet lunch break, a wave of pure terror crashed through him—his
heart pounding, his palms wet—and he had no idea what had triggered it, no image to attach it to.
These unbidden fragments of memory made him desperate to control what he could. He began to push the
thoughts away deliberately, telling himself not to think about the room or the voices. When that
failed, he tried to avoid anything that might summon the feeling: he stopped going out after dark,
because the night reminded him of the blackness that followed each click of the light. He also
flinched away from any physical contact—a hand on his shoulder from a colleague, a hug from his
girlfriend, even a friendly pat on the back—because being touched brought back the sensation of the
ropes binding his wrists.

For weeks, Kwame’s avoidance seemed to work in small doses. He stayed inside after sunset, spent his
evenings in his apartment, and kept his girlfriend at an arm’s length. He told himself it was
temporary, that he just needed to get his head straight. But the night shift at the precinct became
impossible; he traded shifts with a senior officer rather than explain why he couldn’t drive home in
the dark. At work, he found himself stepping back when witnesses reached toward him, his body
tensing before his mind caught up. His girlfriend, Mia, began to ask what was wrong, and he could
only say he was tired. He hated the distance growing between them, but the thought of letting her
hold him made his stomach turn.

The memories kept coming even as he tried to shut them out. The auditory recalls were the worst—a
sudden voice in his ear that made him freeze mid-sentence. And the emotion-without-image episodes
left him shaken for hours afterward, unable to connect the terror to anything real. Each time, his
response was the same: he clamped down harder, told himself not to think, and added another
restriction to his life. He stopped going to the gym because the dark locker room felt too close. He
started leaving the lights on at home. Mia noticed he no longer touched her first, and when she
reached for him, he would shift away almost reflexively.

By the time he sought help, Kwame had been living this way for three months. He came to the clinic
not because the avoidance felt wrong, but because his captain had pulled him aside and told him he
was losing his edge. What finally pushed him through the door was the fear that Mia might leave, and
the thought of losing her was worse than the nightmare he was already trapped in. He sat in the
intake chair with his hands gripping his knees, unable to say much more than “I don’t know how to
stop this”—and hoping, for the first time, that someone could help him find the words.
