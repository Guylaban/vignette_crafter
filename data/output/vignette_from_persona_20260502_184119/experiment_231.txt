Raj Patel is a 37-year-old clinical psychologist, married and South Asian, who sought treatment six
months after surviving a terrorist attack at a downtown train station. He had been commuting home
after a late patient session when a detonated pressure-cooker bomb plunged the platform into
darkness, followed by a series of gunshots. Trapped under debris, he could not move or call
out—utterly helpless—and the blackness was punctuated only by the pop of gunfire echoing off
concrete. He was pulled out hours later, physically unharmed but profoundly shaken.

In the weeks that followed, Raj found himself raw in ways his clinical training could not repackage.
Every evening, as dusk settled, his heart would pound; he would check locks repeatedly, certain that
darkness invited danger. The mere sound of a car backfiring or a dropped book made him flinch and
reach for his phone, ready to call 911. These triggers—especially moments when he felt something
beyond his control, and the quiet dread of waking in the pitch-black bedroom—began dictating his
life. When a trigger hit, the first thing Raj did was pour himself a glass of whiskey, sometimes
two, to dull the jolt. He also started to mentally escape: during a difficult therapy session or
even while having dinner with his wife, he would drift into a neutral fantasy of floating in a calm
ocean, shutting out the present.

His clinical knowledge made the irony unbearable. He had taught patients about cognitive
reappraisal, yet he could not stop the thought that the world was fundamentally dangerous—that
another attack could happen at any moment. He became convinced that all his careful planning was
pointless: bad things happened no matter what you did, and the person he had become—jumpy,
withdrawn, dependent on alcohol and daydreams—felt unrecognisable. This sense of helplessness and
alienation only deepened his reliance on his maladaptive strategies. On a particularly bad night,
after waking in dark panic, he would drink more and then spend the next day avoiding any potential
stressor: he cancelled all evening appointments, asked his supervisor to reassign trauma cases, and
stopped driving near the station.

His avoidance, however, created new triggers. By shrinking his world, Raj ensured that any
deviation—an unexpected phone call, a sudden change in schedule—felt catastrophic, reinforcing his
belief that he could not control events. The drinking left him groggy, and on one occasion he woke
disoriented at 3 a.m. in a dark room, the memory of the gunshots surging back. Similarly, his
fantasy escapes, while soothing in the moment, left him feeling detached and unreal, which fed the
appraisal that he no longer recognised himself.

Professionally, his reputation suffered. Colleagues noticed his reluctance to take on complex cases;
one patient’s report of a noisy construction site triggered a visible panic. His wife grew
frustrated with his irritability and his insistence on sleeping with the bedside lamp on. Raj knew
he needed help, but the very thought of admitting he had PTSD felt like confirmation that he was
beyond repair—that even a psychologist could not fix himself.

What finally brought him to my office was a minor incident: a car door slamming in the parking lot
sent him scrambling under his desk during a supervision session. His supervisor gently told him he
was burned out and needed treatment. Raj came reluctantly, confiding that his greatest fear was that
no therapy could undo the belief that the world was unsafe and that he had become a stranger to
himself. He hoped, he said quietly, to at least stop the drinking and the escaping—because he was
tired of feeling like a ghost in his own life.
