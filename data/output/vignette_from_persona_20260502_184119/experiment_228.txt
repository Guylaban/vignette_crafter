Minh’s trauma began thirty years ago when his five-year-old daughter Linh was diagnosed with acute
lymphoblastic leukemia. He spent every evening at the children’s hospital after work, sitting by her
bedside as the antiseptic smell of the ward—bleach, iodine, hand sanitizer—clung to his clothes.
What he could never shake was the sound of the IV pump and the sight of doctors performing lumbar
punctures and bone marrow aspirations. Those months became a blur of fear and helplessness, but the
memory itself never blurred: it stayed razor-sharp and immediate, as if he were still there, a film
that played over and over no matter how many years passed.

Decades later, at seventy-five, Minh works part-time as a cybersecurity analyst. The job demands
periodic health screenings, and just a routine blood draw now sends his heart racing and his palms
sweating—a strong physical reaction he cannot control. The moment the nurse swabs his arm with an
alcohol pad, the hospital smell hits him, and suddenly he is back in that sterile room, watching
Linh’s small body stiffen in pain. These triggers do not just provoke anxiety; they pull the entire
memory into the present, making it feel as if it is happening right now rather than something long
over. The smell, the sight of the needle—they are the direct doorway to the film that plays over and
over.

After a particularly vivid flashback at work—Minh had briefly lost track of where he was, believing
for a moment that he was again at Linh’s bedside—he found himself hyperaware of every antiseptic
smell in the building. The memory had primed him to detect triggers that he might otherwise have
ignored. That evening, a whiff of hand sanitizer from a colleague sent him reeling again. The
memories themselves were creating a world full of triggers, so that even a faint hospital odor on a
passing stranger’s clothes could start the cycle anew.

What frightened Minh most was the way his own fear amplified the memory. The moment his chest
tightened with dread, the memory would snap into sharper focus—the exact shade of the hospital
curtain, the sound of the monitor alarms—as if his body’s alarm system was rewinding the film. He
had come to believe that if he could just stop the physical reaction, he could keep the memory at
bay, but the opposite seemed true: the stronger his fear, the more the memory consumed him, pulling
him deeper into the past.

He avoided any mention of hospitals, changed the channel when medical dramas appeared, and could not
bear to visit his daughter when she was pregnant, because seeing her belly triggered images of the
neonatal intensive care unit where other babies had fought for life. This avoidance had cost him
precious time with his family; his daughter had felt hurt by his distance during her pregnancy. But
Minh was certain that his presence would only lead to a full-blown flashback, and he had learned
that when the memory flooded back, it was impossible to explain to anyone what had happened. When he
tried to describe it, he lost the thread and could not finish a sentence.

Finally, after his annual physical—a required procedure for his job—left him trembling and
disoriented for two days, his daughter insisted he see a therapist. Minh comes now because he is
tired of being a prisoner to a past that feels more real than the present. He is afraid that the
memory will never fade, but he is also hoping that someone can help him find a way to live without
feeling like he is still in that hospital room.
