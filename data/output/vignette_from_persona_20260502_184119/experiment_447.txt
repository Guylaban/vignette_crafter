Mai was 69 when it happened, a Tuesday in late October. She had been a lawyer for forty-three years,
and the office still smelled of old law books and her husband’s cologne from the morning. The client
was a man in his fifties, soft-spoken at first, but when she explained why his appeal was unlikely
to succeed, something shifted in his voice. It dropped, became deliberate. He looked at her in a way
that made her skin shrink—a long, slow survey from her face to her neck and down. She told herself
to stay calm, but her heart started hammering against her ribs, and before she could stand, he was
around the desk, one hand on her shoulder, the other in her hair. It lasted maybe ninety seconds.
She did not scream. Afterward he left, and Mai sat frozen, her body humming with a terror she had
never known. She did not call the police. She went home and told her husband she had a headache.

In the weeks that followed, Mai tried to return to work as if nothing had happened. But the world
had been remade without her permission. The sound of a man’s voice with a certain low cadence—a
client, a judge, even a stranger in an elevator—would make her heart lurch, and then the fear would
wash over her like a hot, shameful wave. She began to avoid hearings where she knew the opposing
counsel was male and loud. She left in the middle of a deposition once, her hand pressed to her
chest, certain she was having a heart attack. That was when she started to believe her own body had
turned against her. It had failed to fight, and now it could not be trusted not to betray her with
its racing, its trembling, its sudden collapses into panic. The world, she came to see, was a
dangerous place where a quiet Tuesday could destroy you. She had to be on guard all the time.

Her hypervigilance made her scan every room for exits and for men who looked at her too long. She
noticed her own heartbeat constantly now—the moment it sped up, she would brace herself for danger,
and that very brace made it worse. When something took longer than expected, a conference that ran
late or a traffic light that stayed red too long, she was certain something terrible was about to
happen. At night, the sense of safety drained away completely; she kept the bedroom lights on until
her husband gently turned them off, and then she lay awake, listening. When she felt the familiar
pull of guilt—*I should have screamed, I should have pushed him away*—her chest would tighten and
her pulse would race, and that racing itself became a trigger, a loop she could not break. One
evening, during a dinner with old friends, a guest asked if she was feeling unwell, and Mai realized
her hand was pressed to her chest again, her heart pounding. She excused herself and did not return.
She stopped accepting invitations to evening events altogether.

The hardest thing was that her job, which had once given her purpose, had become a minefield. A
delay in court proceedings would flood her with dread; she would imagine the case turning violent,
the judge attacked, herself helpless. She started delegating male clients to a younger partner,
though she could not explain why. Her husband, a kind man who had never pressed her, asked if she
was angry with him. She was not. She was just—*afraid* was too small a word. She was convinced that
any moment, any sound, any look could unravel her. It was her husband who finally said, after she
had spent another night rigid beside him, “Mai, you can’t keep living like this.” She agreed, though
what she was most afraid of was that therapy would only confirm what she already believed: that her
body was broken, that the world was too dangerous, and that the part of her that had felt safe was
gone forever.
