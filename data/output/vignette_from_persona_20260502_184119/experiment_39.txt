Minh was 66 when his wife Linh died, though the word “died” never felt like enough to describe what
happened. They had been together forty-two years. He was a public defender, she a retired
pharmacist. The evening it happened, he came home late from a bail hearing to find the back door
jimmied open, the kitchen ransacked, and Linh lying motionless on the living-room floor. The coroner
said it was a blow to the head, quick. But Minh’s memory of that night was never quick. It arrived
in broken shards: the slant of the overturned floor lamp, the sound of his own voice calling her
name, the metallic taste in his mouth. Most haunting was the auditory fragment—a scraping noise,
like shoes on linoleum, that he could not place as having happened then or having been invented
afterward. What he knew for certain was the grief that crashed over him whenever a piece of that
night surfaced, leaving him hollow and breathless.

In the months that followed, Minh began to change in ways he did not fully recognize. He started
checking the doors and windows of his small house every evening—sometimes twice, then three times,
until the ritual became a kind of prayer. He told himself it was reasonable, that a break-in had
happened once and could happen again. But the checking did not bring relief. Instead, it kept him
locked in a state of low-grade alarm. He grew watchful of people on the street, especially men who
walked with purpose or glanced in his direction. At the courthouse, where he still argued motions
daily, he found himself scanning the gallery for anyone who looked like a threat. A raised voice in
a hallway would make his heart bang against his ribs, and he would stand frozen, certain that danger
was imminent, even when nothing was wrong. These false alarms left him shaken and ashamed.

The memories fed the vigilance: each auditory flash or fragmented image renewed his conviction that
safety was an illusion. And the vigilance, in turn, summoned more memories. He would be standing at
the sink, checking the lock for the fourth time, and the sound of that scraping linoleum would hiss
in his mind. He began eating more than usual after these episodes—a second dinner, a bag of chips,
anything to blunt the crawl of fear. Overeating became a nightly habit, a way to turn off the
circuit of recollection and hypervigilance, though it only made him feel heavier and more trapped.
He also stopped watching crime dramas and the evening news; any story about a home invasion or
violent death sent him straight to the refrigerator.

By the second year, Minh’s world had shrunk. He still went to work—he could not afford to stop, and
the caseload was relentless—but he no longer accepted assignments that involved violent offenders,
fearing that just being near them would trigger a flashback. He avoided the grocery store at dusk.
He called in sick on the anniversary of Linh’s death, not because he was ill, but because staying
home felt safer, even though being home meant hearing phantom noises and checking the locks again.
The grief that accompanied each memory was so overwhelming that he sometimes sat in his parked car
for twenty minutes after arriving at the office, unable to open the door.

What finally brought Minh to therapy was not the fear itself but a disciplinary warning from his
supervisor. He had snapped at a client during a pretrial interview—the man’s loud, insistent voice
had triggered a false alarm, and Minh had frozen, unable to speak, then walked out. He was put on a
performance improvement plan. That night, alone in his house, he realized his strategies for
managing the past had begun to fail him. He was most afraid now not of another break-in, but of
losing the one domain where he still felt competent: his work. He hoped, more than he could say,
that someone could help him unwind the connections between what he remembered and what he feared,
before his life tightened into something unrecognizable.
