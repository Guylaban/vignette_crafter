Layla, an 18-year-old architecture student of Middle Eastern heritage, came to therapy three months
into her freshman year. The trouble began five weeks earlier, on a Wednesday afternoon in late
September. She had just left her studio and was crossing a campus street when a delivery truck ran a
red light and struck a cyclist. The cyclist, a middle-aged man, was thrown onto the pavement. Layla
heard the crack of the bicycle frame and saw the man’s leg twist at an angle that did not look
possible. She did not know him, but she stayed at the scene until paramedics arrived, standing
frozen while the man screamed. It was over in minutes, but the image of that twisted leg and the
sound of the scream stayed with her.

Since that afternoon, Layla has been unable to shake a persistent sense of threat. She finds herself
scanning the street before every crossing, checking the direction of traffic three or four times. In
her studio, she sits with her back to the wall, facing the door. When someone enters abruptly, she
flinches. She has become acutely aware of the layout of every room she enters—the exits, the
windows, the places where someone could hide. This hypervigilance is not something she chooses; it
happens automatically, like a muscle she cannot unclench. She has started avoiding the corner where
the accident occurred, taking a longer route even when she is running late for class.

Sleep has become a struggle. Layla lies in bed at night with her eyes open, listening. Her dorm room
is on the second floor, but every small sound—a creak, the hum of a radiator, footsteps in the
hallway—makes her tense. She keeps her bedside lamp on because darkness feels dangerous. She does
not know what she is guarding against, but the thought of letting her guard down terrifies her. She
often stays awake until two or three in the morning, then sleeps fitfully and wakes exhausted. Her
architecture professor has noticed her drawings becoming less precise; she has trouble concentrating
on her drafting table.

Strong negative emotions have settled into her daily life. Sometimes, without warning, a wave of
fear washes over her when she hears a car horn or the screech of brakes. She feels a tightness in
her chest and a hot shame that she cannot control her own reactions. She has also felt guilt: she
did not call 911 fast enough, she thinks, even though other people had already dialed. At night,
lying awake, she replays the moment and wonders if she could have done something differently. The
guilt mixes with anger—at the driver, at herself, at the randomness of the event.

Layla has not told her parents about the accident. They live in another state, and she does not want
to worry them. Her roommate has noticed her jumpiness but does not know the extent of it. What
brought Layla to therapy was a panic attack during a lecture. A student dropped a heavy textbook,
and the sound was so similar to the accident—a sharp thud followed by gasps—that Layla’s heart
raced, she broke into a sweat, and she had to leave the room. She sat in the hallway, embarrassed
and shaking, and realized she could not keep managing this alone. She is afraid of what will happen
to her grades, her friendships, and her own sense of safety if she does not get help. She is hoping
the therapist can teach her to sleep again and to stop feeling so afraid all the time.
