Caleb was nineteen, a fast-food worker who had been on the fry station at a chain restaurant two
years ago when the tornado hit. He remembered the way the light turned greenish through the window,
then the sound—a freight train roar that seemed to come from inside his own skull. He had dropped
the basket of fries and run for the walk-in cooler, wedging himself between boxes of frozen patties
as the roof tore away. What stayed with him most was the smell: wet insulation, natural gas, and
something metallic that he could still summon without warning when a customer dropped a metal tray
or when the fryer oil got too hot. Those smells brought the terror back whole, not just the memory
but the actual body panic—his heart slamming, his palms slick—as if he were still crouched in that
cooler waiting to die. The images came too, mostly in fragments: the ceiling peeling back, a soda
machine toppling, the assistant manager’s face streaked with blood.

Caleb had come to believe that he was permanently changed for the worse, that the person he used to
be—the easygoing kid who laughed easily and never worried—was gone. He told his therapist that he
used to be happy, but now he was always miserable, and that misery felt like proof that something
inside him had broken for good. This sense of permanent damage coloured everything. When he thought
about the tornado, he didn’t just remember the fear; he remembered it as confirmation that he was
now a defective person, someone who would never feel safe again. And the memories themselves seemed
to get worse the more he believed that, as if his negative view of himself made the terror more
vivid, more real. He would catch a whiff of something—damp cardboard, propane—and the visual images
would flood in, bringing with them the same conviction: *This is who you are now. You’ll never be
the same.*

To manage the constant feeling of being broken, Caleb had developed a set of strategies that he
hoped would keep things manageable. He rehearsed conversations in his head before any interaction,
even a simple order at the drive-through, because he was terrified of saying something wrong and
then having to deal with the stress of a conflict. He avoided anything that could be stressful: he
turned down extra shifts, stopped seeing friends, stopped calling his mother because he didn’t want
to explain why he sounded so flat. And he tried hard not to think about the tornado—he would push
the images away, change the channel in his mind, keep himself busy. But these strategies only
reinforced his sense of being unrecognisable. The more he rehearsed conversations, the more he felt
like an imposter, someone who had to script his own life. The more he avoided stressors, the more he
felt incapable of handling normal life. And the more he suppressed the memories, the more they
seemed to burst through at unexpected moments, each time confirming that he was beyond repair.

At work, the triggers were constant. The smell of the fryer oil could become, in an instant, the
smell of the cooler. A sudden loud noise—a dropped tray, a slammed door—would send him into
hypervigilance. He would freeze, rehearsing his next line in his head, telling himself not to react,
not to show fear. His manager had noticed that Caleb was jumpy and withdrawn, and had suggested he
take a few days off. Caleb had agreed, but the days at home were worse: nothing to distract him, the
memories pressing in, the rehearsals turning into obsessive loops. He had started to believe that he
would never be able to hold down a job, never have a relationship, never be the person he used to
be. That belief was what finally drove him to the clinic—not the memories themselves, but the
certainty that he had lost himself, and that suppressing, avoiding, and rehearsing were all he had
left, and they weren’t working. He was afraid that therapy would confirm his worst fear: that the
damage was permanent. But he was also afraid of staying where he was.
