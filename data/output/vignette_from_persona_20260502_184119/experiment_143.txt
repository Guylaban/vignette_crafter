Emma was twenty-three when the stalking began. For six months she could not escape the sensation of
being watched—the heavy, deliberate breathing that came from behind her in the supermarket aisle,
the same breathing on anonymous phone calls that left her standing in her kitchen with the receiver
pressed hard against her ear, heart slamming. She reported it to the police twice, but without a
name or a clear description they could do little. The night it ended—or rather, the night she
finally moved out of her apartment—she remembered a single image with excruciating clarity: the
shape of a man’s silhouette against the streetlight, frozen still as she turned. Everything else
around that moment was gone, a blank where sounds and steps should have been, and even now, at
twenty-eight, when she tried to describe what happened she found only fragments that dissolved on
her tongue.

As an elementary school teacher, Emma had always loved the rhythm of her classroom—the shuffle of
papers, the low drone of a reading group. That changed gradually. The first time a child near her
desk took a deep, audible breath during a spelling test, Emma’s entire body went rigid. In that
instant she was back in the supermarket aisle, and the heavy breathing triggered not only a wave of
fear but also a sudden, unwanted memory fragment: the wet pavement beneath her feet, the knowledge
that someone was right behind her. That fragment, in turn, reinforced what she had come to
believe—that her life had been destroyed, that she could never be safe again. The breathing itself
now seemed to confirm her mistrust of everyone around her, and each time she heard it, she felt a
fresh surge of anger and shame.

The presence of police officers near the school grounds became unbearable. Once she saw a patrol car
parked outside the gymnasium, and the sight triggered a vivid flash of the night she had made her
report—the officer’s indifferent face, the form he slid across the desk. That memory then fed her
growing conviction that other people were not to be trusted, and the more she believed that, the
more acutely she felt threatened by the ordinary silence of her classroom after a child finished
speaking. Unusual silence now made her certain that something dangerous was about to crack open the
moment. Whenever she felt that rising threat—a stranger walking too close on the playground, a
parent lingering in the doorway—her mind immediately circled back to fragments of the stalking, and
she would find herself scanning the room for exits, her breathing shallow.

Emma began to avoid the street where she had last been followed, even if it meant a twenty-minute
detour to reach the grocery store. She also rearranged her classroom so that her desk faced the
door, and she flinched whenever a student coughed or panted after recess. The heaviest weight in her
daily life was the loop she could not escape: a sense of threat from any approaching stranger made
her hypervigilant, and that hypervigilance caused her to misinterpret ordinary sounds—a door creak,
a sudden footstep—as the return of the stalker. Her boyfriend noticed that she no longer wanted to
walk anywhere together after dark, and she had stopped accepting dinner invitations from colleagues
because the idea of being in a crowded restaurant, surrounded by strangers’ breathing, felt
impossible.

The belief that she had to be on guard all the time had become so automatic that she could not
distinguish between a safe silence and a dangerous one. When a parent volunteer walked into her room
without knocking, Emma’s heart lurched, and the moment of threat sent her straight back to the blank
spaces in her memory—trying to fill them, failing, and concluding that she was broken. Over time,
her avoidance had shrunk her world. She taught her lessons on autopilot, scanning for heavy
breathing, for police cars, for the subtle sensation of being followed down the hallway. Her
relationship frayed because she kept insisting they eat dinner in silence, without music, so she
could hear whether anyone was outside.

What finally brought Emma to seek help was a morning when she could not make herself enter the
classroom. She had heard a parent breathing hard as she unlocked the door, and the memory of the
supermarket aisle came so sharply that she turned and walked out of the school. Her principal called
a substitute, and Emma sat in her car for two hours, crying, certain that her life was gone. She was
terrified that the stalking had permanently hollowed her out, and she was equally afraid that if she
talked about it, she would still not find the words. Yet something in her desperately hoped that by
naming the fragments—the breathing, the police, the silent moments that felt like waiting for a
blow—she might finally piece together a world she could bear to live in.
