park. What he has instead are photographs in his mind—the blur of bicycle spokes, her hand raised,
the cold weight of metal against his ribs—and sometimes just the raw emotion, the helpless rage,
without any image at all. He was thirty-eight, in a relationship with a woman who had become
controlling and physically abusive. In a crowded park, after an argument, she struck him repeatedly
and then used his bicycle frame to beat him until he fled. He never told anyone; he married a
supportive woman a year later and built a career helping others.

Over the decades, he developed an elaborate system of safety. The triggers—seeing a bicycle, being
alone with women, entering crowded spaces—prompt him to deploy avoidance strategies immediately. A
bicycle rack outside his office makes him change his route; a female colleague asking for a private
consultation leads him to suggest meeting in the open lobby; the sound of a crowd pushes him to
cancel his lunch plans. These triggers also sharpen his threat monitoring: his eyes scan every
woman’s face, and at night he wakes repeatedly to check the locks. More fundamentally, the triggers
reinforce his shattered beliefs—he has come to see the world as fundamentally deceptive, people as
not what they seem, and himself as inadequate for failing to prevent the abuse.

His negative appraisals drive his maladaptive strategies directly. Believing that people are not
what they seem, he avoids forming close relationships with women, and his sense of inadequacy makes
him suppress any emotion, especially anger—he is convinced that showing anger would confirm his
weakness. These appraisals also amplify his sense of threat; he watches others closely for signs of
harm, certain that any woman could be like his abuser. And his appraisals of the world as dangerous
make him more attuned to potential triggers: he now interprets a crowded elevator or a female
therapist’s friendly gesture as signals of imminent danger, which further strengthens his avoidance
and keeps his wardrobe scrutinized for anything resembling the grey shirt he wore that day.

The memory itself feeds the threat system directly. When a flash of photographic recall hits—the
image of her face contorted with rage—his body tenses, he scans the room, and he becomes
hypervigilant for hours. That same memory strengthens his negative appraisals: the inability to tell
a clear story leaves him feeling that his mind has failed him, reinforcing his inadequacy and his
distrust of anyone who seems kind. Threat, in turn, drives maladaptive strategies. When he wakes at
night feeling unsafe, he lies still and clamps down on his emotions, refusing to let the fear show,
telling himself that control is the only way to survive. The hypervigilance also triggers memory
intrusions: the more he scans the environment, the more likely he is to catch a visual cue—the tilt
of a head, the color of a jacket—that brings back a photographic fragment. Threat further deepens
his negative appraisals: he interprets his own constant watchfulness as proof that the world is
dangerous and that he is inadequate for not being able to feel safe. And threat itself becomes a
trigger—his own racing heart and clenched jaw signal danger, making him even more avoidant of any
situation that might provoke that state.

His maladaptive strategies also circle back to the memory and to triggers. By suppressing anger and
controlling his emotions, he prevents himself from processing the event; the feelings stay
encapsulated as raw emotion without image, and the story never coheres. And his avoidance of

must remain vigilant to avoid them, which ensures they remain potent.

checking the locks, feeling the old shame and rage. He decided to seek help, hoping that finally
telling the story might allow him to stop living in the grip of photographs and emotions that have
no name.
