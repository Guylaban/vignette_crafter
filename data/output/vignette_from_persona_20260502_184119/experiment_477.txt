James is a 74-year-old retired combat pilot, a widower for the past eleven years, and a man who has
spent most of his adult life trying to stay in control. That control first fractured in his early
thirties when he was married to a woman who drank heavily. On a night he still cannot fully relive,
she came home from a bar, reeking of cheap whiskey, and attacked him—slapping, scratching, and
throwing a lamp that shattered against the wall. He had spent years in the cockpit mastering calm
under fire, but that night he froze, unable to fight back or even raise his arms to protect himself.
The shame was instant and absolute. He never spoke about it, not to anyone, and when she died years
later from cirrhosis, he told himself the past was buried. But it was not buried. It had only gone
underground.

Now, decades later, the past surfaces in ways James cannot predict and cannot stop. The smell of
alcohol—a stranger’s breath in a grocery store aisle, the faint scent on a neighbor’s coat—triggers
a cascade of physical reactions: his heart pounds, his chest tightens, his hands tremble. He
immediately pushes the feeling down, clenching his jaw and telling himself he has no right to anger.
He has learned to suppress any flash of rage so thoroughly that he often cannot locate the emotion
at all; what remains is a hollow, shaky vigilance. That vigilance is constant. Every night, as he
lies in his bungalow alone, sleep is a struggle because he needs to stay alert—listening for
footsteps, for the creak of a door, for any sign that someone might enter his space uninvited.
During the day, he is hypervigilant in a quieter way: he scans rooms for exits, notes who is
standing too close, and avoids any situation where physical touch might occur. He has not hugged
anyone in years. When his daughter reached for his hand after a family dinner, he flinched so
sharply that she pulled away, hurt. He did not explain.

His occupation once taught him discipline under threat, and that training now fuels his most
entrenched maladaptive strategies. The hypervigilance and physical reactivity convince him that he
must remain constantly on guard, and so he avoids intimacy altogether—no close friendships, no
romantic overtures, even the casual warmth of a neighborly chat feels dangerous. In turn, his
avoidance narrows his world. By never allowing himself to be touched or seen, he ensures that the
rare occasions when touch does occur (a brush against a stranger in line, a doctor’s hand on his
arm) feel catastrophic, reinforcing the original triggers and deepening his vigilance. He also
avoids anything that reminds him of the woman who hurt him: her name, her perfume, the neighborhood
where they lived. But the most potent cue is the alcohol smell, and because he suppresses his anger
so completely whenever it appears, the tension in his body never dissipates. It builds into a low-
grade physical reactivity that keeps him ready to fight or flee at any moment, even in his own
living room.

Last week, his granddaughter—a bright seven-year-old who does not know his history—ran up to him at

nearby hit him at the same instant. His body reacted before his mind could: he stiffened, gasped,
and pushed her away with more force than he intended. She fell and cried. James stood frozen,
horrified at himself, and for the first time in decades he felt a flicker of raw anger—at himself,

The next morning he called a therapist. He is afraid of what might happen if he loses control again,
but he is more afraid of becoming the man who pushes away everyone he loves.
