Ingrid is a 43-year-old chef who nearly died two years ago from sepsis that set in after she tasted
a batch of raw oysters at the restaurant where she had worked for a decade. Within hours she was in
the ICU with a fever of 105, her organs beginning to fail. She remembers the sensation of her own
blood turning against her—the violent chills, the way her skin felt like it was on fire, the terror
that she would never leave that bed. She spent three weeks in hospital and another month recovering
at home, but the body she came back to did not feel like her own.

What frightened her most was that her body kept acting as if the danger had not passed. Even now, a
sudden rise in her heart rate from climbing stairs or a flush of warmth in the kitchen sends her
into a full-blown flashback: she is back in the hospital bed, the beeping monitors, the metallic
taste of fluid on her tongue, the grief of believing she would never feel normal again. The memory
comes with all her senses at once—the smell of antiseptic, the bright overhead light—and it is so
extreme that sometimes she has trouble believing it actually happened to her. When she recalls the
event, the grief washes over her, raw and overwhelming.

Ingrid came to believe that her body is permanently damaged, that it will always be her enemy, and
that she will never be able to feel normal emotions again. These beliefs drive everything else.
Because she is certain her body cannot be trusted, she scans for any physical sensation that might
signal disaster—a racing pulse, a twinge in her chest—and this hypervigilance triggers more
intrusive memories. The memory, in turn, confirms that her body is unsafe, tightening the loop. To
cope, she has learned to numb her feelings: she stays up until two or three in the morning, delaying
sleep because the dark silence leaves room for the flashbacks. She also avoids anything that might
make her feel happy or hopeful, because hope feels like an invitation for her body to betray her
again.

Her occupation as a chef has become a minefield. She can no longer taste raw shellfish—the smell
alone can bring the full sensory reliving—so she delegates that task, making excuses to her sous
chef. The heat of the sauté station, the steam from the dishwasher, even the press of her chef's
coat against her skin when she is sweating can set off her body reacting before her mind has even
registered a threat. She cannot relax even when she knows she is in a safe place, because safety
does not feel real. Over the weeks, the numbing strategies have backfired: the more she avoids
positive events, the more convinced she becomes that she has lost the capacity for joy, reinforcing
her belief that she is permanently broken. Her partner has noticed her pulling away, and she has
stopped going to family dinners.

The cumulative effect is exhaustion. Her delay sleep means she is chronically tired, which only
makes her more reactive to perceived threats, and the reactive body sensations feed the flashbacks
again. She finally sought help when, one evening in the walk-in cooler, a sudden temperature drop
made her gasp and she fell to her knees, certain she was having another medical emergency. She is
afraid that she will never trust her body again, but she is even more afraid that if she does not
stop the cycle, she will lose her job and her relationship—everything she fought to return to.
