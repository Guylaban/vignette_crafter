Rajesh, a 76-year-old South Asian electrician, was mugged six months ago while leaving a late-
afternoon job at a residential renovation. The sun was already low, casting long shadows across the
empty street, when a man emerged from behind a parked van. He smelled strongly of alcohol—stale and
sharp—as he shoved Rajesh against the brick wall, demanded his wallet, and yanked it from his back
pocket before disappearing into an alley. Rajesh was not physically injured, but the suddenness of
the grab, the reek of liquor in his face, and the helplessness of being pinned stayed with him. That
night he woke from his first nightmare: the shove, the smell, the darkness of the alley, his own
heart pounding. He lay in bed for hours, certain someone was outside.

In the weeks that followed, this sense of threat crystallized around specific triggers. Waking from
a nightmare—which happened two or three times a week—left Rajesh disoriented and hyperalert; for the
rest of the night, every creak of the house or distant car door felt like another approach. He began
sleeping with the bedroom light on, his wife’s presence no longer enough to calm him. Crowded places
became unbearable. At the hardware store, a line at the register or a cluster of shoppers near the
power tools sent his shoulders rigid; he found himself scanning faces, checking exits, and sometimes
abandoning his cart to walk out. The smell of alcohol—even a whiff from a stranger on the
sidewalk—flooded his chest with that same helpless freeze he felt against the wall. Each of these
triggers drove a sharp spike in threat monitoring: he grew vigilant not just at night but in
daylight too, feeling threatened by strangers who approached him even to ask for directions or the
time. A sudden movement beside him—a child darting past or a worker raising an arm to wave—made him
flinch and brace.

His work as an electrician, once a source of pride, became a minefield. He used to take emergency
calls at all hours, but now he refused any job after dusk, telling his boss he had “health issues.”
On daytime jobs, he avoided basements and alleys, and he would not work alone if the house was
empty. When a customer’s son ran up to him from behind on a job site, Rajesh spun around so fast he
nearly dropped his voltage tester. The boy’s mother laughed it off, but Rajesh knew his reaction was
not normal. He started leaving job sites early, claiming he felt dizzy. At home, he would not walk
the dog after dinner, and his wife began taking over the grocery shopping because he complained that
the aisles were too crowded.

The cycle became self-reinforcing. A nightmare left him exhausted and on edge, so the next day a
crowded bus stop felt like an ambush waiting to happen. That feeling made him watch the crowd so
intently that he spotted anyone who moved abruptly, which confirmed that his vigilance was
necessary. Over months, Rajesh’s world shrank. He stopped attending the monthly electricians’ union
meetings because the hall was always packed. He avoided the park where he used to walk, because too
many strangers crossed his path. His wife noticed he no longer greeted neighbors; he once crossed
the street to avoid a man who smelled of beer.

What finally brought Rajesh to seek help was an incident at a medical appointment. While waiting in
a crowded clinic lobby, a man with alcohol on his breath sat down two seats away. Rajesh felt his
chest tighten, his palms go cold, and before he knew it he had stood up, walked out, and sat shaking
on a bench for twenty minutes. His wife, who had been inside, found him there and insisted he talk
to someone. Now, sitting in the therapist’s office, Rajesh is quiet at first, then admits he is
terrified that the mugging will happen again—that he will be caught off guard, that he will not be
able to protect himself. More than anything, he wants to sleep through the night and walk into a
store without scanning every face. He hopes, quietly, that talking will loosen the grip of that
strange, rehearsed sense of danger that has taken over his days.
