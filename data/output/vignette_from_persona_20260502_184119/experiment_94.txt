Hassan was on his tractor well before dawn that morning, the headlights cutting through the dust
near the south field. The assault had happened at the same hour two years ago—a man he did not
recognise had come out of the tool shed, grabbed him by the collar, and demanded money. What Hassan
remembered most clearly was the smell: sour sweat soaked into a denim jacket, sharp and close. His
own heart had slammed against his ribs so hard he thought it might tear through. The man pushed him
to the ground, took the cash from his jacket pocket, and was gone. After that, the memory broke
apart. He could not say exactly how he had gotten back to the house; he recalled only the ringing
silence and the feel of gravel against his palms. When his wife asked him what happened, he opened
his mouth and the words scattered. The story felt as though it belonged to someone else—a scene he
watched from a distance, not something he had lived. He gave up trying to explain.

In the weeks that followed, Hassan found he could not let the anniversary of the attack pass
naturally. As the date drew near, he began to schedule extra deliveries, staying out until dark,
anything to keep the calendar from catching up with him. On the day itself he worked from first
light until his hands bled, refusing to come inside for meals. That pattern repeated: anniversaries
were to be buried under work. But it was not only the calendar that set him off. Any time his heart
began to pound—whether from hauling feed sacks or from a sudden startle—his mind pulled toward
revenge. He would catch himself replaying the moments before the attack, searching for details that
were not there, and then his thoughts would shift to what he would do if he ever saw the man again.
The same happened whenever he smelled sweat, even his own after a long day. The scent yanked him
back into that shed, and the rage followed. He began to notice that other men—farmhands, customers
at the market—seemed to watch him too long, stand too close. Their postures felt deliberate. He
responded by working harder, throwing himself into tasks until his body ached, because exhaustion
was the only thing that quieted the rumble of vengeance in his chest.

By the first winter, Hassan's life had narrowed to the field and the machines. He avoided town, sent
his wife to the market, and answered questions about the attack with a shrug. The memory still
refused to cohere; when he tried to tell the sheriff what the man looked like, he lost the thread
halfway through and gave up. The fragmented pieces felt less like a story than a broken mirror he
could not reassemble. That failure—the inability to make sense of it—drove him back to the same
fantasy: what he would do if he got a second chance. He rehearsed it while driving the tractor,
while fixing fences, while lying awake at 3 a.m. It was the only narrative that held together.

His wife finally made an appointment at a community health clinic, telling him she could not watch
him disappear into the machinery any longer. Hassan came reluctantly, his hands calloused, his jaw
tight. What frightened him most was that the revenge fantasies no longer felt like thoughts; they
felt like plans. He was afraid of what he might do if he ever saw the man again—and afraid, too, of
how hard it was to care about anything else. He hoped, though he could barely say it, that someone
might help him put the broken pieces into an order he could bear.
