body stiffened and his eyes rolled back. The diagnosis was bacterial meningitis; Leo survived after
weeks in the ICU. Minh stopped telling the story after a few attempts because he could never get the
sequence straight—the timeline felt scrambled, the details shifting—and the few times he tried, the
memory would flood him with such sensory force that his hands would tremble and his chest would
tighten as if he were back in that living room. He had come to believe that the person he was before
that afternoon no longer existed; the man who had felt competent and calm with children had been
replaced by someone who startled at a child’s cough and who saw hidden threats in ordinary faces.

memory feel less like a recollection and more like a trap he was still living inside. Through the
months that followed, Minh developed a private rhythm of survival. He tried hard not to think about

the paramedic’s gloves. He kept his mind occupied constantly: crossword puzzles, long bus rides,
cooking elaborate meals he barely ate. And he began to avoid medical settings altogether. When Leo’s
