Camila was twelve years old when her body turned against her. What started as a low fever became
pediatric sepsis, and she spent three weeks in a pediatric intensive care unit where the silence
between machine alarms felt more dangerous than the alarms themselves. She can still taste the
bitter potassium chloride they gave her through her IV—a metallic, chemical film that coats her
tongue whenever the memory surfaces. But she cannot hold the memory for long. Something in her
shoves it away before the full picture arrives, and she has never been able to put what happened
into words, not for a therapist, not for her mother, not even for herself.

Now twenty-two, working as an accountant for a mid-sized firm in Miami, Camila has made a quiet life
inside spreadsheets and columns. Numbers are safe. They either add up or they don’t—no hidden
danger. But the office itself is not safe. When the room goes still during tax season, everyone bent
over their calculators, that same hospital silence presses in on her. Her chest tightens, her palms
sweat, and she is certain something terrible is about to break. And when a client’s report takes
longer than expected to arrive, her mind jumps immediately to the worst: the numbers are wrong, the
audit will fail, she will lose her job. That physical jolt—the racing heart, the shallow
breath—pulls the memory with it. She tastes the potassium again, feels the cold of the hospital bed,
and the image flickers for a moment before she slams it shut.

But the damage is done. After these episodes, Camila withdraws. She stops talking to the coworker at
the next desk because she is sure they cannot understand what she has been through. The isolation
feels absolute. She used to be the kind of person who laughed easily, who brought pastries to the
office on Fridays. Now she finds herself unable to remember what that felt like. Happiness is a
foreign language. Every delay, every silence, every flash of taste proves to her that she is
powerless. She cannot stop bad things from happening. She could not stop the sepsis when she was
twelve, and she cannot stop the dread now.

Over the months, Camila has started arriving to work early so she can leave before the office
becomes too quiet. She avoids the break room during slow afternoons. She has stopped checking her
email after six in the evening because a delayed reply makes her spiral. Her manager has noticed,
twice, that she requested to work from home on days when quarterly reports are due—waiting for
numbers is unbearable. Camila knows this is not sustainable. She came to therapy last week because
she spent an entire Tuesday sitting in her car in the parking lot, unable to go inside, the taste of
potassium on her tongue, convinced that if she walked through the door something catastrophic would
happen. She is afraid she will never be the happy person she was before. But she is also, underneath
that, afraid to hope that she could become that person again.
