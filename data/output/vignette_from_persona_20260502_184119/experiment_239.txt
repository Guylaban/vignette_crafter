city. He had been walking with his wife, who was killed beside him. He survived physically, but the
memory of that afternoon never settled—it returned as a complete sensory reliving, overwhelming him

was too raw, too wordless, too much to hold in language.

In the months after, he began to notice changes. A car backfiring on the street would stop him
cold—the gunshot noise alone could send him into a full recall of the attack, his heart racing, the
scene flooding back as if he were still there. He became hyperalert to unexpected sounds and started
watching other people closely for signs that they might harm him. Whenever he entered a room or
building, he automatically scanned for exits. At work in his dental practice, a patient’s mention of
military service or a uniformed officer stopping for a routine check made his chest
tighten—authority figures in uniform were now potent triggers.

He came to believe he had failed at the fair, that he should have seen it coming or shielded his
wife. This self-blame became a recurring rumination, each cycle of guilt pulling him back into the
memory of the event, the sights and sounds returning with fresh intensity. To feel safer, he began
carrying a concealed handgun. The weight of the weapon in his jacket pocket was a constant reminder
of vulnerability—it also made him more sensitive to any sound that could be a gunshot, and he found
himself avoiding news reports about similar attacks because they brought the memory roaring back.

His hypervigilance to threatening sounds kept him on edge, which reinforced his urge to carry the
weapon and to avoid military topics or news. The avoidance, in turn, removed any chance to habituate
to the triggers, so a single unexpected noise remained capable of triggering a full sensory

vivid, and the vivid memory made him more sensitive to any potential trigger—a cycle he could not
break.
