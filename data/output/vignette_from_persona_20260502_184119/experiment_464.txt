VIGNETTE (final)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Rohan was twenty-four when he was posted to a consulate in a city that had become a battlefield. One
afternoon, returning from a meeting, his armoured car lurched to a halt as a suicide bomber
detonated a fuel truck fifty feet ahead. The blast wave punched through the vehicle, glass spraying,
and Rohan was thrown against the door frame. He lay on the floor, ears ringing, the world a high-
pitched whine, and the only thing he could smell was the sharp reek of alcohol from a shattered
bottle that had rolled across his leg from the market stall the bomber had targeted. He saw the
driver's face—lifeless, eyes open—and then someone pulled him out. After that image lodged in his
mind, even the most ordinary things — the colour of a jacket, a car door slamming — began to feel
like warnings he could no longer ignore. In the weeks that followed he could not find words to
describe what he had seen; the images were too clear, too detailed, but when he tried to tell his
supervisor he stammered and fell silent. Each time those images surfaced, the thought that followed
was the same — that he must have done something wrong, that his carelessness had left the driver
dead because of him. Because those images were so vivid and intrusive, he started noticing threads
of the attack everywhere—the colour of a jacket could pull him back, or the sound of a car door
closing—things that had never bothered him before suddenly became loaded with threat. The smell of
alcohol, even a beer at an embassy reception, would snap him back into that moment, his nostrils
filling with petrol and spirits, his heart hammering as if the bomb had just gone off. He learned to
scan every room for exits, for strangers who moved too quickly, and he could not believe anyone who
told him he was safe. Living in that constant state of threat, he found himself circling back to the
same terrible explanation: that the attack had happened because of him, that the danger he felt was
proof of his own guilt.

What terrified Rohan most was the conviction that something about him had caused the attack—that he
had been too careless, too trusting, and that the driver had died because of him. That persistent
feeling of danger kept the trauma memory close to the surface, so that even in quiet moments at his
desk the scene would play through his mind unbidden, as if his body needed to stay ready by
replaying what had happened. He lost his faith in the people around him, in the institution he
served, and in any higher power that would allow such things. The future felt hollow; he stopped
making plans beyond the next hour. These appraisals made every unfamiliar face a potential threat,
and each time a stranger approached him on the street or in a corridor, he felt the surge of the
flashback—the heat, the ringing, the smell—and was certain this was the moment it would happen
again. Once he had concluded that he was somehow responsible for the blast, every ambiguous sound or
sudden movement became a potential accusation — a car door closing too hard, a colleague's laugh at
the wrong moment — and he found himself scanning not just for danger but for evidence of his own
guilt. He began to wear concealing clothing: long coats in warm weather, hoods pulled up, anything
to shrink from notice. He took drugs at night—marijuana mostly, sometimes something stronger from a
contact at a bar—to dull the memories, but the only thing that quieted the loop for a while was
running until his lungs burned and his legs gave out. He pushed himself to exercise compulsively,
sometimes twice a day, because exhaustion was the only state in which the flashbacks receded. Yet
the reprieve never lasted; the moment his body rested, the memories surged back with greater
intensity, as though the avoidance had only fed them.

In the embassy, a colleague’s friendly tap on the shoulder could send Rohan into a frozen state,
eyes unfocused, reliving the moment of impact. He knew this was irrational, and he hated himself for
it, but that hatred only deepened his sense of hopelessness. His girlfriend noticed he flinched when
she hugged him from behind, and he started avoiding her touch; he told her he was just tired. At
work, he began arriving early and leaving late to avoid the packed elevator, and he started drinking
less at functions because the alcohol smell made him leave suddenly. But feeling emotionally
vulnerable—in a meeting, on the street, sitting in his own apartment—was the worst trigger, because
it carried no external cue; it came from inside, and when it arrived, he would reach for a pill or
go for a punishing run no matter the hour.

The very act of hiding himself made him feel more watched, more suspicious, and the paranoia fed
back into his need to conceal. Living at that pitch of alertness, he found that ordinary stimuli—a
car idling too long, a door left ajar, a stranger who held his gaze a second too long—all registered
as warnings, each one confirming what his body already believed. After a run, when his mind was
blank, the olfactory recall would sometimes hit him without warning—the phantom smell of alcohol and
diesel—and he would be back inside the memory, unable to speak. Lately, even ordinary sounds like a
car backfiring or a door slamming had begun to throw him into defensive mode, his body bracing, his
vision narrowing. He had come to believe that safety was a lie other people told themselves, and
that he could never trust it again.

What finally brought Rohan to therapy was a moment at a diplomatic reception when a woman
accidentally knocked over a tray of glasses. The crash sent him diving under a table, shaking, in
front of dozens of colleagues. He could not explain it. That night, his girlfriend found him sitting
in the dark, and he told her he did not know who he was anymore. He is terrified that he is broken
beyond repair, but he is also afraid that if he stops the exercise and the drugs, the memories will
swallow him whole. He came because he has no other choice, and because somewhere underneath the
hopelessness, he still wants to believe there is a future.
