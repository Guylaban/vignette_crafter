James is a 68-year-old African American man, a widower and a policy analyst who spent decades
reviewing dense legislative documents. Three years ago, a sudden septic infection nearly killed him;
he spent eleven days in intensive care, his organs failing one after another. He remembers the
moment a physician took his hand and told him to prepare for the worst. Even now, the memory does
not feel like something that is over—it returns as if he is still lying in that bed, the beeping
machines pressing in from all sides, and when it comes he is so overwhelmed by emotion that he has
to stop whatever he is doing. Sometimes, in contrast, he can recall what happened and feel
completely numb, as if it happened to a stranger, which only deepens his suspicion that he will
never be able to feel normal emotions again.

After he recovered, James tried to resume his policy work, but his concentration fractured. He would
sit at his desk, a report open, and suddenly his body would feel constantly tense and ready to react
to danger—his shoulders locked, his jaw tight—for no reason he could name. This hypervigilance bled
into his home life; he now feels on guard even inside his own apartment, scanning the hallway before
he unlocks his door. The physical vigilance itself seems to summon the intrusive memory: once he
becomes alert, the hospital room rushes back with its sterile smell and the weight of the
physician’s hand. In turn, every time the memory surges and feels present rather than past, his body
responds as if the threat is immediate, ratcheting his tension higher. That cycle has convinced him
that his body keeps reacting as if he is still in danger even when he is safe, and he has come to
believe he can no longer trust his own body. He interprets his own physical state as proof that he
is permanently broken: “My body is my enemy now,” he told his general practitioner, who referred him
to a trauma therapist.

The causal links tighten into a self-reinforcing loop. James’s appraisal that his body is unreliable
and that he has lost his capacity for normal feeling directly amplifies his threat monitoring; he
remains on high alert because he expects his own flesh to betray him again. That sustained vigilance
makes the intrusive memory more likely to break through, particularly when he is alone in his quiet
apartment. Each re-experiencing episode then confirms that his body is still fighting a battle that
ended years ago, reinforcing the appraisals that keep him trapped. At work, his difficulty
concentrating has become a problem: he has missed deadlines, and a junior analyst now reviews his
drafts before submission. He worries his colleagues see him as fragile. What finally brought James
to seek help was not the occupational slippage, but the growing isolation. He has stopped calling
his adult daughter because he cannot tolerate the moment when the memory overwhelms him mid-
conversation, and he is afraid she will see him fall apart. He sits in the therapist’s office with
his hands gripping the armrests, certain that no treatment can give him back the trust in his own
body he lost on that hospital bed.
