Minh is a 69-year-old plumber who has been married to his wife, Linh, for over forty years. He still
remembers the week he was taken captive twenty-three years ago, when a routine plumbing job at a
warehouse turned into a nightmare. Three men grabbed him as he worked alone in the basement, bound
his hands behind his back, and kept him tied to a pipe for four days. The rope cut into his neck,
sending a sharp, burning pain that would not stop. He felt nauseous from the fear and the stale air,
vomiting into a bucket while the men argued above him. To this day, the memory plays in his mind
like a film he cannot pause—the dim light, the sound of their boots, the pressure on his throat.
When he tries to explain what happened, the thread of the story slips away; he knows what he
endured, but the words simply will not come.

In the months after his release, Minh found himself constantly on edge. The memory itself kept him
alert; even at home, he felt a knot in his stomach and a readiness to defend himself, as though the
men might burst through the door. He began to detach from the memories, forcing himself to think of
something else every time the image surfaced, but the more he pushed it away, the more it returned—a
loop of film that played just when he tried to sleep. He also avoided the anniversary of his
capture, making sure he was too busy with work to notice the date, yet the memory always found him
anyway, often on the day itself or the day before.

Now, years later, the triggers are everywhere. Being alone with any man—a customer in a basement, a
neighbor in the elevator—makes his throat tighten. He recognizes the sick feeling in his stomach and
the familiar ache in his neck as soon as a male client asks him to work in a closed room. Those
physical sensations immediately pull the memory back into sharp focus, the film rolling once more.
In response, he has learned to plan an escape route before entering any new place, checking windows
and back doors, telling himself it is just caution. That planning, however, keeps his mind fixed on
the possibility of danger; when a man makes a sudden movement near him, Minh’s heart pounds and his
body reacts as if the captivity is happening again.

The memory itself has become a trigger. A quiet evening at home, a fleeting thought about the event,
and suddenly his stomach turns and his neck aches. He cannot find words to describe the experience
to his wife, so he stays silent, which only makes the memory feel more isolated and more present.
His avoidance of anniversaries has also backfired: the more he tries to ignore the date, the more
sensitive he becomes to any reminder in the days around it, and every male voice or sudden noise
puts him on high alert. That hypervigilance—always scanning for exits, always ready to bolt—has only
deepened his sense that danger lurks around every corner, even in his own living room.

It was Linh who finally urged him to seek help after she found him pacing the kitchen one evening,
unable to explain why he could not sit still. What frightens Minh most is not the memory itself but
the feeling that he has lost control—that the film will keep playing, the nausea will keep rising,
and he will never find the words to make anyone understand. He hopes only to quiet the loop, to feel
safe in his own home without having to scan the door.
