Layla still remembers the morning the shelling began. She was 32, a prosecutor in her home city,
when the war reached her neighbourhood. She had stayed in her apartment against orders, believing
she would be safe. Then the voices came—men shouting in Arabic, boots on the stairs, pounding on
doors. She hid in the bathroom, convinced they were tracking her, that someone was watching from the
window across the street. For twelve hours she did not move, listening to the sounds of forced entry
and gunfire two floors down. She escaped the city three days later, and eventually resettled in the
United States with her husband. But the feeling of being followed never fully left her.

Now, at 44, Layla works as a prosecutor in a busy district attorney's office. The courthouse
hallways are long and crowded, and every time someone walks behind her for more than a few steps,
her chest tightens and she has to glance over her shoulder. She tells herself it is irrational—she
is safe here—but the sensation is immediate and physical. A colleague's voice that carries an accent
similar to the men that day can make her heart pound for minutes afterward; she will excuse herself
to the restroom and stand with her back to the wall until the alarm fades. The courtroom itself is
worse. From the prosecutor's table she feels the eyes of the gallery, the judge, the
defendant—watched, observed, pinned in place. She has started to sit with her back to the wall even
in meetings, and she wakes most nights at 3 a.m. to walk through her apartment and check that all
the doors are locked, sometimes twice.

This hypervigilance has reshaped how Layla sees herself and the world. She used to describe herself
as a happy person, but now she says she is always miserable, hollowed out by a constant low dread.
The smallest disruption—a dropped stack of files, a raised voice in chambers—leaves her feeling as
though she cannot handle anything at all. She has come to believe that the world is fundamentally
dangerous, that no place is truly safe except her own home, and even that only partially. It is not
a thought she voices aloud; it is the quiet assumption beneath every decision she makes.

The more she scans for threats, the more she finds them. Her vigilance sharpens her awareness of
sounds—the unexpected slam of a door, a car backfiring on the street—and each time her body responds
before her mind can catch up. That reaction, in turn, confirms that danger is near, and she begins
to listen more intently, to watch more closely, and to feel even more certain that someone is
tracking her movements. She has stopped taking the subway because the tunnels feel too enclosed and
the crowds too anonymous; she drives a route that avoids any street where she once had a startle
response. At work she asks for an office with a door she can lock, and during trial breaks she sits
in the small windowless conference room rather than the attorneys' lounge, because there are fewer
people to watch her.

Her husband has noticed the change. He asks why she no longer wants to go to the grocery store or
eat at restaurants. Layla tells him she is tired, but the truth is she can only feel safe in the
apartment, and even then she checks the locks twice before bed. The brief periods of calm she finds
at home have begun to shrink; a loud motorcycle outside or a neighbour's raised voice can shatter
them for hours. She used to take pride in her ability to stay composed in court, but now she dreads
each hearing, sure she will be exposed as someone who cannot cope.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment in the hallway last week. A defendant she had
convicted years ago was released on parole, and she saw him across the lobby. He did not approach
her, but the instant she recognised his face, she felt a surge of terror so acute she had to lean
against the wall. She was certain he was watching her, that he had followed her into the building,
that she was about to be cornered again. It took her ten minutes to breathe normally. She knew,
rationally, that he had no reason to target her. But the feeling did not listen to reason. That
night she told her husband she needed to talk to someone, and she made an appointment. She is most
afraid that she will never feel safe again, that her career—the one thing she has rebuilt from
scratch—will be destroyed by the very vigilance that once kept her alive.
