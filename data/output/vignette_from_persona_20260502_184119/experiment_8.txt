blur of sirens and the paramedic’s voice saying his blood pressure was dropping. The hospital
smelled of antiseptic; a doctor’s face appeared above him, then vanished. He spent six days in the
ICU with a central line and a ventilator, and when he finally went home, he carried the discharge
papers like a death sentence he had somehow escaped.

In the months that followed, Duc could not shake the feeling that he had come back defective. “There
is something wrong with me as a person,” he told himself, and this belief soon seeped into
everything. He felt hollow—dead inside—and that emotional numbness only confirmed his worst
suspicion: he was weak. The weakness was the worst part, because it meant he could not trust his own
body to keep him alive. As a UX designer, Duc spent his days fine‑tuning interfaces for other
people’s ease, yet he could not engineer safety for himself. When a colleague said, “You look
healthy now,” he smiled but felt nothing; he knew better.

The first time he drove past the hospital on his way to work, his chest tightened and his hands
gripped the wheel until his knuckles went white. He began taking a different route. Soon he was
avoiding any medical cue—a Red Cross van, a doctor’s office sign, even a box of surgical masks in a
drugstore. His wife noticed he flinched when she mentioned her annual physical. “I just don’t want
to think about it,” he said, and he meant it: he actively pushed away any memory of the ICU. The act
of avoidance, however, did not calm him. Instead, it sharpened his hypervigilance. On the rare
occasions he allowed himself to remember the beeping monitors, he felt a surge of anxiety that
lasted for hours, leaving his body tense and ready to react. That constant alertness, in turn,
reinforced his belief that he was inherently fragile—why else would his body stay on guard? The
causal loop tightened: because he saw himself as weak, he avoided more; because he avoided, the
world felt more dangerous; because it felt dangerous, he believed even more strongly that something
was fundamentally broken in him.

his voice pitched high. He could not trust reassurances. “No one can convince me I’m safe,” he
admitted to himself. Even after his doctors had cleared him, he felt no relief; his body stayed
coiled, as if danger were always two seconds away. That physical tension exhausted him, and
exhaustion fed his sense of being weak. He started avoiding situations that put his body under any
stress—a brisk walk, climbing stairs, even carrying groceries—because the rise in heart rate felt
too close to the panic of the ICU.

Work suffered. He stopped volunteering for projects with tight deadlines because the threat of
delays was unbearable. He declined team‑building hikes, claiming he was busy. His wife grew worried.

only way he could keep a lid on the terror, but the words felt stuck. Emotional numbness had
hollowed out even his marriage.

What finally brought him to a therapist was a single moment: his boss scheduled a quarterly review,
and Duc spent three days convinced he was about to be fired. The review went fine—praise, even—but
the dread did not lift. He sat in his car afterward, trembling, and realized this was not about his
job. He was exhausted from living in a world where every small delay felt like the beginning of the
end, and he no longer knew who he was without that fear. He wanted to trust safety again. He wanted
to believe he was not weak. But mostly, he wanted the constant weight in his chest to go away.
