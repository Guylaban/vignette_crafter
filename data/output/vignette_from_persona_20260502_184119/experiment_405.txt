Minh was forty minutes into a routine chambers review when the sky outside his window turned the
colour of a fresh bruise. He had seen that grey-green light once before—during the late afternoon of
the typhoon that collapsed his apartment building two years earlier. The memory came not as a
thought but as a full-body assault: the concrete groan overhead, the antiseptic sting of dust and
blood, the snap of a gurney being unfolded on the wet street. For ten seconds he was back under the
debris, unable to breathe, waiting for the next wave. When he surfaced, his hands were shaking so
badly he had to grip the edge of his desk until the feeling passed.

That was the first time Minh had a flashback in open court. He had been a judge for six years, known
for his calm, deliberate manner. The event that rewired him happened during what was supposed to be
a quiet night at home—a Category 4 storm that made landfall faster than forecasters predicted. He
remembered the water rising in snapshots: the window bowing inward, his wife screaming, the ceiling
caving, and then the emergency responders carrying him out past a row of ambulances whose sirens
were still wailing. He spent three days in a hospital that smelled of bleach and antiseptic, his
lungs filled with floodwater and debris.

After discharge, Minh tried to return to normal. He quickly discovered that normal was no longer
available. The first time he drove past an ambulance on the freeway, his vision narrowed to a tunnel
and he had to pull over. He began to notice every hospital smell—the hand sanitizer in the
courthouse lobby, the cleaning fluid in the restroom—and each one dragged him back to that hospital
bed. But the most reliable trigger was weather: any shift toward heavy cloud cover, a sudden drop in
barometric pressure, or the sound of steady rain against the courthouse windows would summon the
full reliving experience.

His response was to clamp down. Minh decided that relaxing was a luxury he could no longer afford.
He stopped sitting in his chambers between hearings; instead he paced, reviewed files, kept his mind
occupied. He told himself that if he let his guard down even for a moment, the memory would rush in
and he would lose control in front of a jury. He made a conscious effort to suppress any thought of
the typhoon—when a news report mentioned the storm anniversary, he changed the channel. Better to
stay busy, he reasoned, than to risk being caught off guard.

The suppression itself became a trap. The harder Minh tried not to think about the disaster, the
more attuned he became to any cue that might signal its return. A sudden gust of wind outside his
window would make him freeze, searching for the smell of floodwater. And because he never let
himself relax, his nervous system stayed cranked to its highest alert, so a distant siren or the
whiff of rubbing alcohol would hit him like an electrical shock. Avoiding going out entirely became
his safest option. He stopped driving to the deli he used to visit, cancelled weekend plans with
friends, and began leaving the courthouse by a side exit to avoid the ambulance bay near the main
entrance.

His wife, Linh, grew frustrated. She didn't understand why he refused to take a weekend trip to the
coast, or why he flinched whenever she used bleach to clean the bathroom. Minh couldn't explain it
without admitting that the memory owned him. At work, he cut his lunch breaks short, never allowing
himself to sit still long enough for the images to surface. But the strategy backfired: the constant
vigilance exhausted him, and his caseload began to suffer. He lost patience during a deposition when
the air conditioning hum sounded like rising water, and he snapped at a clerk who walked into his
chambers holding a container of antiseptic wipes.

What finally brought Minh to a therapist's office was the episode in the courtroom—the ten-second
sensory reliving that left him breathless and pale in front of a packed gallery. He understood now
that his attempts to think less, feel less, and go out less had only made the trigger system more
sensitive. He was terrified that if he stopped suppressing, the memories would drown him. But he was
also terrified of what might happen the next time the sky turned grey.
