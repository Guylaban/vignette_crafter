Priya was twenty-two when she killed someone. It happened on a Tuesday morning in November, on the
five-lane stretch of highway she took to her UX design job in the suburbs. She was merging onto the
expressway when a sedan in front of her braked suddenly; she swerved left without checking her blind
spot. The motorcycle was in it. The rider, a father of two named Carlos, was thrown thirty feet and
died later that evening in the ICU. His wife and teenage daughter, who had been waiting for him at a
diner near the accident site, became the faces Priya could not erase. She never saw the impact, but
the sound lodged in her chest like a splinter.

In the weeks that followed, Priya told herself the story on a loop—if she had checked her mirror, if
she had braked instead of swerving, if she had left for work five minutes later. She could not stop
revisiting those moments, rearranging the details as though the right sequence would undo what had
already happened. Her husband, Ravi, tried to draw her into conversations about dinner, about
weekend plans, but her mind kept circling back to the highway. She began to feel that her own
thoughts were a trap: the more she tried not to think about the accident, the sharper its details
became. At work, she learned to keep a neutral expression when a colleague with Carlos’s same salt-
and-pepper beard passed her desk, or when a woman with long dark hair—like his wife’s—walked into
the coffee shop downstairs. Her body would go cold, her palms damp, and she would excuse herself to
the bathroom, where she would stand with her eyes closed and whisper, *Stop. Stop.*

The trigger-to-avoidance loop tightened fast. Every time she saw a face that reminded her of the
family, she would spend the rest of the afternoon replaying the accident, running through
alternative scenarios in excruciating detail, trying to find the one where Carlos walked away. That
rumination, in turn, primed her to notice more reminders the next day—a motorcycle’s engine revving
outside the office window, a news segment about traffic safety that made her flinch. She stopped
driving altogether, convinced that even being a passenger would force her to relive the moment Ravi
turned onto the highway and her entire body seized up. Instead, she took the bus, which added forty
minutes to her commute and often passed the diner where Carlos’s wife had been waiting. The sight of
that parking lot, the memory of the girl’s face from news photos, would send her straight back into
the prevention rumination she was trying to escape. At the office, she began eating lunch at her
desk with the door closed, avoiding the break room where a framed photo of the company’s charity
run—faces she didn’t know—suddenly looked too much like a crowd of victims. She tried harder to
suppress the intrusive images, but the effort left her exhausted and jumpy, so that any unexpected
noise or a coworker touching her shoulder became a fresh trigger.

What Priya had come to believe, without ever saying it aloud, was that she had proven herself
dangerous. Her husband had to drive her everywhere now; she could no longer run simple errands
alone. When a visiting client from India asked her opinion on a new app interface, Priya stumbled
over her words, certain that everyone could see the memory written on her face. She had not told
anyone at work about the accident, but she was sure they would sense her incompetence, her
culpability. The more she isolated herself, the more she felt the weight of the unspeakable. At
night, she lay awake beside Ravi, rehearsing alternative timelines—if the traffic had been lighter,
if she had slept better, if she had taken the exit before the merge. The preventing rumination had
become her only compass, even though it led nowhere.

Three months in, Priya finally told Ravi she needed help. What pushed her was a meeting with her
team lead, who gently asked whether she had been sleeping. Priya had dissolved into tears at her
desk, unable to explain that she was not sad, but terrified—terrified of the next trigger, and of
the next spiral of thoughts that would follow. She made an appointment with a therapist who
specialized in trauma. On the intake form, under *What brings you here?*, she wrote: *I can’t stop
thinking about the day I killed a man. And I can’t keep living like this.*
