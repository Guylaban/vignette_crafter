Arthur was sixty-eight and had spent thirty years as a school counselor, a job built on steady,
quiet connection with children and families. That foundation shattered one Tuesday afternoon during
a meeting with a parent who had been drinking. The man’s alcohol-heavy breath filled the small
office as he accused Arthur of mishandling his son’s case. The assault itself lasted only a few
moments—a punch, a fall, the back of Arthur’s head hitting the edge of a metal filing cabinet—but
each instant burned into his memory as a series of vivid snapshots: the parent’s reddened face, the
reek of bourbon, the antiseptic sting of the emergency room where he later came to. He could not
remember the whole event as a continuous story; instead, fragments would appear without warning and
disappear just as quickly, leaving him drenched in the same terror he had felt on the floor of his
office.

In the weeks that followed, Arthur found that certain sensations could trigger those fragments
instantly. The smell of antiseptic from the school nurse’s station would hurl him back to the
hospital gurney—the white ceiling tiles, the distant beeping, the cold. The smell of alcohol on a
visitor’s breath, even faintly, sent his heart racing as the snapshot of the parent’s fist flashed
before him. Most disturbing of all, when a colleague or a student tried to share something
personal—a gesture of emotional closeness—Arthur felt the same visceral threat: the betrayal of
trust, the sudden violence he had not seen coming. At those moments the memory fragments would flood
in, each one carrying that original terror intact.

To survive, Arthur learned to check out. When a trigger hit and the memory pieces began to arrive,
he would deliberately try to leave his own body—blanking his mind, staring at a fixed point, feeling
himself go numb and distant. He also started avoiding people. He ate lunch alone at his desk instead
of the staff room, took the long way to his office to avoid the nurse’s station, and cut meetings
short. He avoided intimacy with his wife, pulling away when she reached for his hand, no longer able
to tolerate the vulnerability that closeness required. Each small withdrawal felt necessary to keep
the memories at bay.

But his precautions only made things worse. By avoiding every whiff of alcohol or antiseptic, every
moment of emotional openness, Arthur never learned that these triggers could be safe. Instead, they
loomed larger, more unpredictable. His dissociation left him half-present in the world, so when a
trigger did catch him off guard—a student crying at his door, the scent of hand sanitizer—the
contrast was jarring, and the memory fragments hit harder than ever. He had come to believe that any
hint of intimacy was a prelude to attack, any smell a signal of imminent danger.

At work, his colleagues noticed he had grown distant. Students who used to trust him with their
problems now hesitated at his closed door. At home, his wife told him she felt like a stranger.
Arthur finally understood that his strategies were costing him the very connections he valued most.
He came to therapy hoping for a way back—to stop the snapshots from ambushing him, to feel safe in
his own body, and to let someone close without waiting for the blow.
