which only made the memory of the flood feel more impossible. Three years earlier, during a monsoon
in his native Mekong Delta, he had been trapped on a neighbor’s rooftop for fourteen hours while

neck, leaving a dull ache that never fully faded. More viscerally, he could still taste the
floodwater—a mix of mud, gasoline, and rotting fruit—as if it had just coated his tongue. Stranded
alone, he had watched entire homes collapse, and when the water finally receded, he had felt a quiet
certainty that he no longer belonged anywhere, that ordinary life was a stage he could only observe
from behind a sheet of glass.

The causal loops began almost imperceptibly. At work, Kien would lean over his keyboard and a
familiar twinge would seize his neck; in the same moment, the metallic-gritty taste would flood his
mouth, and the memory would return so vividly it felt unreal. *Did it really happen?* he would
wonder, but the emotion—a paralyzing helplessness—washed over him without any clear image, leaving
him breathless. That disorientation fed a deeper distrust: he scanned his coworkers’ faces and
concluded they were not what they seemed, that their easy laughter hid a capacity for cruelty or
indifference. He had come to believe that no one else could understand what he had survived, and
this isolation made him hypervigilant. As he surveyed the open-plan office for any sign of threat,
the tension in his shoulders and neck tightened, reawakening the exact somatic trigger that had
started the sequence. It became a self-reinforcing rhythm: neck pain conjured the taste, the taste
triggered the estrangement, and the estrangement kept his body coiled, ready for disaster.

His job required collaboration, but meetings felt unbearable. When a teammate casually mentioned a
coming storm, Kien’s stomach would drop and a wave of physical illness—nausea, sweating—would roll
through him. That feeling of being unwell did not just cue the memory; it also confirmed his belief
that he was fundamentally set apart, that others lived in a safe world he could no longer access. He
started eating lunch alone in the stairwell, avoiding the break room where talk of weekend plans
became another reminder of his own unspoken story. The taste would reappear even without a trigger—a
sudden bitter phantom on his tongue during a presentation—and he would feel the full emotional
weight of the flood, heart pounding, before any image formed. That emotion alone was enough to send
him into a spiral of distrust and longing for a place that, he was certain, no longer existed for
him.

By the time Kien sought help, the cycle had cost him. He had missed three deadlines, stopped
attending team stand-ups, and his manager had expressed concern about his “distance.” What finally
drove him to a therapist’s office was an episode in which a colleague complimented his wireframe,
and instead of feeling pride, Kien felt a sudden, cold certainty that the man was lying, that no one
could genuinely appreciate his work because they did not see him at all. The neck pain flared that
afternoon, and the taste returned, and for a long hour he sat frozen at his desk, wondering if the
memory was real or if he was losing his mind. He was most afraid of one thing: that the loop would
tighten until he no longer recognized any part of his life as his own. But he was quietly hoping
that someone might finally help him understand why a taste, a twinge, and a distrust of others had
come to dictate everything.
