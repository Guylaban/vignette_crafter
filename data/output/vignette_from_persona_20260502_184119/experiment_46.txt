highway after a twelve-hour shift at the investment firm. He had been doing the same commute for
thirty years and could have navigated it with his eyes closed—except that night he was more tired
than he realised. A deer froze in his headlights; he swerved; the car spun across the median and
slammed into a concrete barrier. The smell of gasoline flooded the cabin, and for a moment—he cannot
say how long—everything was silent except for a low hiss from the engine. Then another car, unseen,
hit the wreckage from behind, and the screech of tearing metal and the crunch of glass filled his
ears. He climbed out through the shattered window, bleeding from a gash on his forehead, and stood
in the darkness waiting for sirens. He survived physically, but something in his mind got stuck that
night.

In the weeks that followed, Keoni began to notice that ordinary sensations could send him straight
back to the crash. The distinctive sweet-sharp smell of gasoline, for example, would arrive before
his conscious mind knew what was happening—one whiff and he would be sitting in the ruined car
again, heart hammering, the hiss audible in his head. The same thing happened whenever he walked
into a room that was completely dark, or when he heard the particular screech of tyres braking hard.
Those triggers did not just bring the memory; they convinced him the memory was happening now, not
then. He would hear the impact sound in his skull, actual hearing of metal and glass, and feel the
seatbelt yank across his chest. Because the memory felt so present, his body reacted before he could
think—his hands would clench, his breath would stop, and he would find himself scanning the room for
exits, even if he was standing in his own kitchen.

Keoni’s mind, trying to protect him, began to interpret these experiences with a rigid logic. He
came to believe that he had to be on guard all the time; you never knew what could trigger the next
flashback, so he had to watch constantly. This hypervigilance made him notice every car sound more
acutely, and he started avoiding thoughts about the crash altogether—if he let himself think about
it, he was certain he would fall apart and never get back up. Ironically, trying not to think only
made the memories more intrusive. He would be at his desk, running numbers, and the smell of a
colleague’s coffee would inexplicably remind him of gasoline, and then the memory would surge. His
constant vigilance also led him to scan for exits in every building he entered, and sometimes that
scanning itself—the rapid eye movements, the mental mapping—pulled up the image of the dark highway
because his brain associated any threat assessment with that night. His body would tense before his
conscious mind caught up, and that bodily reaction, in turn, could trigger the full memory.

At night, Keoni often woke in the small hours to check whether he was safe. The darkness of his
bedroom became a trigger; he would lie still, listening for car sounds, and the more he listened,
the more he heard—a distant engine, a tyre on gravel—each sound threatening to bring the crash back.
His wife, Lani, would find him sitting upright at three in the morning, staring at the door. He had
become convinced that if he did not stay alert, something terrible would happen again. This belief
kept him in a state of constant threat monitoring, which made him more sensitive to triggers—any
sound, any darkness, any smell was a potential signal. He started taking different routes to work to
avoid the stretch of highway, but even the office parking garage was difficult because it was dark
and had the faint scent of exhaust fumes. As a financial analyst, Keoni needed to focus on complex
spreadsheets and quarterly reports, but his mind was now wired to detect danger, not dividends. His
performance slipped; he began leaving early, claiming headaches. Lani noticed he was irritable and
distant, but he could not explain why he needed to check all the windows before sitting down to
dinner.

By the time he sought help, three years after the accident, Keoni had become a prisoner of his own
caution. What finally brought him to therapy was a moment in the grocery store: a child dropped a
can of soda, and the loud clatter sounded exactly like the impact of the second car. He dropped to
the floor, hands over his head, and when he came back to himself, strangers were staring. He knew
then that he could no longer manage this alone. What frightens him most, he told the therapist, is
the thought that one day a trigger will hit and he will never return to the present—that the memory
will finally consume him. But underneath that fear, there is also a fragile hope: that maybe, with
help, he can walk into a dark room without feeling the metal crumple around him again.
