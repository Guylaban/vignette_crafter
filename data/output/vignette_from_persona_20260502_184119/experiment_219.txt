Saeed had spent his career reading landscapes—decades as an environmental scientist studying soil
erosion in the arid hills of the Southwest. He knew the way water moved through a wash, the patience
of rock becoming sand. But nothing in that knowledge had prepared him for the moment his truck
struck Elias on the unpaved access road. It was late afternoon, the light slanted, and Saeed had not
seen him step out from behind the chaparral. The thud, the terrible stillness, then the sound of
Elias trying to breathe—a wet, shallow rasp that Saeed would hear again and again in the months that
followed.

In the immediate aftermath, Saeed could not speak. He knew what had happened—the angle of the truck,
the weight of the bumper—but when the paramedics asked him to describe it, the words would not come.
They stuck somewhere behind his teeth, as if language itself had been bruised. Elias survived, but
with a fractured pelvis and a collapsed lung that left him with permanent difficulty breathing.
Saeed visited the hospital once. He sat in the waiting room for an hour and then left without going
in. He never visited again.

At first, the memory was a splinter he could not remove. It would surface at unexpected moments, and
when it did, it did not feel like remembering. It felt like the truck was still rolling, the sun
still low, Elias still on the ground. Saeed would be sitting at the kitchen table and suddenly his
hands would tremble, his chest tighten, and he would be there again. The experience was so vivid, so
sensorily complete, that afterward he sometimes had to touch the chair beneath him to believe he was
home. And then, just as quickly, a strange detachment would settle in: the whole thing seemed too
extreme to have actually happened to him. He would think, *This can’t be real. That can’t have been
my life.*

The triggers were quiet, domestic things. His wife, Leila, liked to hug him when he came in from the
garden. She would wrap her arms around his shoulders from behind, and for a second he would feel the
warmth of her body, the soft pressure. But then—without warning—the hug would become Elias’s wife’s
embrace in the hospital corridor, the one that had never happened, and the memory would crash in,
his heart hammering as if he were back behind the wheel. He learned to stiffen when Leila
approached, though he could not tell her why.

He also learned to dread feeling good. A pleasant afternoon birdwatching, a light laugh at a
television program—a moment of ease might open a trapdoor. The happiness would be there, and then it
would be gone, replaced by the sharp replay of the accident, the breathing, the guilt. He had come
to believe that any relaxation was a betrayal, and that the memory was waiting to punish him for it.

The heavy breathing sounds were the hardest. In a grocery store, a man with asthma wheezing past the
dairy case; at the nature center where he still volunteered, a child panting after a short hike.
Each time, Saeed felt the world dissolve into that late afternoon light. He would stand frozen, the
reliving so complete he half-expected to see Elias’s face. The memory always came with the same
fragmented quality: he knew the sequence of events, but he could not describe it aloud. If anyone
asked what was wrong, he would shake his head, the words lost.

Now, nearly two years later, Saeed is sitting in a therapist’s office for the first time. Leila
urged him after he pulled away from her embrace and walked outside, shaking. He is afraid that the
memories are taking over his life—that he cannot be happy, cannot be held, cannot hear a heavy
breath without being pulled back into that road. He hopes the therapist can help him find the words
he has lost, and maybe, just maybe, to believe that what happened is truly over.
