Mai was seventy-one when she finally walked into a clinic, her hands trembling against her thighs.
The captivity had happened in 1973, when she was a young woman in Da Nang, taken by Viet Cong
soldiers who kept her in a concrete bunker for eleven weeks. She had been a clerk for the provincial
administration, and the soldiers believed she had information about American troop movements. They
did not believe her when she said she knew nothing. The bunker had no windows, only a narrow slit
near the ceiling that let in a stripe of daylight, and she quickly learned to read the time by its
movement across the dirt floor. At night, the guards would shine a flashlight in her face every
hour, and she learned to keep her eyes open, to stay awake, because the moments of darkness between
the light felt worse—they could be doing anything in the dark, and she would not know. She was
released after a prisoner exchange, but something in her had stayed in that bunker.

In the years that followed, Mai emigrated to the United States, married, earned her CPA, raised two
children, divorced. But the belief that the institutions that should have protected her had
failed—the South Vietnamese government that never accounted for her, the U.S. embassy that refused
to intervene—hardened into a conviction that no authority, no structure, could keep her safe. That
conviction meant she felt exposed and vulnerable wherever she went, especially at night, when the
world turned dark and she could not see what might be coming. She had come to believe that she could
only feel safe in her own apartment, behind a deadbolt and a chain lock, with all the lights on.
Even then, she found it harder to feel safe after sunset, and she would sit in her armchair until
two or three in the morning, reading financial reports on her tablet, because the thought of closing
her eyes in the dark made her chest tighten.

Her work as a freelance financial analyst allowed her to stay home, but the very vigilance that kept
her awake also made her feel contaminated by what had happened. The memory of the grime on the
bunker walls, the smell of her own unwashed body, the sense that she had been dirtied by those
weeks—it clung to her like a second skin. When she tried to push those thoughts away, telling
herself to stop thinking about it, the effort itself seemed to prove that she could not cope with
even the slightest upset. A missed email, a scratch on her table, a flickering lightbulb—each small
upset felt like a disaster, and she would think, *I can't handle this, I am broken.* The more she
tried to suppress the memories, the more she felt unable to cope, and the more she believed she was
still contaminated by the past. She would also catch herself wondering how her life would have been
different if the event had not occurred—if she had stayed in Vietnam, if she had not been captured,
if she had married someone else. These counterfactual thoughts only deepened her sense of
helplessness, and the helplessness made her feel more threatened, more vigilant.

She stayed up late not because she was not tired, but because falling asleep meant losing control.
And losing control—surrendering to the dark—felt like the same thing that had happened in the
bunker. Her delay of sleep was a strategy to keep watching, to prevent something bad from happening,
but the strategy itself kept her in a state of heightened threat: the later she stayed awake, the
more sensitive she became to every creak and shadow, and the more convinced she was that she was not
safe. This cycle tightened over the decades. She divorced in part because her husband could not
understand why she insisted on sleeping with the light on, why she flinched when he touched her
unexpectedly. She had come to accept that she would never feel clean, never feel capable, never feel
safe.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a night in October when a thunderstorm knocked out the
power in her building. She found herself sitting in absolute darkness, unable to move, her heart
hammering, tears streaming down her face, and for the first time in fifty years, she thought about
the slit of sky in the bunker and how she had watched it like a dying woman watches water. That
night, she called her daughter—a therapist in Chicago—and said, *I think I need help.* When Mai sat
in the intake chair, she told the clinician what she was most afraid of: that she would never be
able to feel safe enough to sleep through the night, that the contamination would never wash off,
and that she was too old to change. But she also said, quietly, that she was tired of staying awake.
