Amina, a 63-year-old financial analyst, had spent decades building a life in the United States that
looked stable from the outside—a secure job, a quiet apartment, a careful routine. But the stability
was an illusion, held together by rules she never consciously chose. She had arrived as a refugee,
fleeing a conflict that had stripped her of everything familiar, and what she had carried with her
was not just survival but a permanent, unspoken vigilance.

The event that had upended her life occurred during the final days of her escape. Amina and her
younger brother had been traveling through a forested region, trying to reach the border after their
village was attacked. They were stopped at a military checkpoint by armed police officers who
dragged them into the dense bush. For hours, she was forced to kneel on the damp earth, forced to
watch as her brother was beaten, forced to comply with every shouted command under the threat of
being shot. The forest—humid, alive with the sounds of insects and unseen animals—became the stage
for her complete powerlessness. She survived because she obeyed, but the price was a deep,
unreachable fracture in her sense of safety.

In the decades since, Amina had learned to navigate the world by staying small and compliant. As a
financial analyst, she was known for her precision and her quiet reliability—she never questioned a
directive, never challenged a deadline, never raised her voice in a meeting. She avoided any
situation that might involve disagreement or confrontation, because conflict, she had come to
believe, was the first step toward danger. Authority figures unsettled her. When a police cruiser
appeared in her rearview mirror, her hands would sweat and her mind would race through escape plans,
even though she had done nothing wrong. She avoided being around people in uniform whenever
possible, taking longer routes to work to bypass the police station, choosing shops that did not
have security guards at the door. At work, she accepted extra assignments without complaint because
saying no felt like drawing attention to herself—and attention, in her experience, was never safe.

Her greatest fear, however, was not for herself. Amina worried obsessively about bad things
happening to people she cared about. She never had children—she told herself it was because she had
focused on her career, but the truth was she could not bear the thought of bringing a child into a
world she knew to be dangerous. She poured her protective energy into a small circle of friends and
her surviving family still abroad. When a phone call went unanswered, her mind would race to
catastrophe: they had been arrested, attacked, killed. A single afternoon of silence from her cousin
in another city could send her into hours of torment, replaying scenarios she could not stop.

The connection between her triggers and her maladaptive strategies formed a loop that grew tighter
over time. When she saw a police officer outside her office building, her immediate response was to
avoid—to stay inside until the officer left, to take a different exit, to cancel any plans that
might involve encountering authority again. Each successful avoidance felt like proof that danger
had been real, and each narrow escape made the next trigger feel more threatening. The more she
avoided, the more she scanned her environment for potential threats, and the more she found them. A
wooded park near her apartment became a place she could no longer walk through, because it reminded
her of the forest where she had been forced to her knees. A supervisor asking her to stay late for
an impromptu meeting became a demand she could not refuse and could not bear, leaving her trapped in
her own compliance.

What finally brought Amina to seek help was a moment of recognition she could no longer ignore. She
had declined an invitation to her closest friend's retirement dinner—a friend she had known for
twenty years—because the restaurant was adjacent to a small park with trees, and she could not bring
herself to explain why. Her friend, hurt and confused, asked if something was wrong. Amina said no.
But sitting alone in her apartment that evening, she realized she had spent decades saying no—to
invitations, to relationships, to the ordinary life that others seemed to inhabit without effort.
She was exhausted by the constant vigilance, the endless mental calculations required to navigate a
world full of hidden dangers. She was tired of being afraid of a forest she had not seen in forty
years.

She came to therapy because she wanted her life back, though she was not sure what that meant
anymore. What frightened her most was not the memories themselves, but the possibility that she had
already lost too much time—that the life she had saved by fleeing was not, in the end, a life she
was actually living. But beneath the fear was something fragile and persistent: a small hope that at
sixty-three, it might not be too late to learn how to stop surviving and start, finally, to live.
