Mark Stevenson, a 52-year-old school principal, had run his suburban elementary school for over a
decade. He was known for his steady presence, his ability to de-escalate tense parent meetings with
a calm voice and open body language. That changed on a Tuesday afternoon in late October. A father,
furious over a suspension decision, stormed into Mark’s office. Before Mark could rise from his
chair, the man grabbed a heavy metal paperweight from the desk and swung it. Mark raised his arm to
block, but the blow connected with his temple, knocking him to the floor. He remembers the impact,
the cold tile against his cheek, the sound of his own breath, but the precise sequence between the
first hit and the staff bursting in is gone—a blank where he knows something happened but cannot
deliberately retrieve it.

What returns instead is a film that plays without warning. The memory comes as a sudden, vivid
replay: the door swinging open, the rage in the man’s face, the weight of the paperweight in his
hand. When it hits, Mark’s body locks into the same position it was in when he fell—shoulders
hunched, hands braced, neck twisted toward the floor. He feels the pressure of the carpet against
his jaw, though he is standing in his kitchen or sitting at his desk. And with that bodily
reenactment comes a surge of alertness that does not fade. His heart hammers; his muscles coil. He
becomes, as he describes it, “a live wire.”

That hypervigilance has reshaped how he moves through the world. He cannot enter a room without
scanning for exits—the back door of the cafeteria, the window in the library office, the service
entrance near the gym. He notices weapons everywhere: a pair of scissors on a teacher’s desk, a
heavy stapler on a counter, the pointed end of a letter opener. Any sudden sound—the slam of a
locker, a dropped book, a child’s shout—makes him flinch hard, his shoulders rising and his head
ducking. At faculty meetings he chooses a seat with his back to the wall, his eyes fixed on the
door. He has come to believe that he must always know his escape route and the potential threats in
arm’s reach.

The memories trigger this state most powerfully. An unexpected flash of the assault—the man’s voice,
the thud of the paperweight—can send Mark into a prolonged period of scanning and startle that lasts
hours. He finds himself checking the parking lot for unfamiliar cars, noting the placement of fire
extinguishers, cataloging every heavy object in a room. His wife has noticed that he no longer sits
comfortably in restaurants; he angles his chair so he can see the entrance. At a recent school
assembly, the microphone screeched with feedback and Mark jumped from his chair, knocking over a
stack of programs. Staff looked at him with concern; he laughed it off, but the embarrassment was
sharp.

He sought help because that incident made him realize he is not the principal he used to be. He is
tired of bracing for an attack that already happened. He wants the memories to stop hijacking his
body, and he wants to walk into his own school without cataloging every exit and every object that
could become a weapon.
