David is a 65-year-old prosecutor who has spent decades in the courtroom, a man who built a career
on order and accountability. That foundation cracked on a late October evening three years ago, when
he left the courthouse after a long trial and took his usual shortcut through a dimly lit parking
garage. Two plainclothes officers, responding to a misidentified suspect description, slammed him
against a concrete pillar before he could show his badge. He remembers the incomprehensible
chatter—their overlapping voices, the crackling radio, muffled orders he could not parse—and the
sickening shock of being treated as a criminal. The officers realized their mistake within minutes,
but the damage had already lodged itself in David’s chest like a splinter.

In the months that followed, he found himself unable to pass a police cruiser without his pulse
hammering. Authority presence became a sudden, visceral trigger: the sight of a uniform, the distant
wail of a siren, even a patrol car parked outside a coffee shop. Other triggers crept in more
subtly. In the courtroom, where he had always thrived on structure, he now flinched at moral
violations—a lawyer’s snide remark, a witness lying under oath, any hint of people misbehaving. The
incomprehensible chatter of the garage replayed whenever he heard multiple overlapping voices, such
as the low-grade hum of a crowded jury room or the simultaneous cross-talk in the hall. Each trigger
instantly launched him into a state of hypervigilance: he began watching others closely, scanning
their faces and movements for signs that they might harm him, his eyes darting from doorway to
doorway. He lay awake night after night, replaying the garage, and when he did sleep, he woke
gasping from dreams of being pinned down.

To cope, David developed a set of strategies that initially felt protective but soon tightened into
a rigid cycle. At home—and even in his office when he could get away with it—he put on loud music or
the television, drowning out any background noise that might startle him. Sensory distraction became
his first line of defense: if he could not hear the chatter, perhaps he could not be pulled back
into that garage. He also began constantly checking his surroundings, scanning the perimeter of any
room he entered, noting exits and potential attackers. This vigilance extended to his colleagues: he
kept asking his wife, Ellen, and a trusted paralegal to reassure him that he was okay, that he
seemed normal, that there was no threat. Each time they reassured him, the relief was brief—and the
need returned stronger.

The strategies themselves became fuel. The loud music and television muted his ability to hear real-
world sounds, leaving him more startled when a sudden noise broke through, which then amplified his
hypervigilance. His constant scanning exhausted him, making it harder to sleep, and sleep
deprivation worsened his threat-monitoring. Reassurance seeking, meanwhile, created a quiet
dependency: Ellen grew weary of the repeated questions, and their conversations became tense, which
David interpreted as proof that she was hiding something or that he was failing. That only pushed
him to watch her more closely, searching for signs of harm or disappointment, and to seek even more
reassurance.

At work, the cycle was most damaging. A prosecutor’s world is full of moral violations and
incomprehensible chatter—the very triggers that now hijacked his attention. In the middle of a
cross-examination, his gaze would drift to a bailiff’s belt, and he would lose the thread of his
argument. He began avoiding the hallways where police officers congregated, taking longer routes,
scheduling meetings to avoid chance encounters. His productivity slipped, and a younger colleague
was assigned to co-lead a high-profile case. David had come to believe that he was broken, that the
garage had revealed a fundamental weakness that made him unfit for the job he had loved. The thought
terrified him.

What finally brought David to seek help was not the professional decline, but a moment at home.
Ellen found him crouched in the corner of the living room, the television blaring, his eyes fixed on
the front door, because a neighbor had slammed a car door and he had been certain it was a squad
car. She did not raise her voice. She simply said his name, waited, and then asked if he wanted to
talk to someone. He was most afraid that the therapist would tell him he was too old to change, that
this was simply who he was now. But underneath that fear was a sliver of hope that someone might
explain why the garage still owned him, and that maybe—if he could understand the loop—he could step
out of it.
