captive for four days in a windowless room, bound and gagged, the only constant a deep, suffocating
silence that stretched between her captors’ rare words. She eventually escaped when one of them left
a door unlatched, but the memory of that stillness remained. It felt so extreme that even now she
sometimes doubted it had happened to her—like a story she had been told rather than lived. Yet it
forced its way into her mind without warning: while she was checking supplies in the ambulance bay,
during dinner with her husband, in the middle of the night. When it came, the emotion was so
overwhelming she had to stop whatever she was doing, pressing her palms against her thighs until the
wave passed.

Those episodes began to frighten Carmen in a new way. Her reactions, she told herself, meant she was
going crazy—a fear that took hold and would not let go. She became certain that the world was a

happening to her. This conviction, in turn, seemed to pry the memory wide open. The more she
believed she was losing her mind, the more the captivity scenes intruded, as if her own terror was
the key that unlocked them. Each involuntary recall—the taste of the gag, the pressure of the ropes,

become a trigger: at home, when the house fell quiet, her heart would race and she would freeze,

guard. She frequently looked behind her while walking from room to room, scanning for a follower she
knew was not there. At work, she avoided quiet stretches of road and insisted on having the
ambulance radio on at all times, rationalizing it as professional habit.

called or visited. Her husband, Jorge, did not understand why she flinched when he entered a room
silently, or why she searched every closet before they left the house. Carmen could not explain it;
all she knew was that the cycle tightened with every passing month.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning at the station when a prolonged silence in the

ambulance. Her supervisor urged her to see someone. Carmen agreed, but what frightened her most was
the thought that she might truly be beyond repair. She hoped, against the weight of her certainty,
that someone could show her a way out.
