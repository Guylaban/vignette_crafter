Mariana was twenty-four when she was abducted from her apartment and held for three days by a
trafficking ring. During that time she was tortured—beaten, burned, and systematically humiliated.
One of her captors was a woman who would enter the concrete room alone, mock her, and inflict pain
while Mariana lay bound on a mattress, her own blood and bruises visible, with no way out. That
specific image—the female guard, the sight of her wounds, the total entrapment—became the fixed
point around which everything else revolved.

After her rescue, Mariana returned to her job as an HVAC technician. She had always been good with
her hands, and the work paid the bills. But within weeks she found herself scanning every room
before she stepped into it. At night she checked her apartment door three times before sleeping and
would wake, heart pounding, to verify the locks again. A sudden bang from a dropped tool made her
flinch so hard her coworkers noticed. She began to believe that she could never know who would harm
her next—that anyone, even a woman, might be a threat. This fear of harm kept her alert, and the
alertness itself confirmed that she was no longer safe anywhere, that something in her was
permanently broken.

The memory of the female tormentor intruded unpredictably, often when Mariana least expected it. She
would be driving home and suddenly feel the shame of that powerless moment, as if the event were
happening to someone else, a stranger whose degradation she could only watch. The shame was
overwhelming, forcing her to pull over and breathe. The more she believed she had permanently
changed, the more alien and shameful the memory felt—it seemed to belong to a person she no longer
recognized. And each time the memory crashed in, her threat level spiked again. She would start
checking her rearview mirror for followers, then check locks once more when she got home.

At work, triggers accumulated. A female client alone in a house made Mariana’s stomach clench; she
began avoiding assignments where a woman would be home by herself, making excuses to her dispatcher.
Seeing a visible injury on a coworker—a cut, a bruise—sent her adrenaline soaring; she would stare
at it and then quickly look away, her startle response primed. She found herself trapped in hallways
or small boiler rooms and her breath would quicken, because having no options reminded her of the
cell. These triggers created a constant undercurrent of threat that eroded her concentration. She
started checking doors and windows at client sites, even when there was no reason. Her
hypervigilance also made her see triggers everywhere: a woman walking toward her on the street
became a potential captor, a Band-Aid on a child’s arm became a sign of violence.

The more Mariana checked and started, the more she doubted herself. *I can’t trust that I’ll do the
right thing*, she thought after fumbling a simple repair because she was too busy scanning the room.
Her fear of harm deepened: if she had to be this watchful, then danger must be real. The self-doubt
and sense of permanent damage fed directly back into the memory—each flashback felt more shameful,
more alien, because it fit the person she now believed herself to be: broken, unreliable, unsafe.
And that memory, in turn, made her more sensitive to triggers. A woman’s voice in the next room
would immediately pull her back to the guard’s mocking tone, and then the cycle tightened again.

A few months ago, her supervisor put her on a team working in a women’s shelter—a project Mariana
couldn’t refuse without explanation. The first day she walked in and saw several women, some with
visible injuries from domestic violence, and felt the walls close in. She nearly fled. That
afternoon she called a mental health clinic. What brought her there was not a single symptom but the
crushing weight of the loop: the memory, the fear, the checking, the avoidance, all feeding each
other. She was terrified that she would not be able to keep her job, and even more terrified that
she was permanently damaged and would never feel safe again. But she had come anyway, desperate for
something to stop the spiral.
