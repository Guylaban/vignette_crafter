Nathan is a 33-year-old prosecutor in a Midwestern district attorney's office, married for six years
to a high school teacher. He had been leading a high-profile terrorism-related trial when a bomb
detonated in the courthouse parking garage. He escaped with minor injuries, but two colleagues and a
security guard were killed. In the aftermath, Nathan could not hold a coherent sequence of the
minutes before and after the blast. What he carried instead were fragments—the pressure wave, the
silence afterward, the smell of gasoline and burnt rubber. That smell, or anything that remotely
resembles it, still pulls him back as though no time has passed. The memory feels more real than
ordinary recollections; it has a vivid, present-tense quality that leaves him disoriented, as if he
is still standing in the smoke.

Nathan works long hours, often arriving home exhausted. Fatigue now functions as a trigger: when he
is drained, the intrusive images surface more easily, and with them comes a wave of self-
condemnation. He cannot forgive himself for not doing more, for not warning people fast enough. In
his darker moments he is certain he should have protected others and failed. These appraisals
tighten around him when he encounters any reminder. A colleague’s cologne that carries a hint of
alcohol sets off the same loop: the scent appears, and within seconds Nathan is mentally back in the
garage, replaying his inaction. He has begun avoiding after-work social events where drinks are
served, telling his wife he is too tired. The exhaustion itself then becomes another reminder of his
perceived weakness, feeding the guilt.

His guilt, in turn, deepens the fragmentation of his memory. The more he blames himself, the more
the event feels present rather than past, as though he must keep reliving it to find a way to change
the outcome. Yet the story remains stubbornly incoherent; he cannot piece together why he survived
when others did not. This lack of narrative fuels his distrust of people around him. He catches
himself scrutinizing colleagues’ motives, wondering if they are hiding something. At work he avoids
the parking garage and takes a longer route through the building, even though he knows the explosion
was a one-time event. He has started leaving his office early to avoid the echo of footsteps in the
hallway, which can sound like running before a blast.

The memory itself also triggers his physical state. When a spontaneous image of the explosion
surfaces—often when he is tired or hears a sudden bang—his heart pounds and he feels the fatigue
deepen, as though the recollection drains him. The tiredness then becomes another cue. This cycle
has eroded his concentration; he has missed filing deadlines and received a formal warning from his
supervisor. At home he is irritable, and his wife has commented that he seems “somewhere else.” What
finally brought Nathan to seek help was a moment in court: a door slammed, he flinched, and he
realized he had been scanning the room for exits. He is afraid that if he does not get control of
this, he will lose his career and his marriage. But what frightens him most is the thought that
facing the memories will only prove that he really did fail.
