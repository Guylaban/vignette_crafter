Margaret, a 61-year-old policy analyst, had spent decades reviewing environmental regulations, but
nothing prepared her for the moment a chemical fire erupted in the storage room beneath her office.
She was alone in a conference room when the smoke alarm sounded, and by the time she reached the
stairwell, acrid fumes had already filled the corridor. She crouched on the floor, coughing, unable
to see the exit, certain she was about to pass out. Firefighters pulled her out after twenty
minutes, but the memory of that helpless, gasping panic never left her.

In the months that followed, Margaret found herself avoiding the television news—any report of a
factory fire or chemical spill made her chest tighten. She also stopped watching documentaries about
industrial accidents, though she had once found them professionally interesting. At work, she began
scanning every room she entered for exits. When she attended meetings, she always chose a seat with
her back to a wall and a clear path to the door. She told herself it was just common sense, but she
knew it was more than that.

The smells were the worst. A whiff of smoke from a colleague’s cigarette outside the building could
send her heart racing, and she would immediately think, *I can’t trust myself to stay calm*. That
thought—*I can’t rely on myself*—had taken root during the fire, when she had frozen instead of
finding the exit. Now, whenever she caught a burnt smell—from a toaster in the break room or a
passing car—the same self-doubt surged back: *I’ll never be able to feel normal emotions again.*
That negative appraisal, in turn, made her even more vigilant. She would scan the room for threats,
her body already tense, which only confirmed her belief that she was fundamentally unsafe in her own
skin.

The cycle tightened. One day, Margaret smelled alcohol—hand sanitizer from a dispenser in the
hallway—and her stomach dropped. The odor triggered a flash of the chemical fumes, and the thought
came unbidden: *I can’t rely on myself to get out of here.* She immediately began scanning for
exits, her heart pounding, and she chose a seat near the stairwell for the rest of the afternoon.
Later, she spent an hour ruminating about how she could have prevented the fire—if she had checked
the storage room herself, if she had insisted on a better evacuation plan. That rumination, a
Maladaptive Strategy she had developed, only deepened her self-doubt. She told herself she had
failed once, so she would fail again.

Margaret’s checking of her surroundings became constant. She would re-scan rooms she had already
entered, looking for anything out of place. Her physical reactivity—the racing heart, the
sweating—when she smelled smoke or burnt odors made her believe even more strongly that she had lost
control of her emotions. And that belief heightened her threat monitoring: the more she thought she
couldn’t trust herself, the more she searched for danger. The searches, in turn, brought her into
contact with triggers she might otherwise have missed, like the faint alcohol smell from cleaning
supplies in the hallway, which then restarted the whole sequence.

As a policy analyst, Margaret’s job required meetings and public hearings, but she now arrived early
to claim a “safe seat” and avoided the cafeteria, where a faint burnt toast smell sometimes
lingered. She had stopped attending professional development seminars because they might include
video footage of industrial accidents. Her husband noticed she had become withdrawn, refusing
invitations to dinner parties where someone might grill with charcoal. She worried she was letting
everyone down, and that worry—*I can’t do the right thing*—made her avoid even friendly gatherings.

What finally brought Margaret to seek help was a morning when she could not bring herself to enter
her own kitchen because she thought she smelled gas. There was no leak; it was just the smell of a
neighbor’s barbecue drifting in. She stood in the hallway, trembling, and realized she was trapped
in a cycle she could not break on her own. She came to therapy afraid that she would never feel
normal again, but she was also quietly hoping that maybe, this time, she could learn to trust
herself.
