Tran Nguyen was seventy-seven years old, a retired firefighter who had spent thirty-five years in
the San Jose Fire Department, when he finally walked into a therapist’s office at his wife’s
insistence. He had never told anyone the full story of what happened to him in the spring of 1968,
when he was twenty-one and a civilian in a small village in Bình Định Province. One afternoon, a
firefight between North Vietnamese forces and American troops swept through the bamboo groves near
his home. He was collecting water when an artillery shell landed thirty meters away, throwing him
into a ditch and burying his younger brother under rubble that Tran could not move in time. He still
saw his brother’s hand, palm up, the fingers curling, as the next shell hit. That memory—crystal
clear, as if it were happening right in front of him—had never faded. But for decades Tran told
himself that the memory belonged to someone else, to a different man who had not made it out. The
memory felt alien, like a film he was watching of a stranger’s life, yet it forced its way into his
mind without warning, often when he least expected it.

After the war, Tran immigrated to the United States and became a firefighter. The job was a natural
fit: he had always been steady under pressure, and the structure of the firehouse gave him a life he
could control. But the work also exposed him to things that cracked the surface of that control.
Ambulance sirens—especially the low, rising wail of a city medic unit—made his chest tighten.
Explosion sounds, even a car backfiring or a dropped metal ladder in the station bay, sent a jolt
through him that he could not shake for hours. And certain names he heard over the radio or on the
news—Thanh, Minh, names from his old life—would stop him mid-stride, a cold recognition flooding
through him that he could not reason away. Each time he heard one of those names, he felt less like
a person and more like an object, a thing that had been through something unspeakable and was now
just waiting for the next trigger.

Over the years, Tran developed strategies to keep those memories and feelings at bay. He refused to
go to sleep without the television on, because lying in the dark brought the memory of the ditch,
the weight of the rubble, the smell of wet earth and blood. He began checking the corners of every
room he entered—the firehouse kitchen, the garage, his own bedroom—scanning for any sign of threat
with a vigilance that his colleagues called “good situational awareness” but that Tran knew was
something else. After particularly bad shifts, he would sit in his car and imagine getting revenge
on the people responsible for the bombing, even though he knew the faces in his fantasy were
invented, that the actual soldiers were long dead. These fantasies gave him a momentary sense of
power, but they also deepened the belief that he was not like other people—that somebody else,
someone stronger or smarter, would never have gotten into that situation, would never have let his
brother die.

The causal loop was tight and self-reinforcing. A bang—a trash truck compaction, a firework—would
rip the memory into his mind without permission. That memory, so vivid it made him flinch, would
then convince him that he could not handle thinking about the war at all, that if he let himself
feel it he would shatter. The appraisal that he could not cope drove him to avoid sleep even more
fiercely, and it also made him hypervigilant to potential triggers, because he was now interpreting
every sound as the start of something catastrophic. His constant scanning, in turn, made him more
likely to notice—and be caught off guard by—the very triggers he was trying to avoid. And the
revenge fantasies, though they provided a brief outlet, left him feeling like a monster, which
strengthened his sense of dehumanization. The more he felt like an object, the more he avoided sleep
and checking his surroundings, believing that he did not deserve rest or safety.

The toll became visible in his marriage. His wife, Linh, said he had become a ghost in the house. He
stopped sleeping in their bedroom, spending nights on the living room couch with the TV blaring. He
would not drive past the hospital district because of the ambulances, and he had turned down
promotions at the firehouse because he could not handle the office being near the dispatch speakers
that sometimes played emergency audio. What finally brought him to seek help was two weeks ago, when
his grandson—who had the same name as his brother, Thanh—called his name from the backyard. Tran
dropped a glass and stood frozen, his heart hammering, and Linh told him quietly that she could not
watch him disappear anymore. What frightened him most was the thought that if he started to talk
about what happened, the memory would take over completely, and he would be stuck in that ditch
forever. But he also knew, deep down, that he was already living there. He came to therapy hoping to
find a way to sleep in the dark again, and afraid that there might be no way back.
