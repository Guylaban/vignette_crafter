him happened three years ago, while he was caring for the Benson children—twins, aged four. During a
routine afternoon at the community pool, one of the boys, Leo, slipped under the water unnoticed.

minutes—the wet, gasping silence, then the mother’s scream—returned to him now as auditory
intrusions so vivid he would stop mid-sentence, waiting for the sound to pass. He could not tell the
story of that day without losing the thread; the grief rose like a wall, and his words dissolved.

In the weeks that followed, Tane began to see the world differently. He had always prided himself on
being steady, protective. Now he believed he was fundamentally weak—a man who should have scanned
the water more carefully, who should have known better. This belief infused his every waking moment.
He felt less like a person and more like a broken object, a failed guardian. Whenever he saw a
child’s arm or leg—a small hand gripping a stroller, a toddler’s chubby ankle—his stomach clenched.
Those body parts were the same shape and size as Leo’s limbs. The sight conjured not the boy’s face,

to recount the sequence of events. When the auditory recall—Leo’s last exhale, the frantic

each time he could not explain what happened, the belief that he was weak embedded itself deeper.
