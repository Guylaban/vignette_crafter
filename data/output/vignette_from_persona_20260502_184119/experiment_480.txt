Ravi, a 34-year-old delivery driver from a South Asian community in New Jersey, still replays the
moment in August when a child darted between parked cars and into the side of his van. He was making
a routine afternoon drop-off in a residential neighborhood, and by the time he braked, the boy’s
femur had snapped under the front bumper. The child’s mother later told police she had seen Ravi
slow down; he had, in fact, been glancing at his GPS. That fraction of a second is what Ravi cannot
forgive himself for. He should have seen the boy earlier. He should have honked, should have
swerved. Instead, he acted exactly as he did, and now he lives with the certainty that the event
happened because of the way he acted.

Every day, as he climbs back into the van, that conviction sits on his chest. Ravi will never
forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened—not just at the moment of impact but in the
hours afterward, when he sat in the police station and waited, unable to call the hospital or the
family. He had come to believe that he was, fundamentally, a person who fails others when it matters
most. He tells himself he should have protected others and he failed, and that belief has calcified
into something like a fixed truth. During deliveries he catches his own hands trembling when he sees
a child near the curb, and he mutters the words “my fault” under his breath, though no one can hear
him.

Ravi is married. His wife, Priya, notices he has stopped laughing at her jokes and spends evenings
staring at the wall. She does not know the full extent of his thoughts because he cannot bring
himself to say them aloud—saying them would make them real, and he already feels the weight of them
alone. He still works his full route, but every stop near a school or a park makes his stomach
lurch. He has not told his supervisor about the accident because he is certain he would lose his
job, and then what would he be? A man who failed to protect a child and cannot even provide for his
own family.

The one thing Ravi has not done is let himself off the hook. He replays his inaction—the way he
froze for a second before hitting the brakes—and he cannot find a version of himself that is not
guilty. He should have been watching more closely. He should have known. The guilt is not a passing
regret; it is a constant, low hum that sharpens every time he sees a mother with a stroller or hears
a child’s shout from a sidewalk. He has started arriving early to deliveries to avoid rush-hour
streets, but even that feels like a small, cowardly compensation for what he did.

What finally brings Ravi to a therapist is not the guilt itself but what it is doing to his
marriage. Priya told him last week that she feels like she is living with a stranger. He came home
after that conversation and sat in his van in the driveway for an hour, telling himself he did not
deserve to be let off the hook, that he had no right to feel better. But something in him broke
open, and he searched online for a therapist who might understand. He is terrified that talking will
not change anything—that he will always be the man who caused that injury—but he is more terrified
of losing Priya. So he walks into the clinic with his hands shaking, and when the intake worker asks
what brings him here, all he can say is, “I hurt someone, and I can’t stop thinking it was my
fault.”
