Amara, a 43-year-old IT support specialist living in a Midwestern suburb, carries the aftermath of a
childhood that unfolded in a small, rural house with a locked tool shed behind it. Her mother, her
primary caregiver, would order her into that shed, bolt the door from the outside, and beat her with
a garden hose in the dim, earthy light that filtered through the cracks in the wooden walls. The
smell of damp soil and rusted metal, the sound of her own breath inside the confined space, the
helplessness of being trapped—those sensations lodged themselves in her body long before she had
words for them. Now, decades later, Amara believes that there must have been something inherently
wrong with her that made her mother do those things. She has never shaken the conviction that she is
fundamentally defective, that the abuse happened because of the sort of person she is—weak,
deserving of punishment, somehow broken at the core.

That self-appraisal keeps her body in a permanent state of readiness. Her muscles stay taut, her
shoulders hunched, her jaw clenched; she feels constantly tense, as if danger could appear from any
direction at any moment. At work, in the open-plan IT office, she has trouble concentrating on help-
desk tickets because her attention is split between the computer screen and scanning for sudden
movements or raised voices. Sometimes she startles violently when a coworker taps her shoulder, her
heart hammering even after she realizes the threat is imagined. These false alarms—reacting as if
she is in danger when nothing is actually wrong—only deepen her belief that she is broken. Each
racing heartbeat and trembling hand feels like proof that something is wrong with her, that her body
is betraying her because she is, at bottom, defective. She wonders why she cannot just be normal,
and that question loops back into more tension, more scanning.

Amara avoids what she can. She never talks about the abuse; even a casual mention of childhood by a
colleague makes her chest tighten and her mind go blank, and she has learned to steer conversations
away from any personal history. She also avoids natural settings—parks, wooded trails, even the
small courtyard outside her office building. The sight of trees and open grass pulls her back to
that shed’s doorway, to the moment of being pushed inside. And locked spaces terrify her. She
refuses to use the supply closet at work, and when a server-room door clicked shut behind her last
month, her breath stopped and she had to press her palms against the wall until someone opened it.
The trigger—the locked room—does not just happen to her; it is made more potent by her constant
threat monitoring. Because she is always on edge, the click of a lock or the sight of a forest path
does not merely remind her of the past; it feels like an immediate crisis, a confirmation that
danger is real and present. And at the same time, her negative appraisals amplify the trigger: being
in a locked room reinforces her belief that she is the kind of person who attracts harm, that the
environment itself is punishing her for being who she is.

Over the months, this cycle has tightened. She has begun to take longer routes to avoid passing the
office courtyard. She has stopped volunteering for any task that might require her to retrieve
equipment from the storage closet. Her husband, Jerome, has noticed that she flinches when he closes
the bedroom door at night, and he has asked her repeatedly what is wrong—but she cannot bring
herself to talk about it. The marital strain is a quiet, accumulating weight. What finally brought
Amara to therapy was a panic attack last week in a conference room. The door had a faulty latch, and
when it clicked shut during a meeting, she felt her throat close, her vision tunnel, and she fled
without explanation, leaving her team staring. She sat in the bathroom for twenty minutes,
trembling, certain she was falling apart. Now she sits in a therapist’s office, afraid that if she
tells the story aloud she will be confirming once and for all that she is defective—but also
desperate to stop living inside her own alarm system.
