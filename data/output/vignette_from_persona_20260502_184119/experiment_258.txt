Raj is a 52-year-old man who works as a maid at a mid-range hotel in a suburban city. He is of South
Asian descent, divorced, and lives alone in a small apartment. The event that changed everything
happened two years ago, when he was cleaning a hallway on the second floor. He was pushing a heavy
cart loaded with linens and cleaning supplies when one of the wheels caught on a frayed carpet edge.
The cart tipped suddenly, and a metal shelf bracket—meant to hold extra towels—swung out and struck
an elderly guest who was walking past. She fell backward, hitting her head on the doorframe of her
room. Raj remembers it as a sequence of frozen images: the wheel locking, the bracket swinging, the
woman’s eyes widening, the dull sound of skull against wood. The woman sustained a serious traumatic
brain injury and spent months in rehabilitation. She survived, but with permanent cognitive
deficits.

In the weeks and months after, Raj found that certain things could instantly bring the full memory
back without any warning. The clatter of a dropped tray, the squeak of a cart wheel, the sight of an
elderly guest walking slowly—each could transport him back to that hallway. When that happens, he
sees the images again, and he feels a wave of intense rage. It is a hot, violent anger that
surprises him, directed partly at himself for being careless and partly at the hotel for not fixing
the carpet. But the rage never lasts; it quickly collapses into a hollow, sinking feeling. He has
come to believe that the event happened because of the way he acted—he should have checked the
wheel, he should have slowed down, he should have seen her coming. Raj repeatedly tells himself that
he acted in ways that go against who he is; he was raised to be careful, to never cause harm, and
now that principle feels shattered. Nothing in his life feels meaningful anymore. His work, once a
source of modest pride, now feels like a stage where he might again fail someone else.

The memory of the accident directly feeds these negative appraisals. Each time a trigger throws him
back into one of those snapshot moments, he is flooded not only with the visual image but with the
accompanying rage, and from that rage he instantly pivots to self-blame and a sense of violated
values—a loop that has tightened over the past two years. He has stopped taking breaks near the
elevators because the sound of doors opening reminds him of the moment the guest stepped into the
hallway. He drives a longer route home to avoid the street where the ambulance came. He has not
visited his only son in three months, because his son’s apartment is near the hospital where the
woman was treated. These avoidances have drained his life of connection and variety, though they
feel necessary to prevent the memories.

At work, the hotel management has been understanding, but his supervisors have noticed he no longer
volunteers for extra shifts and sometimes freezes mid-task when a guest walks too close to his cart.
Once, he nearly dropped a bucket of soapy water when an elderly woman asked him for directions, and
he had to excuse himself to the supply closet until his hands stopped shaking. The rage on recall
sometimes surfaces there, in that small room, and he has to press his palms against the wall to keep
from hitting something.

Raj finally came to see a therapist because his ex-wife, with whom he still has occasional contact,
told him that he looked “hollow.” He was not sleeping, had lost weight, and had started drinking a
few beers most evenings to dull the images. What frightens him most now is the feeling that the
accident has erased any future he might have had. He is not suicidal, but he wonders what the point
is of going on when he has proven to himself that he can harm another person without meaning to. He
hopes, vaguely, that talking might help him feel less like a stranger to himself.
