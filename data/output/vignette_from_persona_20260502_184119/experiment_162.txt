Margaret was 76, a radiologist who had spent her career in a bustling city hospital, but she hadn't
always been in North America. Thirty years ago, while volunteering on a medical mission in a war-
torn region, she was caught in a bombing that destroyed a part of the field hospital. She remembered
the dust, the screams, the impossible weight of a collapsed wall pinning a young nurse beside her.
That nurse died before Margaret could free her. In the years since, the event had become a shape she
could not hold—when she tried to deliberately remember it, the details slipped away, leaving only a
wordless pressure in her chest and a deep, recurring shame that she had not done enough. In the
weeks that followed, that shame made her want to avoid anything that could stir feeling at all. She
began to refuse invitations from colleagues, stopped watching news reports, and slowly learned to
fill her evenings with solitaire on her phone, scrolling until her eyes ached, just to keep the
memory from surfacing.

The shame also reshaped her sense of the world as dangerous. Every time she caught herself recalling
the bombing—even in fragments—she felt her stomach tighten and her gaze flick to exits, to
strangers’ hands, to anything that might signal a threat. She had come to believe that if she wasn’t
watching for danger, something terrible would happen again. So Margaret began scanning her
surroundings constantly, avoiding any unfamiliar place where she couldn’t pre-assess the risk. The
grocery store felt manageable only because she knew every aisle; a new coffee shop made her turn
away at the door. Strangers who approached her in the parking lot or in the hospital corridor—even
to ask for directions—made her heart race; she was certain they could be carrying a weapon or a
plan. By now, her scanning had become automatic, but it also had the unintended effect of cueing the
very memory she tried to suppress. The sight of a man in a heavy coat could flash her back to the
armed fighters she’d seen that day, and suddenly the shame would wash over her again, as vivid as
the dust in her lungs.

It had become a loop she could not break. The more she avoided feeling—immersing herself in phone
games, refusing to talk about the event when her husband gently asked, canceling dinners with
friends—the more the memory stayed fragmented and hard to access in words. She experienced it as a
jumble of images and sensations, impossible to put into a coherent story, which only deepened her
shame and her sense of being trapped. At the same time, every hour spent on her phone or avoiding
plans also kept her from facing new situations that might disprove her threat beliefs. She never
tested whether a stranger at the bus stop meant harm, never learned that an unfamiliar street could
be safe. So her threat remained high: she not only scanned but now also avoided unfamiliar places
outright, and the avoidance itself fed her conviction that going somewhere new was too dangerous.

Her work as a radiologist once gave her a sense of control—dark rooms, predictable images—but now
even that setting had become charged. A new technician she didn’t know coming into her office made
her flinch. She had started taking extra time to walk the perimeter of the radiology suite before
her shift, checking for anything unusual. The hospital administration noticed she was late to
meetings and reluctant to cover shifts in other wings. She had begun to think about early
retirement, though she had never made a plan for it; making any plan for the future felt like
inviting fate. Her husband, still patient, had stopped asking about holidays abroad. The only future
she could imagine was another evening alone with her phone.

What finally brought Margaret to the clinic was a panic attack in the hospital cafeteria when a loud
tray dropped and she found herself on the floor, shaking, unable to breathe. She could no longer
pretend the scanning and avoidance were just caution. She told the intake clinician she was afraid
of getting worse—of losing her job, her marriage, her mind. But more than anything, she was afraid
that if she let herself truly remember the bombing, she would never stop feeling the shame.
