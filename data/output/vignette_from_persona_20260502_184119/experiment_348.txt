Leilani, a 46-year-old singer and musician from a Pacific Islander community, nearly died two years
ago from a sudden, severe case of pneumonia that landed her in the ICU on a ventilator. The memory
of gasping for air, the antiseptic burn of the hospital corridors, and the cold pressure of the
oxygen mask are still vivid — but it is not the event itself that haunts her. It is what her body
does now. A slight tightness in her chest during a rehearsal makes her freeze; the scent of bleach
in a public restroom sends her heart racing; a routine blood pressure check at the pharmacy triggers
a wave of dread that leaves her shaking. She has come to believe that if she does not stay
hypervigilant, her body might betray her again.

Leilani wakes several times each night, sitting up abruptly to check whether her breathing is
steady, even before her conscious mind has caught up with the panic. "My body reacts before I have
time to think," she says, and this frightens her most of all. She now feels safe only in a handful
of places: her own home, a small practice room at the community center, and her husband's car.
Anywhere outside that circle is potential danger. Because her heightened threat system is constantly
scanning for signs of physical vulnerability, she notices every slight shortness of breath — a
sensation most people would ignore becomes, for her, a signal of impending collapse. The threat also
feeds her growing self-doubt. "I can't trust that I will do the right thing anymore," she confesses.
She sees herself as fundamentally inadequate — a lousy coper who cannot handle what others manage
without effort. Every time her body alarms before her mind, she takes it as proof that she is weak.

These negative appraisals drive her maladaptive strategies. Believing she is a bad coper, Leilani
replays the illness over and over: "If only I had gone to the doctor the first day I coughed, life
would be different." She tells herself she deserves to suffer for not taking better care of her
health — a form of punishment thinking that keeps her trapped in shame. And because she is convinced
she cannot cope with medical settings, she now avoids them entirely. She canceled her annual
physical, refused a follow-up pulmonary test, and asks her husband to pick up her prescriptions so
she does not have to step into the clinic. The threat itself also directly reinforces her avoidance:
the hyperarousal that jolts her awake at night makes even the thought of a hospital feel unbearable,
so she stays away. And her limited safe zone has narrowed her daily life to the point where she no
longer agrees to perform at venues with strong cleaning smells or crowded backstage areas.

Leilani is a professional musician, but her world has shrunk. She used to sing at community
festivals and wedding receptions; now she only practices in her basement, where she controls the air
and the smells. Her husband has grown worried — she has lost income, and she has stopped seeing
friends who might want to meet at a café that smells of hand sanitizer. What finally brought her to
therapy was a rehearsal last month: a short duet made her feel breathless, she became convinced she
was suffocating, and she fled the stage. "I couldn't even finish a single song," she whispered. She
is terrified that her career is over, and she is equally afraid that seeking help will confirm once
and for all that she is a bad coper. But even more than that, she hopes to one day walk into a
doctor's office without her body screaming danger, and to sing again without the fear that each
breath might be her last.
