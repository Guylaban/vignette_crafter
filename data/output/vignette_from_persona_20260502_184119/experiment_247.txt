Sofia was twenty-three when a bomb tore through the warehouse where she stacked pallets. She had
been on the loading dock when the pressure wave threw her against a stack of crates. The next hours
existed only as shards: the crushing weight on her legs, a ringing that swallowed all other sound,
the face of a coworker smeared with dust before the light vanished. Even now, a year later, she
cannot recall being pulled out. There are entire stretches she cannot account for, and when she
tries to fill them she is met with a wall of silence or, worse, a surge of rage so sudden it leaves
her breathless. Describing what happened to the therapist who first interviewed her proved
impossible—she knew the facts, but the words would not come; her throat closed as if the blast had
stolen her voice along with the memory.

In the months that followed, Sofia came to see the world as a place where danger could appear
without warning. She could feel it in her bones: the world was dangerous, and her own body was no
longer something she could trust. Her legs still ached when the weather shifted, and her heart would
pound at a door slamming, at a car backfiring, at any sudden movement in her peripheral vision. She
began scanning the warehouse floor constantly—checking aisles, exits, the faces of coworkers who
approached too quickly. Eye contact felt like a vulnerability she could not afford; she would drop
her gaze and focus on the concrete floor, on the boxes she moved hour after hour. At night, she
found it harder to relax, often staying awake until exhaustion took over, because darkness made the
scanning pointless and the threat felt closer.

The more Sofia scanned and avoided, the more convinced she became that vigilance was necessary—that
if she stopped, something terrible would happen. Her hypervigilance did not ease; it fed itself. A
sudden movement from a forklift operator would make her flinch, and that flinch would trigger a
flash of memory: the pressure wave, the ringing, the rage. In those moments she could feel herself
slipping, and she learned to dissociate deliberately—to blank out, to leave her body staring at the
boxes while she retreated somewhere inside that was quiet. But the dissociation also left her
disoriented, and the next sudden noise would startle her even more, confirming that she had to stay
on guard.

At work, her habits became visible. Her supervisor noticed she never looked up during meetings and
that she seemed to startle when anyone approached from behind. Sofia began taking her breaks alone
in the stairwell, where she could watch both doors. One evening, a coworker touched her shoulder to
get her attention, and Sofia spun around with such force that she knocked over a stack of empty
pallets. The noise brought back the ringing, and she dissociated for several minutes, standing
frozen while the rage at the coworker—at herself—pulsed through her chest. That night she could not
sleep at all, certain that the warehouse would not be safe tomorrow, that she would have to be even
more vigilant.

The cycle tightened. Her fear of sudden movements made her avoid the areas where forklifts operated,
and she requested a reassignment to a quieter section. Her manager granted it, but the new section
had a high ceiling with exposed pipes that sometimes clanked, and every clank sent her into a
startle response that left her shaky for an hour. She began taking longer to complete her tasks.
Twice she called in sick simply because she could not face the walk from the parking lot to the dock
doors, where the morning light hit the same angle it had the day of the blast. The memory gaps
themselves became a source of dread: not knowing what had happened meant she could not predict what
might trigger it, so she treated every ordinary sound as a potential bomb.

What finally brought Sofia to the clinic was a panic attack in the middle of her shift: a box
slipped from her hands, the *thud* sent her into a flashback, and she dissociated so completely that
she walked out of the building without clocking out. She wandered the parking lot for forty minutes
before a security guard found her standing behind a dumpster, unable to explain where she was. The
warehouse referred her for a fitness-for-duty evaluation. Sofia is terrified that she will lose her
job—that she will be seen as unstable, that the world will prove her appraisal true. But she is also
exhausted. She wants to stop scanning, to stop flinching, to stop feeling the rage that surges
without warning. She wants to trust her body again, and to look someone in the eye without feeling
the ground give way beneath her.
