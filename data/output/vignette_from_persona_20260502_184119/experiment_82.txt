completely real, as if she were still there. Every detail was razor-sharp, and she could feel the
grit on her skin.

noises, and left parties early. But what really took hold was a growing sense that she no longer

began to distrust people she had known for years, convinced they were hiding something—maybe even a
capacity for the same violence she had witnessed. She had come to believe that the world was a place
where a normal evening could turn into a nightmare, and that belief gave her no reason to hope for

her again, and the memory would pull her under. And when the memory overwhelmed her—when she could
smell the scorched wiring and hear the wet sound of someone coughing—it only confirmed what she
already knew: that person she had been before the bombing was gone, and there was nothing to look
forward to.

bandmates looked at her with concern, but she could not explain. She had come to believe that they,
too, were not what they seemed.

By the time she turned 37, Jenna had stopped performing altogether. She gave up her regular gigs and

chest. What finally brought her to a therapist was a week of near-daily flashbacks, each one

back to the music, and perhaps to herself.
