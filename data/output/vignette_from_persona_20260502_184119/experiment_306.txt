**Clinical Vignette**

The event that shattered Raymond’s life took place twenty-three years ago, during a business trip to
a country he would rather not name. He was detained by armed men who accused him of smuggling
information. For three days they held him in a concrete room with no windows, questioning him,
beating him, and at one point forcing him to watch as they did the same to his colleague. Raymond
still recalls the sound of the door bolting each time they left—a metallic scrape that locked him
into a darkness he could not escape. What he remembers most vividly is the face of the man who gave
the orders, a man with a scar above his left eyebrow, and the weight of a boot on his chest. But
other parts are missing: he cannot remember how he finally got free, only that he ended up in a
hospital, and that his colleague did not survive. That blank spot haunts him nearly as much as the
clear, brutal images he cannot shake.

In the years since, Raymond has built a quiet life—marrying a patient woman, raising two children,
working as a bus driver for the city transit authority. He thought the past was sealed. But six
months ago, a passenger boarded his bus at dusk, a tall man with a similar build and a scar above
his left eyebrow. Raymond’s heart slammed against his ribs, his hands went numb on the steering
wheel, and for a moment he could not breathe. The passenger was just another commuter, but that
split second of terror cracked something open. Now Raymond finds himself listening—constantly. At
night, he lies awake in bed, straining to hear the sound of the front door being forced, or
footsteps on the porch. His wife has asked why he gets up three times to check the locks, but he
cannot explain. He has come to believe that if he lets his guard down, something terrible will
happen again. This belief is rooted in a gnawing shame: he is certain he was weak during the
torture, that he should have fought harder, that his failure to protect his colleague makes him a
coward. He will never forgive himself for not doing more. That guilt eats at him, and it feeds his
vigilance. Every creak of the house at night, every unexpected knock on the bus door, feels like
proof that danger is waiting and that he is not strong enough to face it.

The memory fragments make everything worse. When he tries to piece together what happened, his mind
offers only the scarred face, the boot, the bolting door, and then a blank. That incompleteness
convinces him he is blocking something else he did wrong, some moment of cowardice he cannot bear to
recall. And when he does recall the clear parts—the terror of the man’s face—the same raw fear
floods back, as if he were still on that concrete floor. That terror drives him to scan every
passenger for scars, every bus stop for signs of ambush. His vigilance, in turn, strengthens his
self-contempt: *See, you’re still scared, still weak.* He has started taking only day shifts,
avoiding the evening runs that once paid better. His supervisor has noticed the requests and the
tension in his driving, but Raymond shrugs it off. He cannot afford to lose this job, yet he feels
it slipping.

Finally, last week, he yelled at a passenger who tapped his shoulder to ask for a transfer—a minor
thing, but the man’s sudden touch sent Raymond spinning into a full-blown panic attack at the wheel.
He pulled over, shaking, and knew he could not keep going alone. His wife found him that night
sitting in the dark living room, watching the front door, and she insisted he call the clinic. What
Raymond fears most now is that talking about the torture will bring back that incomplete, terrifying
memory in full, and that he will finally see exactly what he did wrong. He is not sure he can live
with that answer. But he is even more afraid of the next time a scar catches his eye and he freezes,
mid-route, with a bus full of people depending on him.
