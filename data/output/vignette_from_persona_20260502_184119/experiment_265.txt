Roberto, a 75-year-old QA engineer originally from Mexico, still works part-time testing software
from the home office he shares with his wife of forty-eight years. He came to therapy because, as he
put it, “I’m not myself anymore, and I’m making my wife nervous.” The trouble began seven years ago
when a wildfire swept through their suburban neighborhood. Roberto was at the office that afternoon;
by the time he reached home, flames had already consumed the garage and part of the roof. He and his
wife escaped with only the clothes they wore, but he has never forgiven himself for not being there
sooner, for not grabbing the box of family photographs. “I should have known the wind was shifting,”
he says, his voice tightening. “I should have left work earlier.”

Now any whiff of smoke—a neighbor’s barbecue, a candle left burning—immediately pulls Roberto into a
tight spiral. The moment his nostrils catch that scent, his heart pounds and his hands start to
shake. In the same breath he begins to replay the drive home, thinking of every turn he could have
taken faster, of the minutes he wasted in traffic. That physical reactivity, the sudden racing
chest, itself becomes a cue: he starts scanning the room for smoke, checking the smoke detector,
looking out the window for haze on the horizon. A similar weather pattern—a hot, dry wind out of the
Santa Anas—sets the same cycle in motion. Roberto has come to believe that if he stays watchful
enough, he can prevent a repeat. Yet the vigilance exhausts him and leaves him more sensitive to the
faintest trigger; he now notices smells others do not, and he startles at the crackle of a fireplace
on television.

After a triggering event, Roberto tries to push the memories out of his mind. But suppression rarely
works; instead, his mind circles back to how the disaster could have been prevented. He dwells on
what he did wrong—his late departure, his failure to keep the car packed with essentials—and these
ruminations, in turn, heighten his physical tension. His wife has observed that on days when he is
especially hard on himself, he flinches at loud noises and jumps if she approaches from behind. The
hypervigilance feeds the rumination: being constantly on guard means he registers every small
sensory change, which then gives him more to replay and more to blame himself for missing.

At work, Roberto tests software updates for a logistics company. He used to enjoy the quiet
concentration the job required, but now a coworker’s lunch reheated in the break room
microwave—popcorn burnt—can send him reeling for the rest of the afternoon. He has started closing
his office door and keeping the windows shut, a habit that has raised questions from his team lead.
He avoids walking past the fire extinguisher in the hallway. He has also begun checking the local
weather forecast three times a day, watching for “red flag” warnings, and that monitoring itself
becomes a trigger: each windy day resurrects the guilt he carries about the shifting wind he should
have noticed.

The cycle that has tightened over the past year is the one he now struggles to name: a smell
triggers his body’s alarm, his mind fills with self-blame, the blame makes him more alert to the
next smell, and the alertness makes the next reaction stronger. He no longer invites his
grandchildren over for fear their curiosity might lead them near the kitchen stove. His wife, who
once admired his practical calm, now finds him irritable and withdrawn. Roberto sought help only
after his primary care physician ran a routine cardiac test and found nothing wrong, but the
pounding in his chest continued. What frightens him most, he says, is that he cannot out-think the
smoke—it finds him anyway. He is hoping that talking about it will somehow loosen the grip, though
he worries that doing so will only bring the fire closer.
