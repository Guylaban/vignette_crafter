The toxic exposure happened eighteen months ago, but for Mai, the memory had never settled into the
past. She had been prepping her workstation at the tattoo studio, mixing a new batch of pigment from
a supplier she had used for years, when a chemical reaction she had not anticipated sent a plume of
acrid fumes straight into her face. Within seconds her throat had seized, her lungs had burned, and
she could not draw a full breath. A fellow artist called 911, and Mai remembered the ride to the
emergency room as a blur of sirens and the terrifying sensation of not being in control of her own
body. At the hospital, things only got worse. She was placed on supplemental oxygen, and the staff
came in and out of her room constantly—checking her vitals, adjusting the nasal cannula, asking the
same questions over and over. She recalled feeling propped up on the bed like a specimen, unable to
move without permission, her arms prickling from the IV line they had inserted despite her saying
she did not want one. The room smelled sharply of antiseptic and hand sanitizer, a smell that seemed
to coat the inside of her nose and stay there for days after she was discharged.

Back at home, the physical symptoms faded, but something else had taken root. Mai found herself
hyperaware of scents she had never registered before. The hand sanitizer pump at the grocery store
entrance made her stomach drop. A whiff of rubbing alcohol from a first-aid kit sent a flash of heat
through her chest. She started buying unscented soap and asking her husband to stop using the spray
disinfectant he liked in the bathroom. When her mother called to say she had been hospitalized
briefly for a minor procedure, Mai could not bring herself to visit. The idea of walking into a room
that smelled like that again made her feel like she could not get enough air.

The studio had always been her sanctuary, but now it contained its own version of the same smell.
The alcohol wipes, the sterilizing solution, the barrier film—she could not escape them. She learned
to tolerate them by holding her breath and looking away, but the work itself began to feel
different. Mai had always prided herself on collaborating with clients, listening to their ideas,
incorporating their vision. Now, when a client leaned over the chair to watch her needle work, she
felt a tightness in her jaw that had nothing to do with concentration. She had come to interpret
being watched as a kind of surveillance, as though they were waiting for her to make a mistake, or
worse, trying to catch her doing something wrong. She began positioning her body to block their
view, angling the chair away, asking them to sit back and relax—phrases that used to come easily but
now felt like small negotiations for space.

The same discomfort surfaced whenever a client pushed back on a design choice or asked for a change
mid-session. Mai had always been flexible, happy to adjust. But now, being told what to do—even
politely—stirred something hot and resistant in her chest. She had started to refuse certain
requests outright, citing hygiene or safety reasons, though she knew deep down that her refusal was
about something else. She could not stand the feeling of being forced, even when the force was just
a customer's preference. Several regulars had stopped booking with her, and her income had dropped
by nearly a third over the past six months. Her husband had asked her what was going on, and she had
told him she was fine, because she did not know how to explain it. What was there to say? That a
chemical spill had rearranged her entire sense of safety inside her own shop? That she could not
tell the difference anymore between a client watching her work and a doctor watching her breathe?

What finally brought Mai to seek help was a single moment. A new client came in for a consultation,
sat down in the chair, and the first thing she did was pull out a small bottle of hand sanitizer
from her purse and offer it to Mai. The gesture was kind, meant to be friendly. Mai looked at the
bottle, caught the faint antiseptic scent, and felt her throat close. She excused herself to the
bathroom, locked the door, and stood there with her hands on the sink, trembling. She was not sure
anymore how much more of this she could manage on her own. She was afraid that if she did not find a
way through it, she would lose her business, her steady sense of herself, and the one thing that had
always felt like her own: her art.
