One afternoon, a colleague clapped him on the back in a briefing room. Jabari’s body instantly
locked—the same frozen posture from when he was seven—and the snapshot of his mother’s eyes returned
with a wave of grief so sharp he had to excuse himself. The memory reinforced his belief that he
couldn’t rely on himself; if a friendly pat could incapacitate him, how could he handle a real
threat? That self-doubt drove him to check every corner, every shadow, every unfamiliar street, and
the more he checked, the more he noticed potential weapons and suspicious movements, which triggered
new memories and deepened his sense that he was losing control.

The cycle tightened over weeks. His hypervigilance turned ordinary interactions into triggers: a
shopper acting oddly made him replay the moment his father’s hand connected; the hush in an elevator
brought back the silence before the first blow. Each trigger revived the memory, and each memory
strengthened his helplessness. He began avoiding unfamiliar parts of his beat, asking for
reassignment to the same two blocks he knew, but even there he couldn’t shake the feeling that he
was about to be caught off guard.

What finally brought him to seek help was a night when he pulled over a car for a minor infraction.
The driver’s sudden movement toward the glove box made Jabari’s mind go blank—he later realized he
had frozen, just like when he was seven, while his hand rested inches from his service weapon. He
drove home shaking, certain he was going crazy, unable to trust himself on duty. In the clinic, he
said he wasn’t sure what he was most afraid of: that he would freeze again, or that he would finally
stop caring and react the wrong way. Either way, he wanted the snapshots to stop. "After that image
surfaced, even ordinary sounds—a car backfiring, a raised voice on the radio—felt like warnings that
the past was about to repeat itself."
