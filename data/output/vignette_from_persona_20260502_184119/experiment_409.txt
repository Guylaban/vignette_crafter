longer tell his own. The trouble had begun two years earlier, when his wife, Naomi, suffered a
massive stroke during a routine morning. He had been in the next room, heard a strange thud, and
found her on the kitchen floor, her eyes frozen open, her mouth making sounds that were not words.
In the ambulance, a paramedic’s rapid, incomprehensible chatter filled the space, a stream of
syllables that blurred into noise. Kwame could not make sense of it; all he could taste was the
metallic tang of his own panic, sharp and coppery on his tongue. Naomi survived, but the experience
carved a crevice in Kwame’s mind that kept returning.

In the months that followed, he noticed that certain moments would pull him back into that
ambulance. A colleague at the news desk talking about a retirement party—any mention of the
future—would suddenly surface an image of Naomi’s unresponsive face, and with it the taste of that
metallic panic. When his editor assigned him to cover a story about a family who had lost a child,
Kwame found himself unable to write the lead; the words “loss” and “loved ones” triggered a cascade
of memory that he could not articulate. The worst was the incomprehensible chatter: a crowded
newsroom, overlapping voices in a language he did not understand, and he was back in that ambulance,
the paramedic’s words meaningless, and he could not find the words to explain to anyone what had
happened—not to the doctor, not to Naomi later, not even to himself. In the middle of a sentence, he
would lose the thread, knowing exactly what had occurred but unable to speak it. The taste would
come first, then the failure of language.

As a journalist, Kwame prided himself on clarity and precision. Now he avoided any assignment that
involved future plans, grief, or crowded briefings where multiple voices overlapped. He stopped
attending the weekly editorial meeting because the chatter felt like a trigger he could not control.
At home, he steered conversations away from Naomi’s recovery milestones, especially any talk of what
they would do “next year.” He knew this was irrational—his wife was fine now—but talking about the
future seemed to invite the memory, and the memory brought the taste and the shame of not being able
to explain.

The connection between these triggers and the memory became a quiet loop. The more he avoided
future-talk, the more the loss themes seemed to find him in the news. The more he heard
incomprehensible chatter, the more vividly the taste returned. His editor noticed his declining
output; Kwame had stopped pitching stories. His marriage grew strained—Naomi wanted to plan a
vacation, but every “what if” felt dangerous. What frightened Kwame most was the sensation of losing
his mind: the certainty that he knew what had happened, yet the words dissolved when he tried to
tell someone.

Last week, after a colleague casually described a bereavement interview, Kwame felt the taste rise
again and walked out of the newsroom without speaking. He sat in his car, unable to describe where
he was going or why. That evening, he told Naomi he needed help. What he hoped for was to find a way
to hold the memory without being held by it; what he feared was that his own story, the one he could
not tell, might be the one that finally silenced him.
