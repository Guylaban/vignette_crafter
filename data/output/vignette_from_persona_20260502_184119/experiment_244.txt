Priya was working the night shift when the explosion tore through the emergency department—a man-
made disaster, later classified as a deliberate gas-line rupture targeting the hospital. In the
moments after, she moved on autopilot, triaging the wounded, but the event itself has never settled
into a coherent story inside her mind. What she carries instead are disconnected pieces: the sound
of glass cascading, a colleague’s face lit by fire, the weight of a tourniquet in her hand—images
that come not in sequence but as isolated snapshots that refuse to join. There is no narrative
thread. She sometimes wonders if she actually lived through it, because the memory feels so extreme,
so nightmarish, that her mind rebels against accepting it as real. *Did that really happen to me?*
The question surfaces daily, and she finds no answer. When the fragments begin to surface—at work,
in the sterile quiet of the medication room, or at home when she tries to sleep—she catches herself
pushing the memory away before she can see it through to the end. She feels a wall go up, a reflex,
and then the pieces scatter again, leaving her with only the sense that something terrible happened
but without the full picture.

As a nurse, Priya’s days are spent in the same corridors where the disaster unfolded. The hospital
was rebuilt, the walls repainted, but every shift she is surrounded by the geography of that night.
The memory intrudes without warning—a sudden flash of the shattered triage desk, the smell of smoke
layered over antiseptic—but it never stays long enough for her to grasp it. She has learned to
anticipate the blocking, to feel the familiar resistance rising before the images can complete
themselves, and she has come to accept that her mind will not let her know the whole story. She does
not call it avoidance; she calls it survival. But the fragments remain, stubborn and disorienting,
and with them the persistent conviction that the experience belongs to someone else, that it could
not possibly have happened to her.

Months passed. Priya continued working, partly because staying away felt impossible and partly
because the hospital routine gave her something to hold onto. She did not develop the hypervigilance
her colleagues described, nor the nightmares she read about online. What she has is the
memory—disjointed, unreal, perpetually blocked—and the quiet unease it generates. She finds herself
startled not by alarms or loud noises but by the internal sensation of a memory that refuses to come
into focus. It is like standing at the edge of a dark room and knowing something is there but being
unable to step inside.

What finally brought her to a clinic was a conversation with a coworker who mentioned the
anniversary of the explosion. Priya felt a familiar jolt of unreality, followed by an almost
immediate blanking of thought—the fragments scurrying away. She realized she had never once said the
words *I was there* out loud without feeling like she was lying. She is not afraid of the disaster
itself anymore; she is afraid that she will never know what actually happened to her, that the story
will remain a set of broken shards she cannot assemble. She hopes, vaguely, that talking about it
might make it feel real—but she also fears that if it does become real, it will overwhelm her. For
now, she sits in the therapist’s office and waits, uncertain whether the memory will stay long
enough to speak.
