instantly. The scene seared into her: the heat, the screams, the acrid smoke, and the sight of
crimson spreading across the white floor. In the months that followed, she was diagnosed with a
chronic lower back injury from the impact, and she carried a deep, unspoken guilt about surviving.

The first symptoms were subtle. Elena began waking from nightmares in which she was back in that
chapel—she could hear the ringing, feel the debris hitting her skin. The dream residue, a lingering
sense of unreality, would persist for hours, making her feel unsafe even in her own cell. At the
same time, any sharp physical pain in her back would immediately flood her with the same
overwhelming fear and horror she had felt that morning. The pain became a trigger that seemed to
unlock the entire traumatic memory uncontrollably, as if her body were reliving the moment. She
found that when she tried to deliberately recall what happened—to piece it together for the police
or for her own understanding—the details were fragmented and hazy, yet the memory would force its
way into her mind unbidden, complete with the full sensory onslaught: the smell, the sound, the
sight of blood.

Elena started to avoid what she could. Crowded places like the town market became unbearable; she
would see a crowd and immediately feel her chest tighten with fear, and she would retreat to the
convent. More subtly, she began avoiding emotional intimacy with her fellow sisters. She had come to
believe that getting close to anyone meant risking another loss, and that the only way to be safe
was to stay distant. When she did allow herself to be with others, her mind would go over and over
the details of the bombing, trying to make sense of why it happened and why she survived—a loop of
analysis that only deepened her shame and grief. This overanalyzing did not bring clarity; it
intensified her threat perception, making her feel that danger lurked everywhere.

Over time, Elena became convinced that safety existed only inside her own room and the chapel, and
that anywhere else required constant vigilance. She always scanned for escape routes before she
could feel comfortable in a room, even in the convent’s refectory. The need to plan exits was so
strong that it kept her hyperaware of her body, and then the physical pain from her back would
spike, pulling her back into threat mode. Her world narrowed: she stopped attending community
events, stopped going to the local clinic for fear of seeing blood, and stopped talking to the
younger nuns who reached out to her. She became certain that the only way to control her fear was to
control her environment completely.

But the cost was high. Her Mother Superior noticed that Elena rarely left her room and seemed
hollowed out. In the last months, Elena found herself unable to sleep for more than a few hours; the

frightened her most was the thought that she might never be able to feel at peace again—that the
blood and the guilt would always follow her, no matter how many doors she locked.
