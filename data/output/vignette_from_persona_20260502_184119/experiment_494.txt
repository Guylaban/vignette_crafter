For years, Marcus had trusted heights the way other men trusted level ground. Working on a high-rise
frame in downtown Phoenix, he knew the rhythm—the clang of rebar, the bite of dust, the way your
boots found purchase on a beam even when the wind tried to push you off. Then came the morning a new
crew member, a kid barely twenty, lost his footing on the same scaffold Marcus had just crossed.
Marcus saw him fall—the surprise on the boy’s face, the sickening impact three stories down. He
smelled the blood before he saw it, a metallic sweetness that flooded his nostrils. And he tasted it
too, the copper tang of his own bitten tongue, as if his body had answered the sight with a mouthful
of its own terror. For weeks after, Marcus could not shake the image: the kid’s body, the spreading
stain, the silence. The smells came back unbidden—sometimes the hydraulic oil on the job site
twisted into that same metallic sweetness, and he would gag. And with the smells came the taste, so
vivid he would scrape his tongue against his teeth to prove it wasn’t real.

Those memories did not just haunt him; they rewired what he believed. Marcus had always thought the
world made sense: work hard, watch your step, and you stay safe. Now that belief lay shattered. Bad
things happened for no reason, and he could not stop them—helplessness settled into his bones like a
cold draft. Worse, he began to feel that he was inadequate: he should have shouted a warning,
grabbed the kid, done something. He had not. Those appraisals made the memories return more often.
Every time he pictured the fall, he felt his own uselessness, and that feeling seemed to drag the
memory back with even sharper detail, as if his mind were punishing him by re-running the tape.

To survive, Marcus developed strategies that at first seemed rational. He suppressed any flicker of
anger—angry at the foreman for poor safety lines, angry at the kid for being careless, angry at
himself for freezing. Anger, he decided, was dangerous; it had probably made him miss the warning
signs. So he never allowed himself to feel or express it. When the memories threatened to surface on
site, he deliberately tried to leave his body, blanking out until the moment passed. It worked for a
few minutes, but afterward he felt hollow and disconnected from the crew. At night, when sleep felt
impossible, he drove to a casino on the edge of town. Gambling gave him something to focus on—the
spin of the wheel, the flash of the screen—and the occasional win brought a rush of excitement that
drowned out the taste of blood. He told himself it was just a hobby.

The negative appraisals drove these strategies. Helplessness told him there was no point in
confronting the anger, so he buried it. Inadequacy whispered that he was too weak to handle the
memories head-on, so he dissociated instead. And each strategy, in turn, made the memories more
vivid. Suppressing anger left him tense, and tension seemed to trigger the smells. Gambling kept him
awake until 3 a.m., and the next day exhaustion made the intrusive images sharper. Dissociating left
gaps in his awareness, and those gaps—moments of nothing—became new blanks for the memory to fill
with the image of the kid’s fall.

Over months, the cycle tightened. At work, Marcus avoided the east side of the site where the
accident happened, taking longer routes that his supervisor began to notice. He stopped eating lunch
with the crew because the smell of the grill reminded him of the blood. At home, he withdrew from
his wife, refusing to talk about what he had seen, until the silence and his unexplained absences at
the casino drove her to leave. He was divorced within the year. He told himself it was for the best;
he was inadequate, after all. Who would want to be with someone who could not even save a kid?

Now, nearly a year later, Marcus shows up to the clinic after his foreman threatened to fire him for
the third time. He is tired of the memories, tired of the haunted feeling, tired of waking up with
the taste of blood in his mouth. He is also terrified: if he stops dissociating, what will he feel?
If he lets the anger out, what will he do? He says he just wants to get through the day without
seeing that kid’s face. He does not say it, but what he fears most is that the world really is as
broken as he believes—and that he will never escape the scaffold.
