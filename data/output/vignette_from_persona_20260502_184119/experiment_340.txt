Sarah, a 33-year-old paramedic, still replays the moment in fragments. Eighteen months ago, she
responded to a middle-aged man in anaphylactic shock. In the chaos of the back of the rig, she
miscalculated the epinephrine dose and injected it into an artery instead of muscle. Within seconds,
the man suffered a stroke that left him partially paralyzed. She knows it was her hand that did
it—her mistake, her fault. The memory does not come as a story; it comes as a series of snapshots:
the man’s terrified eyes, the syringe plunger, the sudden flatline on the monitor. Each piece
arrives without warning, and when it does, the same visceral terror she felt that night floods back.
She never knows when the next fragment will hit—while she is driving to a call, while she is trying
to fall asleep, while she is sitting at the dinner table with her husband.

To hold the terror at bay, Sarah has developed a set of strategies that feel necessary in the
moment. When a memory fragment intrudes, she reaches for a drink as soon as she is off shift, or she
scrolls through her phone for hours, or she turns the television volume up until the noise drowns
the images. She refuses to talk about the event with anyone—not her colleagues, not her husband, not
even the mandatory debriefing counselor. What she has not realized is that each time she
deliberately pushes the memory away, it seems to come back stronger the next day, as if the act of
suppression gives the snapshots more power. The same alcohol that deadens the terror in the evening
leaves her restless and on edge the following morning, her heart racing at the smallest unexpected
sound.

The terror on recall has also reshaped how she experiences the world as dangerous. She finds herself
scanning the ambulance bay for signs of emergency, her eyes darting to every phone that rings, every
shout. At night, she is worse. She cannot relax in the dark; she checks the locks on the front door
three times, lies awake listening to the house creak, feeling certain that something terrible is
about to happen. This hypervigilance, in turn, feeds the memories. When she startles at a sound—a
car backfiring, a drawer slamming—her mind immediately jumps to the monitor alarm from that night,
and a new snapshot of the man’s face appears. It is a vicious circle: the more she tries to stay
alert to prevent another catastrophe, the more she is reminded of the first one.

Her concentration has suffered badly. She finds herself losing track of equipment inventories on the
rig, misplacing her radio, failing to follow conversations in the station. This only deepens her
conviction that she is a danger to her patients. She has started to avoid certain kinds of
calls—anything involving anaphylaxis or epinephrine—and when the dispatcher sends her near the
intersection where the incident occurred, her pulse spikes and she has to pull over. Her husband has
grown frustrated with her distance and her refusal to explain why she flinches at every mention of
work. A month ago, he told her that if she did not get help, he could not continue living like this.

She has finally come to see a therapist, reluctantly, at her husband’s insistence. What she hopes
for is to stop the snapshots from coming, or at least to make them less terrifying. What she fears
most is that talking about it will bring the full terror back, and that she will lose her career or
her marriage. But she is also exhausted from the constant watchfulness and the effort of pretending
she is fine. She is willing to try, even though she does not believe it will work.
