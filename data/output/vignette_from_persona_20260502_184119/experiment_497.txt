each evening. The police were called, but the behavior only turned more menacing—phone calls at
night, a tracking device under her car, a message that read *I know where you live*. After six weeks
of this, he was arrested, but Leilani’s sense of safety had already collapsed.

In the months that followed, she came to believe that the world had fundamentally changed. Every
street corner felt like a potential hiding place, every stranger’s glance a threat. She told herself
that her life had been destroyed by what happened—that the person she used to be, the one who walked
home without checking over her shoulder, was gone for good. That belief hollowed her out. She began
to feel that she had to be on guard all the time, scanning doorways, rehearsing escape routes, her
body wired even in her own apartment. And because the world felt so dangerous, she started to
withdraw. She avoided going out after work, cancelled plans with friends, and eventually stopped
leaving her apartment except for the bare minimum—the grocery store at six in the morning when the
aisles were empty. She also avoided any situation that might make her feel happy or hopeful, as if
the faintest spark of ease would invite disaster. A compliment, a funny video, the offer of a coffee
from a coworker—she turned them all away, convinced that relaxing her vigilance was a betrayal of
what she now knew about the world.

The avoidance grew into something darker. Leilani began telling herself that she deserved to suffer
for what happened. It started as a quiet thought—*you should have seen it coming, you should have
been smarter*—and hardened into a daily ritual of self-punishment. She would replay the moments
before the stalking escalated and blame herself for not recognizing the signs. The thought comforted
her in a twisted way: if it was her fault, then maybe she could learn to avoid it next time. But the
comfort never lasted. Instead, she found herself punishing herself for small lapses—for sleeping
through an alarm, for eating a piece of chocolate, for laughing at a colleague’s joke. Pleasure felt
dangerous, and suffering felt just.

At work, her performance began to slip. Auditing demands meticulous attention, but Leilani struggled
to concentrate when every sound—a door opening, a phone ringing—sent her into a spiral of
hypervigilance. She requested to work from home, but her supervisor noticed the drop in productivity

from her family, who lived in a different state. She had no partner; she could not imagine letting

would only prove how thoroughly she deserved to suffer.
