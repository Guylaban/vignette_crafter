Erik, a 45-year-old journalist, had been reporting from a conflict zone when a militia ambushed his
convoy and took him prisoner. For six days he was held in a concrete room, blindfolded, his hands
bound behind his back with a nylon cord that forced his head forward, putting a constant strain on
his neck. His captors drank cheap rum throughout the day, the sour alcohol smell filling the space
each time they leaned close to shove him or bark orders. He was released in a prisoner swap, but the
terror did not end.

In the weeks that followed, the memory of the captivity returned as an unwelcome film that played
without warning. The moment he tried to stretch his neck after a long day at his desk, the phantom
ache of that forced posture would bloom, and with it the full sensory replay—the blindfold’s
pressure, the rough hand on his shoulder, the rum-laced breath. He could be walking through the
newsroom and suddenly catch the faintest whiff of liquor from a colleague’s coffee cup, and his
throat would clench as though the room had shrunk to that cold concrete space. The memory did not
simply visit; it brought the physical triggers along, a dark package that opened itself against his
will.

His hypervigilance grew steadily. The clatter of a dropped keyboard, the slam of a filing cabinet,
any unexpected sound sent him into a state of high alert. These startle responses soon began feeding
the memories: a car backfiring in the street outside his home would throw him headlong into the
image of the militia shouting, the cord digging into his wrists. The more he tried to stay
watchful—scanning bar crowds for the shine of a bottle, turning his head at every sudden noise—the
more the memory hijacked those moments. His constant readiness for danger also amplified the
triggers themselves. When his wife touched his arm from behind, he flinched so hard she pulled back
in hurt; the physical contact landed as a threat, not a gesture. A whiff of rum outside a restaurant
was no longer a passing scent but an alert that set his heart racing. Hypervigilance made triggers
feel inescapable because he was always scanning for them, and the scanning kept him raw.

At work, Erik began to avoid assignments that might take him near any hint of the conflict region.
He turned down a profile on a foreign correspondent who had also been held captive, claiming
scheduling conflicts. In the newsroom, he shifted his desk to a corner where fewer people walked
behind him, and he asked colleagues not to tap his shoulder to get his attention—a request that felt
reasonable to him but seemed puzzling to them. At home, he stopped drinking alcohol entirely; the
smell had become unbearable, and even a wine cork’s aroma at a dinner party could send him into a
cold sweat. His wife grew frustrated with his flinching and his tension, and their conversations
became clipped.

The cycle tightened. He woke from nightmares with his neck screaming, and the pain itself triggered
the memory again, which left him even more wired the next day. He reacted to false alarms
repeatedly—once, he dove behind a parked car when a road crew’s jackhammer started—and each false
alarm delivered another vivid replay of the captivity. By the time he sought help, he was exhausted,
his marriage strained, and his professional reputation fraying from the assignments he had refused.
What brought him to therapy was not the memories themselves but the growing certainty that he could
not keep his life from shrinking any further. He was afraid that the next sound, the next touch, or
the next stray smell would tighten the loop even more, and that he might lose everything he had
built since returning home. He wanted to find a way to stop the film without first having to live
through it again.
