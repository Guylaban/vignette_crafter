Linh, a 57-year-old interior designer of Vietnamese heritage, had been running a pop-up design booth
at a weekend market when a terrorist bomb detonated near the food vendors. The blast threw her
against a concrete pillar; she remembers the sharp, sweet smell of spilled rum from a toppled cart
mixing with the metallic scent of blood. That olfactory memory has become a powerful trigger: the
whiff of alcohol—from a friend’s glass of wine, a cleaning product, even hand sanitizer—suddenly
plunges her back into the chaos. Old photographs of the market district, or a casual conversation
about that day, also flood her with intrusive images.

When a trigger strikes, Linh feels an urgent need to release the building pressure. She has excused
herself to the bathroom and scratched her arm until she felt pain, a self-harm that momentarily
grounds her. Other times she binge-eats, or pushes herself through a punishing run, trying to
distract and exhaust herself. She is convinced she cannot handle even the slightest upset; the world
feels fundamentally dangerous. This low-coping self-view means that when a trigger arises, she never
considers other options—her immediate recourse is the same self-harm or overeating, because she
believes she lacks the strength to cope differently.

Her hypervigilance and conviction that danger lurks everywhere also intensify her threat responses.
She constantly scans her surroundings, watching strangers for signs of hostility. This vigilance
primes her to startle violently when a colleague taps her shoulder; her heart races and she feels
momentarily back in the market. Because she sees the world as a threat, she interprets ambiguous
cues—a quick movement, a sudden scent—as imminent danger, which in turn triggers trauma memories
more often. Each flashback leaves her breathless, acting as if the bombing were happening again, and
that terror drives her to eat compulsively or exercise until her legs shake.

After each startle or flashback, Linh’s thoughts spiral: “See, I can’t handle anything—the world is
not safe.” The threat responses deepen her negative appraisals, and the vivid memories themselves
make the alcohol smell more salient in her mind, so she notices it everywhere. Her maladaptive
strategies also turn against her. After a binge she feels ashamed, more convinced of her own
brokenness; self-harm leaves her feeling estranged from herself, reinforcing her low-coping self-
image. The distraction of overeating eventually fades, and the mental void allows trauma memories to
rush back. Compulsive exercise leaves her physically depleted yet mentally hyperaroused, triggering
intrusive images of the bombing. And when she hurts herself, she becomes frightened of her own
impulses, monitoring herself for loss of control—this vigilance heightens her sense of threat,
making her even more jumpy at unexpected sounds or touches.

Linh works from a home office now, avoiding client meetings in public spaces. She declined a
commission to redesign a community market, citing creative differences. Her marriage has grown
tense; she cannot bear her husband’s casual touch at night, and she has hidden the self-harm marks.
What finally brought her to seek help was a moment last week when, during a routine traffic stop,
the officer’s radio crackled and she smelled coffee—a scent that had not triggered her before—and
she dissociated so completely that she could not answer basic questions. She is terrified that she
is losing control of her own mind, but part of her also whispers that maybe, just maybe, a therapist
can show her a different way to live.
