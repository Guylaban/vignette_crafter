then his chest tightened, and he spent the next two hours coughing in the ER. Medically he was
cleared within days, but something inside him never cleared.

Months later, Duc could still feel that chemical burn at the back of his throat whenever he caught a
whiff of solvent or entered a poorly ventilated space. He began turning down jobs in basements or
tight mechanical rooms; he told his dispatcher he was getting too old for cramped work, but the real
reason was a deep, unshakeable certainty that the air was unsafe. Even when the safety data sheet
showed no risk, even when his supervisor assured him the equipment was sealed, Duc found it
impossible to trust that he was safe. His body stayed braced—shoulders hunched, jaw tight, ready to
flinch at the slightest change in air pressure or a new smell.

This constant sense of imminent danger drove Duc to strategies that made life more manageable but
also more restricted. Because he could not trust that a quiet room was harmless, he avoided relaxing
entirely: after work he would find chores—reorganizing his toolbox, checking the seals on his own
home’s ducts—rather than sit still or lie down. The moment he tried to unwind, his mind would flash
to the basement, to that chemical taste, and he would spring up again. Being alone in the house felt
just as threatening; he believed that if something went wrong, no one would be there to help or to
confirm the danger was real. So he stayed near his wife, María, or called his son just to hear
another voice, even when he had nothing to say. He also began avoiding any potential stressor—social
plans, doctor’s appointments, even the evening news—because any unexpected event might force him to
confront a situation he could not control. His world narrowed to the most predictable routines:
work, home, sleep, repeat.

The strategies created their own weight. Duc’s avoidance of relaxation and solitude meant he was
always in motion or in someone’s company, but he never felt rested. His body stayed on alert; he
checked the front door and back windows three times before bed each night, then again when he woke
to use the bathroom, even though he lived in a quiet suburb with good locks. He told himself he was
being cautious, but he knew it was more than that—it was a compulsion born of the conviction that
danger could breach his walls at any moment.

What finally brought Duc to seek help was a breakdown at work. He was assigned a standard duct-
cleaning job in a school gymnasium; when he walked in and smelled chlorine from the pool, he froze,
broke into a cold sweat, and walked out without a word. His supervisor called him the next day,
concerned. Duc sat in the kitchen with María and admitted he could not keep living this way. He was
terrified that the next job would be impossible, that his body would betray him, that the world he
used to move through easily had become a minefield. He hoped—though he barely let himself believe
it—that talking to someone could teach him how to breathe again without first checking the air.
