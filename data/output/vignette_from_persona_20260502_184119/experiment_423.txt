The stalking began quietly. A stranger she had briefly exchanged words with at a neighborhood café
began appearing on her bicycle commute home. At first, it was coincidence—she told herself. Then he
was outside her building, on the same bike, watching. He followed her for weeks, always on that
bicycle, never speaking, never close enough to confront. She reported him, but without identifiable
threats or direct contact, the police filed it as suspicious behavior, not a crime. He was never
charged. She moved to a new apartment across town, but the damage had already taken root.

As a graphic designer, she now works in a bustling open-plan studio downtown. Every day, she enters
the office and instinctively takes a desk near the emergency exit, always facing the door. If a
coworker approaches from behind, her body tenses before her mind registers the sound. She scans the
room repeatedly, cataloging faces, bags, anything unusual. On a recent walk during lunch break, a
parked bicycle a block away made her freeze, heart hammering, before she realized it was just a bike
leaning against a rack. These startle reactions—false alarms—happen several times a week. She has
come to believe that the world is simply dangerous, that people are not what they seem, and most of
all that the person who stalked her faced no consequences. This injustice feels like a permanent
stain on her sense of safety.

Her husband notices she no longer wants to go to the park or walk to the corner store. She avoids
the neighborhood where the stalker first appeared, but her avoidance has spread: she now checks her
surroundings before leaving any building, and she maps escape routes even in familiar places like
the grocery store. The more she scans, the more potential threats she spots—a man walking too
slowly, a cyclist approaching from behind—and each one reinforces her belief that she cannot trust
her environment. She feels certain that if she lets her guard down, something terrible will happen.
This vigilance also feeds back into her threat system: her constant planning and checking keep her
on edge, so a sudden noise or a bicycle whizzing past triggers a full startle response, as if the
danger were immediate.

She has begun to feel followed again in daily life, even when logically she knows no one is there. A
car slowing near her as she walks home from the train—she is sure it is tracking her. A person
lingering at a bus stop—she interprets it as surveillance. Her negative appraisals shape her
perception; because she believes the world is dangerous and people are deceptive, she reads
ambiguous cues as evidence of being targeted. This, in turn, feeds additional triggers: each time
she feels followed, that feeling itself becomes a trigger, launching another cycle of
hypervigilance.

Her maladaptive strategies have become second nature. At work, she scans the studio so relentlessly
that she misses conversations around her. She always chooses a seat with a clear sightline to the
door, and she plans which way she would run if someone entered with hostile intent. Those very
behaviors—the scanning, the planning—keep her in a constant state of alert, making her more reactive
to benign sounds and more likely to misinterpret a colleague’s sudden approach as a threat. And they
also deepen her conviction that the world is unsafe: if she has to act this way, the danger must be
real.

What finally brought her to therapy was an evening walk with her husband. A teenager rode past on a
bicycle, and she gasped, grabbed her husband’s arm, and insisted they turn back. She could not
explain why a bicycle still terrified her. She knew the stalker was long gone, but the feeling was
as vivid as the first day. She is exhausted. She wants to stop scanning, stop flinching, stop
believing that everyone around her is a potential predator. But more than anything, she needs to
believe that the world can feel safe again, and that the person who hurt her will not continue to
control her life from a distance.
