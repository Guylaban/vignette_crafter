Kiet was fifty-one when the wildfire burned through the suburban edge of the valley where he lived
with his wife, Linh, and their youngest daughter, still in high school. He had been at home that
afternoon, working from the small office he kept off the kitchen, when the wind shifted without
warning. The smoke that had been drifting east curled back, and within minutes the sky turned the
colour of old pennies. He remembered standing at the sliding glass door watching the ridge line
flame, a wall of fire moving faster than anything he had ever seen in policy projections or risk
models. The evacuation order came too late—the road was already choked, the air so thick he could
taste ash on his tongue. He gathered Linh and their daughter into the car, but the car would not
start. For nearly two hours they were pinned inside the house, the sirens distant and useless, the
smoke seeping through every seal until the rooms filled with a grey, stinging haze that burned his
throat and made his stomach clench. They were eventually reached by a search crew, but those hours
had already carved themselves into him. The smoke smell, especially, followed them. For weeks after,
Kiet could not walk into the house without stopping in the doorway, scanning the air before he would
step inside.

The smoke smell was the most direct summons. A neighbour lighting a grill, a passing truck with a
faulty exhaust, the faint woody drift from a fireplace a block away—each time it reached him, his
body reacted before his mind caught up. His jaw would lock, his heart would tip into a hard gallop,
and his chest would feel as though something heavy had settled on it. In those moments a thought
would arrive, unbidden: *I can't trust myself to keep us safe.* It was not the memory of the fire as
much as the memory of his own helplessness, the hours when he had stood useless while his wife and
daughter coughed and he had nothing to offer them. The feeling of being ill—the foggy-headed fatigue
after a bad night's sleep, a sudden wave of nausea from something he ate, even the ordinary headache
that came with flu season—had come to carry the same weight. Each minor physical downturn pulled him
back to that claustrophobic kitchen, where his own body had betrayed him. When he felt unwell, he
found himself thinking: *If I can't handle this, what else can I not handle?* And from that thought
it was a short, terrible slide to the other one, the one he never said aloud: *Maybe I am losing my
mind.*

Kiet worked as a policy analyst for a regional environmental commission, and the job required him to
sit through long planning meetings, multi-stakeholder negotiations, emergency response tabletop
exercises. The unpredictability of those meetings—an agenda changed at the last minute, a new
participant added without notice, a line of questioning no one had anticipated—had begun to feel
intolerable. He would sit at the conference table, his hands flat on the polished wood, and feel the
familiar tightness in his chest as the conversation veered into territory he had not prepared for.
*I have to be especially careful,* he told himself, *because you never know what can happen next.*
That belief, once planted, made him watchful in every room. He scanned his colleagues' faces for
signs of sudden change. He rehearsed exit routes in every unfamiliar building he entered, including
the county office where he had worked for nineteen years. This constant vigilance did not protect
him; instead it made him more sensitive to the very things he feared. In meetings, a colleague
coughing lightly at the wrong moment sent his attention skittering away from the agenda and into a
narrow focus on the sound itself—the dry scrape, the possibility of smoke, the shape of something
dangerous. An unexpected change in the schedule would hit him not as an inconvenience but as a
physical jolt, a signal that the world was about to become unsafe. His hyperawareness seeded more
triggers: the cough he noticed because he was watching for threats became itself a trigger, and the
schedule change he was braced for became itself unbearable.

At home, the pattern tightened. Linh began to notice that Kiet no longer accepted spontaneous
invitations. A weekend trip suggested in the morning, the offer of a barbecue with neighbours, the
sudden arrival of their daughter's friends—he met each with a careful refusal, the same sentence
each time: *It's better if I know ahead of time.* Unpredictability, even the pleasant kind, felt
poisoned. He had come to believe that his own judgment was unreliable, that whatever he assumed
would happen next was exactly what would not happen next, and that he needed to be ready for
catastrophe in every moment. This left him perpetually braced, and because he was braced, every
small deviation felt confirming. The nights were no easier. A sudden ache in his stomach, the kind
that used to pass unnoticed, now sent him sitting upright in bed, his pulse loud, his mind already
running through the worst possibilities. He could not explain it to Linh without sounding
irrational, so he lay still and waited for the feeling to pass, and the waiting itself made it
worse.

What finally brought him to seek help was something smaller and more ordinary than he had expected.
He was in a grocery store on a Saturday afternoon, pushing a cart down the cleaning aisle, when a
woman ahead of him dropped a glass bottle of olive oil. It shattered, and the white film of fine
dust he could not smell but imagined he could—the ghost of smoke, somewhere—made him walk out of the
store, leaving the cart half full. He stood in the parking lot with his hands shaking and thought,
*I cannot keep living like this.* At intake, he spoke of his wife, his work, the meetings he now
dreaded, the sudden physical sensations that seemed to arrive from nowhere and cost him hours of
peace. He was afraid, he said, that the therapist would tell him he was in fact going crazy. But he
was more afraid of staying the same.
