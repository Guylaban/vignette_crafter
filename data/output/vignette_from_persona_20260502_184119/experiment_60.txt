Elena, a 59-year-old divorced physical therapist, grew up in a home where her mother’s anger was
physical and unpredictable. The beatings came without warning—a slap during dinner, a shove against
the wall, being locked in the closet for hours. Now, decades later, she can still recall the cold
tile floor beneath her cheek, but often the memory arrives as a fragment—a sudden image of her
mother’s hand, then nothing—and she feels nothing, as if it happened to someone else, a story too
extreme to have been real. Yet those fragments, when they surface, leave her restless. She finds
herself at the kitchen sink at midnight, scrubbing the same spot until her hands ache, trying to
impose an order she cannot hold onto. The cleaning quiets something, but afterwards the memories
feel closer, as though she has stirred them up.

At work, Elena has learned to position herself with her back against the wall of the treatment room,
where she can watch the door and the rows of patients on the waiting benches. When a stranger—a new
patient—approaches too quickly, her chest tightens and she steps back, her mind momentarily blank
except for the sense that danger is near. That vigilance, she has noticed, often brings back a
fragment of the closet: the dark, the sound of her own breathing. She then finds herself overeating
later that evening, grabbing a second dinner she does not want, trying to fill the hollow dread.

Her mornings start early, before dawn, because she wakes several times during the night to check the
locks, to listen for footsteps. The waking is automatic, and each time she must remind herself she
is safe, but the thought alone can summon the old numbness. To avoid triggering those feelings, she
has begun turning down social invitations, avoiding any situation that might be stressful—even phone
calls from her daughter feel like a test. At the clinic, she schedules only routine follow-ups,
never new evaluations, because the prospect of an unfamiliar patient feels too unpredictable. Her
colleagues have noticed her reluctance and have taken over the more complex cases without asking;
she is grateful, but she also feels the walls closing in.

The compulsive cleaning has become a ritual. Every evening she organizes her living room, aligning
the coasters, wiping the counters until they gleam. It gives her the illusion of control, yet as she
moves through the motions, the memory of being made to scrub the kitchen floor as a child—forced to
clean as punishment—often intrudes, sharp and unwelcome. The connection is a loop she cannot escape:
a fragment triggers a frantic need to clean; the cleaning, rather than banishing the past, brings it
back more vividly.

What finally brought Elena to seek help was a moment in the clinic last month. A male patient, in
his forties, reached out to touch her arm in gratitude after a session. She flinched, nearly dropped
his chart, and for a split second saw her mother’s hand mid-swing. That night she cleaned for three
hours, then ate half a cake. The next morning, she called a therapist. She is afraid that if she
talks about the abuse, the fragments will multiply and overwhelm her. But she is also afraid of the
life she is shrinking into—the clinic, the cleaning, the solitary dinners. She does not know if she
can stop the loop, only that she no longer wants to be alone in it.
