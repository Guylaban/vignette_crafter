Meera pressed herself against the dental chair’s backrest, a reflex that had nothing to do with the
procedure she was about to perform. The patient was an elderly man, and he had asked her to adjust
the recliner—a simple request, but the moment he spoke in that firm, commanding tone, something
tightened in her chest. She was not the one in control here, she thought, and the feeling was too
familiar. It was 2001 when she had last felt that kind of surrender: a late September morning,
walking out of her office after a patient cancellation, when the first plane struck. She had not
witnessed it, but by the time she reached the street, the sirens were already screaming from every
direction, and the smell of smoke and jet fuel hung thick and acrid in the air. She had been caught
in the panicked crowd, pushed against a wall by a man who shouted orders she could not follow, his
hand on her shoulder forcing her down. For those minutes, she had no voice, no choice—someone else
held all the power. That helplessness lodged inside her like a splinter.

In the months and years that followed, Meera learned to avoid the block where it had all happened,
taking a longer route to her practice even though it cost her fifteen minutes each day. She also
began scanning—every new patient who walked into her office, every sound in the hallway, every car
that idled too long outside her window. The scanning had a cost: the more she looked, the more she
saw. One morning, the faint wail of a far-off siren drifted through the closed window, and she
caught herself gripping the dental tray so hard her knuckles turned white. That sound meant someone
was in danger, which meant she might be too. She made a habit of choosing the treatment room closest
to the back exit, and she instructed her assistant to leave the door half-open during cleanings. The
quiet powerlessness she felt when a patient argued about a bill or a technician questioned her
technique would send a jolt through her, and she would immediately glance toward the door, checking
her escape route.

Over two decades, these habits settled into routines, and the routines cost her. She had stopped
attending dental conferences entirely because she could not bear to be in a large hall with only one
set of doors. A colleague had once invited her to a weekend seminar at a hotel; Meera agreed, but
spent the entire first session mentally mapping the fire exits and counting the steps to each one.
She left before lunch, explaining that she felt “off,” and she never accepted another invitation.
Her practice shrank too—she began turning away new patients who seemed demanding or who reminded her
of the man who had pressed her down, afraid that their authority over her would trigger the same
sinking dread. She kept her existing patients, but she rushed through procedures, always one eye on
the hallway, waiting for a sound or a scent that would signal danger.

That is why Meera finally called a therapist. She was eighty years old, divorced, living alone, and
she had realized that her scanning and her avoidance were not protections anymore; they were cages.
She was tired of the constant vigilance, tired of steering clear of the neighborhood she had once
loved, tired of feeling small and powerless in a life that was supposed to be her own. What
frightened her most was the thought that she might spend the years she had left running from a
moment that had already passed—and that she might never learn how to stand still.
