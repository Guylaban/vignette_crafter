Sofia Ramirez, a thirty-eight-year-old factory worker from a close-knit Latin American family, had
never imagined she would become someone who flinched at sirens. Two years ago, her younger brother
Marco was killed in a hit-and-run while crossing the street. She was the one who received the phone
call from the hospital, the frantic voice of a nurse saying, “Ma’am, your brother has been in an
accident.” By the time she arrived, he was gone. The sound of the ambulance sirens that had wailed
past her car on the way there became seared into her memory — a sound that now, on the factory floor
or walking home from her shift, made her stomach drop and her mind race. Those sirens, along with
any mention of someone losing a loved one, or even driving past a cemetery, instantly yanked her
back to that day, and she would begin to replay the details over and over in her head, trying to
understand how it could have happened, trying to find some meaning that never came.

That overanalyzing, that endless loop of *if only I had called him earlier* or *why didn’t he look
before crossing*, had become Sofia’s daily ritual. She found herself asking her mother repeatedly,
“Do you think I could have done something?” and her mother would say no, but the reassurance never
lasted. She also started avoiding anything that reminded her of loss — she stopped visiting Marco’s
grave, skipped family gatherings where his name might come up, and changed her route to work so she
wouldn’t pass the intersection where the accident occurred. These strategies felt necessary to keep
the pain at bay, but they only deepened her growing sense that her life had been destroyed. Each
time she avoided a grief reminder or sought reassurance that failed to stick, the belief that she no
longer belonged anywhere took firmer hold. In her mind, she was no longer the happy, sociable woman
her coworkers used to know; she was now someone perpetually miserable, someone whose life had been
shattered by a single moment.

The negative appraisals themselves made her more alert to triggers. Because Sofia already believed
she was broken and that her happiness was gone forever, she began to notice sirens even when they
were faint in the distance. A coworker mentioning a relative’s illness would send a jolt through
her, because it felt like confirmation that loss was everywhere and that she herself was defined by
it. The factory floor, once a place of routine and camaraderie, became a landscape of potential
reminders: the lunchroom where someone might talk about a funeral, the parking lot where an
ambulance might pass. She felt as though she had become a walking wound, and the world was
constantly rubbing against it.

In the months that followed, Sofia’s avoidance and rumination began to erode her relationships. She
stopped going to her cousin’s wedding because it would be held in a church near a cemetery; she told
her family she was sick. She pulled away from friends who tried to talk about Marco, interpreting
their concern as pressure she couldn’t bear. At work, her supervisors noticed her withdrawn
demeanor, and her productivity slipped. She was certain she was failing, but the thought of trying
to explain why felt impossible. What frightened her most was a feeling that she would never get back
to who she used to be — that the person who had laughed easily, who had danced at family parties,
who had felt she belonged, was gone for good.

She finally came to the clinic on the urging of her mother, who had watched Sofia grow distant and

said, “I’m afraid I’ll never feel like myself again. That I’ll always be this person who can’t even
hear a siren without falling apart.” But underneath that fear was a fragile hope: that maybe, just
maybe, talking about it wouldn’t make everything worse.
