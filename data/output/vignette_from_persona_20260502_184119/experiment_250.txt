followed, Erik began to scan the faces in the briefing room, certain that others knew, that they
were watching him with silent judgment. He took to walking with his back to the wall. At home, a
single-story rental near the flight line, he found himself lying awake at night, listening for
footsteps on the gravel driveway, his heart hammering at the creak of the water heater. Ordinary
sounds—a car door slamming, the clatter of a dropped tool in the hangar—made him flinch so hard that
fellow pilots started to notice.

Erik had come to believe that his reactions proved he was a lousy coper. A combat pilot should be
able to shake off anything. Instead, he felt hollowed out, his faith in people gone. He was certain
that the assault had marked him as vulnerable, and that vulnerability made him a target. This
conviction fed directly into his hypervigilance: because he had failed to protect himself once, he
now had to monitor every unpredictable situation—the sudden appearance of a maintenance crew chief

more he watched for danger, the more danger he saw. A colleague’s casual glance across the tarmac
became suspicious. A late-night engine test spooling up outside his window sent him scrambling to
check the locks.

hyperalert: he scanned the base commissary for men who stood too close, he listened for footsteps in
the hallway during his off-hours, and he woke from nightmares with the residue of the assault still
vivid, his body already braced for an attacker. The dream residue itself became a trigger—lying in
the dark, disoriented, he would strain to hear if someone was actually in the house, and then lie
rigid for an hour, afraid to move.

crew chief noticed that Erik now did a full preflight check twice, as though he could not trust his
own memory. The occupational consequence was subtle but real: he was passed over for a lead-
instructor billet, his commanding officer citing "recent distractibility."

that, if he let his guard down, the betrayal would happen again.
