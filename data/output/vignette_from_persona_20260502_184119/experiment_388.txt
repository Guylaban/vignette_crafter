Malia was thirty-three, a network engineer who spent her days managing server rooms and virtual
private networks, a job that demanded precision and calm. But the calm had been gone for years. It
had evaporated the moment her four-year-old daughter, Leilani, was diagnosed with a rare and
aggressive form of leukemia. Malia remembered the fluorescent lights of the pediatric ICU, the way
doctors spoke in clipped syllables, the endless beeping of monitors. She remembered the
arguments—with her husband at the time, who wanted to pursue alternative treatments, with her
mother, who questioned every medical decision. Treatment worked. Leilani was now eight, in
remission, a happy, healthy child who had no memory of the worst months. But her mother remembered
everything.

Two years after Leilani’s recovery, Malia’s marriage dissolved. She did not fight it. The divorce
was quiet, almost relief, because the arguments had become unbearable—each one reopened the hospital
room, the helplessness, the fear. Even now, a disagreement with a colleague about a cable routing
protocol could send her heart racing. Her body remembered before her mind did: the sudden heat under
her collar, the shallow breath, the tremor in her hands. It was not anger she felt. It was something
older, a sense that the world was about to shatter again.

She had learned to stay away from known people—friends from her old life, relatives who asked too
many questions. She kept her distance from her ex-husband, only texted about drop-offs. She had come
to believe that any close relationship carried the risk of another catastrophe, and so she
maintained a polite, functional isolation. Her colleagues knew her as efficient but distant. No one
came to her desk unannounced. That was the rule.

But the vigilance did not stay at work. At home, in her small apartment, Malia could not settle. She
felt on guard even when the door was locked and the blinds were drawn. A floorboard creak—her
neighbor, she knew—and she would freeze, scanning the room. Unexpected movement nearby, even a cat
darting past the window, made her flinch as if a needle were sliding into her arm. The medical
procedures she had endured alongside Leilani—the blood draws, the spinal taps—had become a permanent
trigger. Now, when she herself needed a routine physical or a mammogram, the anticipation would
build for days. The sight of a stethoscope was enough to cause a wave of nausea and a pounding
pulse, and she often cancelled appointments and rescheduled, then cancelled again.

The more on edge she remained, the more she noticed potential threats—and the more arguments she
provoked. She had started snapping at her team lead for a minor error in a configuration file, then
apologised, then felt humiliated. But the hypervigilance would not quiet. She began to avoid the
break room during lunch because a coworker liked to sneak up behind people. She took her coffee at
her desk, door half-closed, monitoring the hallway for any sudden movement. It was exhausting. She
knew, rationally, that no one was coming for her. But the feeling in her body—the tight chest, the
coiled muscles—would not listen to reason.

What finally brought Malia to seek help was a simple thing: a letter from her gynecologist reminding
her of a Pap smear she had missed twice. She sat at her kitchen table, the paper trembling in her
hands, and realised she could not go through another cycle of avoidance and dread. She was afraid of
what the exam might find—and more afraid of the terror that would accompany it. She wanted to be
able to take her daughter to the doctor without reliving the worst year of her life. She wanted to
stop starting arguments because her nerves were too frayed to tolerate someone else’s uncertainty.
She wanted, she told the intake therapist, just to feel safe in her own body again.
