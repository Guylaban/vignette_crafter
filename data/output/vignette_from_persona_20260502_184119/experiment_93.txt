Thuy is a fifty-four-year-old electrician who nearly died of respiratory failure four years ago. She
had been admitted with severe pneumonia, and the memory of gasping for air in a bright, antiseptic-
stinking room, the sound of her own ragged breathing and the ventilator’s hiss, remains the
clearest, most terrifying moment of her life. She was eventually discharged, but the illness cost
her more than lung tissue; it cost her the marriage she had already been struggling to hold
together. She and her husband separated within the following year, and Thuy often finds herself
thinking about how life would have been different if that hospital stay had never happened — if she
had never gotten sick, maybe they would still be together. That counterfactual replaying has become
almost automatic whenever she is reminded of the event.

Returning to work as an electrician was supposed to help her move on, but Thuy soon noticed that her
body seemed to have its own memory. The first time a coworker, panting after carrying a heavy spool
of wire, stood too close to her, Thuy felt her chest tighten, her hands tremble, and her entire body
go rigid before she even understood why. She had an overwhelming urge to flee, and later she could
not shake the metallic taste of hospital air. Even minor triggers — the antiseptic cleaner used in
the supply closet, a lingering smell that reminds her of the ward — set off the same physical
reactivity: her heart pounds, her muscles brace, and she feels as if another attack is about to
begin. Thuy has come to believe that her body is constantly on alert, ready to react to danger
before her mind has time to catch up. This is especially true around the anniversary of her
discharge; in the weeks preceding it, she cannot shake a pervasive sense of imminent threat.

To manage these intrusions, Thuy has developed a set of strategies that help her get through the day
but also seem to make things worse over time. When a heavy breathing sound catches her off guard,
she mentally steps outside herself and pretends it is happening to someone else, watching from a
distance. This dissociation allows her to keep working, but it leaves her feeling disconnected and
hollow, and afterward her body often feels even more jumpy, as if the suspended alertness has merely
piled on extra tension. The more she dissociates, the more her body seems to brace for the next
trigger; the sense of being always on edge intensifies. Similarly, her avoidance of all medical cues
— she has not stepped inside a hospital since her discharge, and she avoids physical examinations,
canceling follow-up appointments — has made her hypervigilant to anything that might signal illness.
A distant siren or the sight of a white van makes her check her own breathing, and that checking
itself becomes a trigger, because the sound of her own inhale reminds her of the ventilator. The
avoidance thus paradoxically amplifies the triggers, because she never learns to tolerate them and
instead scans her environment for the slightest hint of antiseptic or labored breathing.

The self-reinforcing loop has tightened over months and years. A few weeks ago, while wiring a new
office building, a colleague climbing a ladder began to breathe heavily; Thuy felt her body react
before she could think — a surge of adrenaline, a racing pulse — and then she was instantly pulled
into the dissociation, as if she were watching herself from across the room. That evening she could
not stop thinking about how differently her life might have gone without the illness, and she felt a
fresh wave of dread about her health. This cycle has eroded her professional reliability: she now
refuses any job that involves a hospital or clinic, turning down lucrative contracts because she
cannot face the smell, the sounds, the sense that the illness is waiting for her. Her social world
has shrunk; she lives alone and rarely sees her adult children because they live near the medical
center where she was treated.

What finally brought Thuy in for help was a panic attack she could no longer dismiss. She was in a
hardware store when a customer with a cold coughed and gasped audibly nearby; Thuy froze, her body
seized, and she had to leave her cart and walk out of the store. She is terrified that her health is
failing again, and she is equally afraid that her avoidance is making the world feel impossibly
small. She hopes therapy can help her stop the loop, but she is not sure how she will ever tolerate
the sounds and smells that feel so directly life-threatening.
