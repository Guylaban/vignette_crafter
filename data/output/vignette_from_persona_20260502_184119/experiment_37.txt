Elena, a fifty-three-year-old stock broker living in Manhattan, was mugged eleven months ago as she
left her office building after a late evening. A man in a hooded jacket grabbed her shoulder from
behind, pressed something hard against her ribs, and demanded her bag. She froze, then handed it
over without a word. He ran. She stood there shaking, expecting someone to help, but the street was
empty. When she eventually called the police, the officer on the phone sounded dismissive—"These
things happen, ma'am, you got the card replaced, you're fine." The institution she believed would
protect her had simply shrugged. That moment lodged itself in her chest like a splinter.

Over the following weeks, Elena became convinced the world was a dangerous place. Every corner,
every shadow felt like a potential ambush. She could not shake the sense that no one—not the police,
not the security guards at her building, not her husband—would truly keep her safe. That pervasive
belief began to shape her every action. She started scanning her surroundings constantly: the subway
platform, the lobby, the trading floor itself. Her eyes darted from person to person, cataloguing
movement. When a colleague at the next desk suddenly stood up or a coffee cup clattered, Elena
jumped as though a gun had fired. Her heart would hammer, her palms sweat, and she could feel a wave
of anger or shame wash over her—anger at herself for being so weak, shame at her own fear. That
feeling of threat only drove her scanning deeper; she could not stop herself from checking the door
again and again, because every sudden noise she startled at confirmed that danger was real.

She also began to respond to threats with a quiet, automatic compliance. At work, when a senior
broker raised his voice during a volatile trade, she found herself nodding and agreeing even when
she knew he was wrong. At home, when her husband suggested a weekend trip, she said yes without
voicing her worry that the city was too dangerous to leave their apartment. She had come to believe
that if she did not placate people, something bad would happen—an argument, a loss of control,
another attack. The compliance made her feel hollow, but it felt necessary, like a bargain she had
to keep. To reinforce that bargain, she developed small mental rituals. Before leaving her office
each evening, she would silently count to seven three times, then tap her wedding ring against her
desk. If she missed a count, she would start over, terrified that skipping it would invite another
mugging. She knew it made no sense, yet the fear overrode logic.

The rituals and the scanning fed back into her core belief that the world was fundamentally unsafe.
Every time she performed a mental ritual, she reinforced the idea that she needed special
protection; every time she avoided a confrontation by fawning, she confirmed that the world was too
dangerous to speak up. She also felt increasingly numb, as though something inside had gone quiet.
She stopped feeling much of anything during the day—no excitement about a good trade, no warmth
during dinner with her husband. That numbness itself seemed to prove that she had been broken by the
experience.

In the trading pit, her checking became a problem. She would miss the start of a bid because she was
scanning the doorway instead of the screen. Twice she failed to execute a client order on time
because she had to redo her counting ritual before hitting enter. Her supervisor noticed and pulled
her aside, warning that her performance was slipping. Elena nodded compliantly and promised to
improve, but inside she felt a fresh wave of helplessness. Institutional failure had happened to her
before—now it was happening again, only this time she was failing herself.

What finally brought Elena to seek help was a moment three weeks ago. A janitor walked silently
behind her in the hallway and coughed. She screamed, spun around, and burst into tears in front of a
dozen colleagues. The shame was unbearable. She is terrified that the world will never feel safe
again, but she is even more afraid that she is losing the person she used to be—a sharp, decisive
trader, someone who was not afraid to disagree. She hopes, she says quietly, that therapy might help
her feel alive again.
