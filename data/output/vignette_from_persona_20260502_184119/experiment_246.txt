For two years now, the bomb has been the fixed point around which everything else orbits. Kwame was
twenty-eight when it happened, a tailor with a small shop in a strip mall, and on a Saturday morning
he had gone to the open-air fabric market downtown to find a particular Ankara print a client had
requested. He remembers the shriek of the first blast, the way the air turned solid and hot, bodies
and bolts of cloth flying together. Then there is nothing—a gap, a white ache where twenty minutes
should be—until he is standing in a doorway three blocks away, his hands pressed flat against the
bricks, his ears ringing. The rest is film: the screaming, the smoke, the man beside him with his
leg folded the wrong way, looping over and over even when Kwame tries to stop it. Sometimes the film
plays without feeling. He can recount the sequence to himself, but the horror stays at a distance,
as if it happened to someone else, and that numbness frightens him more than the images.

In the months that followed, Kwame began to believe he had failed a test that mattered. He was
certain he was weak—a weak person, the kind who freezes or runs or forgets, who cannot even remember
how he survived. He started to doubt every decision, from the thread he chose for a hem to the way
he spoke to customers, because he could no longer trust his own instincts. And slowly, quietly, he
came to feel that he no longer belonged anywhere. Not at the market, not at the shop, not among the
friends who had not been there and could not understand. When he tries to recall the bombing now,
these appraisals push through: the memory gaps feel like proof of his failure, the emotionless
replays like evidence that something essential in him has died. The loop tightens—the more the film
runs, the weaker he feels, and the weaker he feels, the more the film runs, each intruding memory
reinforcing his certainty that he is broken.

His body stays ready. Every time a memory surfaces, even the numb replay, his shoulders rise and his
eyes start moving. The first thing he does when he enters any room—his own shop, a friend’s
apartment, the grocery store—is locate the exits. He cannot sit unless his back is against a wall,
because the exposed feeling is unbearable. He is hypervigilant at all hours, scanning for threats
that never come, and this constant alertness feeds back into his appraisals: if he were not weak, he
tells himself, he would not need to be this afraid. The threat state also drives what he does next.
To escape the exhaustion of scanning, he pulls out his phone and disappears into games or social
media for an hour at a time. If a customer asks him a question while he is scrolling, he
snaps—picking an argument, creating a little crisis that drowns out the vigilance for a moment.

The memories themselves push him toward these same strategies. When the film starts playing, and
especially when he hits the gap that he cannot fill, Kwame grabs his phone or starts a pointless
quarrel with his assistant over a misplaced spool. He has stopped making any plans for the future.
He does not schedule vacations or even dinner with friends a week ahead, because planning means
admitting there is a future, and that future feels both impossible and dangerous. He tells himself
there is no point, that he is too weak to follow through anyway. The negative appraisals sustain the
avoidance: believing he belongs nowhere, he sees no reason to make plans; believing he is weak, he
sees no use in trying.

His occupation makes the avoidance harder to sustain. The tailor shop is a small room with one door,
and he cannot rearrange the furniture so that every worktable has a wall at his back. Customers
crowd the counter, blocking the exit, and he finds himself twitching, his eyes darting, his voice
going sharp. Three weeks ago he shouted at a regular for asking about a delivery date—a minor
thing—and she walked out. He has lost two clients this month. That is what finally brought him to
the clinic. Sitting in the waiting room, he chose the chair farthest from the door and pressed his
spine against the wall. He is terrified that this is permanent, that the film will never stop and
the scanning will never quiet, and he is hoping—though he cannot quite say it aloud—that someone can
convince him he is not completely beyond repair.
