Layla is a 43-year-old married Middle Eastern woman who works as a pharmacist. She came to therapy
at her husband’s urging, after months of escalating distress and a series of incidents at work that
had left colleagues concerned. For years she had believed she could manage alone, but the weight of
the past had become unbearable.

When Layla was eight years old, her father—a proud, volatile man who drank whiskey every
evening—began beating her with a leather belt whenever she disobeyed. The punishment was triggered
by small things: a forgotten chore, a word he did not like, a look that displeased him. He would
call her into the living room, order her to stand still, and strike her while the sharp smell of
alcohol filled the air. Her mother never intervened. By adolescence the beatings had stopped, but
the pattern had carved itself into her bones.

Now, three decades later, Layla still responded to the world as if she were still that child. At the
pharmacy, a supervisor’s direct instruction—even a routine request to double-check a
prescription—would set her heart racing and her mouth going dry. The faintest whiff of alcohol on a
customer’s breath could undo her entirely, bringing the memory back not as a story but as a living
moment: the feel of the belt, her father’s voice, the shame of knowing she was about to be hit. The
memory felt more real than the pharmacy around her, and it overwhelmed her with such force that she
would have to excuse herself to the bathroom. Some days the same memories surfaced and she felt
nothing—a hollow numbness, as if the child were a stranger. That emptiness disturbed her because it
confirmed that she had lost something essential.

The triggers did more than activate memories; they also fed the beliefs that held her life in a
tight grip. Each time an order triggered her fear, she came away more convinced that she was
helpless, that she could not stop bad things from happening, that her role as a competent pharmacist
and a good wife was a fraud. Nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore. These beliefs, in turn,
made her more sensitive to potential triggers: she began scanning the pharmacy for signs of
authority figures, bracing herself before every shift, interpreting a manager’s neutral expression
as a prelude to criticism. The memories themselves also sharpened her vigilance—after a particularly
vivid flashback, she would spend the rest of the day jumping at every voice, every footstep, every
order given over the intercom, because the past had proven how easily danger could appear.

To manage the constant intrusions, Layla had developed strategies that felt necessary but that she
knew were destroying her. She avoided authority figures at work, delegating interactions with the
pharmacy manager to a younger colleague. She avoided situations that might make her feel happy or
hopeful, because hope had always led to disappointment. When the memories pressed in and the
helplessness became unbearable, she would hurt herself physically—digging her nails into her arm,
snapping a rubber band against her wrist—because the pain was concrete and it forced the emotional
storm to quiet for a few minutes. The constant sense of threat drove these behaviors: her body was
always braced for danger, and avoidance was the only way she knew to keep it at bay. Yet each time
she avoided a situation, the triggers that remained grew more powerful, because she never gave
herself the chance to learn that they might not lead to harm.

Each strategy carried consequences that deepened her distress. Avoiding authority meant she was
overlooked for opportunities. Avoiding positive events meant her social world shrank. Her husband
had grown frustrated with her refusal to attend gatherings, and she saw the disappointment in his
face. Each time she said no, she confirmed to herself that she had failed in her most important role
as a partner and as a person. That sense of failure made the memories hit harder—because now, when
she recalled her father’s voice, she heard not just his anger but also her own current worthlessness
echoing back.

The sense of threat was constant. Even in bed at night, Layla struggled to fall asleep because she
felt she needed to stay alert. Her body reacted to false alarms: a car door outside, a raised voice
on television, her husband walking into the room too quickly. Her concentration had deteriorated—she
would read a prescription label three times and still not trust herself. The vigilance confirmed,
day after day, that she was not safe, which reinforced her belief that she was helpless and that
nothing she did could change her circumstances.

What finally brought her to therapy was a specific chain of events. A new pharmacy manager, a tall
man with a deep voice, began giving her direct instructions, and each interaction set off a cascade:
the command triggered the memory, which flooded her with helplessness, which convinced her she was
in danger, which drove her to avoid him or, on one occasion, to lock herself in the bathroom and dig
her nails into her forearm until she bled. Her colleagues noticed. Her husband noticed. And for the
first time in years, Layla began to wonder whether the way she was living—always bracing, always
avoiding, always certain the worst was about to happen—might not be the only way. She was afraid of
what therapy might uncover, afraid that if she let herself feel the full weight of the past she
would not survive it. But she was already, she realized, merely enduring. That recognition was what
finally made her pick up the phone.
