Arthur was sixty-two when his wife, Diane, died of a sudden pulmonary embolism on a Tuesday
afternoon. She had been packing clay for his next kiln load—a thing she always did without being
asked—and he found her on the studio floor, her hand still curled around a damp sponge. The
paramedics told him there was nothing he could have done, but the image of that sponge stayed with
him for months, a small, absurd detail that made the whole event feel both grotesque and unreal. He
had been a potter for forty years, and Diane had been his collaborator, his business manager, the
one who kept the orders straight and the glaze recipes organized. Without her, the studio felt
hollow, the kick wheel too quiet.

Two years on, Arthur still works the clay most days, but the rhythm has changed. He no longer allows
himself to feel the small satisfactions that used to punctuate a throwing session—the clean weight
of a centered lump, the sudden smoothness of a pulled handle. When a bowl comes out exactly as he
imagined, he sets it aside without looking at it twice and starts the next piece. Positive emotions
feel unsafe to him now, like a door he is afraid to open because he does not know what will come
through. He has learned to push them away before they can settle.

the trash; her favorite mug, the one she drank tea from every morning, is now wrapped in newspaper
at the back of a closet. He cannot bear to see objects that belonged to her because each one carries
a small, specific memory—the way she hummed while trimming a pot, the laugh she gave when a kiln
shelf cracked. He avoids the farmers’ market where they used to buy vegetables on Saturdays, and he
has stopped driving past the community center where she taught her weekly watercolor class. These
places are grief reminders, and he treats them like landmines.

The most pervasive change, however, is his refusal to make any plans for the future. When a local
gallery asked him to commit to a solo show in six months, he declined without explanation. When his
daughter suggested a family trip for the holidays, he said he would “see how he felt” and then
stopped returning her calls. Even small decisions—what to have for dinner next Friday, whether to
replace the worn-out kiln shelves—feel impossible. Arthur has come to believe that planning ahead is
a kind of arrogance, a presumption that the future will cooperate, and he no longer trusts that.

His wife’s sister, Margaret, visited last month and found the studio cold, the shelves only half
full. She told him he was disappearing into himself. Arthur heard the worry in her voice but could
not summon the energy to reassure her. He spends his days throwing pots that he does not fire,
letting the clay dry and crack on the wedging table because committing to a glaze would mean
deciding what the piece was for. His pottery business, once thriving, has dwindled to a few regular
customers who accept his vague promises of delivery “someday.”

What finally brought him to seek help was the approach of what would have been his and Diane’s
thirty-fifth wedding anniversary. Margaret made an appointment with a therapist and drove him there
herself. Arthur sat in the waiting room with his hands clenched in his lap, certain that talking
about any of this would only make it worse. But he also knew, with a quiet dread, that he could not
go on turning away from every small joy, every memory, every tomorrow. He was tired of living in the
narrow space between what he could not face and what he would not plan. He hoped the therapist might
give him permission to stop holding so tightly to nothing at all.
