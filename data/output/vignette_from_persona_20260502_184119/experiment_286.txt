Mai Tran was fifty-two, a librarian in a small coastal town, when she finally made an appointment
with a trauma specialist. The event that had upended her life occurred four years earlier. Typhoon
Ketsana had struck without warning one autumn night. Mai was alone in her ground-floor apartment;
the power failed, plunging her into complete darkness as floodwater rose past her knees. The worst
part came after the winds died down. A sudden, profound silence settled—no rain, no wind, just the
lapping of water and her own ragged breaths. When daylight came, she learned that a neighbor, a
woman she had spoken to the day before, had drowned in the same flood. Mai had not heard a thing;
the silence had masked the neighbor’s cries.

In the months that followed, the memory of that night began forcing its way into her mind without
warning. Something as simple as a library patron falling silent while reading could instantly return
her to the dark, to the cold water, and to the crushing grief she had felt upon learning of her
neighbor’s death. That grief—overwhelming, fresh—would hit her mid-shelf, and she would have to
brace herself against the bookcase. The silence itself had become a trigger, and she found herself
scanning every quiet room for exits, for things that might suddenly go wrong. Every time the reading
room fell still, her vigilance sharpened: she would grip the desk, her eyes darting from corner to
corner, certain that something terrible was about to happen again.

This hypervigilance only made the intrusions worse. The more she watched for danger in the dark, the
more the memory would surge back—untethered, uncontrolled. She began to experience the memory as a
threat in its own right, so that even recalling the event would put her on edge, sending her
scanning for signs of the kind of sudden change that had preceded the typhoon. Darkness, especially,
became a signal: if she woke in the night, even to use the bathroom, the absence of light would
trigger the full sensory replay—the water, the silence, the grief—and she would lie rigid, convinced
she heard water seeping under the door. That fear, in turn, made nighttime a trigger she could not
escape.

Mai developed strategies to manage. She deliberately tried to leave her body or blank out when the
intrusions came—a kind of mental dissociation that she hoped would stop the images. But instead, the
effort to escape often brought the memory closer, as though her mind was fighting back. She also
began to avoid anything that reminded her of the event: she stopped walking through the park after
work because the dusk felt too close to dusk on the day of the storm; she would not exercise at all,
even though she had once been an avid walker, because physical exertion made her breathless, and
that feeling of being out of breath took her straight back to the water. In the library, she always
chose the chair in the corner of the staff room, where she could see both doors and no one could
approach from behind. She needed to feel safe, and that need dictated where she sat.

Avoiding the dark meant she went home before sunset, curtailing her social life and drawing sharp
questions from her husband. He did not understand why she had become so jumpy, why she flinched when
he walked into a room silently. Mai could not explain. The silence itself had become a trigger, and
when she was confronted with it, her only option was to dissociate—to go elsewhere, inside her head,
until the danger passed. Yet trying to dissociate often caused the memory to intrude more
forcefully, as though her mind was punishing her for trying to escape. And each time she avoided a
circumstance, such as being in the dark, that very avoidance made the trigger feel larger, more
looming, more certain to cause harm the next time she could not avoid it.

She first realized she needed help when a visiting scholar asked her a question in the reading room,
and Mai could not respond. The library had fallen into one of those sudden silences, and she had
frozen, scanning the tables, waiting for something to happen. The scholar’s voice came from far
away, but Mai was back in the dark water, full of grief. When she came to, she had left her body for
a moment—blanked out entirely. The fact that it happened at work, in front of a patron, horrified
her. She made the appointment the next day. What frightens her most now is the prospect of being
trapped in silence again, unable to escape her own memory. She hopes, deep down, that talking will
somehow break the loop—that she can learn to hear quiet without feeling the flood rise around her.
