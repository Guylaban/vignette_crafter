Mai was thirty-two when the stalking began, a former client from her early days as a junior broker
who had become fixated after she helped him turn a modest profit. At first it was just emails, then
late-night voicemails, then the same dark sedan parked outside her building every evening. She
changed her route, switched gyms, stopped going out after dark. But he always seemed to find her.
The night it finally broke her, she was walking home from the train when she heard rapid footsteps
closing in from behind—a jogger, as it turned out—but the terror that seized her was absolute. She
dropped her bag and ran. Later that week, she caught the smell of cheap whiskey on a man standing
next to her on the platform, and her chest tightened so violently she had to sit down on the bench.
Her stalker had always smelled faintly of alcohol in the moments before she saw him, as though he
needed a drink to work up the nerve. Even now, five years later, the scent of whiskey can send her
back to that locked-in feeling, the sense that she is being watched and cannot escape.

The memory of the stalking has taken on a quality Mai cannot fully describe to herself. When she
tries to tell her partner about it, the words feel hollow, like she is describing a movie she saw
once—it cannot possibly have happened to her. And yet, at the same time, the memory is so vivid that
she lives it again: the cold sweat, the racing pulse, the certainty that he is just behind her.
Remembering brings back the same raw terror she felt the night she ran, and she has come to believe
that her mind is betraying her, that there is no safe distance from what happened. This sense of
collapse between past and present means that mundane moments can suddenly feel life-threatening. A
car door slamming outside her apartment is no longer just a sound; it is the sound of his footsteps,
and she is back on that sidewalk, heart hammering, the whiskey smell sharp in her nostrils.

At work, where Mai manages a high-stakes trading desk, her colleagues have noticed she flinches when
the overhead speakers crackle with a sudden announcement. She has learned to sit near the exit, to
scan every face that enters the bullpen, to keep her back to the wall. The trading floor is a forest
of screens and noise, and she cannot control the sounds she hears. A loud burst of laughter from a
nearby pod—someone celebrating a win—makes her stomach drop; the happiness feels like a mockery, a
reminder of the normal life she once had, the kind of happy families she sees in the park on
weekends and now avoids because the sight makes her feel raw and dangerously exposed. She has
started leaving the office at odd hours, taking the longest possible route home to avoid being
followed, though there is no one following her. Her partner has noticed she no longer wants to walk
to the corner market after dinner, and that she has stopped ordering wine with meals, even though
she used to enjoy a glass. The smell alone is enough to trigger a flutter of panic, and she has
learned to steer clear of any restaurant where the bar is near the door.

What finally brought Mai to therapy was not the fear itself but the exhaustion. She had spent years
organizing her life around the possibility that he might come back, that any noise, any smell, any
laughing family sitting on a patio might be a sign that the ground was about to give way. The
memories kept coming, and each time they felt just as real, just as unbelievable. She wanted to
understand why her own mind would not let her rest, and she hoped—though she was afraid to say it
aloud—that she might one day walk past a bar without her pulse quickening, and not have to check
over her shoulder every time a door slammed shut.
