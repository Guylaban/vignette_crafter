Amina was twenty-two when the airstrike hit the hospital where she worked as a trauma nurse. The
building shook around her, and she ran toward the emergency ward; the next thing she remembered was
the heat, the smell of diesel and blood, and a man screaming for his son. She had been pulling a
tourniquet tighter when the second explosion threw her across the corridor. She never saw the man
again, but she could still feel the weight of his blood against her palms, warm and slick. She told
herself she had done everything she could, but the thought never settled.

Over the next three years, that moment refused to stay in the past. When Amina caught a whiff of
antiseptic or heard a sudden loud noise in the quiet of the night shift, her chest would tighten and
the room would dissolve into the same smoke-thick air. She would be back in the corridor, her hands
moving without her permission, her heart pounding as if the bomb were still falling. The memory
always came with a wave of grief so heavy she had to sit down, and often she would feel so
overwhelmed by the emotions that she would have to stop whatever she was doing—once, she left a
patient’s bed mid‑assessment and locked herself in the supply closet until her hands stopped
shaking.

What she did not talk about, not even with her partner, was the humiliation she carried like a
second skin. She had come to believe that she was fundamentally inadequate, that there was something
wrong with her as a person for having survived when others did not. The defective feeling made her
avoid conversations about the war; when her boyfriend asked about her day, she would say she was
tired and change the subject. She told herself that if she ever spoke about the event, he would see
the brokenness in her, and her humiliation would become visible.

Yet the avoidance did not protect her. The more she tried not to think about the hospital, the more
the memory returned—vivid, unbidden, happening right now. During a quiet moment at work, she would
catch herself drifting into a fantasy of a different life, a world where she was a nurse in a safe
city, where no one screamed and no one bled. But the fantasy never lasted. The moment she landed
back in reality, the corridor would flash again, and with it came the familiar shame: *You are
inadequate. You should have saved him.*

Amina’s relationship had begun to fray. She stopped telling her partner when she had a nightmare
because explaining it meant re‑entering the memory, and re‑entering it reinforced her belief that
she was defective. He noticed her pulling away and asked what was wrong; she said nothing was wrong,
then retreated into her own head, into another world where the explosion had never happened. She
began to dread the evening commute home, because the quiet of the car gave her mind space to wander
back to that corridor. Soon she was taking longer shifts, not because she was dedicated, but because
staying busy kept the thoughts at bay. It only made the memories more intense during the rare
moments she stopped.

Last month, during a Code Blue, the alarm sounded and Amina froze. She felt the floor tip, saw the
corridor, and heard the man’s voice again. She could not move. A colleague grabbed her arm and
guided her through the protocol, but later, in the locker room, Amina wept with a grief she could
not name. That night, she finally told her partner that she thought she needed help. She was
terrified—terrified that speaking the words would make the humiliation real, that a therapist would
confirm what she already knew: that there was something wrong with her. But she was also exhausted.
The fantasy worlds she used to escape into were no longer offering shelter; the corridors kept
finding her.
