Priya, a thirty-year-old software engineer, grew up in a home where her father’s moods shifted with
the smell of alcohol and sweat. She was seven when he first struck her—backhand across the face
after a long evening of drinking, his breath sour and his body reeking of stale exertion. The next
time he forced her to finish a bowl of overly spiced dal that burned her throat, the taste of cumin
and chili lingering long after she fled to her room. Over the years the physical abuse repeated, but
the memories never came in a clear sequence. She could not order the blows, the shouts, the smells;
each recollection arrived as a tidal wave of emotion that left her gasping, and she often pushed it
away before she reached the end.

Now, in her own home with her husband, Priya finds that certain triggers still ambush her. A
colleague microwaves leftover curry at the office, and the sharp scent of cumin hits her—suddenly
her heart pounds, her throat tightens, and the fragmented memory of her father’s angry face flickers
before she shuts it down. That same taste, that familiar mix of sweat and alcohol, yanks her back
into the past and leaves her trembling at her desk, unable to concentrate on her code. The trigger
not only forces the memory back but also spikes her sense of threat: after such encounters she
cannot relax, even in her cubicle, and she spends the rest of the day checking her office door again
and again, ensuring it is locked.

At home, the pattern is worse. She lies awake at night, her mind circling through the disjointed
images she cannot complete, and she gets up repeatedly to check the front door and the windows. This
hypervigilance drains her, and in her exhausted state the intrusive memories become more vivid and
harder to block. When she does manage to retrieve a piece of the past—her father’s belt, the smell
of his sweat—she becomes acutely sensitive to any hint of alcohol or body odor in her surroundings,
scanning her husband’s breath when he returns from the gym, or flinching if a guest carries the
faint aroma of beer.

The cycle tightens over months. A whiff of sweat from a passerby triggers the memory of her father’s
rage; the memory floods her with fear; that fear makes her check the locks three times before bed;
the checking keeps her from sleeping; the sleep deprivation leaves her more vulnerable to the next
triggered memory. She has stopped eating at restaurants that serve heavily spiced food, and she
avoids social gatherings where alcohol might be present. At work, she keeps her door closed and
declines team lunches. Her husband notices the ritualistic locking but she cannot explain it—she
simply feels unsafe unless every latch is confirmed.

What finally brings Priya to therapy is a morning when she found herself crying at her desk after a
colleague walked past with a faint scent of stale beer, and she could not stop the flood of images
or the urgent need to check the windows in her remote workspace. She is afraid that the past will
never stay locked away, and she hopes—though she hardly dares say it—that she might one day sit
through a workday without tasting cumin and fear.
