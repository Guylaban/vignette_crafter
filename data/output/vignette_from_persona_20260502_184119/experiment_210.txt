Leila was thirty-four, divorced, and living alone in a small migrant camp near the agricultural
fields where she worked six days a week. The event that shattered her life had happened two years
earlier, during the harvest season. She had been picking tomatoes when a foreman accused a younger
woman—a mother from Guatemala—of stealing water. Within minutes the argument turned violent; the
foreman shoved the woman to the ground, and her baby, who had been strapped to her chest in a cloth
carrier, began to scream. Leila watched the woman’s head hit the irrigation pipe, watched the blood
pool in the dust, watched the baby’s tiny fists flail as it wailed. The foreman walked away. The
baby kept crying. By the time anyone called for help, the woman was unconscious. She survived, but
Leila never forgot the sound of that cry.

After that day, Leila found herself unable to tolerate certain conversations. If anyone at the camp
mentioned the foreman, or the woman, or even the harvest, she felt her chest tighten and her mind go
blank. She began to avoid the other workers’ gatherings, walking home early and eating alone. The
worst trigger, though, was the sound of a baby crying. Whenever a child in the camp started to fuss,
Leila’s hands would shake and she would feel a wave of nausea. To cope, she developed rituals: she
would scrub her small room until her knuckles ached, organizing her few possessions in neat rows.
She told herself that if she could control the order of her space, she could control the chaos of
her mind. She also forced herself to think only positive thoughts—*I am safe, this is fine, nothing
bad is happening*—and when the intrusive images returned, she pushed them out of her mind with all
her strength.

Yet the suppression and the forced optimism only made the world feel more alien. Leila had started
to believe that people are not what they seem. The foreman had seemed ordinary; the other workers
had stood by and done nothing. Now every person who looked at her sideways—a man loitering near the
bus stop, a supervisor watching too long—seemed capable of hidden cruelty. She lost her faith in
people, in life, even in the God she had prayed to as a child. She felt she no longer belonged
anywhere, not among the workers who gossiped about her solitude, not in the camp where every
infant’s wail sent her back to that moment, and certainly not in the country that had promised a
fresh start and delivered only dust and blood.

During the workday, Leila would see workers she did not recognize and assume they were up to
something. When a man repeatedly glanced her way, she took a different path through the fields, her
heart pounding. Those suspicious glances fed her conviction that everyone was hiding something; she
would then retreat further into herself, cleaning her tools with obsessive precision during breaks
rather than joining the other women for lunch. The more she cleaned and suppressed, the more
disconnected she felt, and the more she needed to clean to quiet the fear that she was utterly alone
in a predatory world.

Leila came to therapy because her boss had warned her that if she kept leaving the packing shed
whenever a baby started crying, she would lose her job. That was what finally pushed her to seek
help—the concrete threat of losing the only structure she had left. What frightened her most was the
possibility that she would never be able to tolerate the sound of a baby again, that she would
remain trapped in a world where everyone she met might be hiding something terrible. She hoped, with
a fragile and almost silent voice, that someone could help her stop running.
