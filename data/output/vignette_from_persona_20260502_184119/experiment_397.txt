Mai was twenty-five and working late at her downtown office when a man followed her into the
stairwell and forced her into a maintenance closet. She was held there for nearly four hours, bound
and gagged, while he paced and muttered about her having seen too much—though she had never seen his
face. He left before dawn, and the building's security footage showed nothing useful; the police
never identified him. Mai told herself she should have taken the elevator, should have checked
behind her before entering the stairwell. Those thoughts burrowed in immediately, but at first she
tried to push them aside and return to her life as a tax consultant, reviewing spreadsheets and
advising clients on deductions.

Within weeks, the world around her began to feel alive with threat. Any unexpected movement in her
peripheral vision—a colleague reaching for a file, a stranger stepping into her sightline on the
sidewalk—made her flinch as though she were about to be grabbed. She started sitting with her back
against the wall of her cubicle, repositioning her chair so that she faced the entrance and could
see everyone who approached. At home, she lay awake listening for sounds that did not belong: the
creak of a floorboard, the click of a lock, the whisper of footsteps in the hallway. Every scrape of
the neighbour's key in the door sent her heart racing.

These moments of hypervigilance did not stay contained. Each time Mai startled or froze in response
to a nearby movement, the wave of fear was followed instantly by a harsh internal recrimination:
*Why didn't I notice sooner? Why did I let myself be taken by surprise?* The incident in the
stairwell had happened because of the way she acted—she was certain of that now—and every reflexive
startle confirmed that she still was not doing enough to stay safe. The man who took her had never
been caught, had faced no consequences for what he did, and that absence of accountability made Mai
feel as though the danger was ongoing, unresolved, and somehow her fault to manage. She began to
feel ashamed of her own body, of how it had been helpless and bound, and any reminder of that
helplessness—the feeling of a stranger's hand brushing past her in a crowd, the pressure of a tight
collar—brought a hot, grinding shame that she could not explain to anyone.

Over the months, Mai's avoidance became woven into her daily routines. She took the stairs only if
absolutely necessary and then only with her back pressed against the wall, scanning each landing
before she moved. She asked her manager if she could relocate her desk so that she no longer faced
away from the door—a request she framed as an ergonomic preference. She stopped going to after-work
drinks with colleagues because the unpredictable movement of a crowded bar triggered too many
startle reflexes, and each one replayed her self-blame. The cost was concrete: she had once been on
track for a senior associate role, but her social withdrawal and the repeated absences she took to
recover from sleepless nights began to erode her reputation as reliable.

What finally brought Mai to therapy was a morning when she arrived at her office, saw a maintenance
worker rolling a cart down the hall, and actually dropped to her knees behind her desk before she
could stop herself. A colleague found her there, pale and trembling, and Mai could not pretend it
was nothing. She came to the first session frightened that she was losing her mind, but more
frightened that the therapist would confirm what she already believed—that the event was her fault,
that her body was tainted by what had happened, and that because no one was ever held accountable,
the threat was permanent. She was hoping, without quite believing it possible, for a single night
without listening for the door.
