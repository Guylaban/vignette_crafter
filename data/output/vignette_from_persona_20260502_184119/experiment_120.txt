Robert is a 57-year-old interior designer who was involved in a life-threatening car accident
fourteen months ago. He had been driving to a client consultation in a suburban neighborhood when a
sedan ran a red light and struck his vehicle broadside on the passenger side. His car spun into a
utility pole, and he was trapped in the crumpled wreckage for nearly forty-five minutes while
emergency crews worked to extricate him. He sustained multiple rib fractures, a collapsed lung, and
a complex fracture of his right hand — the hand he uses to sketch, measure, and select fabric
samples. For several minutes during the extrication, he believed he would not survive. He remembers
the sound of twisting metal, the shriek of the impact, and the paramedic saying “stay with me”
through the shattered window. The specific details of that afternoon — the intersection, the angle
of the sun, the way the other car seemed to come from nowhere — remain vivid in a way that ordinary
memories do not.

In the months following the accident, Robert returned to work gradually. His hand required two
surgeries, and he spent weeks in physical therapy regaining fine motor control. He still notices a
slight tremor when he holds a pencil, and his grip fatigues more quickly than before. He has stopped
taking on projects that require him to drive to unfamiliar neighborhoods, and he has begun declining
residential clients who live along the highway where his accident occurred. His professional life,
once a source of pride and identity, now feels constrained by circumstances he cannot control. He
worries that his reputation among colleagues is suffering, as he has turned down several high-
profile projects.

Robert now finds that certain experiences are difficult for him. When he is driving and sees a car
that has been damaged in an accident — even a minor fender-bender pulled over on the shoulder — his
attention narrows to that image. He notices it before he can look away. Car sounds also affect him:
the squeal of tires at an intersection, the rapid honk of a horn, the low rumble of an engine
accelerating quickly. These sounds seem to occupy his awareness fully, leaving him momentarily
removed from whatever he had been doing. Conversations about car accidents also pose a difficulty. A
colleague mentioning a fender-bender or a news report about a highway collision is enough to make
Robert withdraw from the discussion.

Alongside these experiences, Robert has developed a set of convictions about himself that he did not
hold before the accident. He has come to feel that his body is no longer trustworthy. The hand that
once moved with precision across a drafting table now feels foreign to him, capable of failing at
any moment. He believes that he has permanently changed for the worse — that the person he was
before the accident is gone and will not return. When he looks at his reflection, he sometimes does
not recognize the man looking back. He does not know himself anymore, and this sense of identity
loss sits at the edge of his awareness throughout the day. He describes feeling hollowed out, as
though the accident stripped away something essential that cannot be restored. These two sets of
experiences — the moments of intense reaction to accident-related stimuli, and the ongoing sense of
being irrevocably changed — coexist in Robert’s daily life without one seeming to cause the other.
They are simply both present.

What ultimately brought Robert to seek help was a moment during a client meeting two weeks ago. The
client was describing a renovation of a sunroom, and in the middle of the conversation she mentioned
a car accident she had witnessed the previous week. Robert felt his throat close, his palms dampen,
and he excused himself to the restroom where he stood gripping the sink for several minutes. He
realized then that something was not resolving on its own. What Robert fears most is that this
version of himself — the one who startles at a sound, who cannot trust his own hands, who does not
recognize his own face — is permanent. He hopes that therapy might help him find a way back to the
person he used to be, though he is not certain that person exists anymore.
