Margaret has worked as a model for over four decades, and at sixty-six she still booked the
occasional editorial for women her age—until the accident. She was driving home from a late shoot,
exhausted, when she failed to see a cyclist turning through the intersection. The impact threw him
across the hood; he survived, but with a broken pelvis and internal injuries that left him unable to
walk for months. Margaret was not physically hurt, but from that moment her body began to live as
though it were the one that had been struck.

In the weeks afterward, she found she could not relax, even at home with the door locked. Her
muscles stayed tight, her jaw clenched, and every sudden noise—a car horn, a bicycle bell through an
open window—sent her heart racing. She had difficulty concentrating during bookings. In the middle
of a pose, her attention would slide to the memory of the impact, and she would lose focus, missing
the photographer’s directions. Her body felt constantly on alert, waiting for another catastrophe,
though she knew she was safe.

What frightened Margaret most was what this constant vigilance seemed to prove about her character.
Because she could not settle her body or her mind, she became certain that somebody else—someone
more composed, more worthy—would not have gotten into this situation in the first place. The
hyperarousal, she believed, was a mirror of her fundamental incompetence. She felt permanently
humiliated by what happened, as though the accident had stripped away the poise she had built a
career on, and now her twitchy, jumpy body broadcast her shame to everyone. And under it all, guilt
gnawed: she would never forgive herself for not doing more to stop the accident—for not being more
alert, for not seeing the cyclist sooner. The tension in her chest felt like a punishment she
deserved.

The more her body stayed on edge, the more the self-criticism sharpened. Driving became impossible;
she stopped completely, relying on hired cars or friends to take her to jobs, which limited which
bookings she could accept. She avoided the intersection and any news story about cycling accidents,
but the alertness followed her indoors. At a recent shoot for a retirement-living campaign, the
photographer asked her to look calm and content. She tried, but her grip on the prop teacup was
white-knuckled, and she kept scanning the studio windows for movement. She left early, apologizing.
Afterwards she spent the evening replaying the accident, convinced that her inability to relax was
proof she should never have been behind the wheel that night.

Months passed, and the cycle tightened. Her daughter finally insisted she see someone, and Margaret
agreed not because she believed she deserved relief, but because she could no longer bear the cost
of the constant alertness on her work and her few relationships. She came to therapy with her hands
clasped in her lap, describing the accident in a flat voice, then adding, “I know I’m supposed to
move on, but I can’t even sit still without feeling like something’s about to go wrong—and that just
proves I never should have been driving at all.” She is most afraid that the therapist will agree
with her—that she is permanently damaged—but underneath that fear is a fragile hope that someone
might show her a way to be less afraid of her own body’s signals without having to erase the guilt
she carries for the man she hurt.
