Linda was fifty-eight when she finally called the clinic, six years after her husband Mark was
killed in a hit-and-run. She had watched from their front porch as a car struck him while he crossed
the street at dusk—saw the impact, heard the thud, smelled the acrid mix of gasoline and warm blood
as she ran to him. That moment lives in fragments: when she tries deliberately to recall what
happened, she can only pull up pieces—the color of the car, the angle of the streetlamp—but other
parts refuse to surface. Yet sometimes, without warning, a whiff of diesel or the smell of sweat on
a stranger’s coat will drag her back whole, her chest seizing before she even knows why she is
afraid. Anniversaries are the worst: the date itself, the shift in light as autumn deepens, the way
the air feels at that hour—all of it builds into a dread that starts weeks early.

She has not worked since Mark died; she used to do bookkeeping from home, but after the accident she
could not concentrate and stopped taking calls. She lives alone now in the same house, rarely
leaving except for groceries. Every trip outside is a gauntlet. The sight of a damaged car—even a
minor fender bender on the side of the road—freezes her mid-step, her body bracing as if she is
about to be hit. Ambulances are worse: the siren, the flashing lights, the sense of emergency yank
her into a state of alert that takes hours to settle. And because she is always scanning for danger,
she notices these triggers more—a distant siren becomes a focus, a car with a dent draws her eye.
The threat response is immediate: she startles at unexpected movement, a person stepping around a
corner or a bird flying up suddenly, and she finds herself checking the doors and windows repeatedly
each night, sometimes three or four times before she can try to sleep. Sleep itself is broken; she
lies awake replaying fragments, or wakes with her heart pounding, certain she heard something in the
dark.

The connection between memory and threat is equally tight. When the olfactory recall hits—that smell
of gasoline or iron—it comes with a full-body surge of fear, as if the accident is happening again.
That fear then sharpens her vigilance: after a spontaneous memory, she becomes more watchful,
checking the locks more often, avoiding the porch altogether for days. And those memories also prime
her to notice triggers: a smell of diesel in the grocery store parking lot makes her suddenly aware
of every car around her, every ambulance siren in the distance. The cycle feeds itself. Memory
triggers threat; threat makes her hyper-vigilant; hypervigilance makes her sensitive to any
reminder; those reminders then bring back more memory. She has come to believe that she can never
let her guard down, that if she stops watching she will be caught off guard by something even worse.

What finally brought her to seek help was the run-up to the sixth anniversary. She had barely slept
for three weeks, and she found herself unable to step onto the porch at all—the place where she had
stood and watched Mark die. She was terrified that this would never stop, that she would spend the
rest of her life trapped in that moment. She hoped that maybe someone could help her remember Mark
without the fear, without the smell and the sound and the sudden jolt of terror. She was not sure it
was possible, but she was desperate enough to try.
