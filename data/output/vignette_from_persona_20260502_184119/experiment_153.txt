Priya was twenty-two and working as a delivery driver for a regional courier service when she was
mugged one evening in a strip-mall parking lot. A man approached her as she unlocked her car, told
her to hand over her wallet and phone, and pressed something hard against her ribs. She complied
instantly, frozen. The man fled, and she stood there for a long moment before calling the police.
When they arrived, she could barely speak; she kept thinking she should have done something—shouted,
run, fought back. That feeling of having been forced into helpless obedience lodged itself inside
her, and the image of the man’s hand on her shoulder remained as a single snapshot that replayed
without warning.

In the weeks that followed, Priya became acutely sensitive to any situation where she felt pressured
or controlled. Being told what to do by a manager, or even having a customer hurry her, sent a spike
of alarm through her chest. This loss of autonomy was her most potent trigger. She also began to
react to the presence of authority figures: seeing a police car in her rearview mirror made her
heart race, even when she had done nothing wrong. And she started scanning crowds for people who
looked suspicious—anyone loitering, anyone who made eye contact a second too long. Each perceived
threat ignited not only fear but also an involuntary return to that parking lot moment. Sometimes
she would hear the man’s low voice in her head, a fragment of auditory recall layered over an
emotion so intense that her body reacted before her mind caught up.

These triggers drove her maladaptive strategies. To avoid the feeling of being forced into
submission, she began refusing deliveries to unfamiliar addresses and eventually stopped driving
altogether. She told herself the robbery was not that bad, that she was overreacting, a form of
magical thinking that let her push the memory away for a few hours. At night, when she could not
relax and woke repeatedly to check her locks, she smoked marijuana to dull the hypervigilance. The
escape route planning she did before entering any building—scanning exits, noting escape paths—only
reinforced her sense that danger was everywhere, and that isolation was safer than being with others
who might not understand. Her inaction guilt was relentless: she could not forgive herself for not
fighting back, and this self-blame made her more likely to avoid driving, because every time she got
behind the wheel she replayed her inaction and felt the shame all over again. The helplessness she
now carried—a conviction that she could not stop bad things from happening—deepened her threat
perception, making her perpetually alert for signs of coercion or hostility.

Over the months, the vicious cycles tightened. Memory triggered threat: whenever the auditory recall
surfaced, she would wake in the night to check the apartment. Threat then amplified negative
appraisals: her constant scanning made her feel isolated, set apart from friends who could relax.
The threat also generated new triggers—her hyperawareness of suspicious behavior made her see
suspicious behavior everywhere, feeding the loop. In turn, her maladaptive strategies, especially
avoidance of driving, made her more dependent on family for rides, which meant more authority
figures telling her what to do, which triggered her again. She began using marijuana heavily to numb
the guilt and the 4 a.m. replays, but the drug use also left her groggy and more vulnerable to
perceiving threats in ordinary sounds.

What finally brought Priya to a clinic was a near-collision: she had driven for the first time in
two months to attend a family dinner, saw a police car, panicked, ran a red light, and barely
avoided an accident. She now fears that her own mind is conspiring against her—that she will never
feel safe again, that she will always be trapped by the moment she did nothing. What she hopes for,
quietly, is to be able to deliver a single package without needing to map three exits first.
