George, a 76-year-old European American man and a retired hotel manager, still lived in the same
suburban town where he had run a mid-sized hotel for nearly three decades. The traumatic event had
occurred three years ago, during his last year on the job. A maintenance worker had accidentally
mixed two cleaning agents in the hotel’s laundry room, releasing a cloud of chloramine gas. George,
who had been doing a walk-through on that floor, inhaled a lungful before the ventilation system
kicked in. He was not hospitalized, but the coughing, burning sensation, and the acrid, bleach-like
smell stayed with him.

In the weeks that followed, the memory of the event came back not as a coherent story but in
fractured, disconnected pieces—the sudden sting in his throat, the sight of the worker’s panicked
face, then nothing. Sometimes he would feel the full terror of that moment without any accompanying
image, as if his body remembered even when his mind could not. And at odd moments, especially near
cleaning supplies or in certain hallways, that chemical smell would return, vivid and real,
triggering a surge of helplessness. He began to believe his body had betrayed him. He could no
longer trust its reactions—his heart would race, his chest tighten, his throat close up—and he came
to see his own body as an enemy that kept sounding alarms long after the danger had passed. These
negative appraisals deepened his sense of helplessness: bad things could happen anytime, and he
could do nothing to stop them.

That feeling of being at the mercy of his own body made him constantly alert. He felt on guard even
inside his own home, especially when he heard unexpected or loud sounds—a car backfiring, a door
slamming, a dropped pan. Those sounds would jolt him, and the sudden alertness would summon the
fragmented memory pieces: the acrid smell, the burning, the worker’s face. The memory intruded on
the threat, and the threat, in turn, made the memories more vivid and more frequent. He found
himself only able to feel truly safe in a very limited number of places—his bedroom, a specific
chair in his living room, and nowhere else. The hotel itself he now avoided entirely, even though it
had been his workplace for decades; he had retired early on medical grounds. But even at home, the
world outside felt dangerous.

To manage these constant threats, George developed strategies. He kept the television or music
playing loudly almost all day, using sensory distraction to drown out the silence that might let a
random sound startle him. He also monitored his own thoughts constantly, scanning for any sign that
he might be “going crazy”—a sudden flash of fear, a stray image, a scent. When he became aware that
he was alert, he would turn up the volume or retreat to his safe chair. Yet these strategies had a
dark side: they reinforced the belief that his body and mind were unreliable. Every time he needed
to distract himself or check his thoughts, it felt like further proof that he could not trust
himself, that he remained helpless. The more he used these strategies, the more his negative
appraisals strengthened, and the more on guard he felt.

A few weeks before he sought help, George was sitting in his living room when the mail truck
backfired outside. The sharp crack sent him into a full panic: his chest seized, he smelled
chlorine, and he spent the next hour pacing, unable to shake the feeling that something terrible was
about to happen. He realized that his life had shrunk to a single chair, that he could no longer
visit his daughter on the other side of town, and that even the safety of his home felt fragile.
What frightened him most was the thought that his own body would never stop reacting, that he would
always be a prisoner of a memory that had no clear picture but all the force of a live event. He
came to therapy hoping to find a way to trust himself again, even though he was not sure that was
possible.
