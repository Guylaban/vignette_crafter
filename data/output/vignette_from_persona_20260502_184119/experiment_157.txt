Marcus was twenty-four when his girlfriend Jenna came home drunk, the sour smell of whiskey
billowing off her breath. She had always been volatile, but that night she was a different person.
She accused him of things he hadn't done, and when he tried to calm her, she swung. He still
remembers the sound of his own head hitting the tile, the metallic taste of blood, and her voice
screaming something he could never quite make out. The visible bruises on his face took weeks to
fade, but the invisible ones dug deeper.

In the months afterward, he found himself reacting to things that had never bothered him before. The
first time he caught the faintest whiff of alcohol on a witness during an interrogation, his stomach
lurched into nausea and the memory of Jenna’s shouts filled his ears—vivid, uninvited, unmistakable.
That moment, standing there trying to look professional, he felt something crack inside: he no
longer believed people were good. Anyone could flip. Anyone could hurt him. The nausea and the smell
became a direct line to that belief, and he started seeing danger everywhere. As a detective, he
encountered wounded bodies daily, and every visible injury on a victim sent him reeling back to his
own battered face, reinforcing his sense that he was permanently separate from the innocent world.

His distrust grew into a quiet certainty that others were not what they seemed, and that certainty
would not let the memory rest. He replayed the night over and over, trying to find the moment he
should have left, the sign he missed. The memory was so clear—the sound of her voice, the pattern of
the floor tiles—that it felt like a recording he could not stop. To keep it from overwhelming him,
he started numbing his emotions. He avoided relaxing because stillness felt like an open door to the
past. He avoided physical contact entirely, even flinching when his wife Sarah reached for his hand
across the dinner table. He told himself it was just a habit, but the numbness spread, and soon he
felt like a stranger in his own marriage.

The irony was that his very attempts to stay safe made everything worse. By refusing to let his
guard down, he became hypervigilant to every possible trigger: the sudden onset of nausea, the
glimpse of a scar on someone else’s skin, the faintest trace of alcohol at a work event. Each time
he tensed, he felt his alienation deepen, and the memory of Jenna roared back with fresh intensity.
He was caught in a loop he could not name. Sarah tried to talk to him, but he dismissed her
concerns, certain she could not understand. The only thing that made him feel in control was staying
busy, staying distracted, staying untouched.

It was after a case where he had to photograph a woman’s facial fractures that he came home,
showered, and sat on the edge of the bed unable to move. Sarah sat beside him and did not touch him.
That night, he admitted he needed help. He was terrified of what it would mean to let the memories
surface without his walls up, but he was more afraid of losing the one person who still reached for
him.
