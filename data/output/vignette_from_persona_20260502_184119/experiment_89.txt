The accident happened on a Tuesday evening in late November. Tane was driving home from the
fabrication shop where he worked as a welder, his shoulders aching from a long shift spent bent over
steel beams. He stopped at a four-way intersection, checked both ways, and started across. The other
driver came from his left, running a red light at nearly fifty miles an hour. The impact hit Tane's
driver-side door, spinning his truck half a turn before it came to rest against a utility pole. He
remembers the smell first—the sharp, sweet stench of bourbon that poured through the shattered
window from the other driver's car, mixing with the smell of gasoline and hot metal. He remembers
the sound of twisting steel and breaking glass, and the way his body was pinned against the door,
his ribs pressed so hard against the seat frame he thought they would crack. He remembers the
helplessness of being trapped, unable to move his legs, convinced the fuel line had ruptured and the
truck would explode. Emergency responders cut him out with the Jaws of Life. He spent three days in
the hospital with two broken ribs, a fractured clavicle, and a deep contusion on his right thigh
that still ached long after the bruises faded.

In the months that followed, Tane found that the memory of the crash did not behave like an ordinary
memory. It came without warning, and when it came it was not a recollection so much as a re-
inhabiting. He would be standing at his welding bench, the torch in his hand, and suddenly his body
would seize as if it were back in the driver's seat—his shoulders hunching forward, his right knee
pressing against an imaginary floorboard, his torso twisting toward an impact that was not there.
The physical pain would return too, a sharp ache in his ribs, a deep throb in his shoulder, a heavy
bruised sensation in his thigh, exactly where he had been injured, as though the crash were still
happening inside his body. These episodes were not memories he observed; they were events he re-
entered fully, across every sense. He could smell the bourbon, hear the screech of metal, feel the
shock of the collision travel through his frame—and in the wake of this total reliving, his senses
stayed locked on alert, scanning for anything in his environment that matched. The rumble of a
diesel truck on the highway outside the shop would make his chest tighten. The hiss of compressed
air from a colleague's pneumatic tool would send a jolt through him. A whiff of alcohol from a
passerby would stop him cold, his mouth going dry, his heart hammering before his mind had
registered what the smell meant. The sight of a crumpled fender in a junkyard or a dented door in a
parking lot would pull him instantly back into the memory, the two images fusing until he could not
distinguish what he was seeing from what he had lived through. Each trigger seemed to feed on the
memory, and the memory fed back into the triggers, tightening a loop he could not find his way out
of.

At work, Tane learned to manage, though the cost was mounting. He positioned his welding station
with his back to the roll-up door so he would not see headlights sweep across the shop floor. He
stopped taking his lunch break in the staff room because a colleague occasionally brought in a beer,
and the smell, even from across the room, would set him on edge. He took a longer route home to
avoid the intersection where the crash had happened, adding twenty minutes to his commute each way.
His wife, Lani, noticed that he no longer wanted to drive her to the grocery store on weekends, and
that he flinched when she opened a bottle of cooking wine. She did not push him on it, but her
silence felt heavy, and Tane could feel a distance growing between them that he did not know how to
bridge.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment three weeks ago at the shop. A new apprentice had
left an empty bourbon bottle in the break room trash—Tane caught the scent the moment he walked in,
and before he could stop it, the memory hit him full force. He dropped to his knees behind a storage
rack, his body locked in the crash position, his ribs aching, his throat tight, unable to breathe or
speak for nearly a minute. His foreman found him there and insisted he see someone. Tane agreed, not
because he thought talking would help, but because he was afraid that the next time it happened, he
might not be able to get back up. He is afraid of what the loop is doing to him, and more afraid
still that it will never stop.
