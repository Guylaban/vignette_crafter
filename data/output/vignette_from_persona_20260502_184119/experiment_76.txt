Mateo, a 22-year-old product manager, was a civilian caught in the crossfire of an armed conflict in
his home country two years ago. He was walking home from a market when a sudden volley of gunfire
erupted from two directions, pinning him against a wall for what felt like an hour. Shots cracked
from windows and alleyways; bodies fell nearby. He pressed himself flat, counting the shots, certain
the next one would hit him. When the shooting finally stopped, he ran through the smoke, past
shattered glass and blood, and did not stop until he reached his aunt’s apartment. That night, and
for many nights after, he could not relax his shoulders or slow his heart.

Now living in a quiet American city, Mateo works as a product manager in a bright open-plan office.
But his body has not left that alley. He feels constantly tense, as if bracing for impact—his jaw
clenched, his stomach tight, his muscles coiled. Any unexpected movement nearby makes him flinch: a
coworker stepping suddenly into his peripheral vision, a door swinging open too fast, a hand
reaching across his desk. His heart lurches, and he scans the room for threats before he can remind
himself he is safe. This hypervigilance is exhausting, but what exhausts him more is the way it
drives him to do things he knows are not rational. When the tension becomes unbearable, Mateo finds
himself checking his pulse over and over, pressing two fingers to his wrist, trying to convince
himself that his heart rate is normal, that he is not about to die. He sometimes asks a colleague,
“Do I look okay? My face—does it look pale?” The reassurance lasts only minutes before the vigilance
starts again, because the very act of checking his body makes him more aware of every sensation—a
twinge, a flutter, a tightness—which he then interprets as a sign of danger. He has also begun to
hurt himself physically to release the overwhelming feelings: pressing his fingernails into his
palms during meetings, or, in the privacy of his bathroom at night, scratching his forearm with the
edge of a comb until the sting cuts through the fear.

The cycle tightens week by week. The more alert his body remains, the more urgently he reaches for
these strategies to calm himself. But each strategy—reassurance seeking, body monitoring, self-
harm—only sharpens his sense that something is wrong with him, that he must be in danger if he needs
to check so frantically. He has started avoiding the subway because the sudden jolts and crowds
trigger his startle response. At work, he sits with his back to the wall and watches the door, but
he cannot avoid the sudden movements of others. Last week, when a coworker tapped his shoulder
unexpectedly, Mateo spun around so violently that he knocked over his water bottle, then spent the
next hour checking his pulse and asking three separate people if he looked pale. His manager noticed
and asked if he was okay; Mateo lied and said he had not slept well.

What finally brought Mateo to therapy was an incident three days ago. In a meeting, a teammate stood
up abruptly to gesture at a whiteboard. Mateo flinched so hard he scraped his thumb on the edge of
his laptop, drawing blood. As he cleaned the cut in the restroom, he found himself pressing his
thumbnail into the wound, deeper and deeper, until the pain felt like the only real thing in the
room. He saw the look of alarm on his own face in the mirror, and for the first time he admitted he
could not manage this alone. He is terrified that the cycle will never end—that his body will always
be on alert, and that he will keep hurting himself just to feel something other than fear. But he is
also, underneath that hope, afraid that he is broken beyond repair, that the alley he fled has
followed him here and will never let go.
