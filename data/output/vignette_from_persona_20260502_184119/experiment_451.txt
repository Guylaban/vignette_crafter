responders cut him out of the wreckage; he spent three days in intensive care with a collapsed lung,
a fractured pelvis, and internal bleeding. He was told he was minutes from dying. In the months
after the accident, Mateo’s memory of the collision remained stubbornly out of reach. When he tried
to reconstruct what happened, he could only access disconnected fragments—the glare of headlights,
the smell of gasoline, the cold of the hydraulic cutters against his hip—that refused to form a
coherent timeline. He felt as though he was only remembering pieces of the story while the core of
the event stayed hidden.

What Mateo could recall with sharp clarity was the aftermath: the driver had no insurance and faced
only a minor citation; the case was closed within weeks. This injustice burrowed into him. He began
to believe that his life had been destroyed in that intersection—not just his body, but his future
as a musician, his sense of safety, his belief in any kind of fairness. And when the anger rose, it
came with a frightening intensity. He was terrified that he would lose control, that the rage would
spill out in a way he could not take back. Because he feared that loss of control, he started to
avoid any situation that might trigger that anger. He stopped talking about the accident altogether,
changing the subject whenever a bandmate asked how he was healing. He also began to keep himself
constantly surrounded by people—after every gig, he insisted on going out with the whole crew,
staying up late at bars or at someone’s apartment, never letting the conversation quiet down long
enough for his own thoughts to surface. The constant socializing drained him, but it felt safer than
being alone with the fragments of what happened.

proof that the system had failed him, that his story could never be fully told. At the same time,
his belief that his life was destroyed made him deliberately avoid thinking about the event, which
in turn made the memory even harder to retrieve deliberately. The less he tried to recall, the more
disjointed the fragments became. And the constant socializing—meant to keep the thoughts at

the events together, he was actively preventing his memory from cohering.

other musicians over, or he went to open-mic nights just to be in a crowd. He refused to drive past
the intersection where the accident happened, though he never said why. The only time he was alone

water, heart racing, unable to move until the hot water ran cold. His playing suffered—he was
distracted during rehearsals, his timing off, his lyrics suddenly full of images he did not mean to

the picture they make is not as broken as it feels.
