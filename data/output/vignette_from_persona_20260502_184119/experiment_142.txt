Ethan was thirty-four, a firefighter in a mid-sized Midwestern city, married for seven years to a
schoolteacher named Laura. Four years ago, he had volunteered for a two-month deployment as a
civilian relief worker in a conflict zone—a decision he had made believing his emergency training
would be useful. The specifics he still avoided revisiting: a convoy ambush near a checkpoint, then
the chaotic aftermath of an explosion in a crowded market where he had helped pull bodies from
debris. What stayed with him most was the moment when a local woman grabbed his arm, begging for
help, and he had felt a surge of protective warmth—only to see her collapse seconds later from
shrapnel wounds. That surge of positive emotion, he had since concluded, had made him less alert,
more vulnerable. He had learned to push those feelings away immediately.

Back home, the routines of firefighting helped at first. But soon he noticed that even inside his
own house, he was on guard. In the living room, he positioned his chair so that he could see both
doorways. He would tense whenever a stranger approached him at the grocery store—a neighbor asking
for directions felt like a potential threat. In every building, his eyes automatically scanned for
exits; he had become so practiced that he noted the fire escape routes within seconds of entering a
restaurant or a hardware store. His colleagues at the firehouse began to comment on his rigid
posture during downtime.

The maladaptive strategies he adopted only made things worse. He stopped running—his longtime stress
release—because the open streets felt exposed, full of unpredictable people. Without that outlet,
his vigilance sharpened rather than softened. He developed a habit of sitting near any exit when he
was out, and that very habit kept his focus locked on escape routes, which in turn kept his threat-
detection system humming. At home, when Laura tried to hold his hand or laugh at a sitcom, he would
deliberately flatten his mood. He would remind himself that feeling good was dangerous—it had been
the moment he let his guard down in the war zone that had cost that woman her life. Pushing away
positive emotions made him feel safer momentarily, but the emotional numbing left him more alone,
more on edge, more convinced that the world was hostile. He began to monitor Laura’s movements too,
feeling a spike of alarm if she walked up behind him unexpectedly.

These routines solidified over months. He stopped going to the gym altogether, which meant his
physical tension had nowhere to go except to fuel his hypervigilance. At work, during a routine call
to a house fire, he caught himself scanning the burning building’s exits rather than focusing on the
hose line—a dangerous shift that his captain noticed and pulled him aside for. Ethan could not
explain why the memory of a market half a world away felt more immediate than the flames in front of
him.

What finally drove him to seek help was a sequence of small failures: he snapped at a stranger in a
parking lot who merely walked too close; he missed a family dinner because he could not bring
himself to sit in a booth that did not offer a clear escape path; he woke Laura at three in the
morning because he had been lying still, eyes wide, checking every shadow. His marriage was
beginning to strain, and he was terrified that his own hypervigilance was making him a hazard on the
job. He wanted to stop feeling as though every stranger was a threat and every positive moment was a
trap. But the thought of letting go of those strategies felt like stepping into an open field
without cover—and that, more than anything, was what ached behind his eyes when he finally sat down
in the therapist’s office.
