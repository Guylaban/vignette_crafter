Linh was twenty-three and married less than a year when the collision happened. She and her husband,
Minh, were driving home from a dinner with friends. They had been arguing—something about which of
them had forgotten to pay a bill—and the tension in the car was thick. Linh was at the wheel, her
jaw tight, when a sedan ran through a red light directly into their lane. She jerked the wheel left,
but the truck coming the other way was too close. She remembers a screech of metal, glass
shattering, and then nothing until she woke in the hospital with a fractured wrist and a deep,
humming dread.

In the weeks that followed, Linh could not stop turning the moment over. She was certain the crash
had happened because she had been distracted by the argument—because she had let anger get the
better of her. *The event happened because of the way I acted,* she told herself, and the thought
burrowed in. She also became convinced that *people can't be trusted*: the other driver had run a
red light, Minh had pushed her into a fight, the nurses seemed to be hiding something about her
recovery. And underneath it all, a sharp, persistent guilt: *I will never forgive myself for not
doing more to stop what happened.* Even now, she felt she should have somehow seen the danger
earlier, should have reacted faster, should have shielded Minh better. She had failed.

The triggers that amplified these appraisals were everywhere. Hearing the name "Derek"—the other
driver's name, which the police had told her—made her stomach drop and her mind flood with
self‑blame. An argument with Minh over anything, even the grocery list, instantly returned her to
the car, and she would feel her guilt spike: *If I hadn't argued, this wouldn't have happened.*
Uncertainty—not knowing whether Minh would be late from work, whether a doctor’s test was clear,
whether the roads were safe—sent her into a spiral of mistrust and inaction guilt, as if any
unpredictable situation could become another catastrophe she had failed to prevent.

To manage these unbearable feelings, Linh began avoiding situations that put her body under stress.
She stopped jogging, then walking quickly, then driving altogether. She also stopped seeing friends,
convinced that socialising would lead to arguments or that no one could truly be trusted. But the
most frightening strategy she adopted was self‑harm: she would cut her forearm with a kitchen knife,
the sharp pain briefly overpowering the intrusive images and the weight of her appraisals. The
self‑harm was a distraction, a way to stop thinking about the event.

Yet each of these strategies only deepened the cycle. When she avoided physical stress, she felt
weaker and more helpless, which reinforced her belief that she was fundamentally at fault for not
doing more. Avoiding friends isolated her from anyone who might have challenged her mistrust, and
the loneliness fed her guilt. Self‑harm, especially, confirmed her worst view of herself: that she
was the kind of person who would hurt her own body, and therefore deserved the blame she carried. In
turn, these strengthened appraisals made the triggers even harder to bear. An argument, for example,
now carried not only the memory of the crash but also the shame of her recent cutting, making the
trigger itself more potent. And because she avoided so many ordinary situations, uncertainty grew:
she never knew when a name or a tense conversation would ambush her, and that unpredictability
became a new trigger.

At work as a database administrator, Linh’s strategies created real consequences. She started taking
only the most predictable tasks, refusing any project with ambiguous deadlines or unclear
requirements. When a colleague asked for help on a system migration, she felt a wave of uncertainty
and snapped at him, then avoided the office elevator for a week because the enclosed space made her
think of the crumpled car. Her manager noticed her withdrawal and her curtness with team members;
Linh was given a warning about collaboration.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a night when Minh found her on the bathroom floor, a
razor next to her, while she sobbed that she could not stop the thoughts. She agreed to see a
therapist because she was terrified she would ruin her marriage and her job, but more than that, she
was exhausted by the loop she could not name: triggers feeding appraisals, appraisals driving
avoidance and harm, and the strategies themselves making the triggers inescapable. She wanted to
believe recovery was possible, but she was not sure she deserved it.
