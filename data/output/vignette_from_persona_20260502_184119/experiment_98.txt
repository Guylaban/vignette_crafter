Kwame is a fifty-eight-year-old hairdresser who runs a small shop in a busy neighborhood, married
for thirty-two years. Nine months ago he was closing up late on a Thursday evening. A man emerged
from the alley beside the shop, reeking of cheap whiskey, and demanded his cash. When Kwame
hesitated the man shoved him against the wall and snarled, “You think you’re better than me? Look at
you, shaking like a child.” Kwame handed over the day’s earnings. The man shoved him again, spat at
his feet, and disappeared. Kwame stood frozen for a long moment, then locked the door and drove home
in silence.

The first pictures came that night. In his mind the image of the man’s face—pocked skin, bloodshot
eyes—stuck like a photograph he could not look away from. Within days the memory had become a film:
the smell of whiskey, the feel of the wall against his back, the insult, all of it playing over and
over, sometimes in the middle of a haircut. When his wife asked what happened he found he could not
answer. He knew the facts, but the words would not come—the event seemed to live somewhere beyond
language, in sounds and sensations that resisted description.

Now a customer’s criticism—even a gentle correction about a part line or a request for a trim—hits
him like the robber’s words did. Just last week a woman said, “The left side is a little longer, can
you fix it?” and Kwame’s throat tightened. The alcohol smell on a man waiting for a haircut does the
same: the olfactory cue yanks him straight back into that alley, the film starting before he can
stop it. When he notices a person lingering outside the shop—not doing anything overt—he is certain
they are dangerous, watching him, and the replay begins again.

What frightens Kwame most is that the memory does not simply arrive; it changes him. After a
flashback he sits in the back room and goes over everything he might have done wrong. Should he have
closed earlier? Should he have fought back? Should he have called the police? He dwells on these
questions for hours, picking at his own choices until he feels worthless and ashamed. The shame
makes him try even harder to suppress the images, to push them away, but suppression only seems to
make the film run more frequently. He has started avoiding people altogether. He no longer takes
evening haircuts. He leaves the shop early, walking fast with his head down. When a familiar client
sits in his chair he finds himself cutting in silence, unwilling to engage, his hands trembling
slightly. His wife notices that he has stopped attending their weekly dinner with friends. He tells
her he is tired, but the truth is that being around anyone feels like a threat—one wrong word, one
critical glance, and the whole scene will start again.

Avoidance has created a loop he cannot see. Because he avoids social contact, he is never exposed to
people who might confirm he is safe. Instead he grows more sensitized: every person who acts
suspiciously becomes a sign of danger, and that perception triggers the memory, which triggers more
self-blame, which drives him deeper into isolation. Even the salon—once a place of pride—has become
a minefield. A client’s offhand complaint sets off the film, and then for the rest of the day he
replays the criticism alongside the mugging, as if the two are the same failure.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was a morning last month when a man with a whiskey odor
walked into the shop. Kwame excused himself, locked himself in the supply closet, and stood shaking
until the man left. His wife found him sitting on the floor, unable to explain. She pushed him to
call a therapist. Now, sitting in the waiting room, he is terrified that talking about the event
will make the film play endlessly. But more than that he is exhausted—tired of the pictures, tired
of the silence with his family, tired of a mind that turns every small moment into a reminder of his
worst night. He hopes, quietly, that someone can help him stop the film from starting.
