decades scanning every room before he entered. At sixty-five, divorced, and still working as a
special education teacher in a Chicago public school, he carried a past that had never been just the
past. The traumatic event that had shaped him happened during his first deployment in the Vietnam

face-first into muddy water, and when he came up gasping, the taste of silt and gasoline filled his
mouth. For years afterward, the same metallic, earthy taste could rise without warning, especially
when he felt dizzy—a symptom that first appeared after a mild concussion from the blast but had
never fully disappeared. Dizziness itself became a kind of trigger, because every time the room
seemed to tilt, his throat filled again with that phantom taste, and he was back in the mud, waiting
for the next round.

Over the decades, Carlos had learned to live with these intrusions by constructing a careful system
of control. For example, a conversation about the war—even a casual one with a fellow teacher during
lunch—could bring on both the taste and a wave of lightheadedness, so he had come to believe that
strong emotions of any kind were dangerous. He avoided emotional stress by pulling away from
arguments, from celebrations, from anything that might stir him too deeply. In his classroom, he
always chose a desk near the door, and when he took his students to the cafeteria or the playground,
he positioned himself so that he could see every exit. This constant checking of his surroundings
was not a choice; it was a reflex born from years of scanning for signs of threat, a vigilance that
had hardened into a permanent way of moving through the world.

The threat monitoring itself was relentless. Carlos noted that when he heard unexpected loud
sounds—a book slamming, a fire drill alarm, a car backfiring—his heart would hammer and he would
freeze, then automatically begin calculating the nearest escape route. This planning had become
second nature. In a restaurant or a movie theater, he could not sit until he had located every door
and window, mentally rehearsing the quickest path to safety. The paradox was that this very
vigilance made the triggers more frequent. The hypervigilance itself—the scanning, the tensing at
noises—could bring on a wave of dizziness, because his body was always bracing for something. And
once the dizziness started, the taste would follow, and then he would feel compelled to check his
surroundings even more obsessively, which only heightened his alertness. A loop tightened around
him: the threat of dizziness led him to scan, the scanning made him dizzy, and the dizziness brought
the taste, which sent him back into survival mode.

This cycle had cost him. His marriage, already strained by his emotional withdrawal, ended after
fifteen years. His ex-wife had told him she could not live with someone who flinched at her touch
and jumped at every sound. Carlos understood, though he never said so. At work, he managed well
enough because he had structured his life around his routines: he taught the same way each day,
avoided discussing his past, and kept his students in a predictable, low-stimulation environment.

sharp sound that made Carlos jump and then, as the taste rose and the room tilted, he had to excuse

that his strategies were failing. He had come for help because he was afraid, but he could not say
exactly of what—only that the danger he had been watching for his whole life had never shown itself,
and he was tired of watching.
