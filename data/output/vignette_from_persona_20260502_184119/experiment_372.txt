Robert, a 67-year-old hotel manager, was mugged three months ago in the parking lot after a late
shift. As he unlocked his car, a man smelling strongly of alcohol grabbed him from behind, shouted
something unintelligible, and slammed his head against the door before snatching his wallet and
disappearing into the night. The sound of his own skull hitting metal—a dull, percussive crack—still
rings in Robert’s ears at odd moments. What stayed with him most, though, was the humiliation: a
seasoned manager who prided himself on reading people and maintaining order had been reduced to a
helpless victim on the ground, his dignity stripped away in seconds.

In the weeks that followed, Robert found the world had changed. He had always been a practical man,
but now he caught himself scanning every face that entered the hotel lobby. People couldn’t be
trusted—that was the lesson the mugging had etched into him. The stranger had looked ordinary, and
then he had attacked. Robert began to believe that danger lurked behind every ordinary interaction,
that the world was simply a dangerous place now and he had been naive to think otherwise. This
conviction of permanent threat made him constantly watchful. At his desk, he sat with his back to
the wall. In the elevator, he positioned himself near the control panel. His hypervigilance became
automatic, a second skin he couldn’t shed.

That heightened state, in turn, sharpened every cue around him. A guest lingering near the front
entrance too long—“acting suspiciously” in Robert’s mind—made his chest tighten and his attention
narrow. The smell of beer or whiskey on a bar patron’s breath instantly transported him back to that
parking lot, and his pulse would race before he could even name the memory. A dropped tray in the
restaurant, a shout from the kitchen, any sudden loud noise sent a jolt through him, leaving him
breathless and irritable for minutes afterward. Even at home, in the house he had shared with his
wife for thirty years, Robert remained on guard. He slept poorly, starting awake at the creak of a
floorboard, and when his wife touched his shoulder unexpectedly, he reacted with a flinch that
worried them both.

His occupation became a daily ordeal. As a manager, Robert was expected to greet guests warmly, to
move through the lobby with ease. Instead, he found himself circling the perimeter, avoiding
clusters of young men, reading hostility into casual glances. The trigger of “people acting
suspiciously” now seemed to appear constantly—a guest who lingered too long at the elevator bank, a
delivery driver who didn’t match the uniform. Each instance reinforced his sense that the world was
unsafe, and that reinforcement tightened the loop: the more he believed people were untrustworthy,
the more hypervigilant he became, and the more hypervigilant he was, the more triggers he noticed.

The humiliation lingered, too, a quiet burn beneath everything. Robert had always seen himself as
competent, in control. The mugging had cracked that image, and he couldn’t stop rehearsing it—what
he should have done, how he should have seen it coming. That self-critical rumination fed his
mistrust of others and his constant expectation of threat. He had come to believe that safety was an
illusion, and his body remained locked in a state of alarm that no logic could soothe.

What finally brought Robert to seek help was his wife’s quiet observation that he no longer seemed
to live in their home—he was always scanning, always startled, never present. He was afraid that his
vigilance was ruining the one relationship that still felt safe. He wanted to stop feeling like a
target in his own life, to walk through a hotel lobby without his stomach clenching, to hear a
sudden noise and not feel his past slam into him again. He hoped, above all, to find a way back to
the ordinary world he had lost that night in the parking lot.
