Mark, a 21-year-old European male, works as a Public Defender and is currently in a relationship.
His traumatic experience as a refugee has deeply affected his life and mental health. Mark’s daily
routine is marked by constant vigilance and an overwhelming sense of danger that stems from the
harrowing experiences he endured during his journey to safety. The trauma began when Mark was forced
to flee his war-torn homeland, leaving behind everything familiar. The memories of the violence and
chaos are vivid, often replaying in flashes of images and bodily sensations that feel almost real.
These visual memories, combined with the physical reenactment he experiences when they surface, have
left him feeling perpetually on edge. Even now, Mark finds himself jumping at ordinary sounds or
reacting to unusual silence as if danger is looming. Mark’s belief that “the world is a dangerous
place” has become a constant narrative in his mind. This negative appraisal drives much of his
behavior and thought patterns. He constantly monitors his own thoughts for signs of going crazy,
fearing that the trauma will consume him completely. The intense emotions he feels without clear
images further reinforce this sense of danger, leading to an almost obsessive need for control. The
recurrence of these memories has become so overwhelming that Mark avoids talking about them with
others, including his partner and colleagues. He finds it easier to keep silent rather than relive
the trauma through conversation or be reminded by someone else’s accounts. This avoidance not only
isolates him but also amplifies his fears, as he believes sharing might make things worse. Mark's
hypervigilance and constant vigilance have taken a toll on his work as a Public Defender. He finds
it difficult to fully engage in court proceedings or consultations with clients because the
slightest sound can trigger a startling response. This has led to moments of disconnection, where he
momentarily feels his body is reliving the trauma, making it hard for him to focus and be present.
The false alarms Mark experiences further exacerbate his hypervigilance. He often reacts as if he is
in immediate danger even when there’s no real threat. This cycle of fear and heightened alertness
has made it difficult for him to trust his surroundings or those around him, leading to a persistent
sense that something dangerous is about to happen. His partner has noticed the changes in Mark's
behavior but struggles to understand the depth of his fears. The avoidance and hyperarousal have
strained their relationship, as Mark often finds himself withdrawing into his own thoughts to
protect himself from being overwhelmed by external triggers. His belief that he must constantly be
on guard because "you never know what can happen next" has become a self-perpetuating cycle. Mark
finally sought help when the intensity of these symptoms began to affect his ability to perform at
work and maintain his relationships. He was certain that if he didn’t address these feelings, they
would continue to consume him completely. What frightened him most was the fear of losing control
and the belief that the trauma would never truly leave him. In therapy, Mark hopes to find ways to
manage his hypervigilance and reduce the frequency of false alarms. He is seeking strategies to cope
with the vivid memories and emotional intensity without feeling overwhelmed by them. His goal is to
regain a sense of safety and control in his daily life, both personally and professionally.
