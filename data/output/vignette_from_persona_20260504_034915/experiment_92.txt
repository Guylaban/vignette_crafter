### Clinical Vignette: Martha’s Case Martha, a 72-year-old North American woman, has been a network
engineer for over four decades. She was married and lived a relatively stable life until she
witnessed a traumatic event at work where her colleague was seriously injured in an accident. The
incident left her feeling shaken and profoundly affected by the experience. Since then, Martha has
become increasingly wary of certain situations that remind her of the trauma. Crowded places and
specific conversation topics have become potent triggers for her. When she finds herself in a
shopping center or at a public event, the memories flood back, making her feel on edge and unsafe.
She recalls the day vividly: the sounds, the chaos, and the sheer helplessness she felt as she
watched her colleague being rushed away to an ambulance. Martha has come to believe that the world
is inherently dangerous, a sentiment that exacerbates every encounter with potential triggers. This
constant vigilance is exhausting, but she feels it's necessary because you never know what can
happen next. She has started to distance herself from others, feeling like an outsider now that her
worldview has shifted so dramatically. To cope, Martha employs maladaptive strategies such as
forcing herself to think only positive thoughts and avoiding crowded places or the site of the
event. This positive reframing is her way of trying to maintain a semblance of control over her
life. However, these strategies often backfire, leading to more negative appraisals about her
situation. The need for constant vigilance has made public transport, like buses, particularly
daunting for Martha. She dreads the unpredictability and fear that arises from being in confined
spaces with other people. Her avoidance of such situations has led to significant changes in her
daily routine, including canceling work-related meetings and social engagements. This isolation
further reinforces her feeling of being an outsider. Martha’s husband notices her growing withdrawal
and worries about the toll this is taking on their relationship. Despite her efforts to remain
positive, she finds herself becoming increasingly irritable and withdrawn at home. The fear that the
world is a dangerous place has seeped into every aspect of her life, making it difficult for her to
engage in activities she once enjoyed. Her maladaptive strategies have led to more negative
appraisals over time. While trying to think positively, Martha often feels like these thoughts are
superficial and don’t address the underlying fears that continue to plague her. This reinforces her
belief that the world is indeed dangerous, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of anxiety and
avoidance. Martha’s situation has reached a tipping point where she can no longer ignore how deeply
this trauma affects her daily life. She finally decided to seek help because she realized that her
attempts at coping were only making things worse. The fear of losing more control over her life,
both professionally and personally, is what ultimately drove her to take action. Martha hopes for a
way to regain some sense of normalcy in her life—perhaps finding strategies that allow her to
navigate the world without feeling like she's constantly on edge. She wants to reconnect with those
around her and find peace in knowing that safety is possible despite the dangers she perceives.
