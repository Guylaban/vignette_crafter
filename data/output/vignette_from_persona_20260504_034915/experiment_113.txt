hit. This unusual silence had always made him feel something dangerous was about to happen, and now
it haunts his nights. To cope, Daniel has turned to smoking cigarettes, finding that they provide a
brief momentary escape from his intrusive thoughts. He avoids relaxing because he believes it makes
him vulnerable to another disaster. Avoiding relaxation also means he's increasingly withdrawn from
colleagues at work, preferring not to engage in casual conversation or even talk about the event

longer than expected—like a traffic jam or a delayed train—he assumes the worst and feels an intense
sense of dread. He believes he can't stop bad things from happening, which contributes to his
feeling emotionally numb and disconnected from the world around him. The memory of the hurricane is
so vivid that it feels unreal; sometimes when Daniel thinks about it, he smells the sea again and

place, making him more anxious in unfamiliar situations. His heightened sense of threat also leads
to frequent avoidance behaviors, such as skipping social gatherings or even avoiding certain parts
