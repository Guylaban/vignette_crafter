John, a 67-year-old widowed interior designer of Oceania descent, has been grappling with the
aftermath of a traumatic captivity experience. The event itself was deeply searing; John had been
taken captive and subjected to constant shouting in Arabic by his captors. This trauma has left him
with vivid, intrusive memories that resurface unexpectedly, often triggered by sounds or situations
reminiscent of those harrowing days. John’s life has transformed dramatically since the event. He
now meticulously rehearses conversations before any interaction, convinced that spontaneous speech
might betray his fear and vulnerability. These rehearsal behaviors have extended to his work; he
avoids going into client meetings or workshops where enclosed spaces remind him of his captivity.
The avoidance behavior is not just about physical settings but also extends to social engagements,
as John fears being trapped in situations similar to the ones during his captivity. One evening,
while working on a design project at home, a distant noise—a car backfiring—triggered an intense
flashback. He found himself suddenly reliving the shouting and fear from those days. This experience
left him feeling emotionally vulnerable and heightened his sense of danger. Since then, John has
become increasingly hypervigilant; he feels unsafe unless his back is against a wall where he can
see everything around him. John’s memories of captivity are often emotionless or fragmented. He can
recall the events, but they feel as if they happened to someone else, leaving him feeling numb. When
he tries to recount what happened, he struggles to articulate the experience clearly, losing his
train of thought midway through any attempt at explanation. The auditory aspect of these memories is
particularly vivid; during flashbacks, John often hears the Arabic shouting in his head. This
inability to fully process and explain his trauma has exacerbated his negative emotions such as
fear, horror, anger, guilt, and shame. He is certain that his body reacts to perceived danger before
he can consciously think about it, further deepening his sense of vulnerability and hypervigilance.
His maladaptive strategies, like rehearsing conversations and avoiding enclosed spaces, only serve
to reinforce these negative emotions and the intrusive memories. John’s avoidance behavior at work
has begun to affect his relationships with clients and colleagues. He has started missing important
meetings and design presentations, leading to strained professional interactions. This occupational
consequence is a significant source of anxiety for him; he worries that his performance and
reliability are being questioned. What finally brought John to seek help was the realization that
his strategies were not only failing but also making things worse. The intrusive memories and
triggers have grown more frequent and intense, leaving him feeling trapped in a cycle of fear and
avoidance. He is most afraid of losing control completely or becoming too dependent on these
maladaptive behaviors. John hopes that through therapy, he can break free from the overwhelming
sense of danger and find a way to process his traumatic experiences without being overwhelmed by
them. His primary goal is to regain a sense of safety in his daily life and restore some normalcy to
his work and personal interactions.
