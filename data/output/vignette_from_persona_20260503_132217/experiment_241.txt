event was not a distant history for Mateo; it was a sensory prison. When the memory surfaced, it
arrived as a full sensory reliving that felt completely real across all his senses at once—the acrid
taste of cordite on his tongue, the visceral weight of blood soaking his shirt, the deafening roar
that rattled his ribs. Sometimes, his mind would slam the door shut, producing an emotionless recall

stranger. Yet the numbness was always temporary. When the grief returned, it carried a guilt on

his life. Over the months that followed, Mateo’s interpretation of the event hardened into rigid

forgive himself for the inaction that followed. He was certain that he should have protected the
families in that alley and that he had failed, viewing his survival as a moral lapse rather than
luck. These appraisals were not passive reflections; they actively poisoned his recollections.
