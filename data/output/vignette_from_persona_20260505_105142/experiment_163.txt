Akua had always taken pride in her meticulous attention to detail as an architect. She spent years
honing her craft, working on high-profile projects, and building a reputation for herself in the
industry. But all of that changed one fateful day when she accidentally caused serious injury to a
colleague on a construction site. The memory of the incident has haunted her ever since. The more
Akua reflects on what happened, the more she becomes convinced that someone else would not have
gotten into this situation (Self-Comparison). She feels isolated and set apart from others, as if
she's living in a constant state of shame (Isolation). Her self-doubt has grown to the point where
she barely recognizes herself anymore (Unrecognisable Self). These negative appraisals of herself
have led Akua to develop maladaptive strategies to cope with her guilt. She's begun performing
mental rituals, like counting steps or tapping her foot a certain number of times, in an attempt to
prevent anything bad from happening again (Magical Prevention). When the rituals don't work, she
resorts to self-blame rumination, dwelling on what she has done wrong and replaying the incident
over and over in her mind. As Akua tries to make sense of her thoughts and emotions, she finds it
difficult to deliberately recall the details of the incident (Hard to Recall Deliberately). When
memories do surface, they're often accompanied by vivid sensory experiences – the taste of sweat on
her lips, the sound of sirens blaring in the distance (Taste Vividness, Auditory Recall). These
fragmented memories only serve to reinforce her negative appraisals of herself. She starts to wonder
if she's truly capable of designing safe buildings or if she's just a menace waiting to happen.
Akua's memories also trigger false alarms, making her react as if she's in danger even when there's
no real threat (False Alarms). The sound of a car horn or the creaking of a floorboard is enough to
startle her and send her heart racing. She becomes hypervigilant, constantly on the lookout for
potential hazards that might not even exist. This state of heightened arousal makes it difficult for
Akua to concentrate, and she finds herself struggling to focus on even the simplest tasks
(Concentration). In an attempt to regain control, Akua resorts to downward comparison, reminding
herself that things could have been worse (Downward Comparison). She tries to rationalize her way
out of her guilt, but this only leads to more rumination and self-blame. Her mind becomes a vicious
cycle of negative appraisals, maladaptive strategies, and fragmented memories. As time goes on,
Akua's relationships with her colleagues and loved ones begin to suffer. They notice the change in
her demeanor – the constant checking and rechecking, the hesitation to take on new projects. They
try to reassure her that it was an accident, but Akua can't shake off the feeling of responsibility.
She starts to withdraw from social interactions, fearing that others will discover her "true"
nature. It's only when Akua's husband sits her down and expresses his concern about her increasingly
reclusive behavior that she realizes she needs help. The incident has left her with a deep-seated
fear of causing harm to others, and she's desperate to break free from this cycle of guilt and self-
doubt. As she begins therapy, Akua hopes to learn new ways to cope with her memories and negative
appraisals, and to slowly rebuild her sense of self-worth as an architect and a person.
