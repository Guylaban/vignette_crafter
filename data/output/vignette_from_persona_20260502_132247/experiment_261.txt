Marguerite is a 78-year-old married woman of European descent who works as a freelance graphic
designer from her home studio in coastal Louisiana. She was referred for psychological assessment
following a PCL-5 score of 43, obtained through her primary care physician, who had grown concerned
about her declining functional status over the preceding eighteen months.

The event that fractured Marguerite's sense of safety occurred during a major hurricane that made
landfall near her community. She and her husband sheltered in their home as the storm intensified
overnight. In the early hours, a section of the roof gave way, and floodwater began rising through
the ground floor. A neighbour who had forced his way in to help them evacuate arrived reeking of
alcohol — she could smell it even over the rain — and in the chaos he wrenched the front door open
so violently against the wind that the frame splintered. The sound was enormous, indistinguishable
in her memory from the structural collapse happening around her. She watched a close friend's home,
visible from her window, disappear beneath the surge. That friend did not survive.

In the months that followed, Marguerite found that her memory of the event resisted ordinary
narrative. At times she could recount the sequence of what happened with a strange, hollow
detachment — as though she were describing something that had happened to a woman she had read
about, someone remote and fictional. At other moments, without warning, the memory collapsed the
distance entirely, and she was back inside it: the smell of the water, the splintering door, the
darkness. The sheer extremity of what she had lived through made it feel, at times, genuinely
unbelievable — her mind kept returning to the thought that events of that magnitude did not happen
to ordinary people living ordinary lives.

It was this sense of unreality that fed, and was in turn fed by, her deepening conviction that the
world was irreversibly dangerous and that nothing she had believed about safety, fairness, or
predictability could be trusted anymore. She had come to believe that the disaster had been
preventable — that warnings had been ignored, that authorities had failed, that her friend had died
unnecessarily — and the absence of any accountability confirmed for her that the world operated
without justice. These appraisals did not remain abstract. When something triggered the memory — the
slam of a door, a neighbour mentioning a family who had lost someone, the faint smell of alcohol
from a passing car — her sense of threat surged immediately, as though the danger were present and
unresolved, and the appraisals flooded back with renewed force: the world was still unsafe, the loss
was still unaddressed, she was still owed something that would never arrive.

Marguerite responded to this state with strategies that felt, to her, like the only available forms
of control. She ruminated extensively on what might have been done differently — different
evacuation timing, different choices, different warnings heeded — as though the right sequence of
counterfactuals might finally produce a sense of resolution. She cleaned and reorganised her studio
compulsively, particularly after distressing episodes, telling herself that order in her immediate
environment was the one thing she could still govern. She also worked actively to suppress the
memory itself, pushing away images and refusing to speak about the event. These strategies, however,
maintained rather than quieted the disturbance. The suppression kept the memory from being processed
and contextualised, so it retained its raw, present-tense quality. The cleaning and rumination, by
keeping her attention trained on threat and loss, made her more rather than less sensitised to the
triggers she encountered — the slamming door in her own hallway, the grief mentioned in a radio
programme — which in turn reactivated the appraisals, completing the cycle she could not yet name.

Her work as a graphic designer, conducted at home, had narrowed considerably. She kept the studio
door propped open rather than risk the sound of it closing, and she had stopped taking on projects
involving imagery of water or coastlines.

What finally brought Marguerite to seek help was her husband's quiet observation, offered gently one
evening, that she had not laughed in over a year. What she most feared was that she was right about
the world — and what she most hoped was that she was wrong.
