Ingrid Halvorsen is a 45-year-old single combat pilot who presents with a PCL-5 score of 56
following a terrorist attack she survived approximately fourteen months ago. She was off-duty,
moving through a busy transit hub in a major city, when a coordinated bombing tore through the
concourse around her. The blast threw her from her feet, filled the air with screaming and thick
grey smoke, and left her briefly pinned beneath a collapsed partition while sirens began wailing
from every direction. She was physically uninjured in any lasting sense, but the man beside her was
not. She has not spoken publicly about what she saw in those minutes before emergency services
arrived. What she has said is that she felt, for the first time in a decorated military career,
completely unable to act — a woman trained to control supersonic aircraft at altitude, lying on a
floor, unable to move, unable to help.

In the weeks that followed, Ingrid noticed that her body had developed its own alarm system, one she
could not override through discipline or will. Loud noises — the afterburner roar of aircraft on the
flight line, a door slamming in the ready room, even a tray dropped in the mess — sent a cold charge
through her chest that she recognised as dread rather than startle. Crowded spaces, particularly the
briefing rooms and hangars where her squadron assembled, became difficult to tolerate. Most
insidiously, on days when she felt physically unwell — a headache, a low fever, the dull nausea of
fatigue — her nervous system seemed to read the bodily discomfort as a signal that something
catastrophic was imminent, as though illness and danger had been welded together in the concourse
that day.

These triggers did not arrive neutrally. Each one carried an interpretation with it: that she was
not coping, that she had no real capacity to prevent harm, that the world had revealed itself to be
irreducibly dangerous. Ingrid had come to believe, with a quiet but absolute conviction, that she
could not manage even minor stressors — that the version of herself who had flown combat sorties
without flinching had been an illusion, and that the woman on the concourse floor was the true one.
This belief, once established, made the triggers feel more potent. When she entered a crowded hangar
already carrying the certainty that she could not cope, the noise and press of bodies became
genuinely unbearable rather than merely uncomfortable, and her nervous system responded accordingly
— confirming, in her mind, exactly what she feared.

To manage this, Ingrid had developed a set of strategies that made immediate sense but gradually
narrowed her life. She began smoking again after eleven years of abstinence, stepping outside during
briefings and between sorties in a way that her colleagues noticed. She routed her daily movements
to avoid enclosed or confined spaces — stairwells, elevators, the small crew room adjacent to the
flight operations centre — adding minutes and detours to every part of her day. She reminded
herself, repeatedly, that others had died in the attack and she had not, a comparison she used to
suppress the legitimacy of her distress. These strategies, however, kept the triggers perpetually
primed: by avoiding enclosed spaces, she never learned that she could tolerate them, and the
avoidance itself confirmed that they were dangerous. The smoking breaks pulled her out of situations
before any natural settling could occur, and the downward comparisons, rather than providing relief,
quietly reinforced the sense that she was barely holding together.

Ingrid remained superalert throughout her working day, scanning the flight line and surrounding
areas with a watchfulness that went beyond professional situational awareness. She checked behind
her frequently in corridors and parking areas, certain that the calm of a military base was no
guarantee of safety. Sleep had become unreliable — she lay awake cataloguing threats, and the
fatigue that followed fed directly back into the physical unwell feeling that her nervous system now
read as a warning signal, tightening the loop further. Her hypervigilance, sustained across months,
had deepened her conviction that the world was dangerous and that she personally lacked the capacity
to protect herself within it, which in turn made her more alert, more reactive, and more avoidant.

What finally brought Ingrid to seek help was a grounding notice — a temporary suspension from flight
duties pending a medical review after she aborted a routine training sortie, citing an inability to
concentrate. She had protected her career through two wars. Losing it to this, she told the intake
clinician, was not something she was willing to accept. What she feared most, beneath that, was that
the clinician would confirm what she already believed: that she had been permanently changed, and
not for the better.
