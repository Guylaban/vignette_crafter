Arjun is a 35-year-old South Asian man who works as a tax consultant in a mid-sized accounting firm
in suburban New Jersey. He is married, the father of a toddler, and by most external measures has
rebuilt a life of quiet stability since returning from a fourteen-month deployment with an
international humanitarian relief organization in a conflict zone in the Middle East. He presented
for psychological assessment with a PCL-5 score of 52, referred by his primary care physician after
his wife reported that he had become, in her words, "unreachable."

The events that most clearly organized his current symptom picture occurred during the final weeks
of his deployment, when the field hospital where he worked as a logistics coordinator was struck by
shelling. He was present when a section of the supply warehouse caught fire. In the chaos that
followed, he could not locate two local staff members he had been responsible for evacuating. By the
time the fire was suppressed, both had been killed. Arjun had been the one who drew up the shift
schedule that placed them there. The smell of burning materials — acrid, oily smoke — filled the air
for hours afterward, and he stood at the perimeter watching the site, unable to move, certain that
something irreversible had happened because of a decision he had made. He attended both men's
funerals in the days that followed, standing at the gravesides in a country not his own, feeling
entirely outside of himself.

Since returning home, certain experiences carry that afternoon back into his body with a force that
startles him. The smell of smoke — from a neighbor's fireplace, from a candle his wife lights in the
evenings — stops him mid-movement, and when it does, his body appears to know before his mind does:
his shoulders round forward, his head drops, and he finds himself in what feels like a physical echo
of crouching beside rubble. The memories that surface in these moments are not linear; he cannot
reconstruct the sequence of what happened with any clarity, and this disorganization itself becomes
a source of distress, as though the fragmentation proves something damning about his character. When
the smell of smoke reaches him, he will leave the room immediately, sometimes covering his face with
his hands as he goes — a gesture that began as a practical response to the odor but has since become
reflexive. He avoids the firm's annual holiday party, his wife's cousin's wedding last spring, and
any occasion that might require him to feel something resembling hope or pleasure, because he has
come to believe, with a quiet conviction that frightens him, that he no longer has the right to
those feelings. This belief — that he failed to protect the people in his care — has become
structurally load-bearing in his psychology. It shapes how he moves through the world: the avoidance
of positive events is not merely emotional numbing but a form of self-imposed sentence.

The appraisals themselves feed back into his vigilance in a way that has made his nights nearly
unworkable. He is certain that the world is not the ordered, predictable place he once believed it
to be, and this shattered certainty means that ordinary sounds — a door closing in the hallway, his
toddler knocking over a cup — produce a startle response that leaves him shaking. At night, he
cannot feel safe; he lies awake listening to the house settle. His hypervigilant state keeps him in
a condition of readiness that, paradoxically, makes the memories more accessible: the body already

warehouse, and the shame that follows the memory makes him feel more isolated from his colleagues
than before. His avoidance of the firm's client site visits, which began because one location is
near a crematorium, has been noticed by his supervisor and attributed to disengagement. The more he
withdraws, the more his sense of isolation solidifies into something that feels like evidence rather
than symptom. He came to seek help not after a single crisis but after his wife found him sitting in

what he was waiting for. What he most fears is that there is no explanation that does not confirm
what he already believes: that he is, at his core, a man who failed when it mattered.
