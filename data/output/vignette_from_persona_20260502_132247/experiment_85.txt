Ingrid Halvorsen is a 40-year-old Norwegian-American data scientist, married and living in a mid-
sized city in the American Southwest. She was referred for psychological assessment following a
significant decline in functioning over the past fourteen months, since returning from a research
consulting trip to the Middle East, where she witnessed an attack on a civilian settlement near an
Arab village on the outskirts of a contested region. She had been embedded with a humanitarian data
team when fires broke out across several structures — deliberately set, it was later confirmed — and
she watched, unable to act, as families fled burning homes while armed men moved through the smoke.
The smell of burning wood and fabric, the sound of screaming in a language she only partially
understood, the orange light of the flames against the night sky: these details did not leave with
her when she boarded the plane home.

In the months that followed, Ingrid found that her memories of that night did not behave like
ordinary memories. They arrived without warning and with a quality of presence that her
recollections of anything else — her wedding, her childhood, her graduate defense — simply did not
possess. She would be sitting at her workstation, running a model, and something in her surroundings
— a colleague's space heater glowing orange, a news alert mentioning a Middle Eastern place name —
would instantly reconstitute the full scene: not as a recollection but as an event happening now.
During these moments she sometimes heard the screaming again, clearly, as though it were in the room
with her. The memories did not feel like the past. They felt like the present tense of the worst
night of her life.

These intrusions carried their own verdict. Ingrid had come to believe, with a certainty she could
not argue herself out of, that she had failed the people she witnessed. She had data, resources,
contacts — she had stood there and done nothing. She was certain that someone with real courage,
someone less self-absorbed, would never have been in that position to begin with, or would have
found a way to intervene. These appraisals — that she had failed to protect, that she was uniquely
culpable — fed directly back into the memories themselves, pulling her attention toward them,
turning them over for evidence, so that the memories grew more entrenched and more emotionally
saturated the more she judged herself through them.

The memories also kept her nervous system in a state of readiness that made ordinary life feel
dangerous. She had begun checking the locks on her doors and windows each night, sometimes three or
four times before she could sleep, because the image of men moving through firelight had made the
world feel permeable in a way it never had before. When strangers approached her in the grocery
store or the parking lot of her office building, she felt a spike of threat that she knew,
intellectually, was disproportionate — but knowing that changed nothing. The threat perception
looped back: anything that reminded her of that night, even tangentially, re-activated the alarm,
and the alarm confirmed that the world was still dangerous.

Ingrid's primary response to all of this was work. As a data scientist, she had legitimate reason to
spend long hours at her desk, and she used this cover deliberately — throwing herself into projects,
volunteering for additional analyses, staying late so that she would return home exhausted enough to
sleep before the thoughts could settle in. The overworking quieted the surface, but it did not
interrupt the underlying process. The busyness prevented consolidation and reflection, which meant
the memories remained raw and unprocessed, and when she did surface — on weekends, or in the car —
they returned with full force. The avoidance itself became a trigger: the moment she stopped
working, her mind rushed back. And because she had spent months avoiding the memories rather than
tolerating them, the threat monitoring had intensified; she had become more, not less, sensitised to
anything that resembled that night.

What finally brought Ingrid to seek help was a moment at a team dinner when a colleague lit a candle
at the table and she left the restaurant without explanation, sat in her car, and could not stop
shaking for forty minutes. She told her husband that evening for the first time how bad things had
become. What she fears most is that she is permanently changed — that the woman who boarded that
plane is not coming back. What she hopes for, more quietly, is that she might one day feel that her
life is worth the living of it again.
