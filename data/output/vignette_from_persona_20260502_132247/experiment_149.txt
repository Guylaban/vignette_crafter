Rodrigo is a 54-year-old Latino man who has worked as a parole officer for nearly two decades, a
career he chose because he believed in accountability, in systems that could correct themselves, and
in the possibility of rehabilitation. He is married, the father of two adult children, and by most
external measures a composed and authoritative presence. He presented for treatment with a PCL-5
score of 68, reporting that he could no longer trust his own body or his own mind, and that
something had broken in him that he was not sure could be repaired.

Approximately fourteen months before seeking help, Rodrigo was beaten by two fellow officers during
a confrontation that began when he intervened in what he believed was an excessive use of force
against a parolee he was supervising. What followed was swift and methodical: he was taken to a
stairwell, and in the silence of that enclosed space — no radio chatter, no voices, just the sound
of the building settling — the beating began. He remembers the first blow, the cold of the concrete
floor, and then fragments: a boot, a voice saying something low and flat, the feeling that time had
become discontinuous. He was left there. No one came for a long time.

In the months that followed, Rodrigo found that the memory refused to form into a coherent whole. He
described it as trying to hold water — pieces surfaced and then he pushed them back before they
could fully resolve, because completing the picture felt intolerable. What he had managed to
retrieve felt so extreme, so incongruent with his understanding of himself as someone inside the
system rather than subject to it, that he sometimes doubted it had happened at all. And yet that
very incompleteness kept the threat alive. Because the memory had no clean ending, no narrative
closure, his mind remained convinced the danger was unresolved — that something was still coming. He
could not locate the event safely in the past.

The appraisals that formed around the event became the psychological architecture of his daily life.
He was certain he had been betrayed — not by strangers, but by colleagues he had worked alongside
for years, men who understood exactly what they were doing and did it anyway. He was equally certain
that nothing would be done: the internal investigation had been cursory, the findings inconclusive,
and he had been quietly reassigned. What happened to him was deeply unfair, and the institution that
was supposed to protect him had instead protected the men who hurt him. These convictions did not
remain abstract. They fed directly back into the triggers themselves: when he witnessed someone in
his caseload being disrespectful or defiant — a parolee cutting him off, raising their voice — the
moral violation activated not just professional irritation but the full weight of his appraisal that
authority cannot be trusted and that transgression goes unpunished. The boundary between then and
now collapsed.

At work, the environment had become a landscape of threat signals. Unusual silence — an empty
corridor, a room that went quiet when he entered — produced an immediate and visceral alarm, the
same silence that had preceded the beating. He found himself scanning every room he entered,
cataloguing exits, watching hands, reading faces for signs of what was about to happen. The startle
response had become so pronounced that a door slamming two offices away could leave him gripping his
desk. When stress accumulated across a workday, his heart would begin to race, and the racing itself
became a trigger — his body's signal interpreted as confirmation that something dangerous was near,
which in turn sharpened his vigilance, which surfaced more fragments of the memory, which deepened
his conviction that the threat was ongoing and that no institution would intervene on his behalf.

His wife had noticed the change before he had named it. He had stopped talking about work. He had
stopped sleeping through the night. He had become, in her words, someone who was always listening
for something she couldn't hear.

What finally brought Rodrigo to seek help was a moment at work when he found himself standing in a
stairwell — identical in layout to the one where the assault occurred — and was unable to move for
several minutes. He told his intake clinician that he was not afraid of dying. What frightened him
most was that the people who were supposed to be on his side never would be, and that he had begun
to believe it had always been that way.
