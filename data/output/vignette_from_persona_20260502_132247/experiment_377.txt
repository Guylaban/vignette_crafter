Aleksander Voss, a 45-year-old investigative journalist of Polish-American descent, presented for
psychological assessment following a period of sustained functional decline that had begun to
threaten both his marriage and his career. He had been held captive for eleven days by a
paramilitary faction while reporting in a conflict zone three years prior. During that time he had
been kept in a dark, damp room, his wrists bound behind him, a rough hood periodically pulled over
his head. His captors smelled strongly of cheap alcohol when they came close, and on at least two
occasions he had been struck across the back of the neck and shoulders. He had been certain, on the
third night, that he was about to be executed. He was not. He was eventually released through
diplomatic negotiation, flown home, and told by his editors that he had been extraordinarily brave.
He tried to believe them.

The first months after his return were marked by a vigilance that never fully switched off.
Aleksander had come to believe that the world was fundamentally unpredictable in a way it had not
seemed before — that danger could arrive without warning, as it had in that room. He was superalert
in nearly every environment, scanning exits in restaurants, flinching at car horns, sleeping with
the bedroom door ajar so he could hear the hallway. When unexpected sounds reached him — a door
slamming in the newsroom, a colleague dropping a keyboard — his body responded before his mind could
intervene, flooding with the certainty that something was wrong even when he could see plainly that
nothing was. This state of chronic threat monitoring meant that his nervous system was rarely at
rest, and in that state of heightened arousal, his memories of the captivity became far more
accessible than he wanted them to be. The sound of raised voices from across the open-plan office
was enough to drag him back into the dark room, and once the memory began, it did not merely visit —
it played in full, a film he could not pause, carrying with it the same terror he had felt on the
night he believed he would die. He would find himself gripping the edge of his desk, heart
hammering, sweat cold at his collar, not entirely certain for a moment which reality he was in.

What made this loop particularly punishing was that the memories themselves had begun to sensitise
his body to specific sensations that had been present during the captivity. A stiffness in his neck
— common enough for a man who spent long hours at a desk — could, without warning, pull the full
weight of the trauma forward, as though the body remembered what the mind was trying to contain.
Being touched on the shoulder by a colleague, even in greeting, produced an involuntary recoil that
he found deeply embarrassing and could not explain in professional terms. The smell of alcohol at a
work function, which as a journalist he encountered with some regularity, was perhaps the most
destabilising trigger of all — it carried the proximity of his captors so precisely that his threat
system responded as though they were in the room, sending him into a state of false alarm that could
take hours to subside. He had begun declining evening press events, offering vague excuses to his
editor, and his wife had noticed that he no longer joined her when friends came over for wine.

These withdrawals had accumulated quietly into something larger. His editor had asked, with careful
concern, whether he was ready to return to international assignments. His wife had told him, gently
but with evident strain, that she felt she was living with someone who was always waiting for
something terrible to happen. Aleksander himself had described his inner life as exhausting — the
watching, the replaying, the way his own body had become a source of ambush.

What finally brought him to seek help was a morning when he woke from a nightmare, reached for his
wife's hand in the dark, and found that even her touch made him flinch. He had lain there afterward,
ashamed and frightened, and thought: this is not who I am. What he most hoped, he said quietly in
the first session, was to be able to sit in a room without guarding the door.
