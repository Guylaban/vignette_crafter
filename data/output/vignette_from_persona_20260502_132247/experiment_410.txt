Miriam Holst is a 39-year-old dentist of German-Austrian descent who presents with a PCL-5 score of
38, seeking help approximately fourteen months after a life-threatening illness that she describes,
with characteristic understatement, as "something I thought I had put behind me." Two winters ago,
Miriam was admitted to hospital following what began as what she assumed was a manageable
respiratory infection. Within days she was in an intensive care unit, intubated, her body failing in
ways she had no framework to anticipate. She had spent her career understanding anatomy,
understanding the body as a system she could navigate and correct — and then her own body became
something alien and ungovernable. She remembers, in fragments, the particular sharp cold of the
antiseptic smell that saturated everything around her, the sensation of having no choices about
anything — when she could move, who touched her, whether she could speak — and the faces of nurses
she could not name looking at her with an expression she interpreted as pity. She survived. She
returned to work. She told almost no one what had happened.

The months that followed were quieter than they should have been. Miriam resumed her practice,
resumed her relationship with her partner, resumed the outward shape of her life. But certain
moments began to rupture that surface. Walking into her own clinic each morning, the antiseptic
smell that had always been simply professional background became something else — a sudden
narrowing, a return of the ward, the helplessness, the tubes. In those moments, what flooded back
was not just sensory but interpretive: she had come to believe, with a conviction that felt more
like knowledge than opinion, that she was fundamentally weaker than she had ever allowed herself to
appear, that the illness had exposed something true about her. Looking at old photographs of herself
before the admission — taken at a conference, at a dinner with friends — she found herself studying
a person who no longer existed, and the grief of that recognition confirmed what she feared most:
that she had been permanently changed for the worse, diminished in some way that could not be
reversed. These triggers, in other words, did not merely distress her — they actively fed the
appraisals, sharpening her conviction that she was broken.

It was that constellation of beliefs — the weakness, the permanent damage, the sense that her body
had become unreliable in a way no amount of clinical knowledge could remedy — that began to organise
her behaviour. She could not trust my body, she told her intake clinician, and so she stopped
looking at it. She avoids mirrors, avoids the moment of dressing, avoids any deliberate attention to
her physical self, as though not looking could contain what she fears she would find. She has
withdrawn from friends and from her partner in ways she does not fully articulate even to herself,
cancelling plans, offering thin excuses, finding reasons to be alone — because talking about what
happened feels like proof of the weakness she is trying to conceal. The avoidance, she believed, was
protective. What she could not see was that it was also generative: the withdrawal from others left
her beliefs about herself unchallenged and unwitnessed, and the silence around the event allowed
those beliefs to calcify. The less she spoke, the more certain she became that she was permanently
altered; the more certain she became, the more intolerable any reminder felt — so that even a
patient's passing comment about a hospital visit, the faint chemical smell on a colleague's hands,
could send her back into the same tight loop of shame and confirmation.

What finally brought Miriam to seek help was a moment at work three weeks ago. A patient asked,
during a routine examination, whether she had ever been seriously ill herself. Miriam found she
could not answer. She excused herself, stood in the corridor for several minutes, and returned
without responding to the question. That evening she told her partner, for the first time, that she
was not managing. She comes to therapy hoping, tentatively, that she can recover some version of
herself she recognises — and afraid, more than anything, that what she has lost is simply gone.
