for an initial psychological assessment reporting a PCL-5 score of 73, indicating severe
posttraumatic stress. He was referred following a workplace incident approximately fourteen months
ago in which he witnessed a colleague fall from scaffolding three stories above the ground. Dwayne
had been working directly below when the man came down, and he reached him first. What he saw in
those minutes before the ambulance arrived — the specific colour of the blood pooling on the
concrete, the smell of dust and copper that hung in the still air, the involuntary taste of bile
that rose in his own throat as he knelt beside his colleague — lodged themselves in his nervous
system in ways he could not have anticipated. His colleague survived but with catastrophic injuries,
and Dwayne returned to work the following week believing that getting back on site was the right
thing to do.

It was not. Over the weeks that followed, the memories returned not as narrative recollections but
as vivid sensory fragments — pictures that arrived without invitation, sudden and total: the angle
of a limb, the shadow of the scaffold against the sky. More disorienting still was the way smell and
taste had become encoded alongside those images. The sharp mineral scent of freshly poured concrete,
which surrounded Dwayne every working day, could without warning carry him back to the moment he
knelt on that ground. On one occasion, a mouthful of coffee in the site break room produced the same
acrid taste he had experienced that afternoon, and he found himself gripping the edge of the table,
momentarily unable to locate himself in the present. These sensory memories, arriving unbidden in
the environment where he spent most of his waking hours, began to reshape what he understood about
himself and the world. He had come to believe that his beliefs about how things worked — that effort
and caution could keep a man safe, that competence mattered — had been entirely destroyed by what he
had witnessed. Worse, the vividness and uncontrollability of the memories reinforced a deepening
conviction that he was fundamentally inadequate: a man who could not protect a colleague, could not
manage his own mind, and could not stop bad things from happening no matter what he did.

It was this cluster of appraisals — the helplessness, the inadequacy, the sense that the world had
been revealed as arbitrary and indifferent — that drove Dwayne toward the coping strategies he now
relied upon. He began gambling on weekday evenings at a casino forty minutes from his apartment,
initially telling himself it was a way to unwind. In truth, the noise and controlled risk of the
casino floor offered the only reliable interruption to the intrusive images; the excitement of a
hand of cards was one of the few states that temporarily displaced the sensory memories. He also
began, with some deliberateness, to dissociate — to practise a kind of mental departure from his own
body when the images came, blanking out, as he described it, going somewhere else inside himself.
And he suppressed his anger with a rigidity that had become a point of private discipline: he never
allowed himself to feel or express it, certain that if he permitted any emotion to surface, the rest
would follow and he would not be able to contain it. These strategies, born directly from his
conviction that he was powerless and broken, in turn kept the traumatic material from being
processed. The memories remained raw and unintegrated, and each time they returned with their full
sensory force — the smell, the images, the taste — they confirmed again that he was a man who could
not control his own inner life, deepening the appraisals of inadequacy and helplessness that had
generated the avoidance in the first place.

Over time, the consequences accumulated in concrete ways. He had been passed over for a crew foreman
position he had been informally promised, his supervisor citing a change in his reliability and
focus. His divorce, finalised two years earlier, had left him living alone, and the gambling had
quietly eroded what savings remained. It was the foreman decision — the occupational loss that made
the inadequacy feel externally confirmed — that finally brought him to seek help. What he most
feared, he said in the intake session, was that what he had seen on that site had shown him
something true about himself and the world that could not be undone. What he most hoped, though he
said it quietly and without much conviction, was that someone might tell him otherwise.
