Kofi is an eighteen-year-old African American man who joined the police force only months ago,
driven in part by a conviction — formed early and held tightly — that someone should be able to stop
violence before it destroys a family. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 77, reporting a
constellation of symptoms severe enough that his supervising sergeant recently placed him on
restricted duty after Kofi froze during a domestic disturbance call. That incident, and the shame
that followed, is what finally brought him through the door of the department's counselling service.

The trauma Kofi carries is not a single night but a pattern that repeated across most of his
childhood: his father's rages, the sound of furniture overturned, his mother's muffled crying
through a closed door, and on the worst occasions, the specific, terrible stillness that fell over
the apartment just before the violence escalated. He was rarely the direct target, but he was always
present — small, motionless in a corner, his body braced. He remembers one night with particular
acuity: he was perhaps nine years old, standing in the kitchen doorway, watching his father lift a
glass bottle from the counter. The image lives in him as a sequence of frozen frames — his father's
hand around the neck of the bottle, his mother's face turning, the particular quality of silence in
the room. Each moment is a photograph rather than a film, and they arrive without warning.

Since beginning his patrol duties, Kofi has found that the job delivers his triggers with near-daily
regularity. When he responds to calls in unfamiliar neighbourhoods, the moment he steps out of the
cruiser and scans the environment, his eyes move involuntarily to potential weapons — a loose brick,
a kitchen knife visible through a window, a man holding a belt — before he has consciously
registered anything else. That hypervigilance, already present, sharpens further when he encounters
unusual silence on a scene; the absence of noise carries for him the exact atmospheric charge of
that kitchen doorway, and the stillness alone is enough to pull the frozen images forward into full
awareness. When those memories surface on the job — the bottle, the braced body, the grief that
accompanies every recollection of his mother's face — his threat monitoring intensifies in response,
his body reading the past as present danger, and he begins scanning even more compulsively for
anything that might function as a weapon. The loop is not subtle. A colleague touched his shoulder
unexpectedly last month while he was filling out a report, and Kofi's body went rigid in the posture
of that nine-year-old in the doorway — his shoulders drawn in, his chin dropping — before he could
locate himself in the present. The physical reenactment was visible enough that the colleague asked
if he was all right.

What frightens Kofi most, beyond the memories themselves, is what he has concluded about their
meaning. He has come to believe that he cannot rely on himself — that a man who freezes at a
shoulder touch has no business carrying a badge — and this conviction that he is fundamentally
unreliable has begun to colour how he reads the world around him. Because he cannot trust his own
capacity to protect himself or others, every unfamiliar environment feels unassessable, and he
avoids them when off duty, keeping to the same three or four blocks near his apartment. He is also
increasingly certain that his reactions — the bodily reenactments, the intrusive grief, the way
silence makes his pulse climb — mean that something in him has broken in a way that ordinary people
do not break. The fear that he is going crazy is not a passing thought; it is a conclusion he
returns to daily. These appraisals of helplessness and self-distrust feed directly into his threat
monitoring: if he cannot rely on himself to judge danger accurately, then everything must be pre-
emptively treated as dangerous, and so his vigilance never fully disengages. That sustained state of
threat, in turn, makes him more sensitive to the very triggers — sudden touch, suspicious behaviour,
unnatural quiet — that collapse the distance between now and then.

Kofi comes to therapy hoping, cautiously, that someone can tell him he is not losing his mind. What
he is most afraid of is that they cannot.
