Linh Tran is a 48-year-old Vietnamese American woman who works as a private tutor, primarily
teaching middle and high school students in their homes and at a local community center. She was
referred for psychological evaluation following a disclosure to her primary care physician of a
prolonged history of physical abuse by her former husband, which ended approximately two years ago
when she left the marriage and moved in with her sister. Her PCL-5 score of 78 places her in the
severe range, and her presentation across the intake and first two sessions suggests a deeply
entrenched trauma response organized around persistent threat perception and a fractured
relationship with her own memory.

The abuse lasted nearly eleven years and escalated in its final period to incidents of severe
physical violence — grabbing, striking, and on one occasion being slammed against a kitchen counter
with sufficient force to fracture her wrist. What is clinically significant is not only the severity
but the unpredictability: her former husband's violence was often preceded by no discernible
warning, or by cues so mundane — the sound of a cabinet closing too loudly, the angle of a look she
gave him — that she could never reliably anticipate it. He would sometimes approach her from behind
as she stood at the bathroom mirror. He kept a hunting knife on the kitchen counter. These details,
ordinary in isolation, became the architecture of her current fear.

Linh's memories of the abuse do not present as a coherent narrative. They come instead as
disconnected fragments — a flash of his hand, the particular sound of his voice dropping low, the
cold surface of the counter against her back — that arrive without context or sequence, as though
the events happened to someone else and were photographed rather than lived. When these fragments
surface, the terror she felt in those moments returns with full physiological force, her heart
accelerating, her breath tightening, the fear indistinguishable from the original. This quality of
her memory — intrusive, sensory, and stripped of temporal context — feeds directly into how she
understands herself now. She has come to believe that the woman she was before the marriage is
simply gone, that she has been permanently altered in some essential way, and that this damage is
not something therapy or time can reverse. She describes herself as watching other people — her
students' parents, couples at the community center, neighbors chatting outside — through glass, as
someone who no longer fully belongs to the ordinary social world. This sense of permanent outsider
status is not merely a feeling of sadness; it has become a conviction that organizes her daily
experience.

That conviction, in turn, shapes how she holds the memories themselves. Because she believes herself
to be irreparably changed, she approaches her own recollections with a kind of braced dread, as if
revisiting them will only confirm the damage further — and so when fragments do surface, they carry
the full weight of that belief, intensifying the terror rather than allowing any processing or
resolution.

The memories, when triggered, activate an immediate behavioral response. Linh scans every room she
enters — her students' kitchens, the community center hallway, the parking lot — cataloguing exits
and potential weapons with a speed and thoroughness she cannot suppress. She checks mirrors
compulsively, in her car and in bathrooms, alert to who might be approaching from behind. She
monitors people around her with particular intensity, reading posture and facial expression for
signs of danger. In her tutoring work, this means she is often only partially present with her
students; part of her attention is always elsewhere, tracking. Several families have commented that
she seems distracted, and one family recently ended their arrangement with her, a loss she
experienced as both practical and confirming — further evidence, she felt, that she no longer
functions as she once did.

These strategies, though they feel necessary, sustain rather than reduce her sense of danger. The
constant scanning and mirror-checking keep her nervous system primed for threat, so that ordinary
stimuli — a raised voice in the next room, a man standing too close in a hallway — register as
urgent warnings and pull her back into the fragmented, terror-laden memories she is trying to
outrun. She notices knives on kitchen counters at every tutoring visit. Her body responds before she
has time to think.

Linh came to seek help not after a single crisis but after her sister gently pointed out that she
had not left the house on a weekend in three months. What she is most afraid of is that she is too
far gone to recover. What she is most hoping for, tentatively and with visible difficulty saying it
aloud, is to feel like she belongs somewhere again.
