Adaeze is an eighty-year-old Nigerian-born pastor who has served a small African immigrant
congregation in Minneapolis for nearly two decades. She carries herself with the deliberate
composure one might expect of a woman who has shepherded others through grief, but beneath that
bearing is a clinical picture that has been quietly tightening for years, and which she can no
longer contain alone.

The events that brought her to this country were not a single incident but a compressed season of
violence that she describes only in fragments. During the civil conflict that forced her family's
displacement, Adaeze was detained at a checkpoint by armed men. She remembers the particular weight
and shape of the weapon one of them pressed against her shoulder as he searched her belongings — the
cold geometry of it — and the way another man moved suddenly toward her from behind, his footsteps
arriving before she could turn. She was treated, in her words, as though she were cargo to be
inspected and discarded. She was eventually released, but the crossing that followed — crowded
buses, strangers pressed against her in transit, the constant surveillance of uniformed figures —
stretched the original terror across weeks of flight.

The memory of the checkpoint does not arrive cleanly. It forces itself into her mind without
invitation, carrying with it the sounds she heard that day — voices she cannot fully make out, a
metallic scraping she has never been able to name — and it carries such an extremity of experience
that she sometimes finds herself unable to believe it happened to her at all. It is this quality of
unreality, she says, that makes the memory most disturbing: it sits outside the story of who she
understands herself to be. When the memory surfaces, it reactivates her conviction that what was
done to her was a profound injustice that the world has simply absorbed and moved past, and that she
herself has been rendered less than human by the way she was handled. These appraisals, once
activated, do not settle — they return her to the emotional state of the checkpoint, making the past
feel present, and making the present feel dangerous.

Adaeze now rides the city bus to reach her congregation each week, and it is on that bus that the
clinical picture becomes most visible. The smell of diesel, the press of bodies, the sight of
anything that resembles a weapon — an umbrella carried at an angle, a long-handled tool —
immediately places her back at the border. These triggers send her into a state of hypervigilant
scanning: she watches every passenger's hands, tracks sudden movements in her peripheral vision, and
turns to check behind her repeatedly before she can sit down. What frightens her is not only the
memory but the certainty, which the triggers reinforce, that danger is present and that no one
around her recognises it. Her sense of injustice sharpens in these moments — she had come to believe
that a woman of her age, her faith, her service, should not have to feel this way, and the fact that
she does confirms something degrading about how the world has treated her.

To manage this, Adaeze has constructed a set of habits she regards as practical. She wears loose,
layered clothing that obscures her shape, reasoning that not being noticed is safer than being seen.
She has stopped discussing the refugee experience with anyone — including her husband, who knows
only that she finds the subject painful — and she has declined, for years, to seek any medical or
psychological help, certain that opening those memories deliberately would destroy her. These
strategies have narrowed her world considerably: she no longer visits congregation members who live
across town, and she has reduced her pastoral duties to those she can reach on foot. But the
avoidance has not brought quiet. By keeping the memories unexamined and unspoken, she has
inadvertently preserved their power; the concealment means that ordinary situations — a parishioner
reaching into a bag, a stranger standing too close — remain unprocessed triggers, and her vigilance
never finds a reason to soften. A fleeting dissociative feeling occasionally accompanies the worst
moments, a sense of the world becoming slightly unreal, though she rarely names it as such.

What finally brought Adaeze to seek help was a Sunday morning when she froze at the church door,
unable to enter her own sanctuary because a maintenance worker was carrying a long metal pipe across
the entryway. She stood outside for several minutes, her heart loud in her chest, and understood
that she could no longer lead her congregation through their suffering while carrying this alone.
She hopes, cautiously, that something can be done. What she fears most is that thinking about it
directly will prove her right — that she will not be able to handle it.
