Rangi Parata had spent thirty years reading the sky. As a combat pilot, he had learned to process
threat at speeds most people could not imagine — a flicker of movement at the edge of his peripheral
vision, a change in engine tone, a shadow that did not belong. That particular competence had kept
him alive through two deployments and more training accidents than he cared to count. What it had
not prepared him for was the morning eighteen months ago when his aircraft suffered a catastrophic
hydraulic failure during a routine training sortie over the Pacific, and the sky he had always
trusted became something that wanted to kill him. The aircraft entered an uncontrolled roll at
altitude. For four minutes — an interval that Rangi now describes as both instantaneous and endless
— he fought the controls while the ocean and the horizon exchanged places repeatedly through his
canopy. He managed a partial recovery and ejected at dangerously low altitude, sustaining spinal
compression injuries on landing. He survived. His co-pilot did not.

In the weeks that followed, as Rangi recovered in hospital and then at home, something that had once
felt solid in him began to give way. He had always understood himself through the lens of his
vocation — the steadiness, the technical mastery, the capacity to remain functional under extreme
pressure. Now he sat in his living room in San Diego and felt like a stranger wearing his own face.
He had come to believe, with a quiet and terrible certainty, that whatever the accident had taken
from him was not coming back. The man who had walked onto that flight deck was gone, and what
remained was permanently altered — diminished in some way he could not precisely name but felt
constantly. He noticed that he could not access emotions that had once come easily: tenderness
toward his wife, Mere, satisfaction after a good meal, the mild pleasure of an evening on the back
porch. These were not merely muted; they felt inaccessible, as though the circuitry had been burned
out. He was certain he would never feel normal again.

It was this conviction — that he was now a fundamentally damaged and unpredictable version of
himself — that fed directly into how he moved through the world. Because Rangi no longer trusted his
own responses or his own judgment, he compensated by treating every environment as a potential
emergency. If the man he had been could not be relied upon, then the environment had to be
controlled instead. At restaurants with Mere, he would position himself with his back to the wall
and spend the first several minutes cataloguing exits — the front door, the kitchen corridor, the
fire escape visible through a side window — before he could settle enough to read the menu. At home,
which had once been a place of straightforward ease, he remained on guard in a way that exhausted
him and bewildered his wife. He checked the locks repeatedly before bed, sat facing doorways, and
startled badly at sounds that registered to anyone else as ordinary — a car backfiring two streets
away, the particular pitch of a smoke alarm in a neighbouring apartment. His hypervigilance was not,
in his own accounting, irrational; it was the logical response of a man who no longer believed he
could trust his own capacity to cope if something went wrong. The threat monitoring was, in a sense,
the only remaining competence he felt confident claiming.

Over eighteen months this had narrowed his life considerably. He had been placed on extended medical
leave, and the squadron environment — the sound of jet engines, the sight of flight suits, the
specific quality of early-morning light on a tarmac — had become impossible to approach without a
flooding sense of danger he could not modulate. His marriage, though intact, had grown strained
under the weight of his guardedness and his emotional unavailability. Mere had begun to describe
herself, gently but unmistakably, as lonely.

What finally brought Rangi to the clinic was a conversation with his commanding officer about a
possible return to administrative duties. The prospect of re-entering that world — not the cockpit,
just the building — had produced a fear so acute it frightened him. What he hopes for, he said in
the intake session, is to recognise himself again. What he is most afraid of is that there is no
longer anyone there to recognise.
