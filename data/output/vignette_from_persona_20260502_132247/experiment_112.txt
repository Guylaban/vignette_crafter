Kiet Nguyen is a twenty-two-year-old Vietnamese-American UX designer who presents with a PCL-5 score
of 56 following a devastating earthquake that struck while he was visiting extended family in
central Vietnam eighteen months ago. The quake hit without warning in the early morning, collapsing
part of the house around him. He remembers the particular pressure of a ceiling beam across the back
of his neck, the acrid taste of plaster dust filling his mouth, and the sound of his aunt calling
his name from somewhere he could not locate in the dark. Several family members were injured; one
did not survive. He was evacuated within hours, but the event left a mark on him that has not
loosened its grip.

In the weeks that followed his return to the United States, Kiet noticed that certain experiences
seemed to reach directly into that morning and pull it forward. A stiff neck after long hours at his
workstation — entirely ordinary for someone who spends his days arranging wireframes and prototyping
interfaces — would arrive as something else entirely: the pressure of the beam, the dust on his
tongue, the sense that the walls were still moving. The somatic pain in his neck functions as one of
the most reliable triggers he knows, and when it fires, the memory does not return as a coherent
narrative so much as an atmosphere — taste without image, dread without scene, the full emotional
weight of the event arriving before any picture forms. He has described sitting at his desk, feeling
suddenly and inexplicably ill, the nausea and physical unwellness spreading through him, and finding
himself back in that collapsed room without being able to say exactly how he got there. Hearing
colleagues mention anything adjacent to the disaster — a news segment about flooding, a conversation
about structural damage — operates the same way, the triggers pulling the memory forward with a
force that feels disproportionate and humiliating.

What troubles Kiet as much as the intrusions themselves is what they seem to confirm about the
world. Each time the memory surfaces — fragmentary, overwhelming, tasting of plaster — he finds it
harder to hold onto any stable sense of his surroundings or the people in them. The memory carries a
quality of unreality that has seeped outward: the event was so extreme, so far outside anything he
had been prepared for, that he has come to feel that ordinary life is a kind of performance, and
that people around him are similarly performing a safety they do not actually possess. He has grown
convinced that people are not what they seem — that the colleagues who laugh easily at lunch, the
friends who make weekend plans, are either unaware of how fragile everything is or are concealing
it. This appraisal of others as unknowable and fundamentally unreliable has in turn narrowed his
world considerably. He avoids team social events at the design agency where he works, declines
invitations, and has stopped visiting the gym where he used to train, partly because the physical
exertion reliably produces neck tension that he cannot trust not to trigger him. The avoidance has
not gone unnoticed: his manager recently commented on his withdrawal from collaborative sessions,
and a close friend from college stopped reaching out after several unanswered messages.

The sense that people cannot be trusted has itself become a source of new triggers. Moments of
social friction — a misread message, a colleague's ambiguous expression — now carry a charge they
did not before, confirming his belief that he does not belong among people who have not been through
what he has. He has come to feel isolated and set apart in a way that feels permanent rather than
circumstantial, and more recently he has begun to feel that he no longer belongs anywhere at all —
not in the United States, where the disaster feels unreal to everyone around him, and not in
Vietnam, which he associates entirely with what happened. This sense of belonging nowhere has made
ordinary life feel like an extended waiting room, and the isolation itself keeps him alert for signs
of threat, so that even benign physical sensations or passing references to disaster pull the memory
forward again.

What finally brought Kiet to seek help was a presentation at work he could not complete — he had
been storyboarding a user journey when a colleague mentioned an earthquake in the news, and he left
the room and did not return. He is most afraid that nothing will feel real or safe again. What he
hopes, cautiously, is that someone can help him understand why he cannot simply leave that morning
behind.
