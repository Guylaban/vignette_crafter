Marcus is a 34-year-old married school counselor of North American background who presented for
assessment following a significant decline in his occupational and personal functioning over the
preceding eighteen months. His PCL-5 score of 77 places him in the severe range of posttraumatic
symptomatology, and the picture that emerged across the intake and early sessions was one of a man
whose sense of self had been fundamentally fractured by a single afternoon that he cannot fully
leave behind.

Approximately two years ago, Marcus was driving home from work on a rainy evening when, momentarily
distracted by his phone, he struck a cyclist who had moved into his lane. The impact was violent. He
remembers stepping out of the car and smelling the rain mixed with something metallic — blood and
asphalt — and kneeling beside the cyclist, a teenager, whose injuries were severe. Emergency
services arrived within minutes, but Marcus remained on the scene, hands shaking, unable to form
complete sentences when the paramedics questioned him. The teenager survived but sustained lasting
physical damage. No criminal charges were filed, but that legal resolution did nothing to quiet what
Marcus had already decided about himself: that he had done this, that it was entirely his fault, and
that a person capable of such carelessness could not be trusted.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus began to notice that certain things pulled him back to the road.
The smell of rain on warm pavement — something he encountered every morning walking from the school
parking lot — could stop him mid-step, his chest tightening, his body flooding with the physical
reactivity that had no regard for whether he was standing in a hallway full of teenagers or sitting
in his own kitchen. When those sensory cues arrived, the memory did not feel like a recollection; it
felt like a resumption, as though the accident were still unfolding across all his senses
simultaneously, vivid and immediate and inescapable. He found himself unable to describe what had
happened to his wife in any coherent way — he knew the sequence of events, but the words dissolved
before he could speak them, leaving him silent and ashamed in a way that deepened his conviction
that something essential in him had broken.

His appraisals of himself hardened over time into something close to certainty. He had come to
believe that the event had happened because of the way he acted — the phone, the split second of
inattention — and that this was not an aberration but a revelation of who he truly was. He was
certain he could not trust himself to do the right thing in any high-stakes moment, a belief that
made his work as a school counselor quietly unbearable. When a student arrived in crisis, Marcus
would feel the familiar surge of fear and guilt, and the thought that he might fail this child the
way he had failed the cyclist would trigger the memory all over again, pulling him back to the smell
and the sound and the teenager on the wet road. The belief that his life had been destroyed by the
trauma was not metaphorical to Marcus; it was a daily accounting of everything he could no longer
be.

To manage this, Marcus had constructed an elaborate architecture of avoidance. He kept his mind
occupied at all times — podcasts during his commute, administrative tasks stacked deliberately to
crowd out any reflective space, television running through the evening. He avoided thinking about
the event directly and had rerouted his driving routes to avoid passing police vehicles, which
brought the scene rushing back with a physical force that left him sweating and hyperalert. Yet
these strategies had begun to work against him. The constant mental occupation prevented him from
processing anything, and the suppression itself seemed to make the memory more intrusive, surfacing
with greater vividness precisely when he had been most vigilant against it. The avoidance had also
eroded his marriage; his wife described him as absent even when present, and he had declined a
medical check-up twice, unable to tolerate the clinical smells of a doctor's office. He slept
poorly, lying awake in a state of low-level alertness he could not explain or switch off.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a session with a student in which he had frozen
entirely — unable to respond, flooded, staring at the wall — and the student had gone to find
another adult. He arrived at intake frightened that he was becoming a danger to the young people he
was supposed to protect, and most hoping, he said quietly, that someone could help him believe the
accident was something that happened to him, rather than proof of everything he feared he was.
