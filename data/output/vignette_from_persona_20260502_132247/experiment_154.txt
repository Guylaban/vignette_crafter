Priya is a 79-year-old South Asian woman, married and still active as a commercial airline pilot — a
career that has defined her identity for more than five decades. She was referred for psychological
assessment following a routine occupational health review in which she disclosed persistent sleep
difficulties and episodes of intense emotional dysregulation. Her PCL-5 score of 39 places her in
the clinically significant range, and the picture that emerged across intake and early sessions
points to a reactivation of childhood trauma that has quietly structured her inner life for years.

Priya grew up in a household where violence was a nightly possibility. Her father, a man whose rages
arrived without warning, would beat her mother with a particular ferocity that seemed amplified by
the confined silence of the family home after dark. Priya would lie in her childhood bed, alone in
the dark, listening — the smell of antiseptic from her mother's post-injury care drifting under the
door, a specific song her mother used to hum to herself in the aftermath playing softly from the
next room. These sensory details were never processed as past events; they were sealed into her
nervous system as present dangers.

Decades later, those same sensory channels remain open. When Priya is on a layover and sleeps alone
in an unfamiliar hotel room, the darkness and solitude pull her back without warning — she feels,
with a conviction she cannot reason away, that the violence is happening now, not then. The memories
do not arrive as coherent narrative; they come in disconnected pieces, vivid fragments more
immediate and textured than anything from ordinary waking life, arriving in the wrong order and
refusing to settle into the past. When a particular song drifts through an airport terminal, or when
she passes through the medical bay at her airline's hub and catches the sharp antiseptic smell, the
same fragmentary intrusions ignite, and the sense of present danger is indistinguishable from the
original fear. These triggered memory states in turn heighten her vigilance sharply — she finds
herself scanning her hotel room, checking locks, waking in the night to confirm she is safe, feeling
on guard even inside her own home when she returns from a trip.

The hypervigilance does not resolve when the immediate trigger passes. Instead, the elevated arousal
seems to sensitise her to the next encounter — she finds herself noticing the antiseptic smell in
places she would previously have ignored, bracing for songs she has not yet heard, dreading the next
solo overnight. The threat monitoring keeps the traumatic material close to the surface, and when it
surfaces it confirms, in her mind, that she is not managing. She has come to believe, with quiet
certainty, that her reactions prove she is a lousy coper — that a woman of her age and professional
standing should have long since mastered whatever this is. She is certain she cannot rely on
herself, that even a minor upset now will overwhelm her completely. These convictions about her own
inadequacy deepen the sense of ongoing threat, as if her inability to control her responses is
itself evidence that something dangerous is still present.

When the memories break through and the threat feels total, Priya manages the distress in ways that
have accumulated into habit over months. She stays up until two or three in the morning in her hotel
room, eating from the minibar — overeating has become a reliable, if temporary, blunting of the
internal noise. She nurses elaborate revenge fantasies directed at her long-dead father, replaying
confrontations that never happened. These strategies carry their own costs: the late nights and
disrupted eating leave her fatigued and emotionally raw the following morning, which lowers her
threshold for the next trigger and makes the cockpit environment — where she must project calm
authority to a crew — feel like a performance she is barely sustaining. The fatigue also keeps the
traumatic material nearer the surface; a restless night spent half-awake in an unfamiliar room is
itself a condition that recalls the original childhood nights, so that the strategies meant to
contain the distress quietly recreate the sensory conditions that trigger it. Her belief that she
cannot cope is reinforced every time she reaches for food at midnight or retreats into fantasy
rather than sleep, and she arrives at each new day more convinced of her own fragility.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a moment in the cockpit on a transatlantic flight when a
colleague's cologne briefly recalled the antiseptic smell, and she felt, for several seconds,
entirely elsewhere. She said nothing to her crew. What she is most afraid of is that she will never
be free of it — and, more pressingly, that someone will find out.
