warehouse shifts — moving inventory, loading pallets, operating in the low hum of fluorescent light

during a routine break, apparently triggered by nothing his coworkers could identify. He arrived for

picture rooted not in a single adult crisis but in something far older and far less fully formed in
his memory.

Kofi grew up in a rural setting — fields, tree lines, the smell of damp earth and wood smoke — and
it was in that environment, as a young child, that he witnessed sustained and severe domestic
violence between his parents. He cannot reconstruct it cleanly. What he has are pieces: a door
swinging open hard against a wall, the particular smell of his father's breath, a feeling of cold
ground beneath him, the sound of something breaking. He does not have a narrative. He has fragments
that do not connect to one another and, when he tries deliberately to sit with what happened, the
memory recedes — he reaches for it and finds only blankness or a sudden, overwhelming wave of
feeling he cannot attach to any image. This quality of the memory — its fragmentation, its
resistance to deliberate recall, the way emotion arrives without content — means that Kofi has spent
most of his adult life uncertain of what, precisely, he is carrying.

What the memory cannot deliver consciously, the body and the environment deliver without warning. On
days when Kofi's warehouse work takes him to the loading dock at the edge of the property, where
scrubland and open sky are visible beyond the fence, something shifts in him that he cannot name but
cannot ignore. Natural settings — the particular quality of open land, the smell of vegetation —
reach back across seven decades and pull. A door slamming in the warehouse, the kind of sharp
percussive bang that happens dozens of times a shift, can stop him mid-step. The smell of alcohol on
a colleague's breath during a break has, more than once, sent him outside shaking. These fragments
of the environment act as keys to something the memory itself has sealed shut, and the distress they
produce is immediate and physical, arriving before any thought has formed.

When those moments come, Kofi's response has, over decades, settled into a set of strategies that
feel to him like management but function as maintenance. He drinks — not heavily by his own

counter sleep aids in combination. He picks arguments. At work, at home before his marriage ended,

him, because silence and solitude are where the feelings find him. But the alcohol, in particular,
has begun to work against him in a way he is only now acknowledging: it blurs the already fragmented
memory further, leaving him with even less access to what happened, which means the fragments that
do break through feel more alien, more uncontrollable, more likely to erupt in response to the next
door slam or the next stretch of open field. The strategies that were meant to manage the triggers

never have a clear account of what happened in that house. What he wants, he said quietly at the end
of the second session, is to stop being ambushed by a past he cannot even properly see.
