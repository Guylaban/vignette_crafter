Priya is a 67-year-old South Asian woman who has been married for over forty years and has spent the
last two decades working for a small non-profit organization focused on immigrant family services in
her city. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 70, reflecting severe posttraumatic stress, and was
referred for psychological assessment following a disclosure she made to her primary care physician
during a routine appointment — the first time she had told anyone in a clinical setting what had
happened to her.

The assault occurred approximately eight months ago. Priya had stayed late at the non-profit's
office to finish grant documentation, something she had done countless times without incident. A man
she recognized as a peripheral contact of the organization — someone who had attended community
events — found her alone in the building. What followed was a sexual assault that lasted, by her
account, nearly an hour. She did not report it to the police. She drove herself home, showered, and
told her husband she had been held up with paperwork. She has not told him what happened.

In the weeks that followed, Priya continued going to work. She had no language for what she was
experiencing — or rather, she had language, but she refused to apply it to herself. Women her age,
from her background, did not speak of such things, she believed, and the shame she carried was so
dense and so private that it functioned almost as a second skin. She began arriving at the office
only when colleagues were already present and leaving before the building emptied. The late evenings
she had once used for focused, solitary work became impossible. Her productivity declined
noticeably, and her supervisor — a woman she respects deeply — has gently raised concerns about
missed deadlines and uncharacteristic errors. Priya has offered no explanation.

At home, she has grown quieter in ways her husband has noticed but not named. She declines
invitations to community gatherings, particularly those held in the evening or in spaces she
associates, however loosely, with the organization's broader network. She has told herself this is
temporary, that she simply needs time, but eight months have passed and the withdrawal has only
deepened.

Priya's symptoms are pervasive and severe. She experiences intrusive memories of the assault that
arrive without warning — during meetings, while cooking, while lying beside her husband at night.
She has nightmares several times a week. She describes a persistent state of hypervigilance that she
experiences as exhausting and humiliating in equal measure; she monitors exits whenever she enters a
room, scans faces for threat, and startles at sounds in the office hallway in ways she fears her
colleagues have begun to notice. She feels emotionally numb much of the time, disconnected from the
warmth she once felt toward her husband and her work, and she describes a pervasive sense that
something essential in her has been permanently altered. She is troubled by difficulty concentrating
and by sleep that is rarely restful.

What finally brought Priya to her physician was not the symptoms themselves but a moment at work
three weeks ago. A younger colleague — a woman in her late twenties who coordinates survivor
services — asked Priya to co-facilitate a workshop on trauma-informed care for newly arrived refugee
families. Priya sat with the request for two days before realizing she could not enter that room,
could not stand before those women and speak about surviving, without something in her breaking open
entirely. She declined, citing scheduling conflicts, and then went to the bathroom and wept for the
first time since the assault. It was the weeping, she told her physician, that frightened her — not
because it hurt, but because it felt like the beginning of something she did not know how to
control.

She comes to therapy hoping, in her words, to "return to being useful." What she fears most, though
she has not yet said it directly, is that she cannot — that the woman who stayed late to finish the
work, who built something meaningful over two decades, is no longer accessible to her.
