Tane Faleolo is a 24-year-old Samoan-American pharmacist presenting with a PCL-5 score of 58
following the traumatic death of his younger brother, Sione, approximately fourteen months ago.
Sione, then nineteen, was struck and killed by a drunk driver while walking home from a family
gathering on a Saturday night. Tane had been the last family member to speak with him, having argued
with him briefly at the party over something he now considers trivial. He watched the emergency
response from a distance, unable to reach his brother through the crowd before Sione was pronounced
dead at the scene. The smell of alcohol had been everywhere that evening — on the guests, on the
driver when police restrained him, on the pavement itself, it seemed. The details of that night did
not settle into a coherent memory so much as fragment into sensory shards that have never
reassembled into a story Tane can tell, even to himself.

In the weeks that followed, Tane found himself unable to construct a clear account of what had
happened. When he tries to recall the sequence of events, the narrative collapses — he reaches for
it and finds only flashes: Sione's jacket, a stranger's face, the sound of sirens. At other times
the memory surfaces without warning and he feels nothing at all, as though he is reviewing footage
of someone else's loss. But more often, recall arrives with a wave of guilt so intense it becomes
its own kind of punishment, rooted in the conviction that the argument, his failure to walk Sione
home, his inaction in those final minutes, made him complicit. These fragmented, guilt-saturated
memories do not stay contained. They spill outward, sensitising him to anything that echoes the
original night — and so the memories themselves have become a source of new triggers, priming him to
detect threat in ordinary experience.

Tane works in a community pharmacy in a busy neighbourhood, and it is there that the triggers have
accumulated most visibly. The smell of alcohol on a customer's breath — common enough in a retail
setting — can stop him mid-sentence, flooding him with the sensory texture of that night. When he
witnesses a customer being rude to a colleague, or sees someone behaving carelessly or dishonestly,
the moral wrongness of it lands on him with disproportionate force, pulling him back toward the
image of the driver. He has declined every invitation to attend a funeral since Sione's burial, and
when a colleague mentioned a cemetery near the pharmacy, he changed the subject and spent the rest
of the shift unable to concentrate. His broader sense that his life has been destroyed by what
happened, and that he was once a happy person who no longer exists, has made these triggers feel not
like isolated reactions but like confirmation — as though the world keeps providing evidence for
what he already knows about himself. This appraisal of total ruin amplifies the potency of each
trigger, so that situations which might otherwise pass unremarked now carry the weight of proof.

His body has not been quiet through any of this. He is certain, in a way he cannot argue himself out
of, that he is permanently altered — that his nervous system now operates on a hair trigger,
reacting before thought can intervene. He startles at sounds in the dispensary, keeps himself
positioned near exits, and notices his jaw clenched by mid-morning most days. These physical states
of hyperarousal are not neutral; they feed directly back into his conviction that his life is broken
beyond repair, that a body this vigilant belongs to someone who has lost something permanent. The
sense of meaninglessness and destroyed happiness is not merely a mood — it is, for Tane, a
conclusion drawn from the evidence his own body keeps presenting.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at work last month when a customer smelled
strongly of alcohol and Tane had to excuse himself to the back room, where he sat on the floor for
several minutes unable to stand. A supervisor noticed. Tane is most afraid that he will never feel
like himself again — and most tentatively hoping, though he would not easily say so, that someone
might show him he is wrong.
