Fatima is a 51-year-old divorced woman of Lebanese descent who works as a social ministry worker in
a mid-sized city in the American Midwest. She came to the intake appointment alone, though she had
rescheduled twice before finally arriving, and she seated herself immediately adjacent to the office
door — a habit, she explained quietly, that she no longer questions. She has a PCL-5 score of 62,
and the history she offered across the first two sessions was fragmentary, delivered in pieces she
seemed to retrieve with physical effort.

The trauma Fatima carries is not a single event but a sustained architecture of harm that began in
early childhood. Her primary caregiver — her father — was physically violent throughout her
formative years, and the violence was unpredictable in its timing but consistent in its character:
she would be struck from behind, from the side, without warning, often while her back was turned.
The blows came when she was seated, when she was crouching, when she was cornered. The body learned
what the mind could not articulate. Decades later, Fatima cannot describe what happened in any
coherent narrative sequence. When she tries to put the experience into words, the language
dissolves. What remains instead is sensory and somatic — a tightening across the shoulders, a
bracing in the lower back, a posture of anticipatory collapse that her body reassumes without her
permission when the memories surface. She described sitting in a staff meeting last autumn and
feeling, without any identifiable trigger, that her spine had curved inward, that her arms had come
up. Her colleague asked if she was cold. She said yes. When the memories come, they also bring rage
— not grief, not fear, but a white and disorienting fury that she finds more frightening than the
sadness she expected. She has no framework for it.

Because the memories arrive without warning and carry such physical and emotional force, Fatima has
developed a set of strategies that began as reasonable precautions and have since become the
scaffolding of her daily life. She avoids being alone with any other person in an enclosed space — a
rule she maintains with quiet vigilance at work, where her ministry role requires home visits and
one-on-one client meetings. She has declined several case assignments over the past year, citing
scheduling conflicts, and her supervisor has begun to notice. She ensures she is always near an exit
— in the office, in waiting rooms, in the church hall where her ministry team holds community meals.
She has, without announcing it, reorganized her entire spatial relationship to every room she
enters. She has also, with increasing frequency, simply avoided going to work at all, calling in on
mornings when the anticipatory dread becomes too heavy. These absences have cost her professionally
and have deepened her sense of isolation.

The threat she perceives in ordinary environments is constant and exhausting. She is frequently
irritable — snapping at colleagues who approach her desk from behind, raising her voice in ways she
later regrets — and she has come to believe, with a certainty that embarrasses her, that she is
simply dangerous to be around. She checks behind her when she walks down the corridor at work,
scanning for footsteps. She reacts to a door closing too loudly as though something has been
launched at her. This sustained state of alarm feeds directly back into the memories: when her body
is already primed, already scanning, the somatic fragments return more readily, and the rage that
accompanies them feels less like a symptom and more like evidence of something broken in her. The
checking and the avoidance keep her nervous system in a register where the past feels present, which
in turn makes the memories more intrusive, which makes the threat feel more real, which makes the
exits more necessary.

What finally brought Fatima to seek help was a morning three months ago when she sat in her car in
the ministry parking lot for forty minutes, unable to go inside, certain — without reason she could
name — that something terrible was waiting for her in the building where she has worked for eleven
years. She drove home. She did not call in. She sat in her apartment until dark. What she is most
afraid of, she said at the end of the second session, is that this is simply who she is now. What
she is most hoping for is that it isn't.
