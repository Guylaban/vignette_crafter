Adaeze is a 47-year-old Nigerian-American architect, married, and until recently regarded by
colleagues as one of the most meticulous and composed professionals in her firm. Fourteen months
ago, she was overseeing a site inspection at a mid-rise residential project she had designed.
Distracted by a phone call from a contractor and moving too quickly through an incompletely secured
scaffold walkway, she failed to notice that a junior site engineer had followed her onto the
platform. When she stepped back to gesture at a structural detail, she knocked him into a partially
open stairwell. He fell nearly two storeys, fracturing his pelvis and shattering his left wrist.
Adaeze heard the sound before she saw what had happened — a sharp crack of metal, then his voice
cutting off mid-sentence into silence, then the dull concussion of impact below. She was the one who
called the ambulance. She stood at the edge of the stairwell and watched the paramedics work,
tasting the metallic coffee she had drunk that morning as though it had never left her mouth.

In the months that followed, the memory did not behave the way Adaeze expected memory to behave.
When she tried deliberately to reconstruct the sequence of events — to understand exactly what she
had done wrong — she found the account strangely inaccessible, fragmentary, resistant. Yet without
warning, in the middle of a client presentation or lying beside her husband at night, the memory
would return in pieces she had not chosen: the crack of the scaffold, the engineer's voice stopping,
the taste of cold coffee flooding her mouth, and sometimes his cry from below rendered so precisely
in her mind that she would turn her head, certain someone nearby had called out. These unbidden
intrusions carried with them a quality of present-tense danger that her conscious knowledge of
safety could not override, and she would find herself scanning the room, heart accelerating, certain
that something was about to go wrong — even in her own kitchen, even at her desk. The intrusive
sensory return of the memory was reliably triggering a state of alarm, and in that alarmed state she
found the images intensified rather than resolved, the sounds sharpening rather than fading.

Adaeze had come to believe, with a conviction that felt irrefutable, that another architect — a more
careful one, a better one — simply would not have been on that walkway distracted, would not have
moved without checking. This belief sat at the centre of everything. She began dwelling on every
decision she had made that morning, rehearsing the chain of her failures in exhaustive detail, and
this rumination seemed to pull the fragmented memory back into circulation, feeding its frequency
rather than containing it. At the same time, she had developed a set of private mental rituals at
work: before entering any construction site, she would silently count through a sequence of safety
checks in a fixed order, and if interrupted she would start again, convinced that completing the
ritual correctly was the only thing standing between her professional actions and another
catastrophe. She told herself, on harder days, that at least the engineer had survived, that it
could have been worse — but the comparison offered only seconds of relief before the self-
recrimination resumed. These strategies, which she understood intellectually to be irrational,
paradoxically kept the threat feeling immediate and the memory close to the surface, so that her
body remained in a state of readiness that exhausted her.

The hypervigilance had spread. Ordinary sounds on site — a dropped tool, a shout across scaffolding
— would send a jolt through her that left her shaking. She had reduced her site visits to the
minimum her role permitted, delegating walk-throughs to junior colleagues and offering professional
reasons that were not entirely false but were not the whole truth. Her husband had noticed the
withdrawal; she felt unable to explain to him, or to anyone, the person she had become, and that
incapacity deepened her sense of isolation. The woman who had once moved through construction sites
with quiet authority felt unrecognisable to her now.

What finally brought Adaeze to seek help was a meeting with the recovered engineer — a routine
professional encounter, months after his return to work. She heard his voice in the corridor before
she saw him, and her body responded as though the fall were happening again. She sat in her car for
forty minutes afterward, unable to drive. She came to her first session hoping, above everything, to
feel like herself again — and afraid that she no longer knew who that was.
