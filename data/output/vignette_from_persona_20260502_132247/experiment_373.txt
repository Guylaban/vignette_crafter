Rajan is a 58-year-old South Asian widower who works as a fisherman along the Gulf Coast. He was
referred for psychological assessment following a motor vehicle accident fourteen months ago in
which a truck ran a red light and struck the driver's side of his car at high speed. The impact
killed his passenger — a close friend and fellow fisherman with whom he had worked for nearly two
decades — and left Rajan with fractured ribs, a lacerated forearm, and a period of unconsciousness
whose exact duration he cannot account for. He woke to the sound of metal settling and an
extraordinary, suffocating silence before the first sirens reached him. His wife had died of cancer
three years prior, and the loss of his friend compounded a grief that had never fully resolved. He
presents now with a PCL-5 score of 65, describing symptoms that have progressively narrowed his
world.

In the weeks following the accident, Rajan found that certain moments cracked open without warning.
Driving past intersections, he would catch a glimpse of a car moving too quickly at the periphery of
his vision and find himself back inside the wreckage — not remembering it, but inhabiting it, the
present moment dissolving into the impact as though no time had passed at all. The memory carried
the same terror he had felt in those first conscious seconds: the silence, the blood on his hands,
his friend motionless beside him. What disturbed him most was that he could not put it into
language. He knew what had happened — the sequence of it, the facts — but when he tried to speak
about it, the words simply were not there, as though the experience existed in a register that
language could not reach. These intrusive returns were not confined to traffic. Conversations about
loss — a neighbor mentioning a bereavement, a radio segment on grief — could pull the memory forward
just as forcefully, the terror arriving intact, undiminished by the months between. In this way the
triggers themselves became sites of re-experiencing, each one confirming to Rajan that the event had
not truly ended.

That sense of unfinished danger reshaped how he understood himself and the world around him. He had
come to believe that his body could not be trusted — the unconsciousness during the accident, the
way his hands had shaken for months afterward, the chest pain that doctors attributed to anxiety but
which felt to him like evidence of structural failure. He no longer recognized the man who had once
hauled nets in the pre-dawn dark without a second thought. The accident had also corroded his sense
of other people. Strangers, he was now certain, were unpredictable in ways that were not visible on
the surface — the truck driver had seemed, by all accounts, an ordinary man. Talking to someone he
did not know became a source of dread rather than the casual exchange it had once been, and these
appraisals of an untrustworthy world and an unreliable self kept the memory close, as though his
mind could not file away an event whose implications remained so unsettled. The memory, in turn,
reinforced what he feared: that he was permanently altered, that people concealed their dangers,
that his body would fail him again when it mattered.

This constellation of beliefs fed directly into a state of near-constant physiological vigilance. At
home in the evenings, alone since his friend's death, Rajan found himself listening — for what, he
could not say — and an unusual silence in the house felt less like peace than like the pause before
something terrible. He scanned the water and the dock each morning before boarding his boat,
cataloguing anomalies, and the habit of threat-monitoring sharpened the very triggers he was trying
to anticipate, so that a car on the road near the marina, or a stranger approaching the dock,
arrived already loaded with danger. The hypervigilance kept the memory primed, and the memory kept
the vigilance alive.

Over the past several months Rajan has spoken less to the other fishermen he works alongside,
deflecting conversations and leaving the dock early to avoid the informal exchanges where loss might
surface. A longtime docking partner has stopped seeking his company. He presents now because his
daughter, visiting from Houston, told him plainly that she was frightened for him. What he most
fears is that the man he was before the accident is simply gone. What he most hopes, though he holds
the hope cautiously, is that someone might help him find words for what he cannot yet say.
