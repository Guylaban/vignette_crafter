Yasmine Khalil is an eighteen-year-old woman of Lebanese descent who recently began working as a
junior drafter at a small architectural firm in Detroit, having completed an accelerated pre-
professional program. She presented for an initial intake following a referral from her university's
counseling center, where a brief screening had flagged her PCL-5 score of 59 as clinically
significant. Though young for her role, Yasmine had always been described by teachers and mentors as
unusually focused and self-possessed. That quality, she would later say, was precisely what made the
months following the event so disorienting — she no longer recognized herself.

Approximately four months before seeking help, Yasmine was walking home from the firm on a mild
weekday evening when she witnessed a man attack a woman on the pavement less than twenty feet ahead
of her. The assault was sudden and prolonged. Yasmine froze at the corner, unable to move or call
out, watching the woman fall and hearing sounds she has since described only obliquely — a crack, a
cry that stopped too quickly. By the time other pedestrians intervened and someone called 911,
Yasmine had backed against a building wall, shaking. She was not physically harmed. She gave a brief
statement to police and walked the remaining six blocks home alone. That night she did not sleep.

What has persisted since is a state of near-constant physiological and emotional arousal that
Yasmine finds both exhausting and, in a strange way, logical to her. She has come to believe, with a
conviction that feels less like a thought and more like a bodily fact, that the world contains
sudden violence and that she failed to read its approach the first time. The shame of having frozen
— of having watched without acting — sits alongside a fear that is harder to name: that her
stillness that evening was not an anomaly but a revelation of something true about her. Horror at
what she saw, guilt about what she did not do, and a low, persistent anger at herself have become
the emotional weather of her days.

At the firm, where she spends long hours bent over drafting software in an open-plan office near a
street-facing window, the threat she carries internally meets the external world in specific, daily
ways. The sound of raised voices from the street below, or the sudden movement of a colleague
crossing quickly behind her chair, is enough to send her pulse spiking. She has quietly repositioned
her monitor so that her back is no longer to the room's main corridor — a small adjustment her
supervisor has not questioned, though Yasmine is aware it marks something. She scans the office when
she arrives each morning, notes who is present, and does not fully settle until she has accounted
for the exits. This hypervigilance is not experienced as irrational; it feels, to Yasmine, like
competence — the alertness she should have had that evening on the pavement.

Sleep has become its own ordeal. Yasmine lies awake most nights in a state she describes as watchful
rather than anxious, as though rest itself represents a lapse in the monitoring she now considers
necessary. She has begun leaving a light on and keeping her phone within reach, checking it
periodically through the night without clear purpose. She is typically exhausted by mid-afternoon
but cannot surrender to sleep when darkness comes. The fatigue compounds the emotional volatility
she already carries — the fear sharpens, the guilt resurfaces more easily, and the sense of shame
about her frozen response on the pavement becomes, in those late hours, nearly unbearable.

What finally brought Yasmine to seek help was a conversation with her mother, who noticed that her
daughter had stopped taking the evening walks she had loved since childhood. Yasmine could not
explain it in a way that felt adequate, and the inadequacy of her own words frightened her. She
arrived at the intake session composed and articulate, describing her symptoms with the precision of
someone who has spent considerable time observing herself. What she said she most hoped for was
simple: to be able to walk home from work without scanning every figure on the street for the shape
of danger.
