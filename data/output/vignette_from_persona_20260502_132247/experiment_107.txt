Tariq is a 40-year-old married software engineer of Syrian descent, living with his wife and two
children in a mid-sized American city. He was referred for psychological assessment following a
natural disaster — a tornado that struck his neighbourhood approximately eight months ago,
collapsing part of the building where he had been sheltering with colleagues during a work event.
The structure partially gave way without warning: a roar that seemed to come from everywhere at
once, the crack and groan of beams, the sensation of the floor tilting, debris striking him as he
ran. He was trapped for nearly two hours before emergency responders reached him. Several people
were seriously injured. Tariq was not hospitalised, but the physical examination he underwent at the
scene — the latex-gloved hands, the bright ambulance interior, the clinical smell — left an
impression that fused itself to the terror of those two hours. He walked away. He has not felt safe
since.

The memories that followed do not arrive as recollections so much as reinstatements. When they come,
Tariq hears the sound — the specific grinding collapse of the ceiling — as though it is occurring in
the room around him. The sequence refuses to hold still: he cannot say with certainty what happened
first, what he did before he ran, whether he called out or was silent, and this disorganisation
frightens him almost as much as the content itself. At their worst, the memories return with full
sensory force, the smell of dust and insulation, the cold of the floor, the weight of something
across his legs — and in those moments he is entirely back there, not remembering but reliving.
These intrusions, in turn, have deepened a set of beliefs that have quietly taken over his inner
life. He has come to feel that someone else — someone more competent, more alert — would never have
been caught in that building, would have read the signs and left earlier. The memory, arriving
without warning and without order, seems to confirm to him that he is permanently damaged, that
nothing good lies ahead, that the person who once moved through the world with ease and even
happiness is simply gone.

Those beliefs, once established, have made the world feel actively dangerous. Tariq is certain, in a
way he cannot argue himself out of, that threat is always nearby and that he will be slow to see it.
At work — an open-plan office where colleagues move between desks and approach from behind — he
finds himself scanning constantly, tracking the periphery of his vision. Unexpected movement nearby
sends a jolt of alarm through him before he has time to think. If a colleague places a hand on his
shoulder, he flinches hard enough that people have noticed and stopped touching him. Before he can
settle anywhere — a meeting room, a restaurant with his wife, even a new section of the office — he
locates the exits first. This hypervigilance, exhausting as it is, also keeps the memory close: the
body on alert seems to prime the mind for intrusion, and the intrusions, when they come, reinforce
his certainty that danger is real and that he cannot trust his own reactions.

To manage all of this, Tariq has developed habits that feel like coping but function as traps. He
forces himself, consciously and effortfully, to think only positive thoughts whenever distress rises
— a strategy that requires constant mental labour and leaves him feeling fraudulent when the
memories break through anyway. He has stopped going anywhere near hospitals or ambulances; a route
change he made months ago to avoid the medical centre near his office has quietly become permanent,
adding twenty minutes to his commute and raising questions from his wife. The avoidance and the
forced positivity together seem to keep the threat system primed — what he will not approach, he
cannot learn is safe — and his appraisals of himself as broken and uniquely unfortunate have only
hardened. He also finds himself, in quieter moments, running through thoughts about the building's
owners and whether they were negligent — a rumination that provides a brief sense of agency but
leaves him more agitated than before.

What finally brought Tariq to seek help was a moment at his daughter's school recital, when a door
slammed backstage and he dropped to a crouch in the aisle. His wife looked at him with an expression
he describes as frightened. He sat through the rest of the performance in silence. What he fears
most, he said at intake, is that he is right — that he really has become someone who cannot be
trusted to hold things together. What he hopes, more quietly, is that he is wrong.
