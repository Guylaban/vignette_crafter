Aroha is a twenty-year-old Māori woman who works as an interior designer and recently married the
man who would go on to abuse her. The relationship deteriorated quickly after the wedding. Over
several months, her husband's rages escalated from verbal humiliation to physical violence — he
would grab her by the throat in the kitchen, slam her against walls in darkened hallways, and scream
at a volume that left her ears ringing long after the room fell silent. The last incident occurred
when she was alone in their apartment at night; he cornered her in an unlit room, and the
combination of the darkness, his proximity, and the explosive sound of his voice fused into a single
overwhelming experience. It is this constellation of details — loud sounds, enclosed spaces without
light, the presence of men in close quarters — that now reaches forward into her daily life with a
force she struggles to contain.

Aroha sought help approximately three months after leaving the relationship, presenting with a PCL-5
score of 37. She works in a field that requires her to visit construction sites and meet with
contractors, the majority of whom are men. On a recent site visit, a nail gun fired nearby and she
felt her body lock — heart hammering, jaw clenching, every muscle braced as though impact were
seconds away. That state of physical readiness does not fully subside between episodes; she
describes her body as permanently tensed, and she scans constantly, checking over her shoulder in
corridors and car parks, unable to shake the conviction that someone is behind her. When a trigger
fires — a raised male voice in an adjacent office, a moment of being alone with a male colleague to
review plans — the threat response ignites immediately, and she finds herself engineering reasons to
leave the room, rescheduling meetings, and choosing routes through the building that keep her near
other women. She has begun avoiding any circumstance that carries even a faint resemblance to the
abuse: she will not enter a room with the lights off, refuses to remain alone in a building after
hours, and has declined several evening client consultations that would have advanced her career.

These avoidance patterns have quietly narrowed her world, and the narrowing itself has become
evidence for a belief she cannot shake — that her life has been destroyed, that she no longer
belongs anywhere, that people cannot be trusted. When she avoids a colleague or declines a meeting,
she is left alone with her thoughts, and the isolation confirms rather than relieves her sense of
permanent displacement. The avoidance also means she never stays long enough in a triggering
situation to learn that danger does not follow, so the situations themselves retain their charge;
the mere anticipation of encountering a loud noise or being alone with a man is enough to reactivate
the body-level alarm she is trying to escape.

Her memories of the abuse are not orderly. When she tries to describe what happened — to herself, to
the one friend she has partially confided in — she loses the thread, the account fragmenting before
she can reach its end. More disturbing to her are the moments when the full emotional weight of the
abuse arrives without any accompanying image: a wave of terror and shame that seems sourceless,
which she finds more frightening than the flashbacks themselves because she cannot locate what
triggered it. When she does recall the events clearly, the dominant feeling is not fear but guilt —
she returns compulsively to the question of what she did to provoke him, replaying her own words and
choices in the weeks before each incident. This self-blame loops back into her negative appraisals:
if she caused it, then she is the kind of person who invites harm, which means she belongs nowhere
safe. These appraisals in turn keep the threat system elevated; believing she is permanently damaged
and untrustworthy of protection, she remains vigilant as though danger is her natural condition. The
memories themselves can act as triggers — a recalled fragment of his voice or the sensation of
darkness is enough to send her body back onto alert, which then drives her further into avoidance
and deeper into the belief that ordinary life is no longer available to her.

What finally brought Aroha to seek help was a morning when she sat in her car outside a client's
office for forty minutes, unable to go in because a male contractor's van was parked outside. She
missed the appointment entirely. She told the intake clinician that she was most afraid she would
never trust anyone again — and most hoping, quietly, that she was wrong.
