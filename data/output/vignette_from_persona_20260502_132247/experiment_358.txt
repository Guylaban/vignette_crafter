Gerald is a 67-year-old European American school principal, married for over three decades, who
presented for an initial psychological assessment approximately four months after surviving a life-
threatening motor vehicle collision. He was driving home from a late administrative meeting when a
vehicle ran a red light and struck his car broadside at high speed. The impact was sudden and total
— the shriek of metal, the explosive crack of airbags, the sensation of the world spinning sideways.
He was extracted by emergency personnel and hospitalised overnight with chest injuries and a
concussion. By all external measures, he recovered. Internally, he has not.

Gerald reports that his memories of the collision do not behave like ordinary memories. He cannot
narrate the event in any coherent sequence — when asked to describe what happened, he falls silent
and shakes his head, saying the words simply will not come. What does come, unbidden and with
terrible regularity, is the film: a loop of sensation and image — the flash of headlights, the sound
of impact, the smell of deployed airbag — that replays as though the event is happening at this very
moment. He has woken his wife reaching out to brace against a dashboard that is not there.

These intrusive memories are reliably detonated by the world around him. The slam of a locker in the
school corridor, a raised voice during a staff meeting, the sight of two cars pulling too close
together in the car park — each of these collapses the distance between then and now. Gerald has
come to understand this as evidence that he cannot trust his own nervous system, and this conviction
— that he has been permanently and fundamentally altered — sits at the centre of how he now
interprets everything. He is certain someone more capable, more composed, would not have been in
that intersection at that moment. The world, he believes, is no longer a place that follows
reasonable rules; danger arrives without warning and without logic, and he is uniquely ill-equipped
to survive it. These appraisals have made the triggers more potent still: because he already
believes catastrophe is imminent, the sound of shouting in a hallway or the sight of a car braking
sharply does not merely startle him — it confirms what he already knows, and the memories flood back
with renewed force.

The threat he carries is constant and exhausting. He cannot fall asleep without lying rigid for an
hour, listening. He cannot sit in a staff meeting without monitoring every sound. When a parent's
phone call takes longer than expected to connect, he is immediately certain something terrible has
occurred. This unrelenting state of vigilance feeds directly back into his appraisals — the fact
that he cannot relax, even in his own office, feels to Gerald like proof that the damage is
permanent, that he is broken in a way that will not heal. And the hypervigilance itself makes the
triggers everywhere: loud noises that once meant nothing now arrive as alarms, and the memories
follow within seconds.

To manage all of this, Gerald has constructed a set of strategies that have quietly made things
worse. He has been avoiding going to work as much as his role will allow — arriving late, delegating
morning assemblies, leaving before the end-of-day rush in the car park. This avoidance has not gone
unnoticed; two senior staff members have raised concerns about his absence from key decisions, and
he is aware his authority is eroding. He forces himself to think only positively, suppressing any
thought that touches the accident, which leaves the memories with nowhere to go and ensures they
return with greater force. He has been eating excessively in the evenings — a habit that crept up
over the first weeks and has now become a nightly ritual of distraction. Each of these strategies,
in their own way, sustains the belief that the world is too dangerous to engage with directly, and
that he is too damaged to cope — beliefs that keep his threat system primed and his triggers hair-
trigger sensitive.

What finally brought Gerald to seek help was a morning three weeks ago when he could not make
himself walk into the school building. He stood in the car park for twenty minutes and drove home.
He told his wife that evening, for the first time, how frightened he was — not of the school, but of
never being himself again. That is what he most fears: not another accident, but that the man who
walked into that intersection is gone, and that no one, including him, has noticed yet.
