Rajan is a 44-year-old South Asian man who came to the United States as a refugee in his late
twenties, following years of displacement, violence, and loss in a conflict zone he has never fully
been able to describe in words. What he has managed to convey, across several halting sessions, is
the texture of a single afternoon that seems to have crystallized the entire experience: a
checkpoint ambush in which uniformed soldiers moved through a crowd with weapons drawn, people fell,
and Rajan — frozen, he insists, inexcusably frozen — watched a younger man he had traveled with
collapse beside him without intervening. He survived. Most others in his immediate group did not. He
crossed a border within days, and has lived in the United States for over fifteen years since,
working as a chef in a mid-sized restaurant kitchen in a city he chose partly because it felt
anonymous enough to disappear into.

The memories of that afternoon do not arrive as coherent narrative. Rajan describes them as sensory
fragments he cannot assemble into language — the sound of boots on gravel, the particular quality of
light, the weight of his own stillness. Sometimes he can recall the sequence of events with clinical
detachment, as though reading a report about someone else, the affect drained entirely from the
recollection. Other times, without warning, the memory arrives as pure grief — an overwhelming,
suffocating sorrow that he cannot locate or explain to colleagues who notice him go quiet mid-
service. This fractured, wordless quality of the memory sustains his belief that he is permanently
and irreparably changed. He has come to understand his inability to narrate the event as evidence of
something broken inside him, and the guilt — for surviving, for not acting, for being here while
others are not — has calcified into a near-constant internal verdict: that he does not deserve the
ordinary life he has built, and that he is, at his core, a coward.

It is these appraisals that make certain moments in his daily life feel unbearable. The sound of an
ambulance siren passing the restaurant's delivery entrance, a conversation among kitchen staff about
a car accident, the sight of a line cook's deep knife wound — each of these pulls the checkpoint
back into the present with a vividness that his body registers before his mind does. His heart
accelerates, his attention fractures, and he finds himself scanning the kitchen for the nearest exit
before he has consciously registered why. His guilt and self-condemnation seem to lower the
threshold for these intrusions, as though his belief in his own culpability keeps the past
perpetually close to the surface, ready to be triggered by the smallest sensory echo.

Over the years, Rajan has organized his life around avoiding the conditions that provoke these
responses. He declines deliveries that require him to cross open loading docks or parking areas
where he feels exposed and without cover. He has never once eaten at a restaurant near the police
precinct two blocks from his own workplace, and on the one occasion a uniformed officer entered his
kitchen during a health inspection, he became so deferential — agreeing with every comment, offering
no objection — that his sous-chef later asked if something was wrong. This fawning compliance, which
he recognizes in himself with shame, is not experienced as a choice but as an automatic self-erasure
in the presence of authority. These avoidances have narrowed his world considerably. He has not
attended a single outdoor food festival, despite invitations that could have advanced his career. A
sous-chef position at a larger restaurant — one with an open-plan kitchen visible to the dining room
— was quietly declined without explanation.

The avoidance, however, has not brought relief. By keeping him away from the very contexts that
might allow him to disconfirm his fears, it has preserved the sense of threat intact. He remains
superalert in the kitchen, tracking movement in his peripheral vision, noting where the back door is
before he begins any shift. This chronic vigilance consumes the concentration he needs to work, and
his difficulty focusing has begun to affect his performance in ways that have not gone unnoticed.
The hyperarousal itself seems to pull his attention back toward threat, making the triggers feel
closer and more numerous, and the avoidance more necessary — a loop he cannot name but can feel.

What finally brought Rajan to seek help was a moment last month when a young dishwasher arrived at
work with a deep laceration on his forearm, and Rajan, upon seeing it, had to leave the kitchen
entirely and sit alone in the alley for twenty minutes, unable to return. He told his manager he
felt ill. What he could not say was that he had spent those twenty minutes certain he was back at
that checkpoint, and that the grief and guilt that followed felt indistinguishable from the
original. He came to therapy not with hope, exactly, but with exhaustion — and with the quiet,
frightened question of whether the person he was before that afternoon still exists somewhere, or
whether that man did not survive either.
