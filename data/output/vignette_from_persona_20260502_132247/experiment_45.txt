Marcos is a 37-year-old Latino man, currently divorced, who works as a social ministry worker at a
community outreach center in a mid-sized American city. He was referred for psychological assessment
following a disclosure to his supervisor that he had been struggling to function at work, and he
presented to the intake session with a PCL-5 score of 48, indicating significant post-traumatic
stress. The trauma he reported was a period of sustained physical abuse perpetrated by his former
female partner across approximately fourteen months, ending roughly two years ago when he finally
left the relationship.

The abuse typically occurred late at night. His partner would drink, and Marcos came to associate
the sour, heavy smell of alcohol on her breath with what was about to happen — the escalation from
argument to violence. She would often corner him in the hallway of their apartment, and on several
occasions she pinned him against the wall or held him down on the floor, striking him while he tried
to go still, hoping that resistance would make things worse. The sound of a door slamming would
often signal her return home, that particular crack of wood and frame arriving before he could see
her, before he could read how bad the night would be. These details — the smell, the sound, the
sensation of being physically restrained — became engraved in the event with a precision that would
outlast the relationship itself.

In the period following his departure, Marcos noticed that his memories of the abuse were not fading
the way he had hoped. They were vivid and highly detailed, arriving with an almost sensory
completeness — he could hear her voice, hear the door, hear the sounds of the apartment as though no
time had passed. When he recalled what had happened, he was often overtaken by waves of grief so
intense they left him sitting motionless at his desk, unable to return to his work. These memory
intrusions, in turn, seemed to sharpen the triggers around him: the more he re-experienced those
sounds and sensations internally, the more the world outside seemed to be threaded with them. A door
slamming in the hallway of the outreach center where he works would pull him back immediately into
the memory, the auditory echo in his mind fusing with the present sound until he could not fully
separate the two.

The threat he perceived in his daily environment was persistent and embodied. Unexpected movement
nearby — a colleague stepping quickly around a corner, a client reaching toward him — would cause
him to flinch or tense in a way that felt, to him, entirely involuntary and entirely rational. He
had come to believe that danger could arrive without warning, and this belief organized much of his
waking life. At night, he found it nearly impossible to feel safe; he would lie awake listening, and
had developed a habit of checking the locks on his doors and windows repeatedly before attempting
sleep. The sense of threat, once activated, reliably brought the memories closer — it was as though
his nervous system, convinced danger was present, reached into the past to confirm it, and the
detailed, vivid quality of those memories made the confirmation feel irrefutable. When the threat
response was running, the triggers became more potent: the smell of a colleague's beer at a ministry
fundraiser, or a hand placed briefly on his shoulder, could ignite a full re-experiencing that left
him shaking.

Marcos had developed several strategies to manage this, each of which had, over months, narrowed his
life considerably. He avoided the neighborhood where he and his former partner had lived, going
significantly out of his way to do so. When he felt threatened or overwhelmed, he would freeze
entirely — a response he recognized from the abuse itself and that now emerged automatically in
moments of perceived danger. He worked hard to suppress any visible emotional reaction, particularly
at the outreach center, where he felt that showing distress would undermine his credibility with the
clients he served. Yet these strategies maintained the very conditions they were meant to resolve:
the freezing and suppression prevented him from processing what had happened, and the avoidance of
the neighborhood kept the memories uncontextualized and raw. His emotional control at work meant he
rarely spoke about his experiences, and the memories remained sealed, vivid, and easily detonated.

What finally brought Marcos to seek help was an incident at the outreach center when a client,
agitated and unpredictable, grabbed his wrist. He froze completely, was unable to respond
professionally, and had to be relieved by a colleague. He left that day certain that he was losing
his ability to do the work he had built his life around. What he fears most is that he is
permanently changed. What he hopes, with some difficulty, is that he is not.
