Arjun is a 41-year-old South Asian man who works as a park ranger in a mid-Atlantic national forest,
a role he sought out, he once explained to his intake clinician, precisely because it kept him away
from crowds and uniforms. He has been in a relationship for three years, though his partner has
recently begun expressing concern about the emotional distance that has grown between them. Arjun
presented for treatment after a routine park service briefing — attended by several officers from a
neighbouring jurisdiction, all in uniform — left him unable to drive home and sitting in his truck
for two hours, unable to account for the time.

Approximately four years ago, Arjun was taken captive during a hiking trip abroad. He was held for
eleven days by a small armed group whose members wore military-style fatigues and conducted
themselves with the rigid, performative authority of soldiers. During that time he was restrained,
deprived of food as a method of control, and subjected to sustained humiliation — made to speak on
command, to stand and sit on order, to demonstrate submission in front of multiple captors
simultaneously. He was watched constantly. The experience of being seen, scrutinised, and assessed
for compliance became as threatening as the physical deprivation itself. When he was eventually
released, he was physically weakened and, as he would later describe it, "completely emptied out."

In the months that followed, Arjun found that certain moments fractured without warning into what he
calls "photographs" — discrete, frozen images from the captivity, each carrying a precise sensory
weight. One image is of a captor's boots at eye level. Another is of his own hands, bound. When
these fragments surface, he sometimes feels a dull, spreading ache across his wrists and lower back
— the body replaying what the mind cannot fully integrate. On other occasions the same memories
arrive entirely stripped of feeling, as though he is reading a report about someone else. These
memory intrusions are reliably activated by specific cues: conversations about military topics, the
sight of a person in uniform, or the simple, ordinary experience of being looked at. A hiker pausing
to ask him for directions can be enough — the sense of being seen triggers the image-sequence, and
the images in turn confirm what he has come to believe about himself: that he is permanently marked
by what happened, that he was uniquely foolish to have been placed in that situation, that another
man — a stronger, more vigilant man — would never have been captured at all.

This appraisal of permanent humiliation and personal deficiency has not remained static. It has
actively shaped how Arjun moves through his days, and in doing so it has kept the wound open. He
goes along with whatever colleagues or his partner suggest, unable to assert a preference or decline
a request, because compliance feels like the safest posture available — a logic that was installed
during eleven days in which compliance was survival. But people-pleasing returns him, in quieter
moments, to the memory of performing submission for armed men, and the self-disgust that follows re-
activates the very sense of humiliation it was meant to forestall. He also controls his eating with
considerable rigidity — scheduling meals, measuring portions, refusing spontaneity around food —
because this is the one domain in which deprivation cannot be imposed on him again. Yet the
hypervigilance required to maintain that control keeps his nervous system in a state of low-grade
alert, and that alert state makes the intrusive images more accessible, not less. He has entertained
detailed revenge fantasies about the men who held him, and while these briefly restore a sense of
agency, they also require him to mentally re-enter the captivity, sharpening the memory's edges and
intensifying the physical pain that accompanies recall.

The threat-monitoring that now governs his daily life is most visible at work. Unexpected movement —
a deer breaking from undergrowth, a colleague stepping around a corner — produces a full-body
startle that he cannot modulate. He cannot accept reassurance that he is safe; when his partner
tells him he is home, he knows it intellectually and disbelieves it entirely. These moments of
hyperarousal feed back into the memory, making each recollection feel more recent and more
dangerous, and each cycle of threat and recall deepens his conviction that he is irreparably
changed. He feels isolated in a way that has become structural — not merely lonely, but separated
from other people by an experience he cannot translate.

What finally brought him in was not the two hours in the truck, though that frightened him. It was
his partner saying, quietly, that they felt they were living with someone who was still somewhere
else. Arjun wants, more than anything, to stop being watched by his own memory.
