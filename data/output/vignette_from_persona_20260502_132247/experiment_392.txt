Rajan is a 64-year-old South Asian man who has been married for nearly four decades and works the
early morning shift at a large distribution warehouse on the outskirts of the city. He was referred
for psychological assessment following a disclosure to his primary care physician, though he had
been carrying what happened to him largely in silence for almost two years.

The event that precipitated his current difficulties occurred during a visit to relatives in a rural
area. Rajan was detained overnight by a group of men in what he describes as a partially underground
storage structure — dark, earthen-walled, and airless. He was held for approximately eighteen hours,
restrained and unable to leave, uncertain whether he would be released or harmed. At some point
during the night, he became aware of movement near his feet and realised, in the near-total
darkness, that there were snakes in the space with him. He remained motionless for hours, convinced
that any movement would provoke a bite. He was eventually released unharmed, but the experience of
absolute helplessness — pinned between human threat and animal danger, in the dark, in the early
hours before dawn — left a mark that has not faded.

In the months that followed, Rajan found that certain moments in his daily life had become
unbearable. The pre-dawn hours of his warehouse shift — the same time of day he had been held —
began to feel charged with a formless dread he could not explain to colleagues. Photographs of
snakes in a safety pamphlet posted near the loading dock once sent him to the bathroom shaking.
Night-time itself, and the simple act of waking in the dark in his own bedroom, had become triggers
that pulled him back into the storage room with a vividness he found almost physically painful.
These moments of intrusion began to organise his behaviour: he started avoiding sleep, lying awake
rather than surrendering to darkness, and when he did sleep, he kept a lamp burning on his side of
the bed. He had also begun to check his body obsessively — pressing fingers along his arms and legs
each morning, searching for signs of injury or illness he could not rationally account for — and he
sought reassurance from his wife and adult children with an urgency that had begun to strain those
relationships. His wife, who initially responded with patience, had grown quietly exhausted by his
nightly need for confirmation that he was safe.

What Rajan had not anticipated was that the strategies he adopted to manage his distress were
themselves keeping him in contact with the very cues he feared. Lying awake in the dark prolonged
his exposure to the nighttime environment that triggered the memories; the lamp he kept on created a
ritual that marked every night as dangerous, and his body-checking in the early warehouse hours drew
his attention precisely to the time-of-day cues he was trying to outlast. The avoidance had become a
kind of maintenance.

He had come to believe, with a quiet but settled certainty, that he simply could not cope if the
memories were allowed to surface fully — that thinking about what happened would undo him entirely.
This conviction sat alongside a deeper fear: that bad things happened to him because he was
powerless to prevent them, that the world had shown him something true about his own vulnerability.
These appraisals fed directly into his vigilance. Certain that threat could arrive without warning,
he had developed a habit of looking behind him on the warehouse floor, scanning the shadows between
shelving units, remaining hyperalert throughout his shift in ways that left him exhausted by mid-
morning. At night, the same sense of helplessness made it harder to feel safe — the darkness outside
his bedroom window seemed less like ordinary night and more like the absence of protection.

There were moments, he admitted, when he was not sure his life was worth continuing. He said this
quietly, without drama, as though reporting a weather condition.

What finally brought Rajan to seek help was a conversation with his youngest son, who had noticed
that his father had not slept properly in months and who asked, gently, whether something was wrong.
Rajan came to the first session hoping, more than anything, to be able to sleep in the dark again —
and to stop feeling, at 64, like a man who had run out of safety.
