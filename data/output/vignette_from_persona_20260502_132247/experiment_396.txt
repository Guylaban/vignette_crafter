Rajan is a 41-year-old South Asian man, married and working as a graphic designer at a mid-sized
creative agency in the city. He was referred for psychological assessment following a disclosure to
his GP of persistent sleep difficulties and what he described, haltingly, as "not being able to
trust anything anymore." His PCL-5 score of 40 places him in the clinical range for PTSD, and the
picture that emerged across the intake sessions points to a traumatic encounter with law enforcement
approximately fourteen months ago that has quietly restructured the architecture of his daily life.

The incident occurred when Rajan was stopped and detained by police officers outside his office
building late one evening. What began as a stop-and-search escalated into a prolonged detention
during which he was held against a wall, questioned aggressively, and ultimately handcuffed in view
of passing colleagues. A colleague — someone Rajan had considered a friend and confidant — had
called the police after misidentifying him in a security alert, a fact Rajan learned only afterward.
The smell of burnt rubber from a nearby vehicle exhaust hung in the cold air throughout. He was
released without charge, but not before the officers' uniforms, their authority, and that acrid
smell had been pressed into him in a way that would not easily lift.

In the weeks following, Rajan found that certain things would pull him back without warning. The
smell of something burning — a toaster in the office kitchen, a colleague's scorched coffee — would
arrive first in the body before the mind caught up, a sudden constriction in the chest, a sense of
imminent threat. Seeing a police officer on the street, or anyone in uniform — a security guard at
the shopping centre, a postal worker — would produce the same effect, the present moment dissolving
and the night of the detention reasserting itself with visceral immediacy. These triggers did not
merely remind him of what happened; they seemed to confirm his now-settled belief that the world is
a dangerous place and that people, even those he has known for years, cannot be trusted. That
conviction, in turn, made the triggers feel more potent still — ordinary encounters became threat-
laden, and the threshold for alarm fell steadily lower.

When the memories do come, they are not orderly. Rajan describes knowing, in some general sense,
what happened, but when he tries to put it into words — to his wife, to his GP, in the intake
session — he loses the thread. He will begin a sentence and find it dissolving, the sequence
slipping from him. What arrives most reliably is not narrative but feeling: a wave of intense guilt,
as though he had done something wrong, as though the detention were a verdict on his character
rather than an error. That guilt is bound up with the sense of betrayal — that someone he trusted
handed him to a system that treated him as a suspect — and this appraisal of the world as hostile
and people as unreliable floods back every time the memory surfaces, deepening it, making it harder
to metabolise.

In response, Rajan has built a set of habits that feel like protection but have become their own
kind of confinement. Before any interaction at work — with a manager, with a client, with a security
guard in the lobby — he rehearses what he will say, running through scenarios in his mind to ensure
nothing can be misread or used against him. This rehearsal, however, keeps the threat logic active,
confirming to himself that interactions are dangerous and that the world requires this level of
vigilance, which in turn makes uniform-wearers and authority figures more alarming, not less. He has
stopped making plans for the future — holidays, career moves, even weekend arrangements — because
planning feels futile when danger can arrive without warning. He scans rooms, monitors strangers,
and has become, by his own account, exhausting to be around. His wife has noticed the withdrawal. A
collaborative project at the agency, one that would have required client-facing presentations, was
quietly declined. The rehearsing and the avoidance have not softened the memories; if anything, the
vigilance keeps him primed, so that when the smell of something burnt drifts through the office, the
memory returns as raw as it ever was.

What brought Rajan to seek help, finally, was his wife telling him she felt she was living with a
stranger. What he most fears is that she is right. What he most hopes is that he can find a way to
explain what happened — to himself as much as to anyone else.
