Tariq Mansour is a forty-one-year-old Syrian-American dancer and choreographer who has been married
for eleven years and currently teaches contemporary movement at a mid-sized arts conservatory in
Detroit. He was referred for psychological assessment following a conversation with a colleague who
noticed that he had been withdrawing from rehearsals, arriving late, and on several occasions simply
standing motionless at the studio door as though unable to cross the threshold. His PCL-5 score of
58 places him firmly within the clinical range for PTSD, and the picture that emerged across the
intake and first two sessions is one of a man quietly disintegrating beneath a composed exterior.

Tariq left Aleppo in 2013, but the event that brought him to the present moment of seeking help
occurred in the spring of that year, when he was caught in the aftermath of a shelling in his
neighbourhood. He had been walking back from a rehearsal space — movement had always been his way of
making sense of the world — when the strike hit a building two streets away. What followed was not a
single explosion but a prolonged period of exposure: the low, reverberant rumble of secondary
collapses, the acrid smell of disturbed earth and broken concrete rising from the rubble, and the
repeated calling of names — neighbours and rescue workers crying out for survivors buried beneath
the debris. He helped dig for several hours. He did not find everyone alive.

In the years since resettling in the United States, Tariq built what appeared from the outside to be
a full and purposeful life. But certain things remained unbearable in ways he could not explain to
his wife or colleagues. The low percussion of a sound system warming up in the studio — a deep,
resonant thud — could stop him mid-sentence. The smell of freshly turned soil in the community
garden he passes on his walk to the conservatory has, on more than one occasion, caused him to grip
the fence and stand there, unable to continue walking. And when a student or colleague shares a name
common in the Arab world — Khalid, Rami, Yusuf — he sometimes has to leave the room under the
pretence of checking something in his office. These triggers, rooted so precisely in what he heard,
smelled, and called out during those hours of digging, reach him with a force that feels entirely
disproportionate to the ordinary moments in which they arrive.

What concerns Tariq most is not the triggers themselves but what they seem to confirm about him.
When a rumble from the sound system floods him with the sensory weight of that afternoon, he does
not experience it merely as an unpleasant memory — he experiences it as evidence. Each intrusion
renews his conviction that he is permanently altered, that something essential was extinguished in
Aleppo and has not returned. He has come to describe himself, haltingly, as feeling dead inside —
not depressed in a way he can name, but hollowed, as though the emotional register that once made
him a dancer capable of moving audiences has been permanently disconnected. The triggers do not just
startle him; they remind him of this hollowness, and in doing so deepen it. He has lost faith — in
people, in the idea that the world holds any reliable goodness, and in the spiritual framework that
once gave his life coherence. What perhaps frightens him most is the certainty that others can see
all of this simply by looking at him, that his damage is legible on his face and body in a way no
amount of professional composure can conceal. In the studio, where his body has always been his
instrument, this belief is particularly corrosive.

It was his wife, Leila, who finally insisted he speak to someone. She had noticed that he no longer
danced at home — not even the small, unconscious movements that had always been part of how he
inhabited a room. He came to the first session hoping, above all, to feel less transparent — and
afraid that he had already waited too long.
