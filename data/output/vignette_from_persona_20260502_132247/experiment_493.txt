Kiet Phommasack is a 26-year-old Lao-American man who works as a bank teller at a mid-sized branch
in a suburban strip mall. He was referred for psychological assessment following a brief
conversation with his primary care physician, who noted that Kiet had lost considerable weight and
described sleeping "maybe two or three hours a night." Kiet is recently divorced and lives alone in
a one-bedroom apartment. He presented to the intake session with a flat affect and a habit of
positioning his chair so that his back faced the wall — something he did without apparent awareness
until it was gently noted.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Kiet was working his regular shift when two armed men entered the
bank and initiated a robbery that escalated into a prolonged hostage situation. During the ordeal,
which lasted nearly four hours, Kiet was ordered at gunpoint to open his drawer and assist in moving
customers to the back of the branch. At one point, one of the perpetrators struck a colleague across
the face when she moved too slowly. Kiet was standing close enough to hear the impact. What stayed
with him — what still replays with a quality he describes as "happening right now, not like a
memory" — was the moment he complied with the gunman's instructions to push a frightened elderly
customer toward the back, physically steering her by the shoulders while she wept. He did what he
was told. He has not forgiven himself for it.

In the weeks following the incident, Kiet began to notice that the ordinary geography of his job had
become saturated with threat. The sound of the branch door opening, the sight of someone in a hood
or with their hands in their pockets, the particular angle of light through the front windows in the
late afternoon — each of these had become a signal. He began checking behind him constantly while
walking from the parking lot to the entrance, certain that the sensation of being watched was not
paranoia but information. He woke most nights between two and four in the morning and moved through
his apartment checking the locks, the windows, the closet. He was irritable in ways that frightened
him — snapping at customers over minor delays, raising his voice once at a colleague in a manner
that left them both shaken.

What made this worse, in a way that felt almost circular, was what Kiet had come to believe about
himself. He was certain he was no longer the person he had been before the robbery — someone who had
prided himself on steadiness, on being the calm one in a crisis. He had acted in a way that violated
something central to his sense of himself, and that conviction sat in him like a stone. He could not
rely on his own judgment or his own instincts anymore. And it was precisely this belief — that he
was compromised, untrustworthy, diminished — that kept his nervous system in a state of permanent
vigilance, as though his body had concluded that if he couldn't trust himself to act well under
pressure, he had better watch everything else twice as carefully. The hypervigilance, in turn,
confirmed his worst appraisals: every night check, every flinch at the door, every moment of
irrational anger at the teller window felt like further evidence that he was broken.

Kiet responded to this by working longer hours. He volunteered for extra shifts, stayed late
reconciling accounts, and filled his evenings with tasks that kept his hands and eyes occupied. At
first this seemed to help, but over months it deepened his isolation and quietly reinforced his
belief that he was no longer capable of a normal life — that he needed to be constantly busy simply
to function, that stillness was something he had lost the right to. His marriage, already strained
in the aftermath of the attack, ended when his wife told him she could no longer reach him. He had
made himself unreachable; he had understood this even as he did it.

Kiet came to seek help after a customer at the bank — a young man in a dark jacket who reached
suddenly into his bag — caused Kiet to step backward and knock over a display stand. His manager
spoke to him privately afterward. What Kiet told the intake clinician he was most afraid of was that
there was nothing left to recover — that the person who had walked into that bank fourteen months
ago had simply not walked back out.
