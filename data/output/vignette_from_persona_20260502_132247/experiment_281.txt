Hilde is a 74-year-old woman of European background who works part-time as a physical therapist at a
rehabilitation clinic in the mid-Atlantic United States, where she has lived for several decades.
She came to her current therapist describing a cluster of distressing symptoms that had intensified
over the past year, though their roots reached back to a period of displacement and violence she
experienced as a young woman in her country of origin. She had never spoken about those events in
any clinical setting before.

When Hilde was in her early twenties, she and her family were forced to flee their home during a
period of civil unrest. The night of their departure was chaotic and terrifying: soldiers entered
their neighborhood, and the air was thick with the smell of burning buildings several streets away.
There was shouting, the sound of boots on gravel, and the sharp, unmistakable odor of alcohol on the
breath of one of the men who stopped and interrogated her at a checkpoint. She was separated briefly
from her husband, and for several minutes she did not know whether she would be allowed to pass. She
was. They escaped. But something in her nervous system did not.

For decades, Hilde managed. She built a career, raised children, and became a respected clinician.
But as she aged and her life quieted, the past grew louder. Now, when a patient at the clinic
arrives smelling of alcohol — not uncommon in a rehabilitation setting — Hilde finds herself flooded
without warning. The smell does not remind her of the checkpoint; it returns her there. Her memories
are not recollections so much as reinhabitations: vivid images of the soldier's face, and layered
beneath them, the sounds of shouting and boots she hears again as if through the walls of that
night. The memory feels entirely present, unanchored from the fifty years that have passed, and in
those moments she is certain she is in danger. This sense of immediate threat is not abstract — her
body braces, her breath shortens, and she scans the room for exits. The burnt smell from a nearby
cafeteria exhaust vent, which she passes each morning entering the clinic, has become its own
reliable portal back. Both smells now function as reliable triggers that pull the memory forward
and, with it, the full weight of felt threat.

What frightens Hilde most is that the danger feels real and current. She has come to believe that
strangers approaching her — whether a new patient walking toward her in the corridor or a neighbor
pausing on the footpath — represent a genuine risk she cannot dismiss. She listens at home for
sounds that might mean someone is entering, and her husband has noticed that she startles at the
sound of the front door. This vigilance keeps her nervous system primed, and a primed nervous system
finds the memories closer to the surface; the slightest auditory or olfactory cue is enough to
trigger another intrusion. The loop has tightened over months: the memories sustain the threat
perception, and the threat perception makes the memories more accessible, more vivid, more present.

To manage, Hilde has developed several strategies that she knows, on some level, are not helping.
She drinks a small amount of alcohol each evening, telling herself it steadies her — though she has
noticed, without fully confronting it, that it sometimes sharpens rather than dulls the intrusive
images. Her constant environmental scanning at work and at home keeps her threat system activated,
which in turn makes memories more likely to surface unbidden. She also dwells, at length, on who she
was before — the woman who did not startle, who did not check the locks twice, who did not feel her
heart lurch when someone entered a room. This dwelling does not soothe her; it keeps her oriented
toward loss and, by returning her attention to that earlier self, brushes against the memories of
what changed her.

What finally brought Hilde to seek help was a session with a patient in which she smelled alcohol on
his breath, heard a door slam in the hallway, and found herself unable to continue the appointment.
She excused herself, went to the bathroom, and sat on the floor for ten minutes. She told her
therapist she was most afraid that she would have to stop working. What she hoped for, quietly, was
to feel that the past was actually over.
