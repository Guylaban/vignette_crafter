stone — became the image that would never fully form into language and never fully leave. The
sequence of what happened that afternoon remains fractured: she knows certain things occurred, but
the order collapses whenever she tries to reconstruct it, and the attempt to put the experience into
words produces only a kind of blankness, a sense that language itself is inadequate to what she
endured. When the memories do surface — unbidden, incomplete — what rises with them is not grief but
rage, a white and wordless fury that frightens her with its intensity.

The triggers that pull her back are specific and merciless. Unwanted thoughts intrude without
warning, often while she is working at her sewing table — the quiet rhythm of stitching offering no
real protection. Certain conversation topics, especially anything touching on war, soldiers, or

finger, a stain on fabric brought in for repair — is enough to collapse the distance between then
and now. Each time a trigger fires, it does not merely recall the past; it confirms her darkest
present-tense conviction: that nothing good can happen to her anymore, that the person she once was
— someone capable of happiness — is simply gone. The triggers, in this way, do not only retrieve the
trauma; they actively reinforce the belief that she is beyond recovery, and that belief, in turn,
keeps her hypervigilant to anything that might provoke the memories again, narrowing her world
further with each cycle.

The memories themselves, fragmented and nonverbal as they are, have quietly shaped the architecture
of her daily life. Because she cannot organize what happened into a coherent narrative, the past
remains unresolved and present, a wound without edges. This unprocessed quality feeds directly into
her conviction that she is permanently changed — emotionally dead, she said, in a flat voice that
seemed to confirm the claim. And from that conviction, a particular logic has taken hold: if she
allows herself to feel something good, she will only lose it again. So she has learned, over
decades, to preempt that loss. She avoids situations that might bring pleasure or hope. When warmth
arises — a kind word from a customer, a moment of sunlight through the shop window — she pushes it
away before it can settle, because positive emotion has come to feel like a trap. She has stopped
seeing friends. The social world that once sustained her has contracted to almost nothing, and the
isolation this produces circles back to deepen her certainty that she is miserable and will remain
so — that the woman who used to laugh, who used to feel, is irretrievable.

Marguerite's grief over who she used to be is quiet but total. She does not cry easily. She
describes herself as feeling dead inside with the matter-of-fact tone of someone reporting the
weather. What finally brought her to seek help was not a crisis but an accumulation: her daughter's

in any meaningful way, the understanding that she had been disappearing for a very long time. What
she fears most is that it is too late. What she hopes, in the smallest and most guarded part of
herself, is that someone might tell her otherwise.
