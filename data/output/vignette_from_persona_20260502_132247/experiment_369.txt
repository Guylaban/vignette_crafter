Marcus is a 48-year-old married man who owns a small hardware store in a mid-sized Midwestern town.
He presents with a PCL-5 score of 57, seeking help for the first time following what he describes as
"finally hitting a wall." The events that fractured his life occurred roughly fourteen months ago,
when his adult son was killed in a car accident — a crash Marcus did not witness but arrived at
shortly after, in time to see the wreckage, the emergency personnel, and his son's body still
partially visible before a sheet was drawn. That image — the stillness of it, the ordinary afternoon
light, the smell of gasoline and something else he cannot name — has never left him.

In the weeks following the funeral, Marcus noticed that the memory did not behave like other
memories. It did not recede. Instead it returned with the quality of something happening now, not
then — vivid and sequential, playing through like footage he cannot stop or fast-forward. The
details are almost unbearably precise: the angle of the car, the particular sound of a radio still
playing somewhere in the wreckage. He has described lying awake at night with the sensation that he
is standing at that roadside again, not remembering it but inhabiting it. This quality of the memory
— its presentness, its relentless clarity — became the engine of much of what followed. Because the
memory felt live rather than past, Marcus began organizing his days around not activating it.
Thinking about the event directly felt not like grieving but like re-entering the scene itself, and
so he stopped allowing himself to do it.

The avoidance spread methodically. He stopped attending church, where eulogies and hymns associated
with loss could surface without warning. He declined a neighbor's invitation to a memorial service
and, when a regular customer mentioned losing a parent, Marcus found a reason to step into the back
room. He has not visited his son's grave. At the hardware store, where he spends most of his waking
hours, he has rearranged his schedule to avoid a particular employee who knew his son well and
occasionally mentions him. Customers sometimes make small talk about family, about loss — ordinary
conversation — and Marcus has become skilled at redirecting or cutting exchanges short. He smokes
more than he ever has, stepping outside several times a day, and he has come to understand this not
as a habit but as a valve. He also began smoking in moments when the memory threatened to surface,
and when the heightened vigilance that now characterises his days became too loud to sit with.

That vigilance is constant. Marcus startles at sounds in the shop — a dropped tool, a door swinging
open — with a physical jolt that embarrasses him in front of customers. Unexpected movement in his
peripheral vision, a person stepping quickly into the aisle, produces a reactive alarm that he
cannot suppress. He carries a persistent weight of strong negative emotion: fear, guilt, and a shame
he cannot fully articulate but which seems connected to the belief that he should have known, should
have been there, should have done something. When this threat-saturated state is at its height, it
pulls the memory closer — as if the body's alarm system and the stored scene are tuned to the same
frequency — and it also lowers the threshold for triggers, making even oblique references to loss
feel like direct contact with the event.

The avoidance that was meant to contain all of this has instead preserved it. By not allowing
himself to think about the event, Marcus has ensured that the memory retains its raw, unprocessed
quality — still vivid, still present-tense, still playing on its loop. And because he avoids grief
reminders so systematically, he remains sensitised to them; the world seems populated with potential
ambushes, which sustains the hypervigilance that in turn keeps the memory primed. His wife has
noticed his withdrawal. She has stopped mentioning their son in his presence. That silence between
them is, he said quietly in the first session, one of the things that finally made him come.
