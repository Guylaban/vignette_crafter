Priya is a 32-year-old South Asian woman who works as a school counselor at a mid-sized public
middle school in the Chicago suburbs. She came to the clinic eight months after returning from a
humanitarian education trip to a conflict-affected region overseas, where she had been volunteering
with a nonprofit organization supporting displaced children's schooling. She is married, and her
husband was the one who finally persuaded her to seek help, after months of watching her withdraw
from the life they had built together.

The incident that changed everything occurred on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon. Priya had been
escorting a group of children from a temporary classroom to a distribution point when shelling began
in the surrounding area. The group scattered. In the chaos, Priya ran for cover and lost sight of
several of the children she had been responsible for. She pressed herself against a wall, pulling
her headscarf tighter around her face as if making herself smaller might somehow undo what was
happening. She did not know, for hours, whether all the children had reached safety. Though she
later learned that none had been seriously hurt, the memory of crouching against that wall — hearing
the sounds, feeling the vibration through the concrete, not knowing — had lodged itself into her
with a permanence that no subsequent reassurance could dislodge.

Back in Illinois, the shame arrived before the fear had even fully settled. Priya had come to
believe, with a certainty that felt almost physical, that she had failed — that a stronger, more
capable person would have gathered those children and shielded them, not fled into her own terror.
She thought of herself as weak, a word she turned over quietly and often, and she was increasingly
convinced that she was no longer the person who could do the work that had once defined her.
Counseling children, sitting with their pain, being the steady adult in the room — all of it now
felt like a performance she was no longer qualified to give.

These appraisals did not remain internal. They drove a set of behaviors that, over the following
months, became the architecture of her daily life. She stopped watching the news entirely, unable to
tolerate footage of conflict zones, the particular sound of distant explosions carried through a
phone speaker, the sight of dust rising over a building. She stopped using public transport — the
press of strangers in an enclosed space, the unpredictability of it, felt unbearable — and now drove
alone to work even when her car was in the shop and her husband offered to drive her. She began
wearing high-necked, loose-fitting clothes that covered her arms and throat, a habit she had
developed almost unconsciously, as though minimizing her visible surface area might protect her from
being seen, noticed, caught out in some unspecified way.

The tragedy of these strategies was that they quietly confirmed everything she feared about herself.
Each morning she arrived at school having already avoided three things before 8 a.m., and the
avoidance felt like evidence — proof that she was fragile, diminished, unfit. The children she
counseled came to her with their ordinary middle-school distress, and she sat across from them
feeling fraudulent, certain that her inability to watch the news or ride a bus rendered her
unqualified to help anyone navigate difficulty. The concealing clothing, the careful routes, the
silence around what had happened — none of it brought relief, only the sustained low hum of a person
managing rather than recovering, and each act of management seemed to deepen her conviction that
there was something fundamentally broken in her now.

Her husband noticed the gradual narrowing of her world before she fully acknowledged it herself. It
was a conversation with a student — a girl describing anxiety about a family trip abroad — that
finally cracked something open. Priya found herself unable to respond with her usual steadiness, and
afterward sat in her office for twenty minutes before she could return to the hallway. She called
her husband that afternoon. What she most fears, coming into treatment, is that the person she was
before — the one who flew toward difficulty rather than away from it — is simply gone. What she is
most hoping for, though she can barely say it aloud, is that she is wrong about that.
