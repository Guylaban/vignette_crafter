Mere Tuilagi is a 66-year-old Fijian-American woman, divorced, who has owned and operated a small
gift and import shop in a mid-sized Pacific Northwest city for nearly two decades. She presented for
intake with a PCL-5 score of 78, describing a prolonged period of deterioration following an
incident of police violence that occurred approximately fourteen months prior to her first
appointment.

The event took place on a weekday afternoon, in the late-golden light of early evening — the same
hour at which Mere typically closed her shop and walked to her car in the adjacent lot. Officers
responding to a reported disturbance in the area stopped her without warning, forced her to the
ground, and restrained her with sufficient force to leave bruising across her wrists and face. She
was alone. No one intervened. The physical pain was acute, but what she describes most haltingly —
when she can describe it at all — is the sensation of being watched from the surrounding windows
while she lay on the pavement, helpless and humiliated. She was released without charge.

In the weeks that followed, Mere found that the memory of the event refused to organise itself into
anything coherent. There are portions she cannot retrieve at all, no matter how deliberately she
reaches for them, and the fragments that do surface carry a quality of unreality — so extreme, she
has told her therapist, that she sometimes wonders whether it truly happened to her. More
distressingly, she cannot seem to put what occurred into words; when she attempts to recount it,
language fails her entirely, and she is left with only a physical residue — a lurching dizziness, a
sudden lightness in her head — that arrives without warning and without narrative context. This
fragmented, wordless quality of the memory has itself become a source of alarm, because the gaps and
the surreal texture of what she does recall now function as their own kind of cue: when a fragment
surfaces, it pulls with it the full sensory weight of the original scene, and she is back on that
pavement, certain she is being watched.

The hour of late afternoon has become almost unbearable. Each day, as the light in her shop shifts
toward the angle it held that evening, Mere feels the dizziness rise and her attention fracture. To
manage this, she has rearranged her closing time, asking her part-time assistant to lock up so that
she herself leaves before the dangerous hour arrives. She avoids the parking lot entirely, parking
two blocks away and varying her route. The anniversary of the incident — a date she cannot forget
even as she tries to — she now spends away from the shop altogether, booking herself onto day trips
or simply staying in bed with the curtains drawn. Over months, these strategies have narrowed her
world considerably. A long-standing relationship with a neighbouring business owner has cooled; she
no longer lingers after hours to share tea, and her absence has been noticed and misread as
coldness. Occupationally, her reduced hours and her tendency to leave the floor to her assistant
during the late-afternoon rush have begun to affect revenue.

What Mere had not anticipated was that the avoidance itself would feed what she was trying to
escape. By never remaining in the shop at closing time, she has never been able to test whether she
could tolerate it, and the hour has grown more menacing in her imagination. Her compulsive spending
— sudden online purchases made late at night, a distraction that brings brief relief and later shame
— has added a layer of self-reproach that compounds everything else. She has come to believe, with a
quiet and devastating certainty, that she is inadequate: that a stronger woman would have recovered
by now, that her continuing dizziness and her wordless terror and her inability to simply remember
what happened and move past it are evidence that she is, as she put it in session, "losing her
mind." The appraisal that she is a lousy coper and possibly going crazy has made her more reluctant
to examine the memory directly, which in turn keeps it fragmented and unprocessed, which in turn
keeps the triggers potent — and the loop draws tighter.

What finally brought Mere to seek help was a morning when she opened her shop, saw the afternoon
light already beginning its slow shift, and found she could not make herself step inside. She stood
on the pavement — the same posture, she realised, as that evening — and wept. She is most afraid
that she is permanently changed. She is most hoping, she said quietly, that someone can help her
find the words.
