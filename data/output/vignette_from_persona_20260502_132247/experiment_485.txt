Rosalinda is a 78-year-old widowed Latina woman who works as a call center agent from her apartment
in a mid-sized American city. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 73, indicating severe posttraumatic
stress, following her direct exposure to a terrorist attack approximately three years ago. She had
been visiting a public market — a place she had frequented for decades, a ritual she had shared with
her late husband — when the explosion occurred nearby. The blast killed and injured dozens of people
around her. In the immediate chaos, she watched strangers fall, heard screaming she still cannot
fully account for, and found herself pinned briefly beneath debris before being pulled free. She
does not know exactly how long she was there. She does not know what happened to a woman she had
been speaking with at a neighboring stall moments before. That gap — that woman's face appearing and
then simply ending — is one of the places where memory fails her entirely.

Rosalinda describes the event itself in fragments. She knows what happened in the broadest sense,
but when she attempts to put it into words — in session, or when a caller at work mentions losing
someone, or when a colleague asks how she is doing — the language simply does not come. She reaches
for the experience and finds something wordless and physical instead: a tightening in her neck, a
dull ache that spreads upward, a bodily knowing that resists narration. She has come to understand
this not as a limitation of memory but as evidence of something worse — that what happened to her
was so catastrophic it lies outside the boundaries of ordinary human experience. This interpretation
has deepened her sense that she has no future worth describing, that the woman she was before the
market no longer exists and the woman she is now is someone who cannot be known or reached.

The fragmented, nonverbal quality of her memory does more than frustrate her attempts at speech — it
keeps the trauma perpetually unresolved, so that ordinary moments can ambush her without warning. At
work, she spends hours on the phone, and it is precisely there, in the call center, that triggers
find her most reliably. When a caller speaks about losing a loved one — a recurring theme in the
customer service calls she handles — Rosalinda feels the floor shift beneath her. The conversation
pulls her back not into a clear narrative but into the sensory residue of that day: the neck pain
returns, the words leave her, and she is briefly nowhere she can name. These intrusions at work have
begun to erode her sense of competence in the role she took on after her husband's death to maintain
independence, and she has come to believe she has failed in the most important thing left to her —
being someone capable of holding herself together and caring for herself in widowhood.

Over the months following the attack, Rosalinda built a careful architecture of avoidance. She does
not go near the market or the surrounding neighborhood. She has stopped acknowledging the
anniversary of the attack, filling that date with deliberate blankness, avoiding news and phone
calls alike. She has withdrawn emotionally from her adult children and the few friends who remain,
finding that closeness requires a kind of self-disclosure she can no longer manage — and that
attempts at intimacy bring her closer to the event, as if being known means being seen in it. This
withdrawal has kept the traumatic material sealed and unprocessed, which means the fragments never
organize into something she can hold at a distance, and the triggers remain as potent as they were
in the first weeks. The avoidance has also confirmed, in her own mind, that she is helpless — that
bad things will find her regardless, that no action she takes can create safety — and this belief
has settled into a quiet, pervasive hopelessness.

What finally brought Rosalinda to seek help was a call at work last month in which a woman described
losing her husband in a crowd. Rosalinda was unable to finish the call. She sat afterward for a long
time, unable to speak, her neck aching, and thought: I cannot go on like this. What she is most
afraid of is that it is already too late — that the person she was is gone. What she is most hoping,
tentatively, is that someone might help her find words for what she cannot yet say.
