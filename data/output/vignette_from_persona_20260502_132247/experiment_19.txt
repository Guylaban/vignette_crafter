Priya is a 45-year-old South Asian woman who works as a plumber in a mid-sized city in the American
Midwest. She lives alone and has been single for several years, a circumstance that, since her
illness, has come to feel less like a lifestyle choice and more like an exposure she cannot afford.
Eighteen months ago, Priya was admitted to the intensive care unit following a sudden and severe
episode of bacterial meningitis. She had been on a routine job — replacing corroded pipes beneath a
commercial kitchen — when she felt an abrupt wave of dizziness so disorienting she had to lower
herself to the floor. By the time her co-worker called an ambulance, she was struggling to draw a
full breath, and the fluorescent lights of the hospital corridor were the last thing she registered
clearly before losing consciousness for nearly four days. She woke to the smell of antiseptic, to
tubes in her arms, and to the news that she had come very close to dying.

In the months that followed, Priya returned to work but found that the job she had done competently
for over a decade had become a landscape of hidden threats. Crawling beneath a sink one afternoon,
she felt the enclosed space press the air from her chest, and in the shallow, effortful breathing
that followed, the ICU came back with sudden and total clarity — not as a memory she was recalling
but as something happening again. That breathlessness, and the dizziness that sometimes accompanied
it after exertion in tight spaces, had become the most potent triggers in her daily life. Even the
faint antiseptic smell that lingered in the cleaning products stored in some clients' utility rooms
was enough to stop her mid-task, her hands trembling on a wrench. Each time these somatic and
olfactory triggers fired, they confirmed what Priya had come to believe with growing certainty: that
her body was no longer a reliable instrument. The dizziness, the shortness of breath — these were
not the ordinary sensations of physical work but evidence that something could go catastrophically
wrong at any moment, without warning, as it had before. She had come to feel that she had to be
especially careful at all times, because the next collapse might come from nowhere, and she was
certain that when it did, she would face it alone.

That conviction — that she could not rely on other people — had its roots partly in the illness
itself. She had been unconscious and helpless while others made decisions about her body, and she
had woken feeling that the experience had exposed a fundamental truth about her vulnerability. These
appraisals of her body as treacherous and the world as unpredictable drove her, with a logic that
felt entirely reasonable to her, toward a set of protective strategies. She began refusing any
situation that placed physical demands on her body beyond the minimum required: she declined jobs
involving confined spaces when she could, avoided lifting heavy equipment, and stopped going to the
gym. She cancelled a follow-up appointment with her neurologist, telling herself she did not want to
hear bad news, and she had not seen a doctor since. She also stopped pursuing the psychological
referral her GP had suggested, reasoning that talking about what happened would only make the
sensations worse.

The problem was that these strategies, however sensible they felt, had quietly deepened the very
beliefs they were meant to protect her from. Because she had avoided any physical exertion, she had
no evidence that her body could be trusted under stress — only the absence of a test she had refused
to take. Her world had narrowed accordingly. She had turned down a major commercial contract because
it required sustained physical labour, and a colleague she had worked alongside for years had
stopped calling after she cancelled plans three times in a row. The isolation reinforced her belief
that she could not rely on others, and the avoidance of medical care left the appraisal of her body
as unreliable entirely unchallenged, growing more entrenched with each passing month.

Priya sought help after a morning when she became so dizzy on a residential job that she sat in her
van for forty minutes, unable to go inside. She is most afraid that the symptoms mean something is
still physically wrong with her. What she is hoping for, though she would not quite say it aloud, is
to be able to work a full day without bracing for the worst.
