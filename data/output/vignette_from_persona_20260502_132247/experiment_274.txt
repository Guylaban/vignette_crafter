Linh is a 48-year-old Vietnamese-American woman who works as a waitress at a mid-sized family
restaurant in her city. She is divorced and lives alone. She was referred for psychological
assessment following a disclosure to her primary care physician, and she arrived at the intake
appointment guarded, speaking quietly and rarely making eye contact.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Linh became aware that a man she had briefly dated was following
her. What began as repeated appearances at her restaurant — sitting in her section, watching her
move between tables — escalated over several months into notes left on her car, phone calls at 2
a.m., and on one occasion, the discovery that he had been standing outside her apartment building
for hours. The stalking reached its peak on a night when she returned home from a closing shift to
find her front door unlocked. She did not know whether he had been inside. She stood in the doorway
for what she later described as a very long time, unable to move or speak. Police involvement
eventually led to a restraining order, but by then the damage, as Linh understood it, was already
done.

In the weeks that followed, something in Linh's memory of those months refused to settle into
ordinary recollection. She finds that she cannot put what happened into coherent language — when she
tries to describe the period to anyone, including her therapist, the words do not arrive in the
right order, and what she produces feels disconnected from anything she recognises as herself. The
memory, she says, feels like it belongs to a different woman entirely. And yet it does not stay at a
distance: it forces itself into her mind without warning, most often when she is mid-task at work —
carrying plates, taking an order — and the intrusion leaves her physically shaken, heart hammering,
skin flushed. These unbidden returns of the experience feed directly into how she now understands
her life: she has come to believe that the woman she was before — someone she remembers as genuinely
happy, someone who found pleasure in small things — is simply gone. Nothing in her days feels
meaningful. She describes herself as feeling dead inside, a phrase she returns to with a flatness
that suggests it is not metaphor but diagnosis.

This profound sense of inner loss has in turn shaped how vigilantly she monitors the world around
her. Because she has concluded that safety is no longer something she can take for granted, she
listens for sounds at night — footsteps in the hallway, the particular creak of the building's
entrance — and she avoids any place she has not been before, reasoning that unfamiliar environments
offer no baseline against which to detect danger. At the restaurant, this plays out concretely: she
has quietly arranged her sections so that she works only the tables nearest the kitchen, where she
can see both the entrance and the back exit simultaneously. When a new customer sits in an
unfamiliar part of the floor, she finds reasons to ask a colleague to cover. The hypervigilance is
exhausting, and its exhaustion deepens her conviction that her life has become unrecognisable.

The sense of permanent diminishment and the constant state of alert together drive a set of
strategies that have narrowed her world considerably. She avoids the friends she socialised with
before the stalking began — seeing them, she fears, would require her to account for who she has
become, and she cannot bear that reflection. Any situation that might involve disagreement or
confrontation she sidesteps entirely, even minor ones at work, because conflict now registers in her
body as threat. She has also begun to avoid looking at or touching her own body, as though it too is
a site she no longer trusts. These withdrawals, over the months, have had the effect of sealing off
the very contexts in which the memory might be processed or spoken — without social contact, without
embodied presence, without the friction of ordinary disagreement, there is nothing to interrupt the
memory's uncontrolled returns, and so it continues to arrive uninvited, sustaining the dread and the
emptiness in equal measure.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a shift at the restaurant last month in which she froze
completely when a male customer raised his voice at a colleague near her station. She stood holding
a tray, unable to move, until a coworker touched her arm. She is most afraid, she says, that this is
simply who she is now. What she is hoping for, though she offers it tentatively, is to feel
something again.
