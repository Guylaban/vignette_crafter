Cassandra Volk is a 35-year-old single actress who has worked steadily in regional theatre and
independent film for over a decade, a career that has always required her to move fluidly through
crowded spaces — green rooms, subway platforms, busy streets between auditions. She presented for
treatment reporting a PCL-5 score of 49, describing a presentation that had quietly dismantled her
professional and personal life over the preceding eighteen months.

The event that precipitated her difficulties occurred on an afternoon commute when Cassandra
witnessed a man being violently assaulted on a subway platform by two individuals, one of whom was
armed. The assault was prolonged and chaotic. What she recalls is not a coherent scene but a series
of sensory fragments — the sound of impact, the colour of the assailant's jacket, the way other
passengers pressed back against the tiled wall. She could not intervene, could not speak, and was
still standing on the platform when transit police arrived. No arrests were made that day, and as
far as Cassandra later discovered, no one was ever charged.

In the weeks that followed, she found she could not construct a reliable account of what had
happened. Her memories arrived in disconnected pieces that refused to assemble into sequence — a
flash of the victim's face, the metallic smell of the platform, the sound of shoes on concrete — and
when she tried to put the experience into words for friends or a brief initial consultation with a
counsellor, language simply failed her. This fragmented, nonverbal quality of the memory meant that
it never felt finished or past; when it surfaced, it did so with the full weight of the present
tense, as though she were still standing on that platform. The reliving experience in turn
reinforced her conviction that she remained in danger, that whatever had happened had not truly
ended, and this sense of unresolved threat made it harder still to hold the memory at any distance.

What frightened Cassandra most, in the months that accumulated after the assault, was the belief
that the world had revealed itself to be a place where violence could occur in full view of
witnesses and simply be absorbed — that the people responsible faced no consequences, that the
systems she had implicitly trusted had processed the event and moved on while she had not. She had
come to understand herself as permanently marked by this, set apart from the people around her who
seemed able to ride the subway, pass a uniformed officer, or sit in a restaurant without calculating
sight lines. The sense of being an outsider, of inhabiting a different and more dangerous version of
the city than everyone else, was not abstract; it was the specific texture of her daily life. These
appraisals — that accountability had failed, that she was now permanently different — fed directly
into a vigilance that was exhausting to maintain. She scanned every room she entered for the safest
seat, always positioning herself with her back to a wall and a clear view of exits. The sight of a
police uniform or a visible weapon, even on a security guard outside a theatre, was enough to
collapse the distance between present and past and plunge her back into the fragmented sensory world
of the platform.

She stopped taking public transport entirely within two months of the assault, rerouting her life
around car shares and walking distances that made her rehearsal schedule increasingly difficult to
manage. Her director had begun scheduling around her in ways that were noticed by the rest of the
cast, and the professional embarrassment compounded her sense of isolation. On the evenings when the
vigilance became intolerable, she found herself spending impulsively — clothes, equipment, things
she did not need — a behaviour that offered brief relief but left her feeling, in the morning, that
she had again done something that separated her further from the stable, functional person she
believed others to be. The avoidance and the spending had become their own evidence: each time she
rerouted her journey or sat scanning a waiting room, she confirmed to herself that the threat was
real and that she alone understood it, tightening the loop between her appraisals and her monitoring
until the two were nearly indistinguishable.

Cassandra sought help after a costume fitting during which a prop weapon was left on a table and she
left the room abruptly and could not return. She told her intake clinician that she did not expect
to feel safe — she had stopped believing that was possible — but she hoped, with some uncertainty,
to feel less alone in what she was carrying.
