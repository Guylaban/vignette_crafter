Adaeze is a 38-year-old Nigerian-American woman who works as a commercial fisherwoman along the Gulf
Coast, rising before dawn most mornings to haul nets with a small crew. She is married and, until
approximately fourteen months ago, described herself as someone who handled difficulty with
pragmatic calm. That self-understanding shattered on the evening her younger brother Emeka was
struck by a car while crossing an intersection near her home. Adaeze had been on the phone with him
seconds before impact — she heard the screech of tires, his voice cut off, and then nothing. She
arrived at the scene before the ambulance, and the image of his body against the crumpled hood of
the vehicle is one she has not been able to leave behind. Emeka survived, but sustained a traumatic
brain injury that has permanently altered his personality and independence. In Adaeze's mind, the
accident did not end that night. It has continued, quietly, ever since.

From the beginning, Adaeze was consumed by a belief that she had caused what happened. She had asked
Emeka to meet her that evening; she had chosen the time and the location. She became convinced that
the event happened because of the way she had acted — her insistence, her poor choice of meeting
place — and that she had failed in her most fundamental duty as an older sister. She has never
forgiven herself for not doing more to stop what happened, turning over and over the moments before
the call, the routes she could have suggested, the words she could have said to delay him by even
thirty seconds. These appraisals of guilt and self-blame are not passing thoughts; they are the
architecture of her inner life, and they generate a persistent state of fear, shame, and barely
contained anger that surfaces without warning. At work, she has snapped at her crew over minor
equipment issues, and her husband has learned to give her space in the evenings, a distance that has
grown wider and more habitual over the months.

The physical world is full of reminders that bring her distress crashing back. On the water, when a
colleague mentions a road accident heard on the morning radio, Adaeze's chest tightens and her hands
go cold — the conversation itself enough to pull her back to the sound of that phone call. Cars with
visible collision damage, glimpsed on the drive to the docks, produce an immediate visceral
reaction: nausea, a surge of adrenaline, the body rehearsing emergency. These triggers arrive
without warning and without mercy, and in their wake Adaeze has developed a set of strategies that
she relies on to keep herself functional, or at least upright. She stays up until two or three in
the morning — not because she cannot sleep, but because she has learned that exhaustion eventually
overtakes memory, and lying down in quiet darkness too early leaves her alone with the images. She
spends money impulsively — small purchases at first, then larger ones — in a pattern she recognises
as irrational but cannot easily stop, the brief comfort of acquisition offering a few minutes of
relief. And she returns, obsessively, to the question of prevention: what she should have done, what
she could still do differently in some imagined revision of that evening.

Yet these strategies carry their own costs. The late nights and impulsive spending leave her
depleted and ashamed, which deepens her conviction that she is someone who fails the people she
loves — feeding directly back into the guilt and self-blame that drive the cycle. The rumination,
rather than resolving anything, keeps the event perpetually present, so that even stimuli she might
otherwise have habituated to — the anniversary of the accident now approaching on the calendar, a
car commercial glimpsed on her phone between fishing runs — land with undiminished force, triggering
the same cascade of physical alarm and emotional flooding all over again.

What finally brought Adaeze to seek help was a conversation with her husband, who told her quietly
that he was frightened — not of her, but for her. She came to the intake appointment guarded and
precise, describing her symptoms with the same methodical attention she brings to her work on the
water. What she is most afraid of is that the guilt is accurate — that she truly is to blame, and
that therapy will confirm it. What she is most hoping for, though she said it only once and quickly,
is that she might one day stop hearing the silence at the end of that phone call.
