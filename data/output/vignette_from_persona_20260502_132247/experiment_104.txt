Marama Tuilagi is a fifty-three-year-old widowed woman of Samoan descent who owns and operates a
small gift and sundry shop in a mid-sized coastal town. She was referred for psychological
assessment following a significant decline in her ability to manage the shop and maintain daily
functioning, presenting with a PCL-5 score of 70. Her difficulties trace back to the sudden death of
her husband, Sione, approximately fourteen months ago. Sione collapsed at home on a Friday evening
after consuming a considerable amount of alcohol — a habit that had grown quietly over the years —
and was found unresponsive by Marama in the early hours of the morning. Despite her desperate
attempts to rouse him and the arrival of paramedics, he was pronounced dead at the scene. The smell
of alcohol still on his skin, the stillness of the room, the particular quality of the light — these
details are not memories Marama can choose to recall or set aside. They are things that happen to
her.

In the weeks following Sione's death, Marama found herself unable to attend his funeral with any
degree of composure. She stood at the graveside feeling, she later described, as though the ground
beneath her was not solid — as though she were watching the scene from somewhere slightly outside
her own body. She could not piece together a coherent account of that period, nor of the night she
found him. When she tries to tell herself what happened, the story breaks apart. What she has
instead is a film that plays without her permission: the weight of his arm, the smell, the silence.
The memory does not arrive in sequence. It arrives whole and without warning, forcing itself into
her awareness while she is ringing up a customer, unlocking the shop, or simply sitting still.

The shop has become a particular site of difficulty. Marama stocks a small selection of wines and
spirits for local customers, and on several occasions each week she encounters the smell of alcohol
— from deliveries, from opened bottles, from customers who have been drinking. Each time, the smell
lands in her body before it reaches her thinking mind. Her heart accelerates, her chest tightens,
and she finds herself scanning the room as though something is about to go wrong, reacting as if she
is in danger even when she can see plainly that she is not. She cannot relax even in the quietest
moments of the day; the shop, once a source of pride and routine, now feels like a place where the
next intrusion is only moments away. These triggered states do not resolve quickly — they pull the
memory back to the surface, and the memory, in turn, sharpens the sense of threat, confirming to her
nervous system that danger is present and real.

What has deepened the clinical picture considerably is the weight of Marama's appraisals about what
has happened to her and to her life. She has come to believe, with a quiet and settled certainty,
that her life has been destroyed — not damaged or disrupted, but ended in some essential way. She
does not recognise herself. The woman who ran the shop with energy, who organised community
fundraisers, who laughed easily — that person feels entirely foreign to her now. She is certain she
has no future worth imagining. These beliefs do not sit passively alongside her symptoms; they
actively sustain them. When she appraises her life as destroyed, the grief-laden conversations she
overhears between customers, the funeral notices in the local paper, the cemetery visible from the
road she drives each morning — all of these land with renewed force, triggering fresh waves of
intrusion and alarm. And when the alarm state itself confirms that she cannot function normally,
cannot relax, cannot feel safe, it feeds directly back into her conviction that she is permanently
and irreparably changed. The memory, replaying its fragments, reinforces the appraisal; the
appraisal colours every encounter with the world, making ordinary reminders feel like evidence of an
irreversible loss.

Marama presented for help after a longtime customer gently told her she seemed unwell. What she most
fears is that she is right — that there is no version of herself left to recover. What she most
hopes, though she barely allows herself to say it, is that someone might tell her otherwise.
