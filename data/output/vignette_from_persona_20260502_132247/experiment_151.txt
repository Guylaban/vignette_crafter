Viktor is a 60-year-old man of Eastern European descent who works the early shift at a large
distribution warehouse on the outskirts of the city, where he has lived with his long-term partner
for the past eleven years. He was referred for psychological assessment following a visit to his
primary care physician, who noted elevated scores on a routine screening questionnaire and a marked
deterioration in Viktor's functioning over recent months. His PCL-5 score of 62 places him firmly in
the severe range, and the picture that has emerged across the intake and first two sessions is one
of a man quietly consumed by something he has never been able to put into words.

The precipitating event occurred decades ago during Viktor's military service, in a close-quarters
engagement in which several members of his unit were killed. Viktor was one of a small number who
survived, and the specific circumstances of that survival — the position he happened to occupy, the
decision he made in the seconds before the worst of it — have never left him. He walked away. Others
did not. In the years immediately following his discharge he managed, as many do, through a
combination of forward momentum and deliberate not-looking. He built a life, found a partner, took
the warehouse job, and told himself that what had happened was behind him.

It was not. When Viktor tries to recall the event — and sometimes the recall comes without
invitation — what surfaces is not a coherent narrative but something more like a wall of sensation:
the particular weight of the uniform he was wearing, the smell of the air, and then a terror so
immediate and physical that it is indistinguishable from the original moment. He has told his
partner almost nothing, in part because he genuinely cannot find the language. He knows what
happened. He knows the sequence of events. But when he opens his mouth, the words simply are not
there, and the failure to articulate it has quietly reinforced his conviction that what he carries
is unspeakable — something outside the range of normal human experience, something that marks him as
permanently altered. These qualities of the memory itself — its vividness, its wordlessness, its
capacity to return him bodily to that day — have deepened his belief that the world remains
fundamentally dangerous, that threat is always proximate, and that he survived something he had no
right to survive.

It is this last conviction that sits at the centre of Viktor's presentation. He feels guilty for
being alive. He has come to believe, with a quiet certainty that resists challenge, that his
survival was a kind of theft — that something was taken from the men who died and given,
undeservedly, to him. This survival guilt has corroded his sense of who he is; he no longer
recognises the person he was before, and the man he sees now feels like an impostor wearing borrowed
time. These appraisals have driven him toward a set of strategies that feel, to Viktor, like the
only rational response to an intolerable situation. He avoids thinking about the event with a
vigilance that costs him considerable mental energy. He has not returned to the neighbourhood where
his old barracks were located, even when a route through that area would have been convenient. And
in a detail that struck his clinician as particularly telling, he stopped wearing a particular style
of heavy work jacket — one that, in cut and colour, resembled the uniform from that day — despite
the cold of the warehouse floor.

The avoidance has not protected him. If anything, the years of not-thinking have kept the memory raw
and unprocessed, so that when it does surface it retains its full original terror, which in turn
confirms his belief that he is fundamentally changed and that the world is a place where catastrophe
waits. His partner has noticed his withdrawal and has stopped asking questions, and Viktor has
interpreted this as further evidence that he is now someone unknowable — even to the person closest
to him.

What finally brought him in was a moment at work, three months ago, when a younger colleague arrived
wearing a jacket that stopped Viktor cold in the middle of the loading bay. He stood there, unable
to move, certain for several seconds that he was back. He has not told anyone what happened in those
seconds. What he is most afraid of, he said quietly at the end of the second session, is that there
is no longer a version of himself worth recovering.
