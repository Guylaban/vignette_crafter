Ingrid Halvorsen is a 41-year-old married firefighter of Norwegian descent who presented for
psychological assessment following a life-threatening illness that occurred approximately fourteen
months prior to her intake appointment. Her PCL-5 score of 68 places her well within the severe
range, and the clinical picture that emerged across the first several sessions is one of a woman
whose professional identity — built on physical competence and the capacity to function under
pressure — has been quietly dismantled from the inside.

Eighteen months ago, Ingrid was admitted to the intensive care unit following a rapid-onset septic
infection that had gone unrecognised for several days. What began as fatigue she attributed to a
demanding rotation at the station deteriorated within seventy-two hours into organ compromise, a
central line placed in her neck, and a period during which her husband was told she might not
survive the night. She was conscious for much of it — conscious enough to feel the cold antiseptic
swabbed across her skin before each procedure, to watch the IV lines running into her arm, to hear
the mechanical rhythm of monitors she could not silence. She has described lying in a position of
enforced stillness, arms extended and restrained by tubing, unable to move without triggering
alarms, and she has said, more than once, that she felt less like a patient and more like a body
being worked on.

The memories of those days do not come to Ingrid in narrative form. She cannot tell the story the
way she tells stories about fires — with a beginning, a sequence, a resolution. Instead, what
returns are fragments that seem to bypass language entirely: the smell of the ward, the particular
angle of a fluorescent light, the weight of a drip line pulling at the back of her hand. When these
fragments surface, she does not merely remember being in that bed — she finds her arms tensing, her
neck stiffening, her body resuming the position of enforced stillness as though the memory is stored
not in her mind but in her musculature. The terror that accompanied the original event returns with
the memory at full intensity, unchanged by the fourteen months that have passed, and this has
convinced her that she has not, in any meaningful sense, recovered.

Because the memories arrive with such force and feel so physically total, Ingrid has developed a set
of strategies aimed at keeping them at a distance. She redirects her attention aggressively —
filling her off-shift hours with activity, refusing to allow silence, reaching for her phone or the
television the moment her mind begins to drift toward anything resembling the hospital. She avoids
any situation that carries emotional weight, having learned that strong feeling of any kind seems to
lower whatever barrier she has constructed. Most concretely, she has declined two routine medical
appointments since discharge and has begun rerouting her drive to the station to avoid passing the
hospital where she was treated. These strategies have not resolved the memories, however — they have
preserved them intact, unprocessed, exactly as raw as the day she was discharged, ensuring that when
intrusions do break through, they carry the same undiminished terror. The avoidance has also had a
professional cost: Ingrid works on a crew that responds to medical emergencies alongside paramedics,
and she has twice requested reassignment away from calls flagged as medical, something her captain
has noted and questioned.

Her body, meanwhile, operates as though the threat has never fully passed. She scans her environment
constantly — at the station, at home, in ordinary social settings — and her physical state is one of
sustained readiness that she cannot voluntarily lower. This hypervigilance means that ordinary
sensory cues carry enormous weight: the antiseptic smell of a cleaning product used at the station,
the sight of a colleague's blood-glucose monitor, the procedural preparation before a routine
physical — each of these lands in her nervous system as a signal of danger, activating the same
physical reactivity that accompanied the original crisis, which in turn makes the underlying
memories feel closer and more present. The loop is one she has begun to recognise without being able
to interrupt it.

What finally brought Ingrid to seek help was a mandatory occupational health review she could not
avoid. She sat in the waiting room, smelled the antiseptic, and left before her name was called. She
told her husband that evening, for the first time, that she was frightened she was no longer fit to
do her job. What she hopes for, she said at intake, is to be able to walk back into a medical
setting without her body deciding she is dying.
