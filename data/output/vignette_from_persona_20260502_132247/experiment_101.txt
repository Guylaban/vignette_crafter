Walter Briggs, a 67-year-old widowed military medic of North American descent, presented for an
initial intake following a life-threatening motor vehicle collision that occurred approximately
fourteen months prior. Walter had spent decades working in high-pressure medical environments,
trained to remain composed under conditions that would overwhelm most people. That history made what
happened on a wet November evening all the more destabilising. He had been driving home from a
veterans' support meeting when a driver who had been drinking crossed the centre line and struck his
vehicle head-on. The impact was catastrophic. Walter remembers the smell of alcohol drifting through
the shattered windshield as the other driver stumbled from his car, and then the acrid bite of
something burning — plastic, rubber, perhaps electrical — rising from beneath his own hood. The
sounds of bending metal and shattering glass were immediate and total. He lay trapped for nearly
forty minutes, conscious throughout, running through triage protocols in his mind with a detachment
he now finds disturbing.

In the weeks that followed, Walter noticed that his memory of the event did not behave the way he
expected memory to behave. When he attempted to describe the collision to his physician, he would
lose the thread mid-sentence, the account dissolving before he could complete it. More troubling was
the quality of the memory itself — it felt, he said, as though it had happened to someone else, as
though he were reading a file on a stranger rather than recalling his own experience. And yet
certain fragments were involuntary and vivid: he would hear, without warning, the grinding shriek of
metal, as real and present as if he were still inside the car. These intrusive auditory memories
left Walter convinced that something was structurally wrong with his mind, and that conviction
deepened into a belief that he was, in some essential way, weak — that a man with his training
should not be falling apart over something he had survived. He had come to believe the collision was
partly his fault, that he had been inattentive, that a better version of himself would have seen it
coming. This self-blame and the accompanying sense that he could no longer trust his own judgment
fed directly into a hypervigilance that made ordinary life feel hazardous. He was certain that the
next threat was always imminent, and his body agreed: loud sounds — a car door slamming in the
parking lot of the veterans' clinic where he still works part-time — produced a full startle
response that he found humiliating and that reinforced his belief in his own weakness.

The negative appraisals did not stay abstract. Because Walter believed himself to be unreliable and
fundamentally compromised, he began monitoring his own thoughts with exhausting vigilance, scanning
for further evidence that he was losing his grip on sanity. He worked hard to suppress any thought
connected to the crash, and he stopped driving altogether — stopped even accepting rides from
colleagues at the clinic, which meant he now relies on a community transport service to reach the
very workplace where, as a medic, he is expected to project steadiness. The avoidance and
suppression, however, had the paradoxical effect of keeping the trauma close. Efforts not to think
about the event seemed to prime the very memories he was trying to contain, and the auditory
fragments returned with renewed force. Each intrusive recall renewed his appraisal of himself as
broken, and that appraisal in turn made the sensory world feel more dangerous. The smell of alcohol
— encountered once when a patient arrived at the clinic having been drinking — sent his heart rate
surging and brought the crash imagery flooding back. The burnt smell from a faulty piece of
equipment in the clinic's break room produced the same cascade: physical reactivity, then memory,
then the familiar loop of self-recrimination. His threat monitoring kept him scanning for these
cues, and in scanning for them he found them everywhere, so that the triggers themselves multiplied.

What finally brought Walter to seek help was a conversation with a younger colleague who noticed he
had not driven himself to work in months and asked, gently, whether he was all right. Walter said he
was fine. That night, he could not sleep. He is most afraid, he said at intake, that he will never
trust himself again. What he hopes for, more quietly, is that the memory might one day feel like his
own.
