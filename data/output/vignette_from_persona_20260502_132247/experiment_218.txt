Linh Tran is a 36-year-old Vietnamese-American urban planner currently living with her partner in a
mid-sized city, where she works for the municipal planning department designing green infrastructure
and public spaces. She presented for treatment with a PCL-5 score of 56, describing a clinical
picture that had been quietly dismantling her life for the better part of two years. Before entering
therapy, she had told almost no one what she had experienced. Her partner knew only that something
had happened overseas, and that she no longer slept well.

Three years ago, Linh had taken a six-month leave from her job to volunteer with a humanitarian
relief organization deployed to an active conflict zone. She had been stationed at a field camp near
a rural forested region, coordinating logistics for food and medical supply distribution. The camp
came under mortar fire on a morning she had been reviewing maps outside the main tent. The attack
was sudden and sustained. She remembers the sound of shouting — colleagues, then strangers, then no
one she could identify — and the sensation of running through dense vegetation toward a drainage
trench. She lay there for what she later learned was forty minutes, listening to the sounds of the
camp being destroyed. Two colleagues were killed. Linh was physically unharmed, but she did not
leave the trench until long after the shelling stopped.

When she returned to work in the United States, the symptoms emerged slowly, then overwhelmingly.
The most immediately destabilizing were the flashbacks — moments in which she was no longer sitting
at her planning desk but was back in that trench, heart slamming, the air thick and wrong.
Unexpected movement nearby — a colleague crossing her peripheral vision, a door swinging open —
would trigger a full-body startle response that left her gripping her chair, certain for a fraction
of a second that she was in danger. At night, she could not allow herself to fall asleep without
scanning her apartment, reasoning, without fully articulating it, that sleep meant vulnerability.
The threat she had experienced in that conflict zone had not registered as past. Her body continued
to treat it as present.

This unrelenting state of physiological alertness meant that when memory did surface — in fragments,
in flashes — it arrived already amplified. The memories themselves are disordered and strange to
her; when she has tried to explain what happened to her partner or, once, to a friend, she loses the
thread midway through, the sequence dissolving, and she cannot finish. More disturbing to her is the
quality of the memory itself: it feels, she says, like watching footage of someone else. And yet
when she recalls it, what she feels is not grief or fear but a white-hot rage she cannot direct at
anything specific, which frightens her almost as much as the event itself.

To manage this, Linh developed a set of strategies that made sense in the short term and accumulated
into a kind of trap. She began keeping her mind occupied at all times — podcasts during her commute,
complex spreadsheets at lunch, anything to prevent the memories from surfacing. She tried to push
intrusive thoughts away the moment they appeared. And on the worst nights, when the suppression
failed and the rage and the strangeness of the memory became unbearable, she had begun hurting
herself physically — nothing severe, she insisted, just enough to feel something real, something
located in her own body. These strategies, taken together, had kept her functional in the narrowest
sense. But they had also kept the memories from being processed, so that when suppression failed,
the recall was more fragmentary, more alien, more enraging than before. The effort of constant
mental occupation had made her less present at work, and her supervisor had recently raised concerns
about the quality of her output — a concrete and painful consequence for someone who had once found
her work deeply meaningful.

Her occupation now placed her in regular contact with two of her most potent triggers. Reviewing
proposals for parks and naturalized corridors meant studying aerial photographs of dense tree canopy
and drainage systems — landscapes that looked, in the wrong light, like the terrain she had run
through. And the suppression and distraction she relied on to manage her symptoms had made her more
reactive, not less, so that shouting in the open-plan office — a raised voice, a burst of laughter —
landed in her body as alarm before her mind could intervene.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a moment at work when she found herself in the bathroom,
having left a meeting she could not explain leaving, shaking and unable to account for the previous
ten minutes. She is most afraid, she said quietly in the first session, that she will never be able
to explain what happened — not to her partner, not to anyone — and that the person who went to that
country and the person sitting in this office are no longer the same.
