Priya is a 31-year-old South Asian woman who works as a third-grade teacher at an elementary school
in the suburbs of Atlanta. She is married, and by most outward measures her life before the accident
had been quietly purposeful — she was the kind of teacher parents requested by name, the kind who
stayed late laminating things and arrived early to rearrange the reading corner. That version of
herself feels, to Priya, like someone she is no longer permitted to be.

Fourteen months ago, Priya was driving home from a school supply run on a Saturday afternoon, her
back seat loaded with construction paper and paint sets. At an intersection she knew well, she
misjudged the gap in oncoming traffic while making a left turn. The collision that followed was not
catastrophic by statistical measures, but it was catastrophic to her: the driver she struck, a man
in his fifties, sustained a fractured pelvis and a serious head injury that required weeks of
hospitalisation. In the immediate aftermath, Priya sat on the pavement with the smell of engine
fluid and something sweetly chemical — later she would identify it as the paint sets that had burst
open — filling the air around her. She remembers the event not as a continuous sequence but as a
series of frozen images: the other car's grille filling her windshield, her own hands on the
steering wheel, a paramedic's fluorescent vest, the man's face through a car window before she
looked away. These pictures have never softened into narrative distance. When they surface, they do
not feel like memories of something that happened over a year ago — they feel like something
happening now, accompanied by that same sharp chemical smell, her body responding as though she is
still sitting on that pavement.

In the months that followed, Priya came to believe, with the force of settled conviction, that the
accident happened because of something essential and defective in her — not a lapse in judgment but
a revelation of who she actually was. She had always been praised for her competence, her care, her
reliability. The accident reframed all of it as illusion. She was certain she was no longer capable
of the things that had defined her, and she had begun to feel that the accident had placed her
permanently outside the ordinary human community — that other people moved through their lives with
a coherence she had forfeited. These appraisals did not stay abstract. They reached directly into
her memory, pulling the frozen images forward with renewed intensity whenever she found herself
thinking about what she had done. The belief that she was to blame made it impossible to let the
memory recede; returning to it felt, paradoxically, like the only honest thing she could do.

The appraisals also shaped what became unbearable to encounter. Because she had come to understand
herself as fundamentally incapable and fraudulent, praise felt like an accusation. When a parent
emailed to say Priya had transformed their child's relationship with reading, she found herself back
on that pavement within seconds — the smell, the frozen images, the present-tense terror. Positive
emotions carried the same charge: a moment of genuine warmth in the classroom would tip without
warning into the sensory and visual texture of the accident, as though happiness itself was
something she had lost the right to. Any conversation among colleagues about road accidents — not
uncommon in a school staffroom — functioned as an immediate portal. The memory, once triggered,
would then intensify the triggers themselves: having been flooded by the images once in response to
praise, she became hypervigilant to it, scanning for compliments the way someone scans for a threat.

Over the course of the school year, Priya began deflecting praise with a reflexive brevity that her
colleagues read as modesty. She stopped joining staffroom conversations and began eating lunch alone
in her classroom. She had not told her husband the full weight of what she carries, in part because
she was afraid that articulating it would confirm it.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when a student ran up to hug her
and she stood rigid, unable to respond, smelling paint. She is most afraid that she will never be
the teacher — or the person — she was before. She is hoping, with considerable ambivalence, to be
told that is not true.
