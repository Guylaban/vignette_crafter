Tariq is a 54-year-old Palestinian American man who came to the clinic following what he described,
with considerable difficulty, as "something I have never told anyone fully." He works as a community
organizer in a mid-sized city, a role that has long anchored his sense of purpose and identity. His

finally urged him to seek help after months of watching him deteriorate.

The abuse occurred over a period of approximately eight months, perpetrated by a man Tariq had
considered a close friend and occasional romantic partner — a relationship he had kept private given
the cultural and familial pressures of his community. The violence was primarily physical and
escalated in the final incident, which took place at night in a basement apartment where the two had
met privately for years. The room was dark except for a sliver of light under the door. At some
point during the assault, a helicopter passed low overhead — Tariq remembers the sound filling the
ceiling, the vibration, the sense that the world outside was indifferent. He was left on the floor.
He has not returned to that neighborhood since.

In the months that followed, Tariq's memory of the event did not settle into a coherent narrative.
Instead it arrived in disconnected pieces — a flash of the texture of the concrete floor, the sound
of the helicopter returning unbidden, a fragment of a voice — without chronological structure or
emotional context that he could hold. These intrusive pieces surfaced without warning, triggered by
ordinary moments in his daily life: a dark stairwell in the community center where he works, the
physical layout of a room that echoed the proportions of that basement, the distant thrum of a news
helicopter over a neighborhood meeting. When any of these cues appeared, the full sensory weight of
the event collapsed back into him instantaneously, the auditory memory of that helicopter most of
all — as vivid and disorienting as the night itself. The memories, in turn, fed and sharpened the
triggers, so that darkness and enclosed physical spaces became not merely uncomfortable but acutely
threatening, each one a potential portal back to that floor.

What the memories brought with them, again and again, was a corrosive set of beliefs about what had
happened and why. Tariq had come to believe, with a conviction he could not argue himself out of,
that he had been fundamentally betrayed — not only by this man but by his own judgment, his own
trust. Worse, he was increasingly certain that the event had happened because of something essential
and flawed in who he was: that a man like him, carrying the particular secrets he carried, was the
kind of person to whom such things were done. These appraisals did not remain abstract. They
amplified the memories when they surfaced, giving the fragmented images a weight of shame that made
them harder to integrate and easier to be ambushed by. And they sharpened his perception of threat
in the world around him — he had come to read ordinary social encounters as potentially dangerous,
scanning crowds at community events for men who reminded him of his abuser, his sense of the world
as a place governed by any reliable moral order having collapsed entirely.

In response to the memories and the dread they carried, Tariq had built a careful architecture of
avoidance. He wore loose, layered clothing to public events — a habit he told himself was cultural
but which served to make him feel less visible, less legible to dangerous eyes. He routed his daily
commute to avoid the neighborhood where the abuse occurred. At work, where his role required him to
facilitate meetings in community halls and church basements, he arrived early to ensure lights were
fully on before others entered, and positioned himself near exits. These strategies, over time, had
narrowed his world and deepened his shame: a man who organized others for collective action, who had
built his professional life around presence and trust, now felt perpetually watched and perpetually
exposed, the very vigilance that was meant to protect him confirming, each day, that the danger was
real and that he was right to have been destroyed by it.

What finally brought him in was a panic response at a candlelight vigil — a community event he had
himself organized — when the darkness and the sound of a low-flying aircraft converged and he left
without explanation. He is most afraid that what happened has changed him permanently. He is hoping,
tentatively, to be able to tell the whole story in order, from beginning to end, without losing
himself in the pieces.
