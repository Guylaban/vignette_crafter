Valentina Reyes is a 39-year-old Salvadoran-American woman who has worked for a community non-profit
in a mid-sized city for the past six years, coordinating outreach programs for immigrant families.
She came to the intake appointment accompanied by her husband, though she asked him to wait outside.
She sat with her coat still on throughout the session.

Approximately eight months ago, Valentina was assaulted by an unknown man in the parking structure
adjacent to her office building. She had stayed late to finish grant paperwork and was walking to
her car when the man approached her from behind, forced her to the ground, and held what she later
described to police as a folding knife against her side. She was partially undressed during the
assault before a car entering the structure caused him to flee. She lay on the concrete floor of the
parking garage for some time before she was able to move.

In the weeks that followed, Valentina found that the memories of that night did not arrange
themselves into anything she could hold or examine. The sequence felt shattered — she could not
reliably say what had happened first or last, only that certain sensory fragments surfaced without
warning: the smell of engine oil, the cold of the ground, the metallic glint of the knife. She told
her therapist at intake that she knew what had happened but could not find the words for it — that
when she tried to speak about it directly, language seemed to fail her, and what rose in its place
was not grief but a searing, almost blinding rage. That rage, she said, frightened her nearly as
much as the assault itself. The fragmented, wordless quality of the memory meant it never resolved
into something past; it remained structurally present, and its intrusive return kept her nervous
system in a state of near-constant readiness — she startled violently at sudden sounds, slept
fitfully, and found the night hours almost unbearable, lying awake beside her husband and scanning
the dark even when she could hear him breathing steadily beside her.

What compounded everything was what Valentina had come to believe about herself in the aftermath.
She was certain, in a way she could not argue herself out of, that a stronger woman would not have
been in that parking structure alone at that hour — that someone else, someone less naive, less
weak, would have sensed the danger and left earlier. This belief that she was fundamentally weak and
had somehow invited the situation by her own inadequacy became the lens through which she processed
everything else. When the fragmented memories surfaced, they did not simply bring fear; they brought
fresh confirmation of her perceived failure, and the rage she felt on recall was directed as much at
herself as at her attacker. Her self-blame, in turn, made the memories harder to approach and easier
to avoid, so they remained unprocessed and continued to intrude with full sensory force.

The appraisal of weakness also fed directly into her sense of threat. Because she understood the
assault as a consequence of her own deficiency rather than as a random crime, she could not accept
reassurances that she was safe — not from her husband, not from her colleagues, not from the police
report that noted the man had assaulted two other women in the area. Safety felt like something she
had forfeited. This meant that ordinary triggers carried enormous weight. At the non-profit office,
where her work occasionally brings her into contact with community members who have experienced
violence, there are moments when someone gestures with a tool or a piece of equipment and
Valentina's body responds before her mind can intervene — heart rate spiking, breath shortening, the
night in the parking structure suddenly immediate. The sight of anything that could be a weapon, or
the rare moments in which she feels physically exposed or vulnerable, collapse the distance between
then and now entirely, flooding her with the same unprocessed terror and reigniting the conviction
that she is someone to whom terrible things happen because of who she is.

Over these months, Valentina has quietly withdrawn from the cultural and community events she once
helped organize — the quinceañeras, the neighborhood meetings, the weekend gatherings her non-profit
hosts. She told herself it was tiredness, then workload. Her husband has noticed. What finally
brought her to seek help was a moment last month when a colleague showed a documentary clip
depicting violence during a staff training, and Valentina had to leave the room and sit alone in the
bathroom for twenty minutes, shaking. She wants, she said at the end of the intake, to stop being
afraid of herself.
