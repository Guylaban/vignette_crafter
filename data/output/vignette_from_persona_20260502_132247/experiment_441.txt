Rohan is a 41-year-old South Asian architect, married, who presented for psychological assessment
following a period of sustained physical abuse perpetrated by a previous long-term romantic partner.
Although that relationship ended several years ago, Rohan describes his daily life as one of barely
managed dread, and his PCL-5 score of 70 places him in the severe range of PTSD symptomatology.

The abuse occurred across approximately two years of that earlier relationship. His partner would
drink heavily and, when intoxicated, become physically violent — striking him across the face and
chest, screaming in a way that dissolved into incoherent noise, her words blurring into sounds he
could not parse or predict. On the worst nights, Rohan had been unwell with a winter illness when
she attacked him; the combination of his physical vulnerability and her sudden escalation had left
him unable to defend himself or even fully process what was happening. He remembered lying on the
floor of their apartment, feverish and disoriented, the smell of alcohol sharp in the cold air, the
sound of her voice a wall of unintelligible sound. He had not fought back. He had not left. He had
not told anyone. He had, he now believed, failed every test a man could fail.

The memories do not arrive as narrative. Rohan describes them as a series of photographs — a close-
up of her raised hand, the grain of the floorboards against his cheek, the amber blur of a whiskey
glass — each image vivid and dimensionless, stripped of context but saturated with the original
terror. When these images surface, they do not feel like recollection; they feel like re-entry. The
fear he experiences in those moments is physiologically identical to the fear he felt on the floor
of that apartment, and in the wake of each intrusion, the conviction returns with renewed force: he
had been betrayed by the one person whose trust he had built his life around, and he had done
nothing. This belief — that he is a man who cannot be counted on to act — has become load-bearing in
his psychology, and each time the memories erupt with their full original terror, they seem to him
to confirm it. He had failed in his most important role. He cannot trust himself to do the right
thing.

What makes Rohan's daily life so difficult is that the triggers are everywhere and ordinary. At his
architecture firm, a colleague who speaks quickly and excitedly across an open-plan office sometimes
produces a wash of overlapping voices that, for a moment, becomes incomprehensible — and in that
moment, Rohan's body responds before his mind has caught up. His heart rate surges, his hands go
cold, and he is already scanning the room for threat before he has consciously registered what
alarmed him. The smell of alcohol at a client dinner, or the faint nausea of a seasonal cold,
produces the same pre-conscious mobilisation. These sensory encounters do not merely startle him —
they pull the photographs forward, flooding him with the specific images and the specific terror of
those nights, and with them comes the familiar weight of self-reproach: he should have seen it
coming, he should have acted differently, he was betrayed because he allowed himself to be. The
heightened state of alert that follows these episodes persists long into the evening, and by night
he cannot surrender to sleep because some part of him remains convinced that vigilance is the only
protection he has left.

This vigilance, in turn, keeps the system running. The sustained arousal means that smaller and
smaller cues register as potential danger, which means that the triggers multiply, which means that
the intrusive photographs come more frequently, which means the appraisals of failure and betrayal
are reinforced with greater regularity. His wife has noticed that he flinches when she raises her
voice in laughter. He has declined two client hospitality events this year, citing workload. He has
not explained any of this to her.

What finally brought Rohan to seek help was a morning when he became mildly ill with a stomach
complaint and spent the entire workday unable to concentrate, certain that something terrible was
about to happen. He arrived home, sat down at the kitchen table, and could not stand up for an hour.
He is most afraid that the person he has become is the person he has always been. He hopes, with
some difficulty, that this is not true.
