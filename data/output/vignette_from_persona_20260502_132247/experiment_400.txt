Caleb Hartwell is a 29-year-old single man of North American descent who works as a jeweler in a
small independent shop in his hometown. He came to the intake appointment wearing a jacket despite
the warm weather, choosing a chair in the far corner of the waiting room with his back pressed
firmly against the wall — a posture that, as he would later explain, had become as automatic as
breathing. He was referred for assessment following a period of escalating distress that had begun
roughly eighteen months earlier, after returning from a fourteen-month deployment as a relief worker
in an active conflict zone in the Middle East.

During his deployment, Caleb had worked in a field distribution center providing medical supplies
and food to displaced families. In the final weeks of his posting, the compound came under sustained
mortar fire during a distribution event. He remembers the moment the first shell landed: the
concussive thud that travelled through the ground and into his chest, the sudden cacophony of
children screaming in the crowd, the press of panicked bodies against him as people surged in every
direction. A colleague grabbed his arm without warning to pull him toward cover, and the unexpected
contact sent a bolt of pure terror through him before he understood what was happening. He spent the
next several hours pinned behind a concrete barrier, his heart hammering so violently he was certain
he was dying, listening to the cries of injured children he could not reach. Three children in the
crowd were killed. Caleb was physically unharmed.

Since returning, his body has retained the event in ways that ambush him without permission. The
sound of a child crying — in a supermarket, on the street outside the jewelry shop — can instantly
collapse the distance between present and past, delivering the full sensory weight of that afternoon
without any transitional warning. The memories that surface are not dim or dreamlike; they carry a
vividness and physical immediacy that makes his ordinary recollections of childhood or last week
feel pale and insubstantial by comparison. Sometimes there is no image at all, only the dread — a
wave of grief and terror arriving from nowhere, sourceless, which he finds in some ways more
disorienting than the full flashback. His own heart racing, whether from climbing stairs or drinking
too much coffee, is among the most reliable of these portals: the moment he feels that familiar
pounding, his nervous system interprets it as evidence that something is already wrong, and the
memories crowd in before he can stop them.

Because the memories arrive with such force, Caleb has developed a set of strategies he believes are
keeping him functional. He forces himself to think only positive thoughts when distress rises, and
reminds himself — repeatedly, almost ritually — that it could have been worse, that he survived,
that others suffered more. These efforts at reframing do briefly interrupt the emotional intensity,
but they also require him to actively suppress the content of what he lived through, which means the
memories never settle or integrate; they remain pressurised, and the next trigger finds them just as
raw. The suppression itself seems to prime the system: after a session of forced positivity, he
finds his mind scanning more urgently for the next threat, as though the effort of containment has
heightened his vigilance rather than reduced it. That vigilance — the constant environmental
scanning, the cataloguing of every potentially dangerous object within reach, the inability to sit
with his back exposed — keeps him in a state of sustained physiological readiness that makes his
heart race, which in turn reopens the memory, completing a circuit he cannot find the exit from.
When others tell him he is safe, he recognises intellectually that they mean well, but he cannot
make himself believe it; the threat feels too structurally present to be dismissed by reassurance.

In his daily work at the jewelry shop, the layout of the workspace has quietly reorganised itself
around his needs. He rearranged the back workbench so that it faces the door, and he has asked his
employer not to approach him from behind. Customers who reach across the counter unexpectedly
sometimes trigger a visible startle response that he finds humiliating to explain. He has stopped
attending the monthly jewelers' guild meetings because the crowded room, with its unpredictable
movement and noise, is unmanageable.

What finally brought Caleb to seek help was a moment last month when a child in the shop began
crying after dropping a bracelet, and he found himself crouched behind the display case, shaking,
unable to stand. He is most afraid that what happened to him has permanently altered something
fundamental — that the person who left for that deployment no longer exists. What he is hoping for,
quietly and with some embarrassment, is simply to be able to do his job without bracing for the
world to end.
