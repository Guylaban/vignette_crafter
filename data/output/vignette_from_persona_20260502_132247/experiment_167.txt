Boonmee is an eighty-year-old Cambodian-American woman who came of age during a period of
devastating civil conflict. Now a religious educator at a small Buddhist community center in her
city, she presents with a PCL-5 score of 75, reflecting severe and pervasive post-traumatic symptoms
that have quietly organized her entire life for decades. She sought help only recently, after a
student at the center embraced her unexpectedly from behind and she collapsed against a wall,
trembling and unable to speak for nearly an hour. That moment, she told her intake clinician, made
her realize she could no longer manage alone.

The events that formed the center of her trauma occurred when Boonmee was a young woman in a village
that came under sustained military assault. She remembers the fires most — entire rows of structures
burning, the smell of scorched earth and upturned soil filling the air as people ran across the rice
paddies at night. She remembers being knocked to the ground and held there, a soldier's hands
pressing her down into the wet dirt, the smell of soil filling her nose and mouth. What happened in
the minutes that followed she cannot fully reconstruct. There are gaps — not vagueness, but absolute
absence, as if those moments were never recorded. What she does retain comes to her not as ordinary
recollection but as something almost unbearably vivid, more present than her memory of her own
wedding or the births of her children. And yet, strangely, she also experiences the memory as
belonging to someone else — a woman she watches from a distance, someone she cannot fully claim as
herself.

This fractured quality of memory has fed directly into her most painful beliefs about herself.
Because she cannot remember clearly what she did or did not do, Boonmee has come to believe that
there must be something inherent in her — some flaw of character or spiritual failing — that caused
the event to happen to her specifically. She feels permanently humiliated, not only by what was done
to her body but by the conviction that her body itself became a site of disgrace. She no longer
trusts her physical self; she describes her body as something that betrayed her, something
unreliable and foreign. These appraisals have, over the years, shaped a life of quiet self-erasure.
She avoids forming new friendships at the community center where she teaches, keeping interactions
formal and brief, because she cannot bear the thought of anyone knowing what she carries or what she
believes herself to be.

The sensory world has become treacherous for her. When Boonmee walks through the community garden
adjacent to the center — a space she once tended with pleasure — the smell of turned soil now pulls
the vivid, alien memory forward with a force she cannot resist. Seeing candles lit during
ceremonies, or a fire in a neighbor's yard, produces the same effect. Being touched without warning
is perhaps the most destabilizing trigger of all, and it is one she encounters regularly: a
parishioner's hand on her shoulder, a child reaching for her arm. Each of these moments delivers the
memory not as a thought but as an event, immediate and physical. In response, Boonmee has built an
elaborate architecture of prevention. She checks the locks on every door and window in her home each
night — sometimes three or four times — and keeps her mind in constant motion through prayer
recitation, administrative tasks, and lesson preparation, never allowing silence to settle. These
strategies, which she experiences as necessary and rational, have paradoxically kept the traumatic
material alive. Because she never allows the memories to surface and resolve naturally, they remain
raw and intrusive, and the very vigilance she practices — the constant scanning, the checking —
reinforces the sense that danger is perpetually near, which in turn makes her more sensitive to the
triggers she is trying to avoid.

The loop has tightened over years. The more she manages through avoidance and distraction, the more
her beliefs about her own unworthiness and bodily unreliability solidify, because she has never
allowed herself the experience of surviving a memory without catastrophe. The gaps in her
recollection, which the distraction strategies have helped preserve, leave her shame intact and
unexamined. She comes to therapy now hoping, cautiously, to understand why she has lived this way —
and afraid, above all, that what she cannot remember is worse than what she can.
