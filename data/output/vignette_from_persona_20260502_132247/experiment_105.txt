Adaeze is a 78-year-old widowed woman of Nigerian descent who lives alone in a modest home on the
outskirts of a mid-sized Midwestern city. She has spent the better part of four decades as a potter,
and her small studio — attached to the rear of her house — has long been the center of her daily
life. She came to the clinic after her youngest daughter, visiting from out of state, insisted that
something had to change.

Approximately eight months ago, Adaeze was assaulted in her home by an intruder who entered through
an unlocked side door while she was working late in her studio. The man struck her, demanded money,
and left her on the floor of her kitchen before fleeing through the back. She was not seriously
injured in a medical sense, but the assault lasted long enough — the sound of footsteps approaching
from behind, the sudden loss of control, the man's face close to hers in the dim kitchen light —
that the experience lodged itself in her with a precision that has not faded. She called her
daughter the next morning. She did not call the police until several hours after the attack, sitting
in the dark because she was not certain he had truly gone.

In the weeks that followed, the memory began to behave in ways that frightened Adaeze almost as much
as the assault itself. It did not come when she sought it; it came when she did not. The images —
vivid, pictorial, the man's silhouette in the kitchen doorway, the texture of the floor beneath her
— would surface without warning while she was centering clay or washing her hands at the studio
sink. When they came, she did not remember the event so much as return to it; her heart rate would
climb, her hands would tremble, and she would feel with complete certainty that it was happening
again. These intrusions deepened her conviction that the world had become — or perhaps had always
been — a fundamentally dangerous place, and that she was no longer equipped to move through it
safely. She had come to believe that her own body could not be trusted: it flooded with alarm even
in the middle of the afternoon, even with the doors locked, even when she knew, rationally, that no
one was there. That sense of bodily betrayal hardened into a fear of the memories themselves — she
was certain that if she allowed herself to think about what had happened, she would lose control
entirely, come apart in a way she would not recover from.

It was this belief — that the world was dangerous and that she could not withstand her own mind —
that organized much of what her days became. She began checking the locks on every door and window
before she could sit down to work, and then checking them again an hour later, and again before
dark. She took to scanning every mirror in the house as she moved through rooms, needing to confirm
that no one was behind her. She worked hard to push the memories away the moment she sensed them
approaching, bracing against them as though they were physical intrusions. But the effort of
suppression left her exhausted and hypervigilant, and the memories broke through anyway — each
intrusion reinforcing her belief that she was not safe, that her body was working against her, that
the danger was ongoing. The checking and suppression, which had felt like reasonable precautions,
quietly confirmed to her that there was something real to be guarded against. The loop tightened:
the more she monitored, the more threatened she felt; the more threatened she felt, the more she
checked.

She no longer works in the studio after 4 p.m. She wakes multiple times each night to listen for
sounds at the doors or windows, lying still in the dark until she is satisfied — or until exhaustion
overtakes her. She cannot relax even in daylight, even with her daughter present. The pottery, which
had once been her anchor, now sits half-finished on the wheel.

What finally brought her in was a piece she had been working on for her granddaughter's birthday — a
bowl she could not complete because she could not remain in the studio long enough. She told the
intake clinician that she did not want to be afraid anymore. What she feared most, she said quietly,
was that this was simply who she was now.
