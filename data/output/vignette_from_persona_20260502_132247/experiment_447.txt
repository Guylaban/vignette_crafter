close to her ear, the echo of footsteps in the structure, and the sensation of her own heart
hammering so violently she feared she was dying. She screamed and fought, and he eventually ran. She
drove herself home in silence, and it was only when her husband opened the front door that she
collapsed.

In the weeks that followed, Linh found that the world she had navigated with confidence for seven
decades had been fundamentally rewritten. She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt almost
physical, that the world is a dangerous place — not abstractly, but concretely and personally.
Parking structures, underground lots, any enclosed space with hard surfaces and ambient echo became
territories of threat. More troubling still, she no longer trusted her own body. Her heart, which
had raced so violently during the assault that she had genuinely believed she was having a cardiac
attack, had become a source of dread rather than reassurance. Whenever she felt her pulse quicken —
climbing courthouse stairs, rushing between depositions — the sensation immediately transported her
back to the pillar, to the dark, to the weight of him. These appraisals, the sense that danger is
everywhere and that her body cannot be relied upon to distinguish real threat from memory, actively
sharpened her alertness to potential cues. She found herself scanning for sounds — the scrape of a
shoe on tile, an elevator's mechanical groan, any auditory texture that resembled the acoustics of
that night — and the moment such a sound registered, her threat system ignited fully, as though the
assault were still unfolding.

At work, where she had spent thirty-five years projecting calm authority, this had become quietly
devastating. During client meetings, if a male colleague or opposing counsel looked at her in a way
she read as appraising or sexual, she felt a wave of revulsion and alarm that she struggled to
contain behind her professional composure. She had begun requesting that her paralegal accompany her
to the parking structure and had twice rescheduled evening court appearances, citing scheduling
conflicts. Her colleagues had noticed something had shifted, though no one had asked directly. She
had not told them what happened.

The nights were their own ordeal. Linh had come to feel that darkness itself was a condition of
danger — that the hours between midnight and dawn were structurally unsafe in a way daylight was
not. She lay awake listening, and when her husband's breathing deepened into sleep she felt more
alone and more exposed, not less. If he was slow to answer a text when he was out in the evening,
she was immediately certain something terrible had occurred. The logic was not one she could argue
herself out of: the world had already proven it would harm her without warning, so any unexplained

never again feel safe inside her own body or her own life. What she is hoping for, more cautiously,
is that a version of herself who is not always waiting for the next attack might still be
recoverable.
