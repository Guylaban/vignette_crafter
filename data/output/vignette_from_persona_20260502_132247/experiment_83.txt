Rosario is a 61-year-old Salvadoran-American woman who has driven city buses for nearly two decades.
She is married, lives with her husband in a modest home in a mid-sized city, and until eighteen
months ago was regarded by her dispatcher as one of the most dependable drivers on the route. That
changed on a Tuesday morning in early winter when, stopped at a red light with a full bus, she
watched a sedan run the intersection at speed and strike a pedestrian — a young man who had just
stepped off her bus seconds earlier. The impact was immediate and catastrophic. Rosario saw the
collision through her windshield at close range: the sound, the arc of the body, the blood spreading
across the asphalt. She could not leave her passengers to go to him. By the time paramedics arrived,
he was dead. She has not been able to stop asking herself why she did not call out to him, why she
did not hold the doors a moment longer, why she simply sat there while he died in the street.

In the months that followed, the memory refused to recede. It does not arrive as a narrative she can
observe from a distance — when it comes, Rosario is back at that intersection, the windshield in
front of her, the sound filling her ears, the cold air on her hands on the wheel. She describes it
as completely real across every sense at once: the smell of the diesel exhaust, the particular grey
of the winter morning, the helplessness that floods her chest before she can name it as a feeling.
At other times there is no image at all, only a sudden wave of grief and terror so intense she must
grip a surface to stay upright, the emotional weight of the event arriving without any picture to
explain it. Because the memory carries no clear sense of pastness — it feels, as she puts it, like
it is still happening — Rosario has come to believe that she is not a person who survived something
terrible, but a person who is still inside it.

That belief has shaped everything. She has come to see herself as someone who failed
catastrophically: she should have protected that young man, and she did not. She is certain she will
never forgive herself. On her worst days she is not sure her life holds enough worth to continue, a
thought she has not shared with her husband but which sits with her in the early hours of the
morning. These appraisals, once established, have made the memory more accessible rather than less —
each time she condemns herself, the scene reasserts itself with fresh intensity, as though her guilt
keeps the intersection perpetually lit.

The triggers she encounters on her route are now unbearable. The sight of blood — even a minor
injury on a passenger — pulls her instantly back to the asphalt and the spreading red. A near-miss
in traffic, any moment in which she feels she cannot control what is about to happen, or the flash
of cars converging at an intersection, collapses the distance between then and now. Because the
triggers reliably return her to the memory, and the memory reliably returns her to the conviction
that she is guilty and irreparably damaged, Rosario has developed a set of private rituals she
performs before and during each shift: counting in a particular sequence, touching the wheel in a
fixed pattern, repeating a phrase under her breath — all aimed at preventing another catastrophe.
She understands, dimly, that these rituals do nothing, but their absence feels like an invitation to
disaster. She also avoids any form of physical contact with others, including her husband's hand on
her shoulder, because touch now feels like an intrusion into a body she no longer fully trusts. She
has quietly removed photographs of the young man from news coverage and avoids the street where he
was buried. These strategies bring momentary relief but sustain the architecture of her distress —
each ritual performed confirms that danger is real and imminent, each piece of grief avoided keeps
the loss unprocessed, and each act of avoidance returns her, sooner or later, to the image she is
trying to escape.

Over the past year Rosario has taken four medical leaves, her route coverage has become unreliable,
and her supervisor has gently but formally noted her performance. Her husband knows something is
deeply wrong but does not know what to do with her silence. What finally brought her to seek help
was a shift last month in which a minor fender-bender two cars ahead of her on the route triggered a
full reliving episode while she had passengers on board. She pulled over, shaking, and sat for
twenty minutes unable to move. What she most fears is that she will never be free of the
intersection. What she most hopes, though she says it quietly, is that someone might help her
believe it is finally over.
