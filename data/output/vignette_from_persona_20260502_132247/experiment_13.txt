Callum Reeves is a 32-year-old married man of British descent who works as a private tutor in the
suburbs of Portland, Oregon. He presented for assessment reporting a PCL-5 score of 59, describing a
constellation of symptoms that had been building steadily over the eighteen months since he was
stalked by a former tutoring client — a situation that began as unsettling and escalated, over
several months, into something that left him fundamentally altered in how he understood the world
and his place within it.

The stalking began gradually. A parent of one of Callum's students started appearing at locations he
hadn't mentioned — outside the coffee shop where he prepared lesson plans, in the parking lot of his
gym, eventually idling on his residential street on a motorcycle, the engine cutting out just as
Callum reached his front door. The man never made overt threats; he simply watched. When Callum
reported the behaviour, the response from authorities was slow and the legal process inconclusive.
The stalker's demeanour throughout — eerily calm, almost pleasant — meant that Callum began to doubt
his own perception of danger. By the time a restraining order was finally issued, the damage was
done. What frightened Callum most, in retrospect, was not any single incident but the realisation
that someone could study him, know his patterns, and present an ordinary face to the world while
doing so.

That realisation reshaped how Callum read other people entirely. He had come to believe that people
are not what they seem — that the surface presentation of a person reveals nothing reliable about
their intentions. When, in his tutoring sessions, a parent raises their voice even slightly, or
expresses frustration in what anyone else might consider a measured tone, Callum finds himself
scanning for threat: the tightened jaw, the shift in posture, the controlled anger that he now reads
as a precursor to something worse. These moments of perceived anger, however ordinary, pull him back
into the logic of the stalking — reinforcing his conviction that people cannot be trusted and that
he himself cannot reliably judge who is safe. The cycle feeds itself: the more his appraisals of
others darken, the more triggers he finds in ordinary social exchanges, and the more ordinary social
exchanges become potential triggers.

Callum works from home most days, and the suburban streets outside his window have become a
landscape of threat. The sound of a motorcycle engine — common enough in his neighbourhood —
reliably produces a jolt of alarm that he describes as indistinguishable from the feeling of being
watched. He avoids unfamiliar locations because, as he puts it, he cannot assess the danger when he
doesn't know the exits, the sightlines, the patterns of movement. This hypervigilance keeps him in a
state of chronic readiness that makes sleep nearly impossible; he lies awake cataloguing sounds, and
the exhaustion that follows further erodes his capacity to distinguish genuine threat from false
alarm. He frequently reacts as though he is in danger when nothing is actually wrong — a response he
recognises intellectually but cannot override.

The strategies Callum has adopted to manage this state have, over time, made it worse. He avoids
relaxing deliberately, having concluded that lowering his guard is what allowed the stalking to take
hold in the first place. This refusal to rest sustains a baseline of physiological arousal that
keeps him primed to detect threat in stimuli that would otherwise pass unnoticed. He has also pulled
back emotionally from his wife, Nadia, telling himself that closeness is a vulnerability he can no
longer afford — that people cannot be trusted, and this extends, in his darker moments, even to her.
Most troublingly, he has developed a pattern of punitive self-talk, telling himself that he deserves
to suffer for having failed to recognise the danger sooner. This belief — that his own judgment is
fundamentally untrustworthy — compounds his distrust of others and leaves him caught between
suspicion of the world and contempt for himself.

What finally brought Callum to seek help was a session with a fifteen-year-old student whose father
arrived early and waited in the hallway. Callum heard footsteps pacing and spent the remainder of
the hour unable to teach. He cancelled the following week's sessions and told Nadia he thought he
was losing his mind. He came to the intake appointment hoping, cautiously, that something could be
recovered — though he was not certain he deserved it.
