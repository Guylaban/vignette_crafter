Valentina is a 21-year-old Salvadoran-American animator, currently single, who presented for her
initial intake reporting a PCL-5 score of 39. She grew up in a household where her father's violence
toward her mother was a recurring feature of her childhood — not rare or exceptional, but woven into
the ordinary texture of evenings and weekends. What she remembers most viscerally is not any single
incident but a composite that her mind has fused into something almost cinematic: the sound of
something heavy striking a wall, the particular smell of her father's cologne mixed with cigarette
smoke, and the sight of a framed family photograph knocked from the shelf and left face-down on the
floor. No one was ever held responsible. Her father left when she was twelve and, as far as
Valentina knows, has faced no legal or social consequence for what he did. She has come to believe,
with a quiet and absolute certainty, that the people who cause harm simply do not pay for it.

Valentina works as a junior animator at a small studio, and it is there that the past most reliably
finds her. Her workspace is visually dense — reference photos pinned to corkboards, stills from
productions tacked above monitors — and on more than one occasion a colleague's photograph of a
domestic interior, or a still featuring a certain kind of lighting, has stopped her cold. When such
images appear, the memory does not arrive gradually; it surfaces with a vividness she describes as
more real than anything she actually lived yesterday, accompanied sometimes by the ghost of that
cologne, a sensory intrusion that feels to her like evidence that the past is not past. These
triggers pull the memory forward with such force that she finds herself scanning the studio for
exits, her body already on alert before her mind has caught up. Unexpected movement in her
peripheral vision — a colleague reaching past her, a door swinging open — produces a physical
startle response she cannot suppress, and she has grown chronically watchful in a way that exhausts
her. She is frequently irritable at work, and there have been two incidents of sharp,
disproportionate anger at colleagues, one of which her supervisor noted in a performance review.

Because the memory arrives with such intensity, Valentina has developed the habit of pushing it away
before it can complete itself — she describes a kind of internal flinching, a refusal to let the
scene finish. But this suppression, rather than emptying the memory, seems to keep it pressurised,
so that it breaks through again more readily the next time a trigger appears. The hypervigilance
that the triggers produce also keeps her in a state of low-grade arousal that makes the memory more
accessible, not less — she is always, in some sense, already listening for the sound of something
thrown.

Her avoidance has taken on several forms over the past year. She avoids anything that reminds her of
her father — certain names, certain physical types, certain music — and she has stopped making plans
for the future, as though committing to a future state would be a form of hubris the world would
punish. She controls her eating strictly, measuring portions and refusing spontaneity around food;
she experiences this as the one domain in which she holds genuine authority, though the rigidity of
it has begun to isolate her socially, making shared meals with friends difficult. These strategies,
she knows on some level, are not working: the avoidance keeps the memory unprocessed and raw, so
that when something slips through her defences, the response is all the more acute, feeding back
into the hypervigilance she is trying to manage.

What frightens Valentina most is her own sense of fragility — she is convinced she cannot manage
even minor stressors, that the slightest disruption will undo her. She is haunted by the feeling
that she is helpless against whatever comes next. What finally brought her to seek help was the
performance review, which she experienced not as a professional setback but as confirmation of
everything she already feared about herself. What she is most hoping for, she said quietly at the
end of the intake, is to feel like the past is somewhere she has actually left.
