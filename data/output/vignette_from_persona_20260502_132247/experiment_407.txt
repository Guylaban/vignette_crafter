Marcus is a 47-year-old single man who works as a city bus driver. He presents with a PCL-5 score of
48, describing a cluster of symptoms that have progressively narrowed his life over the past several
months following a traumatic encounter with police.

The incident occurred on an evening when Marcus was stopped while driving home from a late shift.
What began as a routine traffic stop escalated rapidly: officers drew their weapons, ordered him to
the ground, and held him face-down on the asphalt for an extended period before determining they had
the wrong vehicle. He was not physically injured, but the experience of lying prone, unable to see
what was happening, surrounded by shouting voices and the cold weight of a knee on his back, left a
mark that has not faded. The specific texture of that helplessness — the inability to see faces, the
sounds of authority closing in — would become the fault lines along which his symptoms later cracked
open.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus found that the memory did not stay where he tried to put it. It
came back suddenly and without invitation, most often when he was at work. Driving his route through
the city, he would turn a corner and see a patrol car idling at a stop, and the sight of a uniformed
officer was enough to pull him back to the asphalt. The memory arrived not as a recollection but as
a re-immersion — the shouting, the cold ground, the disorientation. When these intrusions struck,
Marcus would instinctively cover his face with both hands, a gesture that reproduced the posture of
the original event and briefly blocked out the world around him. He also began turning up the radio
in the cab to a volume that passengers occasionally complained about, filling his head with noise as
a way of drowning out the internal replay. Talking about the event with anyone — a dispatcher who
asked what was wrong, a friend who noticed the change in him — produced the same flooding, and so he
stopped doing that too.

Marcus had come to believe, with a certainty that felt like knowledge rather than fear, that the
world was a fundamentally dangerous place and that people in positions of authority could not be
trusted. This conviction did not remain abstract. It coloured how he scanned his environment: at
home, he checked his door and window locks repeatedly before bed, sometimes three or four times, and
still lay awake irritable and unable to settle his attention on anything. At work, the
hypervigilance made sustained concentration difficult, and he had received a formal note from his
supervisor about missing a scheduled stop. The belief that people could not be trusted had also
driven him to withdraw from the informal community of fellow drivers — the break-room conversations,
the shared meals — so that what had once been a source of belonging now felt like a space full of
potential threat. His sense of disconnection from his community and culture had deepened as a
result, which in turn reinforced his conviction that the world offered no safe harbour, keeping him
primed and watchful in a way that made the next trigger easier to reach.

The strategies Marcus used to manage his distress had quietly made things worse. The face-hiding and
the loud music offered momentary relief but prevented him from processing what had happened, leaving
the memory raw and close to the surface. His withdrawal from other people meant there was no one to
challenge his growing certainty that the world was dangerous and that trust was a liability, beliefs
that in turn kept his nervous system in a state of readiness that made ordinary stimuli feel
threatening. That state of threat, once activated, made the intrusive memories more accessible, so
that each patrol car or mention of the incident triggered the full weight of the original experience
again. The cycle had become self-sustaining in a way Marcus could feel but could not name.

What finally brought him to seek help was the supervisor's formal note and the quiet recognition
that he was losing the job — and with it the last structured thing in his life. He arrived at the
intake appointment guarded and slow to make eye contact. What he said he wanted most was to stop
feeling like he was still on that road.
