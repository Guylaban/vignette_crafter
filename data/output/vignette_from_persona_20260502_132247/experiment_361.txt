Rodrigo is a 62-year-old Latin American psychiatrist who presents for an initial evaluation carrying
a history he has spent decades attempting to outrun professionally and personally. He is single,
lives alone in a mid-sized American city, and describes his current functioning as "barely
adequate." His PCL-5 score of 47 places him in the moderate-to-severe range, and what emerges across
the first sessions is a presentation shaped not only by what was done to him, but by the
institutional silence that followed.

Approximately thirty years ago, Rodrigo was detained without charge by state security forces in his
country of origin. For several weeks he was held in a facility where interrogators spoke in
overlapping, deliberately disorienting commands — voices layered over voices, questions answered
with questions, language used as a weapon of confusion rather than communication. He was subjected
to systematic physical and psychological torture. The men who held him wore uniforms. When he was
eventually released, no charges were ever filed, no investigation was opened, and the officers
responsible continued in their posts. He emigrated to the United States within a year, completed his
psychiatric training here, and built what appeared from the outside to be a successful career. He
did not seek treatment.

The memories do not arrive as recollections. When something in his environment triggers them, the
full experience returns across every sense simultaneously — the smell of a damp concrete room, the
pressure of restraints, the layered unintelligible voices — and it does not feel like remembering.
It feels like being there. Some moments of the detention are rendered in excruciating clarity;
others are entirely blank, as though those hours were excised. The fragmented, non-linear quality of
the memory means that certain triggers bypass any cognitive preparation and deliver him back without
warning. A news broadcast covering political detentions in Latin America, heard while he is
reviewing patient notes in his office, has done exactly this — the screen's imagery and the
reporter's cadence sufficient to collapse the distance between then and now. The news, in this way,
does not merely distress him; it reinstates the trauma as present-tense reality.

The appraisals Rodrigo carries are not abstract. He is certain — not afraid, but certain — that the
institutions that should have protected him failed him deliberately, and that the men responsible
have lived their lives without consequence. This conviction is not merely a painful belief; it
functions as a lens through which triggers are interpreted. When he encounters a person in uniform —
a security guard at the hospital entrance, a police officer on the street — the sight does not only
recall the past. It confirms, in that moment, that the conditions of his harm remain unremedied and
that authority is still dangerous. The appraisal of institutional failure and absent accountability
sharpens the meaning of each trigger, so that what might otherwise be a neutral encounter becomes
evidence of ongoing threat, and the full memory presses forward again.

Over the years, Rodrigo has built an elaborate system of management that he has mistaken for coping.
He avoids going out wherever possible, scheduling his clinical work to minimize transit and public
exposure. Before any interaction — with colleagues, with patients, with a pharmacist — he rehearses
what he will say, running through likely conversational branches to ensure he will not be caught off
guard by incomprehensible or overlapping speech. When intrusive content begins to surface, he
redirects his attention forcibly to something neutral. Each of these strategies, however, has
quietly maintained the problem it was designed to solve. By rehearsing conversations obsessively, he
never tests whether unscripted speech is actually dangerous, and the anticipatory anxiety remains
intact. By avoiding public space, he has not been able to disconfirm the threat posed by uniforms or
crowds, and the triggers remain fully potent. The redirection prevents the memory from being
processed and leaves it structurally unchanged, ready to return. His avoidance has also cost him
concretely: colleagues have noted his withdrawal, he has declined committee work and conferences,
and he has not attended a professional gathering in four years — a man who trained others in trauma
care, now unable to sit in a room where he cannot control the exits. The isolation has deepened his
sense of having lost his connection to his culture and community, and that loss in turn feeds the
appraisal that something irreplaceable was taken from him by people who will never be named.

Rodrigo sought help after a junior resident, during a case presentation, played an audio recording
of a patient's disorganized speech. The overlapping, incomprehensible chatter filled the seminar
room and Rodrigo left without explanation. He made the referral to himself the same evening. What he
is most afraid of is that treatment will require him to say aloud what was done to him. What he most
hopes is that it will not always feel this close.
