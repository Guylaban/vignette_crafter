Mateo Delgado is a 22-year-old Colombian-American man who works as a junior product manager at a
mid-sized tech firm in Austin, Texas. He came to the clinic after months of escalating distress that
had begun to affect his performance at work and his relationships with colleagues. His presentation
centres on a period he spent as a civilian in a conflict zone in his early twenties — an experience
that has left its mark on how his body and mind interpret the world around him.

Approximately eighteen months ago, Mateo was visiting extended family in a rural region of Colombia
when the area came under sustained armed conflict. For several days, he and his relatives sheltered
in a concrete outbuilding while gunfire and explosions sounded at unpredictable intervals, sometimes
close enough to shake the walls. He described lying flat on the ground for hours, certain that any
movement or sound might draw attention to their location. At one point, a door burst open without
warning — a cousin, not a combatant — and Mateo's body launched itself across the room before he had
any conscious thought. That involuntary lurch, he said, was the moment he understood that something
inside him had changed.

In the weeks after returning to the United States, Mateo found that his body had not registered the
safety of Austin in the way he expected. He described a persistent physical tension that never fully
released — muscles braced, jaw clenched, a low-level readiness that he could not switch off. In
open-plan office environments, where colleagues moved quickly between desks and chairs scraped
unexpectedly across floors, he would startle violently, knocking over his coffee or spinning around
from his monitor. He began scanning the room compulsively during meetings, tracking movement near
the glass walls of the conference room, unable to follow the product roadmap discussions he was
supposed to be leading. He had come to believe that if he stopped watching, something would happen
before he could react — a conviction that felt as certain and physical as gravity.

It was this relentless state of alert that first pushed Mateo toward behaviours that he now
recognises as harmful. When the tension became unbearable — usually late in the evening after a day
of hypervigilance at the office — he began hurting himself physically, pressing his nails hard into
his forearm or snapping a rubber band against his wrist with force. He described these moments with
a mixture of shame and relief: the sharp, localised pain seemed to interrupt the diffuse,
uncontrollable alarm in his body and briefly return him to something he could locate and name. He
also began texting friends and his older sister at odd hours, asking whether he seemed okay, whether
they thought he was handling things normally, whether he was worrying too much — a pattern of
reassurance-seeking that had strained several friendships. And he had developed a habit of checking
his body throughout the day, pressing his fingertips to his chest to feel his heartbeat, scanning
his arms for signs of injury, running his hands along his sides during bathroom breaks as if
confirming he was intact.

The difficulty was that none of these strategies brought lasting relief. The self-harm, the
reassurance, the body checking — each provided a momentary reduction in distress, but in the hours
that followed, Mateo found himself more attuned to threat than before, as though the act of seeking
relief had confirmed to some part of him that there was something real to be relieved from. His body
would tighten again, his vigilance would sharpen, and the cycle would close around him. Colleagues
had begun to notice that he seemed distracted and on edge, and his manager had gently raised
concerns about his focus during a recent one-on-one — a conversation that Mateo found humiliating
and that intensified his monitoring of his own reactions throughout the workday.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment in a team meeting when an unexpected knock on the
glass partition caused him to stand up from his chair involuntarily, drawing stares from the entire
room. He sat back down and said nothing, but that evening he called a crisis line and asked for a
referral. What he most fears, he said quietly in the intake session, is that his body will never
stop waiting for something terrible to happen. What he most hopes is that someone can help him feel
safe in his own skin again.
