Kofi Mensah is a 46-year-old African American man who works as a curriculum designer for a mid-sized
urban school district. He is married, describes his relationship as generally supportive, and came
to the initial intake presenting with a PCL-5 score of 69 — a level of symptom severity that his
wife had, by his account, been urging him to address for months before he finally agreed to make the
call.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Kofi was driving home from a late evening at the district office
when he was pulled over on a highway interchange he uses almost every workday. What began as a
routine traffic stop escalated rapidly. Within seconds of rolling down his window, he found himself
facing drawn weapons, shouted commands that contradicted one another, and officers who, he felt
certain, were not seeing a curriculum designer returning from work — they were seeing something else
entirely. He was ordered out of the vehicle, forced to the ground, and held at gunpoint for what he
later learned was nearly twenty minutes before a supervisor arrived and the situation was stood
down. No charges were filed. No explanation was given. He drove the rest of the way home in silence,
hands trembling on the wheel, and did not speak about it for three weeks.

In the months that followed, Kofi's body began registering danger before his mind had time to catch
up. The hypervigilance that settled into his daily life was not abstract; it was architectural. He
began scanning every room he entered, noting exits, watching hands. At the district office, where he
spends most of his working hours moving between open-plan spaces and conference rooms, he
repositioned himself habitually so that no one could approach him from behind. The sound of a door
opening sharply, a colleague raising their voice down the hall, a phone buzzing unexpectedly on a
hard surface — any of these could produce a startle response so pronounced that coworkers had begun
to notice and comment. When something in the environment reminded him of the stop — the flash of
blue and red light from a passing emergency vehicle visible through the office windows, the crackle
of a radio, even the particular quality of highway noise that drifted in during evening meetings —
his body flooded with physical reactivity: his chest tightened, his breathing became shallow, his
hands went cold. These moments of heightened alertness and physical alarm did not resolve cleanly.
Instead, they opened a door.

What came through that door was not, in any straightforward sense, a memory. This is what Kofi found
most disorienting and most difficult to describe during intake. When the threat response was
activated — when his body was already on high alert — something returned that he could not quite
name. He had come to believe that the event had simply not lodged in his mind the way other memories
did, because he could not seem to put it into words. When he tried to recall what had happened on
that interchange, he hit a wall of emotion so overwhelming that he had to stop — mid-sentence in
session, mid-thought at his desk. What arrived was not an image, not a sequence, not a narrative he
could follow to its end. It was feeling without picture: the absolute certainty of imminent harm,
the specific quality of powerlessness, the cold of the pavement — present in his body, untethered
from any clear scene. The state of alarm that his hypervigilance and physical reactivity produced
seemed to call this emotional residue forward, so that the threat he was monitoring for in the
present collapsed into the threat he had not yet fully processed from the past.

By the time Kofi sought help, he had quietly stopped taking the highway interchange on his commute,
adding twenty-five minutes to a journey he makes five days a week. He had not told his employer why
his productivity had dipped, and he had not told his wife the full account of what happened that
night. What finally brought him in was a moment at work when a colleague touched his shoulder from
behind and he reacted so sharply that he frightened her. He was most afraid, he said, that he would
never be able to simply remember what had happened — that it would always arrive this way, as
sensation without story, as a feeling his body knew and his mind could not hold.
