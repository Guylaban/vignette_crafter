Priya is a 32-year-old South Asian woman who works as a chaplain at a mid-sized urban hospital. She
is divorced and currently lives alone in a small apartment not far from the medical campus. She came
to the intake appointment wearing a long-sleeved cardigan despite the warmth of the day, and she
spoke quietly, choosing her words with visible deliberateness — the way a person does when they have
learned that speaking too quickly leads somewhere they cannot come back from.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Priya was detained by uniformed security forces while travelling
abroad to provide humanitarian chaplaincy services in a region experiencing civil unrest. What
followed was a period of several days during which she was held in a facility she could not
identify, subjected to prolonged physical restraint and interrogation, and exposed to sounds she has
since been unable to name in any clinical or conversational context. The men who held her wore
military-style uniforms. Her wrists were bound. There were sounds — metallic, rhythmic, close — that
accompanied the worst of what happened to her.

When Priya returned to the United States and eventually back to her work at the hospital, the world
she re-entered looked superficially unchanged, but she found that the trauma had restructured her
relationship with ordinary experience in ways she could not fully articulate. The core difficulty
with her memory is not one of forgetting — she knows, with terrible clarity, that something happened
— but of language itself collapsing when she tries to approach it. She can begin a sentence and then
lose the thread entirely, watching the words dissolve before she can place them in any order that
might convey meaning. In sessions she has described it as reaching for something solid and finding
only water. This fragmented, essentially nonverbal quality of the memory means that the trauma has
never been processed as a coherent narrative, and it is precisely this disorganisation that keeps
the sensory residue raw and immediately accessible. Because the memory cannot be contextualised or
linguistically contained, it bleeds easily into the present: the metallic clang of a hospital gurney
being wheeled down a corridor, a security officer's uniform glimpsed through a glass partition, the
brief physical pressure of a colleague's hand on her arm — each of these is enough to collapse the
distance between then and now. The triggers are not reminders so much as re-entries.

Over the months following her return, Priya developed a set of strategies she understood, on some
level, as protective. She began avoiding the hospital's outdoor courtyards and the open plaza near
the main entrance, spaces where she felt exposed and unable to predict who might be present. She
withdrew from the small group of colleagues and friends she had socialised with before her
detention, cancelling plans until the invitations stopped coming; she had come to believe that being
seen by people who knew her before would require an explanation she could not give, and the
anticipated failure of that explanation felt more unbearable than the loneliness. On several
occasions, when intrusive material became overwhelming and words continued to fail her, she hurt
herself — small, controlled acts of physical pain that she described as the only thing that reliably
interrupted the flood. She was not proud of this, but she was clear that it worked, at least
briefly. What she did not yet understand was the cost: the physical self-harm, like the social
withdrawal and the avoidance of open spaces, prevented her from ever sitting with the memory long
enough to begin organising it, and so the memory remained as fragmented and unprocessable as ever,
sustaining the very sensitivity that made the triggers so destabilising.

The loop had been tightening for over a year before Priya sought help. What finally brought her in
was an incident at work: a patient's family member, a man in a security uniform, placed a steadying
hand on her forearm in a hallway, and she found herself unable to speak or move for nearly a minute.
A colleague witnessed it. For Priya, whose professional identity is built on being present for
others at their most vulnerable, this rupture was intolerable. She came to the intake appointment
hoping, above everything else, to find words — not necessarily to speak them aloud, but simply to
possess them. What she feared most was that she never would.
