Mateo Reyes is a twenty-four-year-old Colombian-American man who works as a financial analyst at a
mid-sized investment firm in downtown Chicago. He was referred for psychological assessment
following a brief disclosure to his primary care physician, though he arrived at the intake
appointment guarded and visibly reluctant, as though some part of him had not yet decided whether to
stay.

Mateo served two years in the Army before his honorable discharge at twenty-two, including a seven-
month deployment to a forward operating base in eastern Afghanistan. The event that appears to have
fractured his sense of self occurred during a patrol in which his unit came under sustained ambush
fire in an open, flat stretch of terrain — an exposed hillside with nowhere to take cover. In the
chaos that followed, Mateo made a series of rapid decisions under fire that resulted in the death of
a fellow soldier, a close friend he refers to only as "D." The precise sequence of what happened
remains, by Mateo's own account, unclear to him — he cannot reconstruct it in order, cannot say with
certainty what came first or what he chose versus what simply happened. What he does know, or
believes he knows, is that he survived and D. did not, and that something he did or failed to do was
the reason.

His memories of that afternoon are simultaneously vivid and incoherent. He can describe the color of
the light, the sound of the rifle fire, the specific texture of the dirt, with a clarity that
disturbs him — and yet the sequence collapses whenever he tries to hold it. Fragments arrive without
warning: a muzzle flash, D.'s voice, the weight of his own body against the ground. These sudden
intrusive pieces carry with them an immediacy that makes the past feel present, and it is this
quality — the way the memory refuses to stay fixed in time — that has deepened his conviction that
he is fundamentally changed. The confusion in the memory feeds directly into his appraisals of what
happened: because he cannot reconstruct a coherent account, he fills the gaps with the worst
available interpretation, which is that he failed, that he chose wrong, that the man who came back
from that hillside is not someone worth knowing.

Those appraisals have become the architecture of his daily life. Mateo has come to believe, with a
quiet certainty that he experiences as simple fact rather than distortion, that he has no future
worth imagining — that whatever trajectory his life might have had was closed off on that hillside.
He feels that he acted in ways that contradict everything he understood himself to be, and this
sense of violated identity has left him feeling permanently outside the world other people inhabit.
At the office, surrounded by colleagues discussing markets and weekend plans, he watches them as
though through glass. He is present in body but describes himself as unreachable, a permanent
outsider in his own life. These beliefs, in turn, have driven the strategies he has constructed to
manage his days: he avoids any conversation about his deployment or his time in the Army, deflecting
questions with practiced brevity; he avoids anything that might evoke grief — he has removed
photographs, declined a memorial gathering for D.'s family, and stopped listening to music they
shared. He also avoids open spaces wherever possible, taking interior routes through the city,
choosing enclosed cafés over outdoor seating, something his colleagues have noticed and quietly
stopped questioning.

The maintaining cycle has grown tighter over the past two years. The fragmented, vivid memories
surface without warning and reinforce his belief that he is broken and culpable, and those beliefs
make it intolerable to sit with the memories long enough to process them, so he turns away — from
the grief, from conversation, from exposure — which ensures the memories remain unintegrated and the
appraisals unchallenged. His social world has narrowed considerably; he has no close friendships in
Chicago, and his family, who know only that he "had a hard deployment," sense a distance they cannot
name.

What finally brought Mateo to seek help was a moment at work: standing at a floor-to-ceiling window
on the thirty-eighth floor, looking out over an open expanse of lake and sky, he experienced a
fragment so vivid he gripped the window frame to confirm he was still in the building. He told his
doctor the following day. What he is most afraid of, he said quietly at intake, is that talking
about it will confirm what he already suspects — that what happened cannot be explained in any way
that leaves him as someone other than the man who let D. die.
