Kofi Mensah is a fifty-year-old Ghanaian-American plumber, married for twenty-two years, who was
referred for psychological assessment following a violent assault that occurred approximately eight
months ago. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 73, indicating severe posttraumatic symptomatology,
and describes a clinical picture that has progressively narrowed his world to the point where his
wife has issued what she calls a quiet ultimatum: get help, or she does not know how much longer she
can watch him disappear.

The assault took place on an evening when Kofi had stayed late to finish a commercial job in an
unfamiliar part of the city. As he was loading tools into his van in a poorly lit parking structure,
two men approached from his peripheral vision and attacked him without warning. One struck him from
the side before he could register the movement, and what followed was a sustained and brutal beating
during which Kofi was unable to defend himself or call for help. He lay on the concrete for some
time before a passerby found him. He sustained a fractured orbital socket, cracked ribs, and a
concussion. No arrests were ever made.

What Kofi carries from that night is not a story he can tell so much as a set of images he cannot
stop seeing — the angle of the fluorescent light above him, the texture of the concrete against his
cheek, the shadows of the men's legs. He has found, to his distress, that he cannot seem to put the
event into words in any coherent way. When asked to describe what happened, he goes quiet and his
eyes move as though watching something play out just above the interviewer's shoulder. The memories
come as pictures, vivid and unbidden, and with them arrives an overwhelming sense of guilt — a
conviction, not entirely rational but deeply felt, that he should have done something, that a man of
his experience and physicality should not have ended up on that floor. This guilt, bound tightly to
the images themselves, has come to colour how he understands the attack: not as something that was
done to him, but as evidence of something already lacking in him. These memory intrusions and the
guilt they carry feed directly into a state of near-constant vigilance, as though his nervous system
has concluded that the world that produced that night is still present and watching. Any unexpected
movement nearby — a colleague rounding a corner on a job site, a customer's dog darting across a
hallway — produces a full-body startle response that embarrasses and frightens him in equal measure.

Kofi has come to believe that he is no longer the man he was. For over two decades he has taken
pride in being the kind of tradesman who could work anywhere, in any conditions, alone or alongside
strangers. That identity has been dismantled. He now feels safe only in a very limited number of
places — primarily his own home, and occasionally his brother-in-law's house nearby. He will not
take jobs that require working alone in enclosed or unfamiliar spaces, which in his trade is nearly
every job. His employer has noticed. Kofi is aware that this is costing him professionally and
financially, but what frightens him most is the conviction that he cannot trust himself to do the
right thing if something were to happen again — that some essential competence or courage has been
permanently removed. This appraisal of himself as diminished and unreliable sustains the
hypervigilance rather than easing it, because a man who cannot trust himself must be watched over by
himself constantly. The threat feels everywhere because he has decided, somewhere beneath conscious
reflection, that the protection he once assumed he could provide is gone.

The hypervigilance has its own cost in the night hours. Kofi sleeps poorly, waking at sounds or
movements, and arrives at each morning already exhausted. In that state, the intrusive images return
more easily, and the cycle — memory sharpening threat, threat dragging memory back — has become the
texture of his daily life. He has not spoken to his wife about the guilt. What finally brought him
to the clinic was her telling him, gently but plainly, that she missed him. What he is most afraid
of is that she is right — that the person she misses no longer exists.
