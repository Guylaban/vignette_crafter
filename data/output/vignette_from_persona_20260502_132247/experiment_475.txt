Kiet Phommasack is a thirty-four-year-old Lao-American chaplain, widowed two years ago after the
illness and death of his wife, who came to clinical attention following a street robbery that
occurred approximately seven months before his intake appointment. He presented with a PCL-5 score
of 77, indicating severe posttraumatic stress, and described a presentation that had quietly
dismantled much of the spiritual equanimity he had spent years cultivating.

The robbery took place on a Tuesday evening as Kiet was walking back from a hospital visit to a
parishioner. A man approached him rapidly from behind on a narrow sidewalk, pressed close enough
that Kiet could hear the ragged, laboured sound of his breathing — the hot, irregular rhythm of it —
before demanding his wallet and phone. A second man appeared from a doorway to Kiet's left, boxing
him in. The physical crowding was total and suffocating; Kiet recalls the smell of cigarette smoke
and something chemical, like cleaning solvent, mixing with the night air. He handed over what he
had. The men were gone within seconds. He stood alone on the pavement for a long time before he
could move.

In the months that followed, Kiet found that certain things returned him to that sidewalk with an
immediacy that felt less like remembering and more like re-entering. The memories themselves arrived
primarily as images — the dark shape of the man's arm, the angle of the doorway, the particular
quality of the streetlight — vivid and arrested, like photographs he could not put down. Alongside
the visual fragments came smell: that specific compound of smoke and solvent would surface without
warning, and with it the whole scene reconstituted itself. When these memories broke through, they
were not accompanied by fear alone. What Kiet reported most forcefully was rage — an intense,
disorienting fury that felt alien to him given his vocation and that he had no framework for
managing.

These sensory memories were reliably ignited by a specific cluster of situations. Crowded places —
the hospital corridors where Kiet worked, the packed waiting rooms he moved through daily as part of
his pastoral rounds — compressed him in ways that made the images surge forward. The sound of heavy
breathing, whether from an elderly patient, a crying family member, or simply someone exerting
themselves nearby, functioned as an almost mechanical key, unlocking the auditory texture of that
night and flooding him with the images and the rage that accompanied them. Even encountering people
he associated with the event — a colleague who had been on the ward that evening, the neighbourhood
near the hospital — was sufficient to pull the memories to the surface. In this way, the triggers
did not merely remind Kiet of what had happened; they reinstated it.

What made the clinical picture more complex, and more painful, was what Kiet had concluded about
himself in the wake of the robbery. He had come to believe, with a quiet but corrosive certainty,
that the event had happened because of the kind of person he was — that something in his character,
his carelessness, his passivity, had made him a target. He was certain that another man, someone
more capable or more alert, would simply not have found himself in that situation. Most damaging of
all was his conviction that he was constitutionally unable to prevent bad things from happening to
him — that his wife's death and now this were not coincidences but evidence of a pattern rooted in
who he was. These appraisals fed directly back into the memories, colouring them with shame and
self-contempt that deepened the rage he felt on recall. They also amplified his sensitivity to the
triggers themselves: because he understood crowded spaces and certain people as environments in
which his inadequacy would again be exposed, his vigilance in those settings was absolute, and the
threshold for memory intrusion dropped accordingly.

What finally brought Kiet to seek help was a pastoral visit in which a patient's laboured breathing
caused him to leave the room abruptly and stand in the corridor, shaking, for several minutes —
unable to return, unable to explain himself. He had begun to fear he could no longer do his work.
What he hoped for, he said carefully, was not to stop remembering, but to stop feeling that the
memory proved something true about him.
