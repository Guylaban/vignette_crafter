Tavita Faleolo is a 53-year-old Samoan-American man who has worked as a commercial airline pilot for
nearly two decades. He lives with his wife in a suburb of Seattle and, until recently, was regarded
by his colleagues as one of the most composed and technically precise captains in his fleet. He

that he describes as "the moment I understood I couldn't keep pretending."

Approximately fourteen months ago, Tavita was detained during a layover in a politically unstable
country. What began as a bureaucratic misunderstanding at a checkpoint escalated over several hours
into something he still cannot fully articulate. He was held in a small concrete room, the door
locked from the outside, the silence broken only by the sounds of other men in adjacent rooms — men
who cried out, who wept, who eventually went quiet. His captors said very little. They did not need
to. The threat was communicated through proximity, through the smell of sweat and iron and something
chemical he has never been able to name, through the faces of the men who watched him without
expression. He was released after two days without formal charge or explanation. He flew home. He
told his wife he was fine.

He was not fine. In the months that followed, the memories returned not as narrative but as
sensation — the smell would arrive first, unbidden, and suddenly he was back in that room, the image
so precise and static it felt less like recollection than like being handed a photograph he could
not put down. When the memory returned in this way, the emotional force of it was so overwhelming
that he would have to stop whatever he was doing — pause mid-sentence, pull over if driving, step
away from the cockpit door — simply to survive the wave of it. And critically, the memory did not
stay contained to the past. Once it surfaced, his body read the present moment as continuous with
that room, and he would find himself scanning the faces of passengers boarding the aircraft,
watching for something he could not name but was certain was there.

The triggers that opened this door were specific and, to Tavita, humiliatingly ordinary. Crowded
boarding gates — the press of bodies, the noise collapsing into a kind of sensory wall — would bring
the memory flooding back, and with it an immediate, visceral sense that something dangerous was
about to happen. Unusual silence was, paradoxically, equally destabilising; the quiet of an empty
jetway or a hotel corridor at night carried the same texture as the silence between the sounds he
had heard through that concrete wall, and his body responded accordingly. Most distressing of all
was witnessing people misbehave — a passenger shouting at a gate agent, a man pushing past a woman
in a queue — because something about the casual exercise of cruelty confirmed what he had come to
believe: that people were capable of anything, that the men who had watched him without expression
were not aberrations but a truth about human nature he had been naive enough to miss.

These beliefs had reshaped how he moved through the world. He checked the locks on hotel room doors
repeatedly before he could sleep. He sat with his back to walls in restaurants. He watched the
people around him with a vigilance that exhausted him and that, in turn, made crowded spaces feel
more threatening still — because the more closely he watched, the more evidence of danger he seemed
to find, and the more his body primed itself for the memory to return. The loop had become self-
sustaining in a way he had not noticed until it had cost him something concrete: three months ago,
during a transatlantic flight, he had been so destabilised by an altercation between passengers in
the cabin that his first officer had quietly asked if he was all right. He had said yes. But the
question had not left him.

What finally brought Tavita to seek help was not a single crisis but an accumulation — the
recognition that he was no longer the person his wife had married, that he was managing rather than
living, and that the cockpit, once the place where he felt most himself, had become another room
with a locked door.
