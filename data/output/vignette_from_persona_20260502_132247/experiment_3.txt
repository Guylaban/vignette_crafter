Kwame is a 55-year-old married African American man who has spent the better part of three decades
as a bomb disposal technician — a career built on the premise that chaos can be controlled, that
danger, if approached with enough discipline and precision, can be made predictable. It was this
belief that made the events of eighteen months ago so particularly devastating. Kwame was visiting
extended family in coastal West Africa when a catastrophic tropical cyclone made landfall with
almost no warning. He had been standing in the family compound when the sky collapsed. The roar was
unlike anything he had encountered in his professional life — not the contained, purposeful
detonation he could prepare for, but an all-consuming, directionless wall of sound that arrived
before anyone could think. Structures gave way around him. People were screaming. The world became
unpredictable in a way his training had never prepared him for, and he survived largely by chance,
sheltering in a concrete stairwell while the storm took the roof from the building above him.

In the months that followed his return to the United States, Kwame began noticing that the disaster
had left something behind inside him. The memory of the storm did not present itself as a coherent
narrative. Instead, it came in fragments — the sound of the wind, a face, the sensation of the floor
vibrating — and when he tried to hold these pieces together long enough to make sense of them,
something in him pushed the images away before they could fully form. He was left with an incomplete
picture, a patchwork of impressions from which whole sections seemed to be missing, and when
fragments did surface, they brought with them not grief or fear but an intense, almost blinding rage
that he could not fully explain and that frightened him nearly as much as the memory itself.

These incomplete, rage-laden memory fragments did not stay contained. They began feeding back into
his daily vigilance, so that the gaps in his recollection made the threat feel perpetually
unresolved — as though the danger had never fully ended because he could never fully account for
what had happened. This heightened sense of unresolved threat, in turn, made him hyperattentive to
anything that resembled the conditions of the storm. Unpredictable situations — a schedule change at
work, an unexpected knock at the door — became charged with menace. Loud noises were the worst. On
the job, where controlled detonations and heavy equipment were routine, Kwame found himself reacting
before conscious thought could intervene: his body would flood with adrenaline, his hands would
shake, and he would be, for a moment, back in that stairwell. Shouting from a colleague across a
worksite could trigger the same cascade. He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt
physiological rather than rational, that he was almost always in danger, that his body knew
something his mind was still catching up to.

To manage these states, Kwame developed a set of strategies that made immediate sense but
accumulated costs over time. He began avoiding anything that carried even a mild possibility of
stress — declining call-outs that involved confined spaces, steering around conflict at home,
rerouting his commute to avoid construction noise. He kept his mind occupied constantly, filling
silence with television, podcasts, anything that left no room for the fragments to surface. And he
began eating — large meals, late-night eating, food as sedation — in a pattern his wife had begun to
name with quiet worry. These strategies, however, had the effect of narrowing his world without
quieting it. The avoidance meant he never habituated to the triggers; each time he sidestepped a
loud noise or an unpredictable situation, the threat signal was confirmed rather than corrected. The
constant mental distraction kept him agitated and exhausted, which lowered his threshold for alarm,
so that even minor stimuli broke through and sent his body into a state of emergency before he had
time to think. The loop tightened. His threat system, never given the chance to recalibrate,
remained perpetually primed, and that priming made the incomplete, rage-filled memory fragments more
likely to intrude, which drove the avoidance harder, which kept the danger feeling present and
unresolved.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was a near-miss at work three weeks ago. A colleague dropped
a metal container behind him, and Kwame's reaction — dropping to the ground, heart hammering,
certain for several seconds that something had detonated — was witnessed by his entire team. He has
held his professional identity as the steadiest person in any room for thirty years. What he is most
afraid of is that this steadiness is gone permanently. What he is most hoping for, though he has not
yet found words for it, is to remember what happened completely enough to finally stop being
ambushed by it.
