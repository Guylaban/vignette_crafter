Rohan is a 39-year-old South Asian man who works as an interior designer, married, and presenting
for the first time to outpatient psychological services following what he describes as nearly two
years of feeling "like I'm always waiting for the next disaster." His PCL-5 score of 39 places him
in the clinical range for PTSD, and the picture that has emerged across the intake and first two
sessions is one of a man whose grief has never been permitted to settle — instead calcifying into a
state of chronic vigilance that is quietly dismantling his daily life.

The precipitating event occurred in the autumn of two years ago, when Rohan's younger brother,
Arjun, died suddenly of a cardiac event at thirty-four. There had been no warning. Rohan had spoken
to him the evening before, had laughed with him over something trivial, and then received the call
the following morning. The death was so abrupt, so structurally impossible in the way Rohan had
understood the world to work, that it left behind a wound with no edges he could locate. At the
funeral — held on a grey October morning, the air smelling of damp earth and chrysanthemums — Rohan
stood at the graveside and felt, for the first time, what he would later describe as the ground
shifting beneath him. He did not cry. He stood very still and felt his heart hammering against his
ribs, certain, in that moment, that something catastrophic was still arriving.

It was that funeral, and everything associated with it, that became the fault line in Rohan's
subsequent life. In the months that followed, he found that certain things would detonate without
warning: passing a cemetery on his commute to a client site, overhearing a colleague mention losing
her father, or — most acutely — the approach of October itself. These triggers did not produce
ordinary sadness. They produced something more physical and more immediate: a sudden, overwhelming
sense that the worst was happening again, right now, a flashback quality in which Rohan was not
remembering the graveside but standing at it, the cold air in his lungs, his brother gone. His body
would respond before any conscious thought formed — pulse spiking, jaw tightening, a surge of
adrenaline that left him irritable and short-fused for hours afterward, snapping at his wife, Priya,
in ways he found shameful but could not seem to prevent.

Because these triggers were so destabilising, Rohan began, gradually and then systematically, to
arrange his life around avoiding them. He stopped attending funerals entirely — including,
painfully, the funeral of a close family friend, an absence that caused lasting friction with his
parents. He rerouted his drive to client meetings to bypass the cemetery on Elm Street. As October
approached each year, he became increasingly withdrawn, declining social invitations and
manufacturing excuses. In his design work, he found himself turning down projects involving memorial
spaces or hospice facilities. He had come to believe, with quiet certainty, that if he could simply
not encounter these things, he could hold himself together.

But the avoidance brought its own cost. In the space cleared of reminders, Rohan's mind filled the
gap with something worse: he began mentally rehearsing catastrophic scenarios — Priya collapsing, a
parent receiving a diagnosis, a client's call that would carry terrible news. He rehearsed these
scenarios deliberately, convinced that being mentally prepared would prevent the kind of
annihilating shock he had felt when Arjun died. What he did not recognise was that this rehearsal
kept him in a state of continuous threat anticipation, his nervous system perpetually primed, so
that when a trigger did break through — an anniversary date, an overheard conversation about loss —
his body was already wound tight, and the flashback or the surge of anger arrived with even greater
force. The avoidance that was meant to protect him was feeding the very state it was designed to
prevent, a loop he could feel but not name.

Rohan came to seek help after Priya told him, quietly and carefully, that she felt she had lost him
too. That sentence reached him in a way that months of his own suffering had not. What he fears
most, he says, is that he will never feel safe again. What he hopes for, more tentatively, is simply
to be able to think of Arjun without his body treating it as an emergency.
