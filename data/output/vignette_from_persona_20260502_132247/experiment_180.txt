Dmitri Volkov is a 33-year-old graphic designer of Eastern European descent who was divorced two
years ago and currently lives alone in a mid-sized American city. He presents with a PCL-5 score of
74, indicating severe posttraumatic stress, and was referred for psychological assessment following
a wellness check initiated by his employer after a prolonged and unexplained absence from remote
work deadlines.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Dmitri was returning home late at night from a client dinner when
he was pulled over by police officers who, acting on an erroneous dispatch report, drew their
weapons and ordered him from his vehicle at gunpoint. The stop occurred on an unlit stretch of road,
and Dmitri recalls the darkness pressing in around the flashing cruiser lights, the silhouettes of
uniformed officers moving toward him, and the cold metal of the car door against his palms as he
raised his hands. He was held face-down on the pavement for what he estimates was twenty minutes
before the error was identified and he was released without explanation or apology. No physical
injury was recorded, but by the time Dmitri drove home that night — hands shaking on the same
steering wheel — something had shifted in how the world felt to him.

In the weeks that followed, the incident began to reorganise itself around the sensory details that
had been most overwhelming. Darkness became the first and most persistent trigger. Dmitri found that
waking in the night, even in his own apartment, would return him instantly to the feeling of lying
on cold asphalt with a weapon trained at his back. The specific make and colour of the police
cruiser — a dark-coloured American sedan — meant that similar cars in traffic would produce a sudden
lurch of dread, his breath catching as they appeared in his mirrors or at intersections. Crowded
public spaces brought a different but related terror: too many bodies moving unpredictably, too many
uniforms potentially present, the sense of having no clear exit. At a grocery store six months after
the incident, Dmitri saw a security guard in a dark uniform near the entrance and left his cart in
the aisle and walked out without buying anything. He has not returned to that store.

These triggers — the dark, the cars, the crowds — became the engine of a pattern of avoidance and
self-medication that has quietly narrowed his life. When the triggers activated the raw, unprocessed
fear from that night, Dmitri found that alcohol blunted the physiological edge of it quickly enough
to be useful. What began as a drink before bed to ease the dread of lying awake in the dark became
two, then more, and now extends to daytime drinking on days when he knows he will need to leave the
apartment. He avoids any route where police vehicles are likely to be present, and he has stopped
attending the in-person client meetings that were once a reliable source of professional
satisfaction, because the unpredictability of crowded city streets — and the near-certainty of
encountering uniformed figures — makes the journey feel untenable. When he cannot avoid a public
space entirely, he has developed a habit of covering his face with his hands, pressing his palms to
his eyes and forehead in a gesture that began as a way to block visual input and has become almost
reflexive. His freelance output has declined sharply, and one long-standing client terminated their
contract three months ago, citing missed communications. Dmitri received the email at two in the
morning, unable to sleep, and did not reply for a week.

He avoids military and police uniforms wherever possible — changing the channel when news coverage
shows officers, taking longer routes, declining social invitations where uniformed personnel might
be present. These behaviours have not made him feel safer; if anything, the world has come to feel
smaller and more saturated with threat.

What finally brought Dmitri to seek help was a conversation with his older sister, who flew in from
out of state and found him, at midday, curtains drawn, unwilling to answer the door until she called
his name. He told her he was managing. She did not believe him. He is most afraid, he said during
intake, that what happened to him has permanently changed the way he experiences the world. What he
is hoping for — quietly, and with evident difficulty saying it aloud — is to be able to work again,
to sleep in the dark, and to stop feeling like a man who is always, still, face-down on the
pavement.
