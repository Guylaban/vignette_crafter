Marcus is a 55-year-old divorced fashion designer of North American background who was referred for
psychological assessment following a disclosure to his physician that he had been struggling for
decades with symptoms he could no longer manage alone. Though the referral was recent, the story it
pointed toward began in childhood, in a home where the person responsible for his safety was also
the source of his greatest harm.

Marcus grew up in a household defined by unpredictability. His primary caregiver — a man whose moods
shifted without warning — subjected him to repeated physical abuse across much of his childhood.
What Marcus remembers most viscerally is not any single incident but the quality of the silence that
preceded the violence: the particular stillness of a room just before something terrible happened.
The house would go quiet, and that quiet meant danger. Decades later, sitting in his design studio
in the late afternoon when his assistants have left and the floor falls silent, Marcus finds his
body responding as though he is eight years old again. That unusual silence still carries the
unmistakable signature of threat, and he will often find reasons to keep music playing or leave the
television on rather than sit in stillness. Even inside his own apartment — a space he chose, pays
for, and controls — he remains on guard, scanning for sounds, unable to fully settle. An unexpected
hand on his shoulder from a colleague reviewing fabric samples can send a jolt of alarm through him
that he struggles to explain or conceal.

The memories themselves are fragmented and resistant to narrative. Marcus cannot tell a coherent
story of what happened to him, even to himself. Some days the past surfaces in a dissociated,
affectless way — he can recite facts about what occurred as though reading from a file about a
stranger, feeling nothing. Other times, memory arrives with a flood of guilt rather than anger,
leaving him convinced that something he did or failed to do as a child invited what happened. This
guilt on recall does not stay contained; it bleeds outward into his appraisals of himself and the
world. When his memory is activated — triggered, for instance, by the silence of his studio — the
fragmented, emotionally dysregulated quality of what surfaces reinforces his sense that he is
fundamentally broken, that the damage done to him is permanent and irreversible. He has come to
believe, with quiet certainty, that he has changed for the worse in ways no therapy can reach.

These appraisals — that he is permanently damaged, that people cannot be trusted, that his ongoing
reactions mean he is losing his mind — do not sit passively. They drive how he manages himself day
to day. Fearing that trusting others leads to harm, Marcus ruminates at length on what people have
done to him, replaying slights and betrayals in a way that keeps the threat system primed and the
past perpetually present. He also turns inward with equal force, dwelling on his own perceived
failures and wrongdoing — a habit that, rather than producing resolution, keeps the traumatic
material churning just beneath the surface and makes coherent recall even harder to achieve. He has
quietly restructured his evenings to avoid the conditions that tend to bring on nightmares, going to
bed later, sleeping with light on, refusing afternoon naps — small accommodations that have
gradually narrowed his life. His romantic relationships, already strained by his mistrust and
guardedness, did not survive; his divorce several years ago he attributes, with characteristic self-
blame, largely to his own deficiencies.

The fear that his reactions signal madness has been, paradoxically, what finally brought him to seek
help. A younger colleague recently described him as "unreachable," and the word lodged in him.
Marcus arrived at his first session guarded but exhausted, hoping most of all to understand whether
what he carries is something that can be named — and whether being named might mean it could, at
last, be put down.
