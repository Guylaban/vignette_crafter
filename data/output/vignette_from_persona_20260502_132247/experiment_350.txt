Matthias Brandt is a 37-year-old German-American financial analyst who was referred for
psychological assessment following a period of escalating occupational impairment and a recent
separation from his therapist of two years, who had retired. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 59,
consistent with severe posttraumatic stress. The precipitating trauma occurred approximately three
years ago when Matthias was taken captive during a business trip abroad. He was held for eleven days
in a series of confined spaces — a van, a basement room, and finally a concrete cell — by
individuals whose demands were never made entirely clear to him. The conditions were degrading: he
was kept in near-total silence for long stretches, occasionally broken by the distant sound of
children crying somewhere in the structure above him. His captors handled him with an impersonal
physical efficiency — moving him, restraining him, positioning him — that stripped him of any sense
of being a person in their eyes. He was eventually released without explanation. No one was ever
charged.

In the years since, Matthias has found that the world has become reorganised around a set of sensory
thresholds he cannot control. The sound of children crying — something he now encounters regularly
in the open-plan office building where he works, as a daycare operates on the floor below — pulls
him immediately back into that basement. When the crying filters up through the ventilation system
during the afternoon hours, his body floods with alertness, his attention fractures from his
spreadsheets, and he finds himself scanning the room for exits. That same hypervigilance floods in
when he encounters sudden, complete silence — the kind that falls when a conference call drops or
when the office empties unexpectedly at the end of the day. Both sounds, and the absence of sound,
function as direct activations of his threat system: within seconds of either, he is superalert,
scanning, unable to locate safety even inside familiar spaces. He has difficulty falling asleep most
nights not because he fears nightmares specifically, but because some part of him remains convinced
that lowering his guard is dangerous — that the moment he stops listening, something will happen.

When threat is already elevated, his memories become harder to hold at a distance. Matthias
describes his recollections of the captivity as possessing an almost hallucinatory quality — more
vivid and present than anything from his ordinary life, as though the event is still happening in
some layer of his nervous system. He can sometimes smell the damp concrete and the particular
chemical odour of the restraints, and these sensory intrusions feel less like remembering than like
being returned. The heightened state of arousal that accompanies his vigilance seems to lower the
threshold for these intrusions, so that an afternoon of scanning and listening in the office can
give way, by evening, to hours in which the memories press close and vivid against the present.

What the memories return him to, again and again, is not only the fear but the moral injury.
Matthias has come to believe that he behaved in ways that contradict who he understood himself to be
— complying, acquiescing, doing what was required of him to survive — and this belief sits in him
like a verdict. He feels, at times, like something that was used rather than a person who endured
something. The knowledge that the men responsible have faced no legal consequences compounds this:
justice has not reorganised the world, and so the wrongness of what happened remains, in his view,
unresolved and somehow still ongoing. These appraisals feed back into his sensitivity to the world
in specific ways — physical affection, a colleague's hand on his shoulder, his daughter's hug during
custody visits, reactivates the memory of being handled without consent, and the shame and
dehumanisation surface immediately, making warmth feel like a threat.

Matthias sought help again after missing three consecutive custody visits — not because he did not
want to see his daughter, but because the daycare adjacent to his ex-wife's apartment building meant
that children's voices were unavoidable on arrival. He is most afraid, he says, that he will never
be able to tolerate closeness again. What he is hoping for, more cautiously, is to stop feeling like
the captivity is still in progress.
