Rania is a 52-year-old married woman of Lebanese descent who works as a project manager for a mid-
sized construction firm in the greater Detroit area. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 36 and a
clinical picture shaped by a terrorism-related trauma she experienced approximately fourteen months
ago while visiting family in a city that came under a coordinated attack. The incident unfolded on
an otherwise ordinary afternoon: armed individuals opened fire in a crowded market district, and
Rania found herself pressed against a concrete wall as security forces — police, soldiers, men in
uniform shouting orders she could barely process — swarmed the area. A helicopter circled overhead
for what felt like hours, its rotors filling the air with a mechanical thunder that seemed to press
down on everything below. She witnessed violence directly and at close range. She was not physically
injured, but she returned to the United States a different woman, though it took several months
before she fully understood that.

In the weeks after she came home, Rania attempted to resume her life with characteristic
determination. She managed timelines, supervised contractors, ran Monday morning standups. But the
world had quietly reorganized itself around new fault lines. The first time she encountered a police
cruiser idling outside her office building — a routine presence she had never once noticed before —
she felt a cold wave move through her chest and found herself unable to walk through the front
entrance for several minutes. She stood on the sidewalk, briefcase in hand, telling herself she was
being irrational, which only seemed to make it worse. Helicopter sounds proved equally disabling.
Detroit's airspace carries medical and news helicopters with some regularity, and on days when one
passes over the open-plan office where Rania manages her team, she has found herself gripping the
edge of her desk and losing the thread of whatever conversation she was in. A colleague once asked
if she was all right; she said she had a headache.

It is in these triggered moments — when the sight of a uniformed officer or the distant thrum of
rotors pulls her back toward the market, toward the wall, toward the sounds of that afternoon — that
Rania's most painful beliefs about herself rise to the surface with full force. She had come to
believe, with a quiet but absolute conviction, that she was permanently altered in a way that was
somehow visible to other people. Walking into a client meeting after a triggered episode, she was
certain that everyone in the room could see it on her face, could read the event in her posture or
her eyes — that whatever had happened to her in that city had left a mark that announced itself
without her permission. This sense of exposure compounded her distress in the workplace, where her
professional identity had always been a source of pride and stability. The belief that she was
transparent to others made ordinary interactions feel like performances she was failing.

Alongside this was a grief she found harder to articulate. Rania described herself, in her first
session, as someone who had always been fundamentally happy — not naively so, but grounded, warm,
capable of pleasure. That person felt inaccessible to her now. She did not recognize the woman who
flinched at sirens or avoided the lobby when a security guard was posted there. "I don't know who I
am anymore," she said, not dramatically but with a flatness that carried its own weight. The loss of
her former self felt as real and as irreversible as a physical loss, and it sat beneath everything
else she described.

What finally brought Rania to seek help was a project review meeting last month at which a client
brought two uniformed city inspectors. The sound of a news helicopter passed overhead during the
meeting. She excused herself, went to the bathroom, and did not return for twenty minutes. Her
supervisor noticed. She has not told her husband the full extent of what she is experiencing, and
she is not sure she can. What she hopes for, she said, is to feel like herself again. What she fears
is that there is no longer a self to return to.
