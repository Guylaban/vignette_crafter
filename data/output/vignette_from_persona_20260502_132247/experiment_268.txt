Yasmine is a 36-year-old Lebanese-American woman who works as a construction worker, married and
living in a mid-sized city in the Southwest. She was referred for psychological evaluation following
a PCL-5 score of 74, indicating severe posttraumatic stress symptomatology. The precipitating event
occurred approximately fourteen months ago, when Yasmine collapsed on a job site during a
particularly brutal July afternoon. What followed was a five-day hospitalization for acute bacterial
meningitis, during which she was placed in an isolation unit, lost consciousness twice, and was told
by her attending physician that she had come within hours of dying. She remembers the fluorescent
ceiling tiles of the ICU with a clarity that feels violent — the hiss of monitoring equipment, the
sensation of her body no longer responding to her will, the terrifying helplessness of lying
strapped to a bed while her organs threatened to fail. It was the first time in her adult life that
she had understood, with total physical certainty, that she could not protect herself from what was
happening inside her own body.

In the weeks after discharge, Yasmine returned to work as quickly as her physicians permitted. The
job site — the noise, the physical demands, the sense of doing something tangible — felt like the
only place where her body made sense. She began arriving earlier than her crew and staying later,
filling every hour with tasks, as if stillness itself were dangerous. Her body, however, did not
cooperate with this strategy. At work, the sudden crack of a nail gun or the grind of heavy
machinery would send a jolt through her chest that left her breathless and scanning the site for
threat. She slept poorly, waking multiple times each night with her muscles already clenched, her
heart racing before she was fully conscious. By morning she felt as though she had never rested. Her
body seemed to have concluded that danger was permanent and omnipresent, maintaining a state of
readiness that exhausted her even as it refused to relent.

This relentless physiological arousal began to feed directly into how Yasmine understood herself.
She was certain that no ordinary person felt this way — that the jumpiness, the sleeplessness, the
constant muscular tension meant something was fundamentally broken in her. She had come to believe
that her reactions were signs of losing her mind, and the belief that she was going crazy sat
alongside a deeper conviction that she had no future worth imagining. She felt certain she could not
stop bad things from happening to her; the illness had proven that her body was a site of ambush,
not safety, and this helplessness had calcified into something that felt like fact rather than fear.

In response to these appraisals — the sense of impending collapse, the conviction that she was
beyond repair — Yasmine reached for whatever blunted the sharpest edges. She began eating in a way
she described as compulsive and joyless, consuming large amounts of food in the evenings not out of
hunger but out of a need to feel something other than dread. She continued to bury herself in work
during the day, taking on extra shifts and volunteering for the most physically demanding tasks her
foreman would assign. She described a practiced emotional flatness — a deliberate numbing that she
had cultivated as a kind of internal insulation. These strategies offered brief relief, but over
months they began to generate their own evidence against her. The overeating left her feeling
sluggish and ashamed. The overworking had strained her marriage, her husband reporting that she was
absent even when present. The numbing had made her feel less like herself, which in turn deepened
her conviction that she was going crazy and that the person she had been before the illness was
simply gone. The strategies that were meant to manage the unbearable had quietly become further
proof that she was unraveling.

Yasmine sought help after her husband told her, quietly and without accusation, that he felt he had
lost her. She arrived at the intake appointment guarded but exhausted, saying she did not know
whether she wanted to feel better or simply wanted someone to confirm that what was happening to her
had a name. What she feared most was that it did not — that this was simply who she was now.
