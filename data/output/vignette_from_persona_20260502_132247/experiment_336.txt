Priya is a 37-year-old South Asian woman who owns and operates a small clothing boutique in a mid-
sized American city. She is married, describes her husband as supportive, and presents to therapy
with a PCL-5 score of 72 — a level of severity that reflects how comprehensively her life has
reorganised itself around a single event. Approximately fourteen months ago, Priya was sexually
assaulted by a man she had been briefly involved with before her marriage, a former partner who had
contacted her under the pretense of a business discussion and then forced himself on her in the back
of a rideshare vehicle late one evening. The assault occurred in a confined, moving space from which
she could not exit, with his body blocking hers against the seat. She remembers the pressure of
being pinned, the particular angle of his face above her, the way the city lights slid past the
window — and then a kind of dissolution, as though the experience exceeded what language could hold.
To this day, Priya finds she cannot fully narrate what happened. The event sits in her not as a
story she can tell but as something closer to sensation and image: a weight, a shadow, a smear of
streetlight. She has tried to write it down and stopped each time, the words refusing to cohere into
sequence.

In the weeks following the assault, Priya stopped taking public transport entirely. What had begun
as an understandable aversion calcified into a fixed rule: she drives to the shop, drives home, and
structures every errand around the availability of her own car. This avoidance has cost her
concretely — a wholesale market she used to visit by train is now effectively inaccessible, and she
has lost two supplier relationships as a result. She also began to notice herself becoming
excessively agreeable in situations that felt even mildly pressured: with difficult customers, with
delivery men who lingered too long, with any man whose tone sharpened. She recognised this as
something she was doing to manage fear rather than because she meant it, but she could not stop.
These strategies, rather than quieting the distress, kept her mind continuously circling the assault
— the deliberate avoidance of transit and the performance of compliance both functioning as
constant, low-grade reminders that something had happened that she was still trying to survive.

Priya's shop has become one of the most difficult arenas. She works behind a counter with her back
to an open floor, and she had come to believe, with a certainty that felt bodily rather than
rational, that she was unsafe unless she could see the entire room and feel a wall behind her. She
rearranged the counter accordingly. When customers make sudden movements — reaching past her,
turning quickly, dropping something — she is immediately on guard, her body bracing before her mind
has registered what happened. These moments of perceived threat collapse the distance between the
present and the assault: the vigilance pulls the memory forward, and the memory floods back with the
same terror she felt in that car, not as recollection but as reliving. She suddenly feels as though
it is happening again, and the guilt arrives with it — a corrosive sense that she should have
refused the meeting, should have known, should have done something differently. She spends long
stretches of the workday in a loop of counterfactual thinking, imagining the life that would have
existed had she simply not replied to that message fourteen months ago.

The loop tightens in particular ways. The elevated threat she carries into each day means that
ordinary sensory details — a customer who resembles one of the women she saw that night, hearing a
name that sounds like his, a stranger mentioning an ex-partner in passing — land with
disproportionate force, pulling her back into the memory and the terror it carries. The terror then
amplifies her vigilance, which makes the next trigger harder to absorb. Her compliant, conflict-
avoiding behaviour keeps her in situations she cannot fully control, and the avoidance of transport
keeps her isolated in ways that prevent any disconfirmation of how dangerous the world now feels.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a moment in the shop three weeks ago when a male
customer reached across the counter unexpectedly and she froze completely, unable to speak, shaking
visibly until he left. Her husband had been watching. She is most afraid, she says, that she will
never be able to tell the story of what happened to her — and most hoping that someone can help her
find the words.
