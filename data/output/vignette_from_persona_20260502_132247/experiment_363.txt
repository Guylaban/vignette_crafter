Kayla is a 19-year-old married woman of North American background who is currently unemployed and
living with her husband in a mid-sized city. She was referred for psychological assessment following
a sexual assault that occurred approximately eight months ago, and her PCL-5 score of 61 places her
firmly in the severe range of post-traumatic symptomatology. What brought her finally to the clinic
was not any single crisis but a slow accumulation of losses — the person she had been before the
assault had become unrecognizable to her, and she had begun to fear that version of herself was gone
permanently.

The assault took place on a late summer evening when Kayla had been walking home from a friend's
apartment. A man she did not know approached her from behind in an alleyway; she recalls the smell
of alcohol on him before she even turned around, and the sound of a motorcycle idling somewhere
nearby as the encounter escalated into violence. At one point he shouted at her in Arabic — words
she did not understand but whose tone she understood completely. The assault lasted several minutes.
When it was over she was left on the ground, the motorcycle noise fading into the distance and the
smell of alcohol still thick in the air.

In the weeks that followed, Kayla found that her memory of the event did not behave the way she
expected memory to behave. It did not recede. Instead, certain sensory fragments — the rev of a
motorcycle engine, the smell of alcohol on someone's breath at a family gathering, the sound of
raised Arabic-speaking voices from a neighbouring television — could instantly collapse the distance
between then and now, pulling the full memory back without warning. When those memories returned
they carried their original sensory texture: she would hear, internally, the sounds from the alley,
and find herself smelling what she had smelled that night, as though her nervous system had
preserved the scene in complete fidelity. These triggered memories fed directly into a state of
acute threat — she would find herself scanning the room, checking over her shoulder, pressing her
back against walls in public spaces, and lying awake listening for sounds at the front door that
might mean someone was trying to get in. The memory did not merely recall danger; it reinstated it,
and her body responded accordingly.

The threat monitoring itself deepened the problem. In a state of hypervigilant alertness, ordinary
sounds became suspicious, and the heightened arousal made intrusive recall more likely, not less —
her nervous system primed to detect the very cues that would reactivate the memory. She came to
believe that the world she had understood before the assault — one in which she could move through
public space without being harmed — had been revealed as a fiction. She was certain, in a way she
found difficult to articulate, that she had failed in some fundamental way; that another person,
someone more capable, more alert, more something, would have prevented what happened to her. These
appraisals — that the world was permanently unsafe and that she herself was diminished — kept threat
perception elevated even on days when nothing external provoked it.

Over months, Kayla built her life around avoidance. She stopped going out after dark entirely. She
avoided the anniversary of the assault by spending the day sedated with sleep. She had stopped
looking at her own body in mirrors or when changing clothes, finding that any attention to her
physical self brought the memory of the assault flooding back and confirmed, in some wordless way,
her belief that she was no longer the person she had been. These strategies did bring short-term
relief, but they also narrowed her world severely — she had not worked since the assault, had
declined social invitations, and her husband had begun expressing concern about her isolation.
Crucially, the avoidance prevented her from processing the memory in any integrated way, keeping it
raw and easily triggered. By restricting her environment so tightly, she had also inadvertently
ensured that when triggers did occur — a motorcycle passing the window, a neighbour's shouting —
their impact was amplified by the contrast with the silence she had constructed around herself, and
the old appraisals of helplessness rushed back in full force.

Kayla came to the clinic hoping, cautiously, that something might be recoverable — some version of a
life that did not organize itself entirely around what had happened to her in that alley. What she
feared most was that the therapist would confirm what she already half-believed: that she was right
to think she was no longer capable, and that nothing would change.
