Aleksander Voss is a 26-year-old fisherman of Polish descent who lives with his wife on the coastal
outskirts of a mid-sized American port city. He presented for psychological assessment approximately
eight months after an incident of politically motivated detention and torture that occurred while he
was visiting relatives abroad. During that detention, which lasted several days, Aleksander was
restrained in a fixed position for prolonged periods — his body forced into a compressed, folded
posture — while interrogators used noise, physical pain, and the sounds of other detainees,
including at one point an infant crying in an adjacent room, to disorient and break him. He was
beaten across the back and legs with consistent, methodical force. He was never permitted to face
the door. He was released without charge, flew home to his wife, and told her only that he had been
"roughed up." He did not seek medical or psychological care at the time.

In the weeks that followed, Aleksander noticed that certain moments would pull him back without
warning. The sound of a baby crying — heard through a neighbour's window, or from the back of the
grocery store — would collapse the distance between then and now, and he would find himself flooded
with the memory of that room: the restraints, the particular quality of helplessness, the pain
spreading across his lower back. What began as intrusive recollection quickly acquired a physical
dimension that frightened him deeply. When the memories surfaced, he could feel his body curling
into the position he had been held in, his spine compressing as if the restraints were still there,
and a dull, burning pain would reactivate along the same lines where the blows had landed. Alongside
the physical re-experiencing came a surge of rage so intense it felt ungovernable — not grief, not
fear, but a white fury directed at faces he could still picture clearly.

These memory intrusions, in turn, left him in a state of sustained alertness that did not dissipate
between episodes. He had come to believe, with quiet certainty, that the danger was not over — that
people who had done this once could find him again. He began sitting only with his back to the wall
in any room he entered, scanning exits and watching the door. At home, he lay awake cataloguing
sounds: the creak of the hallway, a car slowing outside, anything that might mean someone was coming
in. At work on the fishing boat, he found himself registering the weight and reach of tools — gaffs,
filleting knives, mooring cleats — not as equipment but as weapons or threats, his eyes moving
across them before he had consciously decided to look. This hypervigilant orientation was exhausting
and total.

The threat monitoring itself fed back into his triggers in ways that compounded his distress. Riding
the bus to the harbour each morning — enclosed, unable to position himself against a wall,
surrounded by strangers whose hands he could not watch — activated the same sense of exposure he
associated with the detention, and on several occasions the bus ride alone was enough to bring the
memories flooding back in their full somatic force. The loop between his sense of threat and the
intrusions had become self-sustaining: the body's alarm would sharpen his attention toward anything
resembling the conditions of the original event, and that sharpened attention would find them
everywhere.

Over months, Aleksander stopped taking the bus and began driving to the dock alone, leaving before
his wife woke. He declined social invitations. His wife, who knew little of what had actually
happened, had grown frightened by his silences and his habit of repositioning furniture so that
chairs faced doors. Their evenings had become careful and quiet. It was she who finally asked him to
speak to someone, and it was partly for her — and partly because the rage he felt on remembering had
begun to feel like something he could not safely contain — that he came. What he feared most was
that the person he had been before that room no longer existed. What he hoped, though he could
barely say it, was that he was wrong.
