Sister Margaret Kowalski is a fifty-five-year-old Polish-American woman who has lived in religious
community for nearly three decades. She came to the clinic referred by her spiritual director, who
had grown concerned over the preceding year about what he described as a marked change in her
character — a withdrawal, a rigidity, and a grief she seemed unable to surrender to God or to anyone
else. Margaret's PCL-5 score of 42 places her in the clinical range for PTSD, and the picture that
emerged across the intake and first two sessions is one of a woman whose faith has become both her
greatest resource and the architecture of a trap she cannot find her way out of.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Margaret was present when a young novice in her community — a
woman of twenty-three named Clara, whom Margaret had been mentoring closely — was struck by a
vehicle while the two of them were crossing the street outside the convent after evening prayers.
The driver ran a red light at speed. Margaret had been half a step behind Clara. She had, in fact,
been the one to suggest they cross at that moment, having glanced at the light and misjudged the
oncoming car's distance. She watched Clara thrown from her feet and lay beside her on the pavement
until the ambulance arrived. Clara survived but sustained a traumatic brain injury and has not
returned to the community. Margaret has not been back to that intersection since.

What Margaret carries from that evening is a memory that is simultaneously overwhelming and
incomplete. Certain moments are engraved with terrible precision — the sound of the impact, the
particular quality of the streetlight on the wet road, the smell of exhaust — while others are
entirely absent, as though cut from the reel. She cannot recall the moments immediately before she
called out to Clara to cross, and this gap has become its own kind of torment. She has come to
believe that the event happened because of the way she acted — her carelessness, her presumption —
and that she failed in the most fundamental duty of a mentor: to protect the person in her care.
Bound up with this is a deeper and more corrosive conviction, one she articulates quietly and
without drama, that she cannot rely on herself. These appraisals sit at the centre of her
presentation. Because she understands herself to be the kind of person who causes harm through
inattention, she has constructed an elaborate system of mental rituals — prayers repeated in precise
sequences, specific gestures made before she accompanies anyone anywhere — designed to forestall
another catastrophe. She performs these rituals before any communal movement, before meals, before
leaving the chapel. In her mind, they are not superstition but necessary correction for her own
unreliability.

The rituals, however, do not bring relief. If anything, they return her attention repeatedly to the
event itself, and the vivid, unbidden quality of her memories intensifies. A car passing quickly on
the road adjacent to the convent grounds, the squeal of brakes on a wet morning, even the particular
angle of autumn light that resembles the streetlamp that night — any of these can collapse the
distance between present and past in an instant, flooding her with the full sensory weight of the
accident without warning. When these intrusions occur, they reinforce her belief that she has not
done enough to prevent another harm, which drives the rituals further. The cycle tightens: each
intrusion feels like evidence that she remains dangerous; each ritual rehearses the logic of her
guilt; and the rehearsal keeps the memory close and volatile. She avoids not only the intersection
but any conversation or object that recalls Clara — photographs in the common room, the novitiate
corridor, the particular hymn they sang that evening — so that whole portions of her daily life in
the convent have been quietly cordoned off. This avoidance, over months, has narrowed her world and
her relationships in ways her community has noticed, even if they have not named it.

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with her prioress, who told her gently but
directly that her withdrawal was affecting the younger sisters. Margaret, who had believed her
rituals and her silence were protecting others, found in that moment that they had instead created
distance. She came to the first session hoping, she said, to be told she was not beyond repair — and
afraid, above all else, that she might be.
