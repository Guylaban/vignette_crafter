Cassandra Tremblay is a 38-year-old bartender currently in a relationship, who presented for an
initial intake following what she described as months of "barely holding it together." The events
that brought her to this point began roughly fourteen months ago, when her younger brother Marcus
was violently assaulted outside a bar in their city — beaten by a group of men after an argument
that Cassandra herself had inadvertently set in motion. She had introduced Marcus to the social
circle where the conflict originated, had encouraged him to come out that night when he had
initially hesitated, and had stepped away from him at the critical moment to take a phone call. She
did not witness the assault directly, but she arrived on scene while he was still on the ground, and
what she saw in those minutes — his face, the concrete, the particular quality of the light — lodged
itself into her with an immediacy that has not softened since.

Cassandra's memories of that night are fragmentary in a way she finds deeply unsettling. Certain
images are almost hallucinatory in their clarity: the specific pattern of Marcus's jacket, the sound
of someone's voice she didn't recognise, the way her own hands looked when she knelt beside him.
Other stretches — the minutes before she arrived, portions of the ambulance ride — are simply gone,
blank in a way that feels less like forgetting and more like erasure. She describes her memory as "a
film with scenes cut out," and the vividness of what remains makes the gaps feel more alarming, not
less. These vivid, image-heavy fragments surface without warning during her shifts — a particular
configuration of low bar lighting, a raised voice across the room — and when they do, they carry
with them a renewed and visceral certainty that she was responsible. The clarity of those images,
she has come to feel, is itself a kind of evidence: if she can see it that precisely, she must have
been at the centre of it. This is how the memories have fed her self-blame, each intrusive image
arriving like a fresh indictment.

That self-blame has shaped almost everything about how Cassandra has managed the past year. She had
come to believe, with a quiet but settled conviction, that her judgment cannot be trusted — that she
misread people, misread situations, and that Marcus paid for it. From this has grown a broader
distrust that extends well beyond that night: she is now certain that people are rarely what they
present themselves to be, and that relying on anyone, including professionals, exposes her to
further harm. It is this reasoning — not indifference, but a kind of wary self-protection — that has
kept her from seeking help until now. She has avoided medical and psychological care entirely since
the assault, telling herself she can manage, that she doesn't need to hand her vulnerability to
someone she cannot trust. She has also quietly cut news from her daily life, muting notifications
and changing the channel whenever a story surfaces about violence or assault, because encountering
those reports pulls the images back with a force she finds unmanageable. The avoidance has felt
rational, even necessary — but its cost has been accumulating. At work, where raised voices and
late-night altercations are occupational background noise, she has developed a rigid emotional
control that her partner and her regulars have both noticed. She does not react, does not intervene,
keeps her face carefully neutral. A colleague recently asked if she was "checked out," and the
comment landed harder than she expected.

The trouble is that the very strategies that have kept Cassandra functional have also kept her
beliefs intact. Her refusal to seek help has meant that her self-blame has gone unchallenged; her
emotional suppression has prevented her from processing the images that keep returning; and the
isolation built into her avoidance has only deepened her conviction that she cannot rely on others.
What finally brought her in was a conversation with her partner, who told her plainly that she had
become unreachable. What she is most afraid of, she said quietly at the end of the intake, is that
they might be right about her — that she really did cause it. What she is hoping for is something
she struggled to name.
