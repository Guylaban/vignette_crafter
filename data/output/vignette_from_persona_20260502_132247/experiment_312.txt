Marcus had spent twenty-three years building a career around the safety of children. As principal of
a mid-sized elementary school in suburban Ohio, he prided himself on knowing every face in his
hallways, every squeaky hinge on every door. He was the kind of man other people described as
unflappable. That changed on a Tuesday afternoon in late October when he was walking to his car in
the school parking lot after a late budget meeting. A man he did not recognise approached him from
behind a row of vehicles, demanded his keys and wallet, and when Marcus hesitated — not out of
defiance but out of sheer disbelief — struck him twice across the face and throat with what turned
out to be a heavy metal flashlight. Marcus fell between two cars, unable to call out, staring up at
the gap of darkening sky above the roofline while footsteps retreated across the asphalt.

He did not lose consciousness, but he lost something else. In the weeks that followed, Marcus found
that the memory of the assault refused to behave the way memories were supposed to. When he tried
deliberately to reconstruct what had happened — sitting with a detective, later with his wife — the
details slipped away from him, assembling themselves only in fragments and out of order. He could
not reliably describe the man's face or the sequence of blows. Yet without any conscious effort, the
memory would return on its own terms: a film that simply began playing, the crunch of gravel
underfoot, the cold metal against his jaw, his body curling instinctively onto its side — and in
those moments he could feel his shoulders drawing up and his neck contracting, his torso folding
exactly as it had between those two cars. The body remembered what the mind could not retrieve on
demand.

It was through these unbidden replays that the world began to feel dangerous in a new and specific
way. Each time the memory surged back — triggered by the scrape of a chair leg, the particular
quality of light on a parking lot surface, the sound of footsteps behind him — Marcus emerged from
it with his nervous system already primed for attack. The replays did not feel like recollection;
they felt like evidence that the threat was still present and ongoing. His startle response became
severe enough that a student dropping a tray in the cafeteria could send him lurching sideways
against a wall. He began scanning every room he entered for its exits — the gymnasium, the staff
meeting room, the church he and his wife attended on Sundays — cataloguing doors within the first
seconds of arrival. His eyes moved constantly to objects that could be used as weapons: a letter
opener on a secretary's desk, a fire extinguisher mounted in the corridor, a stapler left on a
conference table. He was not choosing to do this. He was certain, at some level beneath argument,
that the assault was not over.

His occupation placed him in direct and daily contact with the very environment where it had
happened. Every afternoon, when the last bell rang and staff began filtering to the parking lot,
Marcus found reasons to stay inside. He rescheduled walkthroughs, lingered over paperwork, waited
until the lot was empty before crossing it — and even then he walked close to the building wall, his
back never fully turned to open space. His assistant principal had begun quietly covering duties
that required him to be outside. His wife, Elena, had noticed that he no longer sat with his back to
the restaurant door, that he had stopped attending the evening neighbourhood association meetings he
once chaired, that he flinched when she touched his shoulder unexpectedly. She had asked him,
carefully, whether he was sleeping. He had told her he was fine.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a morning when a seventh-grader — a boy who had done
nothing wrong — ran up behind him in the hallway and Marcus spun around and grabbed the child by the
collar before he understood what he was doing. He stood there, the boy's eyes wide, and felt
something clarify. He was most afraid, he said at intake, that he had become someone dangerous
inside the building he was supposed to make safe. What he hoped for, more quietly, was to be able to
walk to his own car again without rehearsing his own death.
