Hùng Trần is a 75-year-old Vietnamese-American widower who works as a cybersecurity analyst from his
home office in San Jose, a career he has sustained well into his seventies through contract
consulting work. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 74, indicating severe posttraumatic stress, and
has sought help for the first time following a routine medical appointment that left him, by his own
account, unable to function for three days afterward.

The trauma that anchors Hùng's current suffering occurred approximately forty years ago, when his
youngest son, then three years old, was admitted to a hospital in critical condition following a
sudden, severe infection that progressed to septic shock within hours. Hùng remembers sitting in a
corridor while masked clinicians moved in and out of a room he was not permitted to enter, the
sharp, bleaching smell of antiseptic hanging in the air like a presence. He remembers the particular
sound of monitors, the way a nurse's expression shifted when she came to speak with him, and the
moment he was told his son might not survive the night. His son did survive, after days of intensive
intervention, but Hùng has never fully returned from those hours in that corridor.

In the decades since, the memories have not faded. Hùng describes them as a film that plays over and
over — not a recollection he chooses to visit, but something that arrives without permission and
runs its course. What disturbs him most is that the memory does not feel like the past. When it
surfaces, he is back in that corridor: the smell is present, the dread is present, his son is still
dying. He loses the thread of time. When he has tried to explain this to his adult children, or to
his late wife before her passing three years ago, he finds that the account collapses partway
through — he loses the thread and cannot finish, leaving the story hanging in a way that has made
him feel, quietly, that something is broken in him.

The memories do not wait to be invited. Certain things call them forward with a force Hùng finds
overwhelming, and this is where his daily life has become increasingly constrained. The smell of
antiseptic — encountered in pharmacies, in clinics, even in certain cleaning products he once used
without thinking — now triggers an immediate return of the memory in its full, present-tense
intensity. The memory, once activated, keeps the triggers primed: having moved through the
experience again, Hùng finds himself scanning for the smell afterward, braced, so that ordinary
environments feel charged with threat. Pregnancy and newborns, which might carry joy for another
grandfather his age, instead carry the association of a child's fragility and the proximity of loss
— a neighbor's announcement, a photograph on social media, the sound of a baby in a waiting room,
each one capable of pulling him back.

When the memories arrive, Hùng's body responds as though the emergency is current. He experiences
strong physical reactions — his heart rate surges, his hands become unsteady — and the emotional
register is one of fear and horror that he describes as indistinguishable from what he felt in that
hospital corridor. These physical and emotional responses are not merely unpleasant; Hùng has come
to believe that having them means the danger is real and ongoing, that his nervous system is telling
him something true about the present rather than echoing something from the past. This conviction
keeps the memories close: the arousal and the horror feel like confirmation that the trauma is not
over, which in turn makes the memories more vivid and more present, and more likely to be activated
the next time he encounters a medical setting or the faint chemical sharpness of a hospital
corridor.

His work as a cybersecurity analyst has, until recently, offered some insulation — the focused,
solitary nature of the work kept him away from environments he feared. But a contractual requirement
for an annual health screening brought him into a clinic six weeks ago, where the smell hit him
before he had even checked in. He sat in the waiting room for eleven minutes before leaving without
being seen. It was this failure — practical, undeniable — that finally brought him to seek help.
What he most hopes for, he says quietly, is to be able to explain to his son what happened in that
corridor forty years ago. He has never been able to finish that story.
