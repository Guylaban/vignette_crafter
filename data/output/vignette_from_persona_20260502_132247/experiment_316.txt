Minh is a 51-year-old Vietnamese American man who works as a tax inspector for a regional government
office. He was referred for psychological assessment following a disclosure to his primary care
physician of ongoing sleep difficulties, hypervigilance, and intrusive memories connected to a
period of sustained physical abuse perpetrated by his former domestic partner, which ended
approximately three years ago. His current PCL-5 score of 39 places him in the clinically
significant range, and he presents as guarded but cooperative, speaking carefully and with long
pauses.

The abuse unfolded over several years in what Minh had understood to be a committed relationship.
His former partner's violence was characteristically unpredictable — erupting without warning from
ordinary evenings, often when Minh had returned home exhausted after long hours at work. He recalls
one night in particular: he had come home fatigued after a difficult audit cycle, sat down at the
kitchen table, and within minutes found himself shielding his face from blows. There was no
argument, no escalation he could trace. The randomness of it was, in some ways, the most damaging
feature — because it meant that any moment of tiredness or discomfort, any situation he could not
read in advance, became indistinguishable from the conditions that had preceded violence. He had
learned, at a physiological level, that exhaustion and unpredictability were not merely unpleasant
states but signals of imminent danger.

In the years since leaving, Minh has found that these same states reliably pull him back. When he
feels the particular heaviness of fatigue at the end of a working day — sitting at his desk
reviewing returns, or driving home through familiar streets — his body responds as though the threat
is present: his heart rate climbs, he finds himself scanning doorways, pressing his back to whatever
solid surface is nearest so that the room remains visible ahead of him. He frequently checks over
his shoulder when walking between office floors, certain in those moments that someone is close
behind him. He knows, intellectually, that nothing is wrong; yet the alarm is total and convincing.
These episodes of perceived threat, in turn, are themselves triggering — the physiological arousal
and the posture of vigilance recreate something close enough to the original conditions of danger
that fragments of memory begin to surface unbidden: a hand raised, the sound of a chair scraping,
the particular quality of silence that preceded the worst nights. These fragments arrive suddenly
and without coherence, then vanish before he can examine them, leaving him disoriented. There are
portions of those years he cannot access at all, no matter how deliberately he tries, which troubles
him in its own way.

When the memories do force themselves through, they carry with them a weight of interpretation that
has, over time, hardened into conviction. Minh has come to believe that he was fundamentally
betrayed — not merely hurt, but deceived at the level of identity, by someone he had trusted
completely. From this, a broader and more damaging conclusion has followed: that he is not a person
to whom good things happen, that the future holds nothing he can reasonably hope for. He describes
himself, haltingly, as feeling like an object in his own life — acted upon, without agency or worth.
These appraisals do not remain static; they actively shape his daily experience. Because he believes
himself to be without a meaningful future and essentially without personhood, ordinary discomforts
and uncertainties feel freighted with confirmation — any unpredictable moment at work, any colleague
whose mood he cannot read, registers immediately as potential threat, returning him to the vigilant
scanning and the intrusive fragments that characterise his worst days. The cycle is self-sustaining
in a way he has begun to recognise but cannot interrupt alone.

Minh has been married for six years to a woman he describes as patient and kind, though he
acknowledges that his hypervigilance and emotional withdrawal have placed significant strain on
their relationship. His wife recently told him she no longer recognises him at home. It was this —
more than the sleeplessness, more than the checking and the scanning — that finally brought him to
seek help. What he fears most is that nothing can be different. What he hopes, cautiously, is that
he might be wrong about that.
