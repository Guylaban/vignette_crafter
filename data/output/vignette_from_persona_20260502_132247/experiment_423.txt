Mara is a 33-year-old graphic designer of European descent who lives with her husband in a mid-sized
American city. She was referred for psychological assessment following eighteen months of
progressively worsening anxiety, hypervigilance, and social withdrawal that she traces directly to a
sustained stalking campaign carried out by a former colleague. For nearly eight months, this
individual followed her home from work on multiple occasions, sent unsolicited messages, appeared
outside her apartment building, and on one occasion trailed her on his bicycle through several city
blocks as she rode home in the early evening — close enough that she could hear the rhythm of his
wheels on the pavement behind her. She reported the behaviour to her employer and to the police, but
no formal charges were ever filed and the man was quietly transferred to another department. The
case was, in every practical sense, closed without consequence.

In the weeks that followed the stalking, Mara found herself unable to ride her bicycle — once a
daily pleasure and her primary commute — without a cold certainty that someone was behind her. The
sound of a bicycle approaching from the rear, a figure drawing close on the pavement, or the simple
sensation of another person walking at her pace would immediately activate a surge of alarm: her
heart would accelerate, her breathing would shallow, and she would begin scanning doorways and side
streets for a way out. These triggers reliably produced what she described as a full-body sense of
threat, as though her nervous system had concluded the danger was still ongoing. She became easily
startled by ordinary sounds — a door closing, footsteps in a corridor — and she had begun mentally
cataloguing exits before entering any room, a habit she initially experienced as prudent and now
recognises as exhausting.

The absence of any accountability for her stalker had settled into something deeper than
frustration. Mara had come to believe, with quiet conviction, that the world was genuinely dangerous
and that people were rarely what they appeared to be. Colleagues who seemed friendly struck her as
potentially deceptive; strangers who glanced at her in a coffee shop felt like threats. These
appraisals did not remain abstract — they fed directly into her vigilance, amplifying the threat she
perceived in ordinary situations and making the triggers she encountered at work and in daily life
feel more charged. As a graphic designer who frequently works from cafés and shared studio spaces,
she now enters every new environment already scanning for exits and positioning herself against
walls, her back to the room. The belief that no one could be trusted made her reluctant to ask
colleagues to walk with her to the car park, so the checking and the positioning became her private,
solitary system of protection.

Over months, the strategies that were meant to keep her safe began to sustain the very fear they
were designed to manage. Constantly scanning her surroundings meant she was perpetually finding
things to scan — a man standing too long near the entrance, a bicycle locked outside that had not
been there before — and each sweep of the environment renewed the sense that danger was present.
Staying near exits meant she never fully settled anywhere, and her body never received the signal
that she was safe. Her husband noticed she had stopped attending social events and had asked to work
from home on days when she would normally have cycled to the studio; she told him she was tired, but
what she was actually doing was avoiding the street where the stalking had most frequently occurred.
The avoidance had narrowed her life considerably, and the narrowing had, in its own way, confirmed
her belief that the world outside was something to be managed rather than inhabited. The strategies
that felt like control were quietly reinforcing the appraisals that made control feel necessary, and
the appraisals in turn made her more sensitive to the very triggers she was trying to avoid.

Mara sought help after her husband gently observed that she had not ridden her bicycle in over a
year and that she flinched when someone knocked at their front door. What brought her into the
consulting room was less any single incident than the accumulated weight of a life organised around
threat. She hopes, cautiously, to feel safe enough to cycle to work again. What she fears most is
that she never will — and that, somewhere, the person responsible is simply living his life
unchanged.
