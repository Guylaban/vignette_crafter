Kofi Mensah is a 46-year-old Ghanaian-American man who works as a park ranger in a large national
forest in the Pacific Northwest. He is married, the father of two adult children, and by most
outward measures a composed and capable man. He came to the clinic after his wife told him, quietly
and without accusation, that she no longer recognized him — that he had become someone who flinched
at birdsong and could not sit through a family dinner without his eyes moving constantly to the
windows.

Kofi served two tours in Iraq in his late twenties and early thirties. The event that brought him to
this point occurred during his second tour, when a convoy he was part of came under fire in a narrow
desert corridor. A medevac helicopter had been called in, and while it circled overhead, Kofi was
positioned at the edge of the action, held back by a jammed weapon and a radio that had gone dead.
He watched two of his unit members go down in the open ground ahead of him. He could hear the rotors
above and smell the burning fuel from a vehicle that had taken a hit — a dense, acrid smell that
seemed to coat the inside of his mouth. He survived. They did not. He has never fully accepted that
sequence of events as anything other than his own failure.

For years after his discharge, Kofi managed. He found the ranger work grounding — the forest was
quiet, the rhythms predictable. But the management of his symptoms was always more fragile than it
appeared. About eighteen months ago, a wildfire response brought military-contracted helicopters
into the park for aerial water drops. Kofi was coordinating ground operations when the first
aircraft came in low and loud overhead. The sound — that particular heavy, thumping percussion —
broke through whatever containment he had built. He felt himself go briefly unreal, as though
watching himself from a distance, and came back to the present with his heart hammering and his
hands shaking. Since then, aircraft sounds have become a reliable and dreaded trigger. When he hears
a helicopter, even a distant one, his body goes immediately into a state of alert — scanning the
treeline, watching the other rangers for any shift in posture or expression that might signal
danger, bracing for something he cannot name. The sounds pull him into a vigilance that feels not
like anxiety but like necessity, as though the threat is genuinely present and his survival depends
on his attention.

What the threat-monitoring does, over time, is feed back into memory. When Kofi is in that state of
high alert — eyes moving, body tight, cataloguing everything around him — the memories of the
corridor come forward with unusual force. He has described them as feeling more real than his actual
surroundings: he can smell the fuel, hear the rotors in a way that seems to come from inside his
head rather than outside it, and see the ground ahead of him with a clarity that his ordinary
memories do not possess. This vividness is not comfort — it is accusation. Each time the memory
surfaces with that quality of presence, Kofi finds himself back at the question of what he should
have done, and the answer he arrives at is always the same: more. He is certain he has been
permanently altered by what he failed to do that day, that the man who existed before the corridor
is gone and the one who remains is diminished in some essential way. This conviction — that he is
irreparably damaged, that forgiveness is not available to him — does not stay contained to moments
of reflection. It bleeds into his daily state, keeping him primed, so that ordinary discomfort or a
moment of feeling slightly detached becomes evidence that something is wrong with him, which in turn
makes him more watchful, more easily startled, more likely to be undone by the next sound from the
sky.

The occupation that once steadied him has become the terrain where the cycle plays out most visibly.
He has begun avoiding sectors of the park that fall under flight paths for fire operations. A
colleague noticed and asked; Kofi said nothing. What finally brought him in was a morning last month
when a small tourist aircraft passed over a trail he was walking, and he dropped to one knee behind
a tree before he was aware of having moved. He stayed there for several minutes. He is most afraid,
he said at intake, that this is simply who he is now.
