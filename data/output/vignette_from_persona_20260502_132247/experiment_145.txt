Linh is a 49-year-old Vietnamese-American woman, divorced, who works in IT support at a mid-sized
logistics company. She presented for therapy reporting a PCL-5 score of 40, describing a persistent
and exhausting state of vigilance that had, over the preceding months, begun to erode her ability to
function at work and in daily life.

Linh served as a military interpreter embedded with a combat unit during a deployment in her late
thirties. During one operation, her unit came under sustained fire in a narrow corridor; a vehicle
ahead of hers was struck and caught fire, and the air filled almost immediately with the acrid,
greasy smell of burning metal and flesh. A medevac helicopter arrived while the engagement was still
ongoing, its rotors audible before it was visible, and Linh spent what she later described as the
longest minutes of her life crouched behind cover, certain she would not survive. She did survive,
but several colleagues did not, and she carried the memory of that smell, that sound, and the
helplessness of waiting in a way that never fully resolved.

In the years since, Linh has found that the world occasionally reassembles itself into fragments of
that corridor. The smell of something burning in the office break room — a forgotten microwave meal,
a scorched coffee pot — can stop her mid-step, her body flooding with the same physical reactivity
she felt under fire: heart hammering, hands cold, breathing shallow. When these physical reactions
erupt, she becomes immediately convinced that the environment around her is dangerous, scanning the
open-plan office floor with the certainty that someone approaching her desk is a threat. She began
checking behind her when walking between workstations, and colleagues who noticed started giving her
a wider berth, which only deepened her sense of being surrounded by unpredictable strangers. The
triggers, in this way, reliably produce the full architecture of threat — and once that threat-state
is active, she becomes hyperattentive to sounds, which means the routine noise of a helicopter
ferrying medical staff to the hospital two blocks from her office building can re-ignite the cycle
entirely, pulling her back into sensory proximity with the original event.

The threat-state itself has become a driver of further exposure. When Linh is already scanning for
danger, ordinary sounds and smells acquire significance they would otherwise lack — a maintenance
worker's radio, the ventilation system cycling on — and so the threshold at which a trigger
registers drops considerably. She had come to understand, without quite articulating it, that being
on guard made her more vulnerable to being ambushed by memory, not less.

What frightened her most, however, was losing control of herself in public. To forestall this, Linh
developed a set of strategies that she experienced as necessary rather than harmful. She stopped
attending team meetings where emotional conversations might arise, citing scheduling conflicts. She
deflected every colleague who asked how she was doing with brief, closed answers, and she had not
spoken to her ex-husband or her adult daughter about anything connected to her service in over two
years. When the physical reactions became unbearable and none of these avoidances had contained
them, she began, quietly and with considerable shame, hurting herself — small, precise injuries to
her forearm that she described as "resetting" her mind. These strategies, while providing momentary
relief, kept her in a state of chronic arousal: the self-harm and the emotional suppression left her
body never fully returning to baseline, so that she woke each morning already primed, the threshold
for the next trigger lower than the day before. The avoidance of emotional stress meant she never
processed what had happened, which in turn meant the threat-state never resolved — it merely waited.

By the time Linh sought help, she had received a formal warning at work for missing a critical
project meeting she had avoided because the agenda included a difficult conversation with a client.
She sat across from the intake clinician looking composed but exhausted, and what she said she most
wanted was simply to be able to smell coffee burning without believing, for those terrible seconds,
that she was about to die.
