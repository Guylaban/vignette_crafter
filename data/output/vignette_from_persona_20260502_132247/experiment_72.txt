reliable officers in her precinct. She presented for treatment approximately eight months after
being sexually assaulted by a fellow officer following a late shift. The assault took place in a
locker room she had used without incident for years — a space that had felt, until that night,
entirely ordinary.

early sessions, because there are portions she genuinely cannot retrieve — a stretch of perhaps
fifteen to twenty minutes that exists in her memory only as sensation and then silence. What she
does remember returns with a visceral completeness that she finds almost worse than forgetting: the
pressure against her lower back, the cold of the metal locker, sounds she cannot name. When these
memories surface, she reports feeling the same physical pain in her back and abdomen that she
experienced during the assault itself — a somatic echo that her body produces without her
permission. Alongside the pain comes something she describes as a fury so total it frightens her, a
rage that floods her chest and hands and that she does not trust herself to contain. It is partly
because of this — the unpredictability of what her own memory does to her — that she has come to
structure her entire waking life around the avoidance of anything that might stir strong feeling.
She avoids conflict at work and at home with a vigilance that has begun to look, to those who know
her, like indifference. Any situation that carries the charge of confrontation, she steps around or
leaves. The memory, with its pain and its rage, has taught her that emotional intensity of any kind
is a door she cannot afford to open.

What the memory has also done — and this is where the clinical picture becomes most painful — is
furnish her with material for a set of beliefs about herself that have grown more entrenched with
each passing month. She is certain, in a way that resists challenge, that another woman would not
have found herself in that locker room at that hour, would have read the situation differently,
would have left. She turns this over constantly — how her life would look now if she had taken a
different route out of the building, if she had trusted an instinct she insists she must have had.
The counterfactual thinking is relentless and always reaches the same conclusion: that the event
reveals something specific about her judgment, her body, her failure. She has come to believe she is
permanently altered — that the woman she was before is not recoverable, that what remains is a
diminished and unreliable version of herself.

These convictions have not stayed internal. Adaeze grew up embedded in a tight-knit Nigerian
diaspora community in which her career was a source of collective pride. She has withdrawn from that
community almost entirely. She does not attend church, avoids family gatherings, and has stopped
returning calls from women she has known since childhood. She experiences this withdrawal not as a
choice but as a recognition — she no longer feels she belongs among people who knew her before, and
she cannot bear the distance between who she was to them and who she now understands herself to be.
At work, the consequences have been equally concrete: she has requested desk assignments, citing a
knee injury, because returning to active patrol means re-entering the building where the assault

What finally brought Adaeze to seek help was not a single crisis but an accumulation — her husband

not know how to tell him that she no longer knew how to reach herself. She comes to treatment
frightened that the damage is exactly as permanent as she believes it to be, and holding, very
carefully, a hope she has not yet said aloud: that she might be wrong.
