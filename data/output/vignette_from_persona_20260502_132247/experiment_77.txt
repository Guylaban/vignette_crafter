Harald is a 76-year-old divorced man of European descent who has managed a mid-sized hotel in a mid-
Atlantic city for the better part of two decades. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 73, seeking help
approximately fourteen months after a toxic chemical exposure that occurred during a routine
inspection of the hotel's basement utility corridor. A maintenance contractor had improperly stored
and mixed industrial cleaning agents; when Harald opened the corridor door that morning, he was met
with a dense, acrid cloud. He recalls the burning in his throat and eyes, the way his legs gave out,
and the particular smell — sharp, synthetic, somehow sweet underneath — before losing consciousness.
He was hospitalised for four days. Physically, he recovered. Psychologically, he has not.

In the weeks following discharge, Harald noticed that his memories of the event did not cohere into
a narrative he could hold or examine. Instead, they arrived in disconnected pieces — the metallic
taste, a fragment of fluorescent light, the sound of his own coughing — that surfaced without
warning and without sequence. Most disorienting was the way the emotional intensity of those moments
could flood him without any accompanying image: he would be standing at the hotel front desk and
feel, without knowing why, the absolute certainty that he was dying. These intrusive memory
fragments, arriving without context or resolution, began to shape how he understood what had
happened to him. He had come to believe that his body could no longer be trusted — it had failed him
without warning in a place he knew intimately — and that he was fundamentally helpless to prevent
catastrophe from recurring. His body, he felt, had become something alien and unreliable, an enemy
that kept reacting as though the danger were still present.

These appraisals deepened his sense of threat. Because he could not trust his own body to signal
real danger accurately, he began treating the entire world as a potential source of harm. At home —
a modest apartment he had retreated to more and more — he remained perpetually on guard, scanning
rooms, checking locks, unable to settle. Unexpected sounds, the clatter of a dropped tray at the
hotel or a car backfiring outside, sent his heart into his throat and his mind immediately back
toward the basement corridor. The smell was the worst of it: certain cleaning products used by the
hotel's housekeeping staff could summon, with horrible fidelity, the olfactory texture of that
morning, and when that happened the threat Harald felt was no longer abstract. It was immediate,
physical, and indistinguishable from the original.

The state of heightened threat, in turn, kept the memories close to the surface. The more vigilant
he was, the more readily a fragment would break through — a sound, a smell, a flicker of fluorescent
light in a conference room — and the cycle would tighten. To manage this, Harald developed a set of
strategies that made sense to him at the time. He began keeping loud music or the television running
constantly in his apartment, filling the acoustic space so that unexpected sounds had less room to
register. He monitored his own thoughts with exhausting vigilance, scanning for signs that he was
"losing his mind," as he put it — certain that if he caught a thought early enough, he could prevent
a full intrusion. He had not returned to the basement corridor since the exposure, and he had
quietly rearranged his management duties so that a junior colleague handled all utility and
maintenance inspections.

These strategies, however, extracted their own costs. The constant noise and self-surveillance left
Harald feeling more fragmented, not less, and he had come to see his need for them as further
evidence that his body and mind were beyond his control. The avoidance had not gone unnoticed by his
staff; his withdrawal from certain operational areas had created confusion and, eventually, a formal
complaint about managerial inconsistency. He now feels safe only in a narrow circuit — his office,
his apartment, a single café nearby — and the shrinking of that circuit has confirmed, in his mind,
the very helplessness he fears.

What finally brought Harald to seek help was a conversation with his daughter, who lives abroad and
noticed during a video call that he had lost considerable weight and had not left his apartment in
nine days. What he most fears is that he will never recover his sense of competence or safety. What
he most hopes is simply to be able to walk back into his own hotel without feeling that he is about
to die.
