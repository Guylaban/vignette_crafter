Adaeze is a 35-year-old Nigerian-American woman who works as a private tutor, primarily with
elementary-aged children. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 55 following a rape that occurred
approximately fourteen months ago. She was assaulted by an acquaintance in her own apartment on an
evening she had initially described as unremarkable — she had been grading worksheets when he
arrived uninvited. During the assault, a child in the neighbouring unit began crying loudly through
the wall, and that sound continued, unanswered, for the duration of what happened to her. She has
not been able to explain to anyone why that detail feels so central to what she carries now. She
cannot fully put the experience into words at all — the memory resists language, surfacing instead
as sensation, as the particular weight of helplessness, as a physical nausea that arrives without
warning. When she does attempt to recall the event, the terror does not diminish with distance; it
returns at full force, as though no time has passed. And yet the memory also feels strangely
dissociated from her — as if it happened to another woman, someone she watches from a remove, which
makes it simultaneously unreal and inescapable.

It is the memory's unprocessed, fragmented quality that makes her daily work so treacherous. Adaeze
tutors children after school, and when one of her young students cries — over a frustrating math
problem, over a scraped knee — the sound reaches her like a physical blow. In those moments, the
incomplete memory surges forward, and with it comes a cascade of appraisals she has come to
experience as simply true: that she is permanently changed, that something essential in her has been
broken beyond repair, and that she cannot manage even this small upset without falling apart. The
crying does not remind her of the assault so much as it briefly collapses the distance between then
and now, and the certainty of her own damage arrives with the same force as the original terror.

Those appraisals — that she is ruined, that she cannot cope — have come to govern nearly every
decision she makes. She has come to believe that if she allows herself to think carefully about what
happened, she will not survive the thinking. This conviction has made her a dedicated avoider: she
changes the subject when friends or her partner attempt to discuss what occurred, she declines
social invitations that might carry the risk of genuine pleasure, and she has quietly stopped
attending the community gatherings her mother hosts on Sunday afternoons. Her partner has noticed
her withdrawal and named it, gently, more than once. She has not been able to explain it to him,
partly because she cannot find the words, and partly because she is certain that if he understood
how thoroughly she has changed, he would leave.

The avoidance, however, has not protected her. By refusing to engage with the memory, she has kept
it raw and unintegrated, which means it continues to ambush her through the body — the nausea that
rises when something is uncertain or unresolved, the way an unscheduled change to a tutoring session
can send her into a spiral she cannot account for. Her rumination runs alongside the avoidance
rather than replacing it: she returns, repeatedly, to what she should have done differently that
evening, rehearsing her own culpability with a thoroughness that keeps the memory active without
ever processing it. This self-blame, in turn, reinforces her conviction that she is permanently
damaged — that the assault happened because of something in her, and that the damage is therefore
her own to carry. The loop tightens: the more she blames herself, the more she avoids; the more she
avoids, the more the memory remains fragmented and potent; the more potent the memory, the more
unbearable the triggers become; and the more unbearable the triggers, the more certain she is that
she cannot cope.

What finally brought Adaeze to seek help was a session last month in which a child began crying
inconsolably and she had to excuse herself to the bathroom, where she sat on the floor for twenty
minutes, shaking. She told her partner she had a migraine. She is most afraid that therapy will
require her to speak what she cannot yet put into words. What she is most hoping for — though she
would not yet say this aloud — is that the woman she was before that evening might still, somehow,
be recoverable.
