Adaeze Okonkwo is a fifty-four-year-old Nigerian-American federal judge, married for twenty-six
years, who presented for psychological evaluation following what she described, with characteristic
precision, as "a functional deterioration I can no longer attribute to stress." Her PCL-5 score of
57 places her firmly within the severe range of posttraumatic stress, and the clinical picture that
emerged across the initial sessions was one of a woman whose interior life had become profoundly
estranged from the authoritative, composed self she projects from the bench.

Approximately fourteen months before seeking help, Adaeze was taken during an overseas trip to visit
extended family. She was held for several days by individuals whose identities she never fully
established. What she recalls — and the word "recalls" requires qualification — comes in fragments:
the particular weight of a man leaning over her, his breath slow and deliberate against her face; a
cologne, heavy and synthetic, that seemed to saturate the room; the feeling of her wrists bound
behind her, the pressure of the restraints cutting into her skin each time she shifted. She was
subjected to sustained physical and psychological coercion. She was eventually released, and the
official account she gave at the time was minimal. She told almost no one what had happened.

What is clinically striking about Adaeze's memory of the event is not its vividness but its absence.
When she sits quietly and attempts to reconstruct what occurred — to build a coherent narrative the
way she builds arguments in her written opinions — she finds almost nothing there. The memory
resists deliberate retrieval; it will not be summoned. There are stretches of the captivity she
simply cannot account for, gaps that remain opaque no matter how she approaches them. And when
fragments do surface, they feel dissociated from her — she has described the experience as reading a
case file about a stranger, disturbing but not quite hers. This alien quality to the memory has
itself become a source of distress, because it means she cannot locate the event, cannot file it
away, cannot trust that she knows what was done to her.

The memory's inaccessibility does not protect her. Instead, the fragmentary, unintegrated nature of
what she retains means that her nervous system remains exquisitely responsive to the sensory residue
of those days. Because the memory has never been processed into a coherent, time-stamped narrative,
its sensory components function as live signals rather than archived ones — and so the triggers
arrive with the full force of present danger. In the courtroom, when a defendant's attorney leans
close to whisper to a colleague and she catches the sound of heavy, deliberate breathing amplified
by the room's acoustics, Adaeze has found herself gripping the edge of the bench, her pulse spiking,
the fluorescent lights suddenly too bright. A cologne worn by a bailiff she has worked with for
years caused her to recess an afternoon hearing without explanation. And on one occasion, when a
marshal placed a steadying hand on her arm during a crowded corridor exchange, the tactile sensation
of being held — however briefly — produced a wave of terror she described as "total and sourceless."

The cycle compounds itself. Each time a trigger breaks through, the sensory intrusion does not
retrieve a full memory so much as it stirs the unresolved fragments further, leaving Adaeze with the
disorienting sensation of being almost-reminded of something she cannot quite reach. The intrusion
activates the memory system without resolving it, and the memory's fragmented, alien quality ensures
that the triggers remain uncontextualized and therefore perpetually threatening. She had come to
believe, quietly and with the logic of someone trained to draw inferences from evidence, that the
gaps in her memory meant something worse had happened than she could consciously access — and that
not knowing was itself the most dangerous condition she could be in.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning, three weeks before her intake appointment, when
she realized she had spent forty minutes reviewing a routine motion without reading a single word.
She is most afraid, she said, of losing her competence. What she is most hoping for, though she
offered this only at the end of the session and almost as an aside, is to feel that the memory
belongs to her again.
