Marcus is a fifty-year-old European American psychologist who has spent the better part of his
career helping others make sense of their worst experiences. He is married, professionally
respected, and had, until recently, understood trauma largely from the other side of the consulting
room. That changed approximately eight months ago when Marcus was sexually assaulted by an
acquaintance at a professional conference — raped in a hotel room late at night, the man's breath
heavy with alcohol, the sound of a distant ambulance siren cutting through the city outside the
window as it happened. He did not report the assault. He drove home the following morning, told no
one, and returned to work on Monday.

The weeks that followed were marked by a strange blankness that Marcus initially mistook for
composure. He continued seeing patients, writing notes, attending supervision. But the memory of
what had happened resisted any coherent form. He found he could not put it into words — not in any
sentence that felt real — and when fragments surfaced they arrived not as images but as pure
emotional weight: a sudden crush of shame, a wave of something he could only describe as
annihilation, with no picture attached to it, no narrative to contain it. When he did try to recall
the event more fully, something in him intervened before he could complete the thought, as though
his mind were pulling a door shut just before the room came into view. He came to understand, in an
abstract clinical way, what was happening to him — and that understanding made it worse, because it
changed nothing.

What he had not anticipated was how ordinary the world would become unbearable. Walking past a bar
on his way to the office, the smell of alcohol drifting through an open door, was enough to stop him
on the pavement. The smell returned him — not to an image, but to the fact of what had been done to
him, and in those moments the appraisals arrived with a force that felt like verdict rather than
thought: he was not a person anymore, not in any meaningful sense. He was something that had been
used. The sirens were perhaps worse — unpredictable, impossible to avoid — and when one passed while
he was sitting with a patient, he would feel the room tilt and have to press his feet into the floor
to stay present. These triggered states, with their freight of dehumanisation and incapacity, did
not pass quickly. They left him saturated in a shame that made the traumatic material feel even more
inaccessible, as though the appraisals themselves were sealing the memory further from reach — the
worse he felt about what he was, the less he could bear to look at what had happened.

And the reverse was equally true. The fragmented, wordless quality of the memory — its refusal to
become a story he could examine or metabolise — kept the appraisals alive. Because he could not
construct a coherent account of the event, he could not test it against anything. The belief that he
was no longer capable of being the clinician, the husband, the person he had been remained unopposed
by any narrative that might complicate it. He had come to feel dead inside in a way he recognised
from patient descriptions but had never imagined for himself — a flatness that was not peace but the
absence of anything he had previously called himself.

Over months, the appraisals themselves began to function as triggers. Sitting with a patient who
described feeling objectified, or supervising a trainee on a trauma case, Marcus would feel the
familiar sensation of unreality descend — not prompted by a smell or a sound but by the internal
state of feeling like an object rather than a subject. General discomfort, even mild, had become a
signal his nervous system could no longer distinguish from danger.

It was his wife who finally named what she was seeing. She told him, quietly, that he had become
someone she did not recognise. He made the referral to a colleague the following week — not because
he believed it would help, he said at intake, but because he no longer trusted his own judgment
about what he was capable of. What he feared most was that she was right.
