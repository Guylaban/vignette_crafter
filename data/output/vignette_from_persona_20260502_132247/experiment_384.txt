Valentina is a 47-year-old Salvadoran American woman who has been married for nineteen years and
works as a social ministry worker at a Catholic community center in her city. She came to the intake
session speaking quietly, her hands folded tightly in her lap, and described herself as someone who
used to believe that helping others was the purpose that held her life together. That sense of
purpose, she said, now feels like something she is only performing.

Approximately eight months ago, Valentina was raped by a man she had known professionally — a
contractor who had been doing repair work at the community center. The assault occurred in the late
afternoon, in a storage room at the back of the building, after the rest of the staff had left. What
she remembers is not a continuous event but a series of frozen frames: the overhead light
flickering, the sound of tools being set down on a metal shelf, the particular quality of silence
that filled the room just before he moved toward her. That silence — thick, suspended, wrong — is
the detail that has lodged most deeply in her nervous system. She has no fluid narrative of what
happened. She told the clinician that she knows what occurred but cannot find the words to describe
it, as though language itself has been revoked. When she tries to recall it, what surfaces are those
still images, and with them, an immediate and overwhelming wave of guilt — a conviction that she
should have left earlier, should have known, should have done something different.

Those guilt-saturated memories have fed directly into the beliefs that now organize her daily life.
Valentina has come to feel that she deserves to suffer for what happened, a punishment logic she
applies with quiet consistency. She restricts her eating carefully, finding in that control
something that feels like the only domain in which she has agency. She avoids anything that would
allow her body or mind to soften — rest, leisure, stillness — because relaxing feels to her not like
safety but like exposure, like lowering a guard she cannot afford to lower. These strategies, in
turn, have kept the memories from being processed in any narrative or integrated way; by never
allowing herself to settle, she ensures that the frozen images remain exactly as they were,
unexamined and uncontextualized, ready to resurface.

The memories also sustain a vigilance that has become exhausting. Valentina now experiences the
silence of a room — any room, including the office where she meets with community members in need —
as a signal that something dangerous is about to happen. When a colleague is late returning from
lunch, or when a meeting runs past its scheduled time, she assumes something terrible is unfolding.
She cannot relax even when she knows, intellectually, that she is safe. This chronic threat state
has in turn reinforced her appraisals: she has concluded that the world is genuinely dangerous, that
her perception of threat is accurate rather than distorted, and that she was foolish ever to have
believed otherwise. The hypervigilance and the beliefs tighten around each other — the threat
confirms the appraisals, and the appraisals confirm the threat — and her strategies of restriction
and refusal to rest keep both elevated, preventing any disconfirmation from reaching her.

The appraisals themselves have become their own source of suffering. Valentina describes having lost
her faith — in people, in the Church she served, in God — and says that the woman she was before,
someone she remembers as genuinely happy, feels like a stranger. She carries a deep shame about what
happened to her body, a shame she has not disclosed to her husband, who knows only that she has been
"going through something." The distance this has created in their marriage is one of the concrete
consequences she named: they no longer sleep easily beside each other, and she has declined every
invitation to social gatherings their parish community organizes.

What finally brought Valentina to seek help was a moment at work three weeks ago when she froze
completely in the doorway of the storage room — now used by a different staff member — and could not
move for several minutes. A colleague witnessed it. She is most afraid, she said, that she will
never be able to do her work with full presence again. What she is hoping for, more quietly, is to
find words for what happened.
