Aroha Tane is a 27-year-old non-binary special education teacher of Māori and Pacific Islander
descent, currently living with their partner and presenting for an initial psychological assessment
following a period of prolonged stalking that ended approximately eight months ago. Their PCL-5
score of 34 places them in the clinical range, and across several intake sessions they have
described a presentation that has quietly but steadily narrowed their world.

The stalking began during Aroha's second year of teaching, when a former acquaintance began
appearing outside their school, sending messages, and following them on their commute. For nearly
four months, Aroha never knew when this person would materialise — behind them on a footpath,
visible through a classroom window, or simply a presence they sensed before they could confirm. The
experience was defined not by a single violent moment but by its relentlessness: the ordinary sounds
of a school hallway — footsteps approaching from behind, a door opening unexpectedly — became
inseparable from dread. By the time the stalking was formally reported and ceased, Aroha's nervous
system had been tuned to a frequency it could not easily abandon.

Aroha's memories of those months are not a coherent sequence but a series of shards. Some moments
are extraordinarily vivid — the particular sound of a heel strike on linoleum that made them freeze
mid-sentence during a lesson — while entire weeks are simply absent, blank spaces where continuity
should be. They describe feeling like they are only in possession of parts of what happened, with
the rest irretrievable. This fragmentation is itself distressing: when a trigger arrives in the
present — the sound of footsteps behind them in a corridor, the sensation of being watched across
the open-plan staffroom — it does not summon a clear memory so much as it detonates these
disconnected pieces, flooding Aroha with sensation without narrative context, making the past feel
simultaneously present and incomprehensible.

The triggers Aroha encounters most acutely are woven into the fabric of their working day. A school
is a place of constant movement and sound: children running, colleagues crossing behind them, the
shuffle of feet in a crowded hallway. Each of these moments activates the same vigilance the
stalking originally required. When Aroha senses they are being followed or observed — even by a
student simply walking behind them to a resource shelf — the fragmented memories surface, and in
response Aroha begins scanning their environment compulsively, reading every doorway and peripheral
movement for confirmation of threat. This scanning, which offers momentary relief, has over months
become its own engine of distress: the more carefully Aroha monitors their surroundings, the more
anomalies they detect, and the more anomalies they detect, the more convinced they become that the
danger is ongoing and real.

Aroha has also come to believe that relaxing their guard even briefly is genuinely dangerous — that
the moment they stop watching is the moment something will happen. They avoid any state of ease
during the school day, and often into the evening at home. They ruminate frequently about whether
they could have identified the stalker's pattern earlier and acted sooner, replaying the incomplete
fragments of memory in search of the warning signs they are certain they missed. This rumination,
rather than resolving anything, keeps the memories active and raw, which in turn sustains the
hypervigilance that makes every subsequent trigger feel like confirmation. Sudden movements by
colleagues or students put Aroha immediately on guard in a way they find difficult to explain or
conceal, and they have begun positioning themselves with their back to walls during team meetings —
a habit their colleagues have noticed without comment, but which has quietly altered how Aroha moves
through their professional life.

What finally brought Aroha to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when a child in their class ran
up behind them unexpectedly and Aroha startled so severely that they dropped everything they were
holding and had to leave the room. Standing in the corridor afterwards, they thought: this is no
longer just about me. They came to therapy hoping to recover something they can no longer quite name
— a version of themselves that could simply walk down a hallway without listening for what might be
following.
