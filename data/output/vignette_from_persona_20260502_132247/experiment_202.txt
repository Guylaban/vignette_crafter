Declan Harrow is a 29-year-old Coast Guard serviceman of Irish-American descent who presented for
psychological assessment following a mugging that occurred approximately seven months ago. He had
been in a relationship for two years and described his partner as broadly supportive, though he
acknowledged that he had found it increasingly difficult to explain to her what he was going
through. His PCL-5 score of 69 placed him firmly in the severe range, and his presentation was
notable for the unusual quality of his traumatic memory — not its vividness, but its profound
incoherence.

The incident occurred on a late evening in early winter when Declan was returning on foot from a
waterfront bar near his station. Two men approached him in a poorly lit side street, and within
seconds the encounter turned violent. He was struck from behind, thrown against a chain-link fence,
and held at knifepoint while his wallet and phone were taken. The physical injuries were minor, but
something about the way the event unfolded — the sudden loss of agency, the cold metal against his
neck, the smell of diesel and salt water in the air — appeared to have fractured the experience in
ways that defied ordinary processing. Declan told the intake clinician that he had tried, on several
occasions, to write down what happened, and that each time he sat with a pen in hand, nothing came.
He could feel the event somewhere inside him — a pressure, a weight — but the words simply were not
there. "It's like trying to describe a colour you've never seen," he said.

What made this particularly disorienting was that the memories did not arrive as a coherent
narrative. They came instead as disconnected pieces — the sound of footsteps behind him, the
sensation of gravel under his palms, a flash of a streetlamp's orange glow — fragments that surfaced
without context or sequence, each one vivid in isolation but impossible to assemble into a whole. He
could not say with certainty what came first or last. He was not even sure, in some moments, whether
what he remembered was the event itself or something his mind had constructed around it.

Complicating this further were the episodes of emotionless recall that had begun to disturb him in
recent months. There were times when Declan could narrate portions of what happened in a flat,
detached voice, as though recounting something that had happened to someone else entirely — a
stranger he had read about in a report. His partner had noticed this and found it unsettling. She
had expected tears, anger, something. Instead, she sometimes got a kind of clinical recitation,
delivered without affect, after which Declan would fall silent for the rest of the evening. He
himself found these episodes deeply alienating. He was certain that he should feel something, and
the absence of feeling read to him not as relief but as evidence that something fundamental had
broken inside him.

His work in the Coast Guard placed him in an environment that quietly sustained this fragmentation.
The waterfront — the diesel smell, the chain-link fencing around equipment yards, the darkness of
early morning shifts — carried sensory echoes of the night of the mugging, though Declan could not
always articulate why a particular moment on duty left him feeling hollowed out and vaguely
dissociated. He had not requested reassignment or disclosed the full extent of his difficulties to
his commanding officer, partly out of professional pride and partly because he could not find the
language to explain what was wrong. How do you report an injury you cannot describe?

What finally brought him to seek help was a conversation with his partner in which she told him,
gently but plainly, that she felt she was losing him — that he was present in the apartment but
absent in every way that mattered. Declan sat with that for several days before making the call.
What he was most afraid of, he said in the intake session, was that the pieces would never come
together — that he would spend the rest of his life carrying something he could neither name nor put
down.
