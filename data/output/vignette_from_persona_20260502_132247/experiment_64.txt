Fatima is a seventy-one-year-old Yemeni-American woman who has worked as a nanny for the same family
in a quiet residential neighborhood for nearly a decade. She is married, soft-spoken, and was known
among the children she cared for as someone who moved through the world with steady, unhurried
warmth. That sense of steadiness was shattered approximately fourteen months ago when, while walking
two of the children to a neighborhood park, she witnessed a violent assault on a young man just
ahead of her on the path. Several men surrounded and beat him with brutal efficiency, and though she
pulled the children close and turned them away, she could not move — her body locked in place as she
watched, helpless, unable to intervene or call out. The assailants left without consequence. No one
else on the street acted. When police arrived, they took a brief statement and, as far as Fatima
could tell, nothing further was done. The young man was taken away in an ambulance. She never
learned what became of him.

In the weeks that followed, Fatima found she could not return to that park. She began routing the
children's walks in wide arcs around the block where it happened, adding fifteen minutes each way
and offering cheerful, invented reasons to the family when asked. The detour became fixed, automatic
— a private geography of avoidance that now shapes her entire working day. The neighborhood streets
she once walked with ease have become a map of threat, and she scans them constantly, watching other
pedestrians for any shift in posture or gaze that might signal danger. She frequently glances behind
her, certain at times that someone is following, and even inside the family's home she positions
herself near windows or doorways, monitoring. This hypervigilance is exhausting, but to Fatima it
feels like the only reasonable response — the only thing standing between the children and a world
that had proved, in a single afternoon, that it could turn violent without warning.

What frightened her most, however, was not the memory itself but what she had come to believe it
revealed about her. She had been right there. She had done nothing. In her mind, the failure was
total and irrefutable: she was inadequate, a woman charged with protecting children who had stood
frozen while violence unfolded in front of her. The conviction that she should have protected others
and had failed settled into her like something permanent, and it colored everything. The men
responsible had never been identified, never faced any consequence — a fact she returned to again
and again, as though the absence of justice confirmed that the weight of what happened would fall
entirely on her instead.

These appraisals drove her further inward. She began suppressing any thought that surfaced from that
afternoon, pressing it down with deliberate effort, and she became increasingly compliant in her
interactions with the family and with strangers — agreeing to whatever was asked, avoiding any
friction, as though keeping others satisfied might keep her safe. Yet the very strategies she used
to manage her distress quietly deepened her shame. The thought suppression never held for long, and
when intrusive images broke through, she read them as evidence that she was weak, unable to cope,
inadequate in yet another register. People-pleasing, which had begun as a way to feel less
threatened, left her feeling invisible and diminished — and that diminishment folded back into the
belief that she was simply not enough.

The threat monitoring itself became a source of further self-reproach. When she caught herself
scanning a stranger's face or checking the lock on the family's front door for the third time in an
hour, she felt both frightened and contemptible — certain she was becoming someone unreliable,
someone the children could not truly depend on. The loop had tightened over months without her
naming it.

What finally brought Fatima to seek help was a morning when the youngest child, aged four, asked why
she always looked so scared. She could not answer him. She is hoping, tentatively, that someone can
help her feel safe again in the neighborhood where she works — and she is afraid, most of all, that
it may already be too late to become the person she was before.
