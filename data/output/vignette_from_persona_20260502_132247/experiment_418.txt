Linh Tran is a 29-year-old Vietnamese-American combat pilot, currently stationed at a domestic air
base, who presented for psychological evaluation following a referral from her unit's flight
surgeon. She arrived composed but visibly guarded, speaking in measured, economical sentences — the
manner of someone who has spent considerable effort managing what she allows herself to say.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Linh was on approved leave when she received a call that her
younger brother, Minh, had been critically injured in a vehicle collision. By the time she reached
the hospital, the situation had deteriorated. She stood in a corridor watching through a window as
medical staff worked on him, helpless in a way she had never been in a cockpit. Minh survived, but
sustained a severe traumatic brain injury that has left him with profound cognitive impairments. For
Linh, who had always been the older sibling — the protector, the one who got out and made something
of herself so she could take care of him — the experience was not simply grief. It was, she came to
believe, evidence of a fundamental failure in who she was.

In the weeks that followed, Linh found that the memory of that hospital corridor had a quality
unlike ordinary recollection. It did not arrive as a narrative she could choose to revisit; it
arrived as an image — Minh's face through the glass, the particular blue of the surgical draping —
as vivid and fixed as a photograph she could not put down. At other moments it unspooled like
footage she was forced to watch in full, every detail preserved, every second accounted for. These
intrusive replays were not confined to quiet moments; something as incidental as the antiseptic
smell in the base medical bay, or a colleague mentioning a family member in passing, could instantly
collapse the distance between the present and that corridor, pulling her back without warning or
transition.

Those moments of involuntary return deepened her conviction that something was fundamentally wrong
with her. A pilot who cannot hold her mind in the present, she reasoned, is a pilot who cannot be
trusted — and a sister who stood in a hallway while her brother's life hung in the balance had
already proven she could not be trusted when it mattered most. The belief that she had failed to
protect Minh, and the broader sense that she was defective in some essential way, were not
conclusions she reasoned her way into; they felt self-evident, confirmed every time the memory
returned unbidden.

To manage the intrusions, Linh began working hard to suppress any thought that led toward Minh's
injury. She avoided conversations with colleagues who knew her family situation, and when others
spoke about loss — a pilot who lost a parent, a news story about a fatal accident — she would excuse
herself or go silent, routing around the topic with practiced efficiency. She declined two
invitations to join a peer support group and has since stopped accepting social overtures from new
colleagues, certain that closeness would only create more exposure to the kinds of conversations she
cannot afford to have. Over months, this withdrawal has narrowed her world considerably. Fellow
pilots have noticed her increasing distance; a close friend from training has stopped reaching out.
The irony she cannot quite articulate is that the harder she works not to think about the event, the
more insistently it surfaces — suppression, for Linh, functions less like a lid and more like
pressure, and the memories that break through feel more disorienting for having been held back. Each
intrusion, in turn, reinforces her certainty that she is broken in a way others are not, that she
cannot rely on anyone to understand what she carries, and that the appropriate response is to carry
it alone.

What finally brought her to the flight surgeon's office was a near-miss during a training sortie — a
moment of dissociation she has not fully described but that frightened her enough to override her
resistance to disclosure. She is most afraid, she said quietly at the end of the intake session,
that she will never be able to fly operationally again. What she did not say, but what seemed to sit
just beneath the surface, was a fear that she might not deserve to.
