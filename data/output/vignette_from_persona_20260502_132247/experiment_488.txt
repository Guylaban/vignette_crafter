Fatima is a 38-year-old married woman of Lebanese descent who works as a housekeeper in a mid-sized
hotel in the outer suburbs of Detroit. She was referred for psychological assessment following a
physical assault that occurred approximately seven months ago, when a male guest at the hotel
cornered her in a room she was cleaning and attacked her. The assault was prolonged. He had locked
the door behind her, and when she attempted to leave, he blocked her path and forced her back. She
was struck repeatedly and sustained injuries to her ribs and left arm before another staff member
eventually knocked on the door and the man withdrew. Fatima had screamed during the attack — and had
been screamed at — the guest's voice rising to a pitch she described as something she could still
hear inside her skull. She was taken to a hospital, treated, and sent home. She returned to work
within two weeks because the family needed the income. That decision, she would later come to
believe, was the beginning of something she could not undo.

The memory of the assault does not come to Fatima as a coherent story. Some moments are preserved
with terrible precision — the sound of the lock turning, the exact texture of the bedspread she
grabbed when she fell — while others are simply absent, leaving gaps she cannot account for. She
knows something happened in one stretch of several minutes that she cannot retrieve, and the not-
knowing frightens her almost as much as what she does remember. When fragments do surface, they
arrive with a physical accompaniment: a sudden burning along her left forearm, a tightness across
her ribs that mirrors the injuries she sustained. The body, it seems, has kept its own record. These
intrusive memory fragments are themselves potent triggers — when the physical pain returns without
warning, it pulls the rest of the trauma context with it, and Fatima finds herself back inside the
locked room, her nervous system indistinguishable from that afternoon.

It is this quality of the memory — partial, somatic, unpredictable — that makes certain features of
her daily life so dangerous to her. She cannot tolerate being told what to do in a sharp or
insistent tone; when a supervisor directs her curtly or a guest makes a demand she has no choice but
to comply with, the loss of control it represents is enough to flood her with alarm. Shouting, even
at a distance — a television in a guest room, an argument in a corridor — stops her completely. And
when she passes the supply cart of a colleague who was herself once a victim of something Fatima
only vaguely knows about, even that associative reminder is enough to destabilise her. Each of these
triggers — the coercion, the raised voice, the reminder of victimhood — immediately activates a
state of acute threat, as though the assault is not a past event but a present condition that has
simply been briefly suspended.

Once that threat state is activated, Fatima's attention reorganises itself entirely around danger.
She scans every room she enters before she begins work, checking corners, noting exits, listening
for footsteps. At home in the evenings she monitors sounds at the front door, interpreting the
ordinary creaks of the building as potential intrusions. She cannot sustain concentration on
anything — a conversation with her husband, a task she has performed a hundred times — because some
part of her mind refuses to be fully present in any moment that is not also being surveilled for
threat. And here the loop tightens: the hypervigilant state itself generates new triggers, because
sustained scanning means she is perpetually primed to find what she is looking for. A door closing
too firmly, a man raising his voice two rooms away — things she might once have processed and
released now land with the full weight of confirmation.

What finally brought Fatima to seek help was a morning three weeks ago when she froze entirely in a
hotel corridor after a male guest shouted at a colleague nearby. She stood motionless for what her
supervisor later told her was nearly four minutes. She has not been back to work since. What she
fears most is that she is becoming someone her husband and children no longer recognise. What she
hopes, more tentatively, is that the missing parts of the memory might eventually stop feeling like
a threat in themselves.
