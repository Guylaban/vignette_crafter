Valentina Reyes is a 44-year-old Colombian-American diplomat, currently stationed at a mid-level
consular post in Washington, D.C., who presented for psychological evaluation following a referral
from her agency's employee assistance program. Her PCL-5 score of 51 places her firmly within the
clinically significant range for PTSD, and the picture that emerged across the intake and early
sessions was one of a woman whose professional identity and inner life had been quietly hollowing
out over the better part of two years.

Approximately eighteen months ago, Valentina was part of a small diplomatic delegation conducting
humanitarian observation work in a conflict region in Latin America. During a site visit to a rural
area, the delegation was detained at an improvised checkpoint by an armed faction. Valentina was
physically restrained — her wrists bound, her body pressed against a concrete wall by two soldiers
while a superior officer questioned her colleagues at close range. The detention lasted several
hours. During that time, she witnessed a member of a local civilian group being struck for
attempting to speak on the delegation's behalf. The soldiers laughed. The officer gave no
explanation for the detention and no acknowledgment of the delegation's diplomatic credentials. They
were eventually released without formal incident, which in the official record meant no incident at
all. Valentina returned to her post and said very little.

The triggers that now reach into her daily life are inseparable from what happened at that
checkpoint. Any conversation touching on military affairs — briefings, policy discussions, even
casual remarks from colleagues about defense matters — can send her back to that wall. The physical
sensation of being held, even lightly, such as a colleague's hand on her shoulder during a crowded
reception, produces an immediate visceral response that she struggles to name and cannot predict.
And perhaps most destabilizing of all, witnessing even minor social transgressions — someone
speaking dismissively to a junior staff member, a superior cutting off a subordinate in a meeting —
floods her with something that is not quite anger and not quite fear, but carries the texture of
both. These moments do not merely remind her of the event; they seem to confirm something she has
come to believe about the world and about herself.

When these triggers surface, they reliably pull Valentina into a cluster of beliefs that have
settled into her thinking like sediment. She had come to feel that she was no longer a person in any
meaningful sense — that the soldiers at the checkpoint had treated her as an object to be managed,
and that this was, in some way she could not fully articulate, simply accurate. She no longer
trusted her own judgment in high-stakes situations, certain that she would fail to act rightly when
it mattered. And the work that had once given her life its shape — negotiating, advocating,
representing — now felt emptied of significance. Nothing in her professional life felt like it meant
anything anymore. These appraisals were not static; they were activated and deepened each time a
trigger appeared, the dehumanization and self-doubt sharpening in the aftermath of any encounter
that echoed the checkpoint.

Carrying these beliefs, Valentina had developed a set of mental habits that she understood as
protection but that had come to sustain the very distress they were meant to contain. She spent long
stretches of time going over why the event had happened to her specifically — whether something in
her conduct had invited it, whether she had failed to read the situation correctly. She pressed hard
against any spontaneous thought about the detention, trying to keep it below the surface of her
working mind. But the effort of suppression and the circling of rumination kept the trauma
cognitively alive, and this sustained activation meant that the triggers found her more easily — the
threshold for what could pull her back grew lower over months, not higher.

The occupational cost had become impossible to ignore. Valentina had begun declining assignments
that required attending multilateral security briefings, citing scheduling conflicts that did not
exist. A senior colleague had recently noted her withdrawal from exactly the kind of substantive
diplomatic work she had been promoted to do. It was this observation, delivered with concern rather
than criticism, that finally moved her to seek help. What she feared most was that she was no longer
capable of the work — or of being the person who had once believed that work mattered.
