Kofi is a 23-year-old Ghanaian-American urban planner who presented for treatment following a severe
assault that occurred approximately fourteen months ago. He was attacked late one evening outside a
bar by a group of men, at least one of whom had been drinking heavily. The assault began when one of
the men threw a glass bottle that struck Kofi on the shoulder, and what followed was a sustained and
brutal beating during which he was unable to defend himself or escape. He recalls the smell of
alcohol on his attacker's breath as the man leaned over him, and he remembers the sound of shouting
— voices distorted by rage — before losing consciousness briefly. When police arrived, they filed a
report but made no arrests, and Kofi was left with the understanding that the men who had hurt him
would face no consequences. He was discharged from the emergency department the same night with
instructions to follow up with his GP, which he did not do.

In the weeks that followed, Kofi found that the memory did not come to him as a continuous
narrative. Instead, it arrived in fragments — a frozen image of the bottle leaving the man's hand, a
close-up of a face contorted in anger, the specific quality of the pavement against his cheek — each
one vivid and complete in itself, but disconnected from the others. When these images surfaced, the
emotional weight they carried was so immediate and total that he had to stop whatever he was doing.
He could not find words adequate to what had happened; when he tried to tell a friend, what came out
felt thin and wrong, and he stopped trying. This inability to organise or articulate the memory
meant that it remained unprocessed, and its intrusive return began to feel less like remembering and
less like reliving — and the boundary between past and present grew increasingly unreliable. The
fragmented nature of the memory also seemed to keep his nervous system perpetually primed, as though
the unresolved images signalled to his body that the threat was ongoing.

At work, Kofi spends much of his time in open-plan offices and community consultation meetings,
environments where disagreement is routine and voices occasionally rise. He had come to believe that
any expression of anger — even a colleague's mild frustration across a conference table — was a
signal that violence was imminent. He began arriving early to secure a seat near the exit and would
find reasons to leave meetings before they grew contentious. A co-worker's habit of tossing a pen
onto the desk when he was thinking could send Kofi's heart into his throat, his body already halfway
out of the chair before his mind caught up. The startle responses were relentless: unexpected
movement in his peripheral vision, a door closing behind him, someone stepping into his sightline
too quickly — each one landed as a genuine threat, and no amount of reassurance from colleagues or
friends could convince him otherwise. He was certain, in a way that felt beyond argument, that
safety was simply not something that applied to him.

These appraisals — that he was fundamentally unprotected, that institutions had failed him, that he
lacked the capacity to cope with ordinary difficulty — fed directly into the strategies he had
developed to manage his days. He avoided any situation that might involve conflict, which had begun
to limit his professional effectiveness significantly; he had recently declined to lead a community
engagement process he had spent months preparing for, telling his supervisor he was unwell. He had
also begun gambling online in the evenings, drawn by the way the focused excitement of it
temporarily crowded out the images and the dread. But the gambling left him feeling worse about
himself the following morning, which deepened his conviction that he was someone who made bad
decisions and could not be trusted to manage his own life. This self-blame, rehearsed nightly, kept
the negative appraisals vivid and close, which in turn kept his threat system activated — so that
the next day he arrived at work already braced, already scanning, and the cycle resumed. The
avoidance, paradoxically, had also increased his sensitivity: by never sitting with the discomfort
of conflict or anger, he had ensured that each encounter with it felt more dangerous than the last,
and the triggers that had once been specific — the smell of alcohol, a thrown object — had
broadened.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a moment at a neighbourhood planning meeting three weeks
ago when a resident raised his voice in frustration and Kofi walked out of the room, sat in his car
for forty minutes, and could not return. He told the intake coordinator that he did not expect to
feel better, exactly — he was not sure he believed that was possible — but that he could not
continue losing pieces of his life to something he could not even properly describe.
