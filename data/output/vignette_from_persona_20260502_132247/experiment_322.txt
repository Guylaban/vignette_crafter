Rodrigo Mendes is a 58-year-old forensic analyst of Colombian descent who has spent the better part
of three decades studying the aftermath of violence with a professional detachment he once
considered one of his defining strengths. He is married, the father of two adult children, and until
approximately fourteen months ago, he was the kind of man who walked into a crime scene the way
other people walk into a familiar room — purposeful, composed, certain of what he would find and how
he would respond to it. That certainty ended on the afternoon a gas main explosion tore through a
municipal building two blocks from the county forensics lab where he works, a disaster that killed
eleven people and left Rodrigo standing in the street amid collapsing facades, screaming sirens, and
the particular quality of silence that follows a pressure wave — the few seconds when the world
seems to have forgotten how to make sound. He had been outside on a routine errand. The force of the
blast knocked him from his feet. When he got up, the street he had walked a hundred times looked
like something from the case files on his desk, and for a moment he could not locate himself in it
at all.

In the weeks that followed, Rodrigo found that his body had not returned with him to the life he had
before. He began waking in the early hours with his heart already racing, his muscles braced for
impact, as though his nervous system had decided that sleep was a lapse in vigilance he could not
afford. At the lab, the sudden bang of a heavy door or the percussive thud of a colleague dropping a
binder was enough to send a visible flinch through him — a response he found humiliating in a man
whose professional identity had been built on steadiness. He began to feel, at all times, that his
body was running a threat calculation he had not authorised, scanning for danger in the fluorescent
corridors of a building he had entered without anxiety for twenty-two years.

Outside the lab, the hypervigilance extended further. Rodrigo stopped accepting assignments that
required site visits to unfamiliar locations, telling supervisors he preferred to work from
recovered materials. In truth, the thought of entering a space he had not already mentally mapped —
a building he did not know the exits of, a neighbourhood whose sightlines he could not read —
produced a dread so immediate that avoidance felt less like a choice than a necessity. His world
contracted. The drive to work, the lab itself, the route home: these were the coordinates of a life
that had grown very small.

What troubled Rodrigo as much as the symptoms themselves was what he had come to believe they meant
about him. He had spent his career analysing scenes of catastrophe with precision and calm; he had
always assumed that if something terrible happened to him personally, he would process it with the
same professional efficiency. Instead, he found himself jumpy, avoidant, unable to walk into an
unfamiliar parking structure without his chest tightening. He was certain, in a way that resisted
argument, that this constituted evidence of a fundamental deficiency — that his reactions since the
explosion showed him to be, as he put it in his intake session, "a lousy coper." More painful still
was the sense that the competence which had defined him was simply gone. The forensic analyst who
could hold a scene of mass death at an interpretable distance no longer existed, and Rodrigo did not
know what remained in his place. He had come to believe, quietly and with great conviction, that the
world was a more dangerous place than he had previously understood — not in the abstract way a
professional understands risk, but in the visceral, bodily way of a man who knows what a pressure
wave feels like from the inside.

It was his wife, Claudia, who finally persuaded him to seek help after he declined to attend their
grandson's birthday party at an unfamiliar event venue — a refusal that landed between them with the
weight of something irreversible. Rodrigo came to his first session hoping, above all, to be told
that the man he had been was still recoverable. What he feared most was that the answer was no.
