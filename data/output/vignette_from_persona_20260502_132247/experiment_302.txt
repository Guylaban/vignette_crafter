Ingrid is a 51-year-old married civil servant of Northern European descent who was referred for
psychological assessment following a toxic chemical exposure incident that occurred approximately
fourteen months ago at a government building where she works. She presented with a PCL-5 score of
53, indicating severe posttraumatic stress, and arrived at the intake appointment visibly rehearsed
— she had spent the morning, she admitted, going over what she might be asked and how she would
answer.

The incident itself occurred on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon. Ingrid was at her desk when a
maintenance error released an unidentified chemical agent through the building's ventilation system.
There was no warning — no alarm, no announcement — only a sudden acrid smell and then, within
minutes, confusion. Colleagues around her began coughing, speaking in panicked, overlapping voices
that became, to Ingrid's disoriented mind, a wall of incomprehensible noise. She could not
understand what people were saying. Emergency responders arrived in full protective gear, and she
was handled — physically guided, examined on-site, loaded into an ambulance — without ever fully
understanding what had happened to her body or whether she was going to survive. The
unpredictability of the event, its total absence of warning, and the sensory chaos of that afternoon
left marks that have not faded.

What is most striking clinically is the nature of Ingrid's memory for the event. She cannot place it
into a coherent verbal account. When she attempts to describe what happened, she loses the thread
mid-sentence, trails off, and looks genuinely distressed by her own inability to finish. Deliberate
recall feels inaccessible to her — she reaches for the memory and finds something that resists being
held. What remains is not a story but a residue: a smell, a physical sensation, a feeling of not
knowing. This fragmented, nonverbal quality of the memory means that the event has never been
processed into a stable past — it remains, in effect, present. Because she cannot situate it clearly
in time or language, any moment that resembles it — any sudden noise, any approaching stranger, any
situation she cannot predict — carries the full sensory weight of the original. The memory's
incompleteness is precisely what keeps the triggers alive.

And so the triggers are everywhere. Incomprehensible chatter — colleagues talking over each other in
the open-plan office where Ingrid still works — sends her immediately back into the ventilation-
system chaos, her body responding before her mind can intervene. Unexpected sounds bring her to high
alert. Strangers approaching her desk feel threatening in ways she cannot always justify to herself
but cannot dismiss. Unpredictable situations — a meeting agenda changed at the last minute, a phone
call from an unknown number — register as potential emergencies. The threat feels bodily and
immediate: she has come to believe that safety exists only in a very small number of places,
primarily her own home and, to a lesser extent, her car. Everywhere else, she is scanning.

It is this persistent sense of threat that in turn sharpens the triggers. When she is already on
high alert, the incomprehensible chatter of a busy corridor does not need to be loud to overwhelm
her — her nervous system is already primed. And the heightened state of arousal makes deliberate
recall even harder; when she tries to talk about the event in a state of threat, the fragmented
memory becomes more fragmented still, more resistant, as though the body is refusing to cooperate
with the narrative mind.

Over the months since the incident, Ingrid has built a careful architecture of avoidance. She
rehearses every conversation before it happens — what she will say, what they might say back —
because unprepared exchanges feel uncontrollable. She avoids any physical contact with others,
having come to associate being touched with the hands of the emergency responders who moved her
without explanation. She has not attended a medical appointment since the incident, cancelling two
follow-up examinations and refusing to enter the hospital where she was briefly treated. These
strategies reduce her immediate distress, but they also maintain the sense that the world is
dangerous and that she is too fragile to navigate it unprotected — which keeps her threat monitoring
high and her triggers sensitised.

What finally brought Ingrid to seek help was a moment at work three weeks ago when she could not
complete a routine briefing. She stopped speaking mid-sentence, could not find the words, and had to
leave the room. She is most afraid, she says quietly, that something was permanently done to her
brain. What she hopes, with some difficulty, is that someone can help her explain to herself what
happened.
