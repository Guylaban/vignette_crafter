Malee Thongchai is a 44-year-old Thai American woman who has worked as a tattoo artist for nearly
fifteen years, building a loyal clientele in the small Pacific Northwest city where she and her
husband have made their home. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 58, seeking help for intrusive
symptoms and persistent distress she traces back to an incident of toxic chemical exposure that
occurred approximately two years ago.

The exposure happened during what began as a routine afternoon at the studio. A newly purchased
batch of ink solvent, improperly labelled by the supplier, had been sitting open on her workstation.
Within minutes of beginning to use it, Malee noticed a burning sensation in her throat and eyes. The
smell was sharp and synthetic — nothing like the familiar chemical tang she had learned to work
around over years in the trade. Before she could process what was happening, her vision blurred and
her legs gave way. A colleague called emergency services, and Malee regained full awareness only in
the back of an ambulance, oxygen mask pressed to her face, a paramedic she did not know holding her
arm and asking her questions she could not answer. At the hospital, she was treated for respiratory
irritation and mild chemical burns to her mucous membranes. She was kept for observation overnight,
monitored at intervals she had no say in, her clothing exchanged for a gown, her body examined by
rotating staff she had never met. She was discharged the following afternoon with instructions and a
follow-up appointment she attended in a daze.

What has stayed with Malee — what has not loosened its grip in the two years since — is a cluster of
sensory and situational experiences that return her, without warning, to the helplessness of that
afternoon and night. The smell of antiseptic is the most reliable of these. Her studio, which she
once moved through with complete ease, requires the use of sterilising agents throughout the day,
and there are moments — wiping down a tray, opening a sealed autoclave pouch — when the chemical
scent reaches her before she has time to prepare. In those moments, something in her body responds
before her mind can intervene. She has begun leaving sterilisation tasks to her apprentice whenever
she can frame a plausible reason, though she is aware this arrangement is fragile and that her
apprentice has started to notice the pattern.

The sensation of being watched has become its own difficulty. Tattooing is, by its nature, an
observed practice — clients watch her work for hours at a time, and the intimacy of that gaze was
once something Malee welcomed as part of her craft. Now she finds herself hyperaware of eyes on her
hands, her face, her movements. She has described the feeling as similar to being monitored by the
hospital staff — the sense that her body is under assessment, that she may be found to be doing
something wrong or failing in some way she cannot anticipate. On days when a client is particularly
attentive, she finishes sessions feeling exhausted in a way she cannot fully explain to her husband.

The third trigger is perhaps the most difficult to avoid: any situation in which she is required to
do something she has not chosen. A mandatory training session at the studio, a medical appointment,
even a dinner plan made by someone else and presented as settled — each of these can produce a
tightening in her chest and a quality of dread she recognises as disproportionate but cannot
override. She has come to understand, in a quiet and frightened way, that what the exposure took
from her was not only her health for those hours, but her sense that she could be the author of what
happened to her body.

What finally brought Malee to seek help was a conversation with her husband, who told her gently
that she had not seemed present with him in a very long time. She is hoping, she says, to be able to
work again without fear. What she is most afraid of is that the person she was before that afternoon
— capable, steady, in control of her craft — may not be recoverable.
