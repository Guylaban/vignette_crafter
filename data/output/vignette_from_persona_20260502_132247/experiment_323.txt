Valentina Reyes is a 27-year-old Mexican American animator who works for a mid-sized studio in Los
Angeles, where she spends her days building characters from nothing — giving them movement,
expression, and emotional life on a screen. She came to therapy six months after leaving a
relationship that had become physically abusive, describing herself in the intake session not as a
survivor but, with quiet insistence, as someone who had fundamentally failed herself.

The relationship had begun two years earlier and deteriorated gradually, the violence emerging
slowly enough that Valentina had adjusted to each new threshold before she recognized it as one. The
final incident occurred on a Tuesday evening in her apartment. Her partner had struck her
repeatedly, and she had not left immediately — she had stayed through that night and into the
following morning, making coffee, speaking carefully, trying to manage the atmosphere. It was this
detail, more than the blows themselves, that she returned to again and again in session. She had
stayed. She had made the coffee. She had, in her own accounting, participated in something that
violated everything she believed about herself.

In the months since leaving, Valentina has been living with a set of beliefs about herself that have
proven more durable than the relationship itself. She has come to believe that she is permanently
altered — that the woman who once moved through the world with a certain confidence and moral
clarity no longer exists and cannot be recovered. She describes this not as grief but as fact, the
way one might state a diagnosis. When she looks at her work — the characters she animates, the
emotional arcs she constructs — she notices a flatness in her engagement that she interprets as
evidence of this damage. She used to feel that her work came from somewhere genuine in her. Now she
is not sure that place still exists.

Alongside this, she carries a corrosive self-blame that has resisted every counter-argument she has
tried to apply to it. She is certain, at a level that feels pre-rational, that the abuse happened
because of how she behaved — that she was too reactive, too demanding, that she created the
conditions for what occurred. She has reviewed specific arguments she made, specific tones she used,
and constructed a causal chain that places her at its origin. Colleagues at the studio have noticed
she has become unusually deferential in creative meetings, rarely advocating for her own ideas, and
she has turned down two projects that would have required her to work closely with a new director.
She does not frame this as avoidance; she frames it as appropriate caution, as someone who now knows
what she is capable of provoking.

What troubles her most deeply, and what she finds hardest to articulate, is the sense that she acted
in ways that go against who she is. She had always understood herself as someone with clear values —
someone who would leave, who would not accommodate, who would not make the coffee. The fact that she
did none of those things in the way she imagined she would has left her feeling estranged from
herself in a way that is distinct from shame. It is less that she feels bad about what she did and
more that she no longer trusts the person she thought she was. The self she had narrated to herself
over twenty-seven years now seems like a character she animated rather than inhabited — convincing
on screen, hollow underneath.

Valentina sought help after a conversation with her younger sister, who had noticed over several
months that Valentina had stopped coming to family dinners and had become increasingly withdrawn.
Her sister did not push; she simply said she missed her. That was enough. Valentina arrived at the
first session composed and articulate, already anticipating that she would be told the abuse was not
her fault. What she most fears is that therapy will offer her absolution without restoring what she
has lost. What she most hopes for — though she holds this tentatively — is that the person she
believed herself to be was not entirely invented.
