Rodrigo is a 62-year-old married Latin American man who has spent most of his adult life as a
professional dancer and choreographer. He came to the clinic eight months after a life-threatening
motor vehicle collision in which he was broadsided at high speed by a driver who ran a red light.
The impact caved in the driver's side door, and Rodrigo was trapped in the wreckage for nearly forty
minutes before emergency crews cut him free. What lodged most deeply in him was not the initial
crash but the sounds — the grinding shriek of metal, the hiss of the deployed airbag, a car alarm
cycling on and off in the silence afterward — and the absolute inability to move his body, the
instrument he had trusted for four decades. He lay pinned, certain he was about to die, and he could
not do a single thing to prevent it.

In the months that followed, Rodrigo found that the event resisted language entirely. He could not
tell his wife what had happened in any way that felt true; the experience sat in him as sensation
and sound rather than story, fragmented and without sequence. When memories surfaced — and they
surfaced without warning — he would hear the metal shriek again, feel the airbag's chemical sting in
his nostrils, and with those sensory echoes came a wave of intense shame that surprised him and that
he struggled to account for. He had come to believe, quietly and with great conviction, that the
crash had happened because of the kind of man he was — that some flaw in his character, some
accumulated carelessness or pride, had made him deserving of it. This belief that the event
reflected something essential about him meant that the world now felt permanently dangerous: if his
own nature had drawn disaster once, it would draw it again. He was certain that bad things would
keep happening to him and that he was powerless to stop them. That helplessness settled into his
body as a constant low hum of threat, and he began scanning every room he entered for exits the
moment he walked through the door — a habit his students at the dance studio noticed before he did.
He startled violently when a colleague touched his shoulder to get his attention during a rehearsal,
and the shame of that visible flinch in front of younger dancers compounded the belief that
something was fundamentally broken in him.

Because the memories arrived with such physical force — the auditory replays, the shame, the bodily
terror — Rodrigo began working to prevent them. He started suppressing any thought that edged toward
the crash, pushing it down the moment it surfaced. He discovered that staying awake until two or
three in the morning kept him too exhausted to ruminate, and he began deliberately dissociating
during the day, teaching choreography in a kind of blurred detachment, watching himself from a
slight distance as though his body were a student he was correcting. These strategies brought brief
relief but steadily worsened his relationship with his own memory: the event became more fragmented,
less narratable, more lodged in the body. The deliberate blanking-out meant he was never fully
present with the memories long enough to process them, and the fragments that broke through felt
more alien and more shameful each time. The suppression and dissociation also reinforced his sense
that he was a man who could not cope, could not feel normally, could not be trusted with his own
inner life — which fed directly back into his conviction that he was helpless and essentially
damaged, keeping his threat system perpetually primed. He remained hypervigilant in the studio,
scanning the mirrors for movement near the entrance, bracing at every unexpected sound, and that
vigilance pulled the fragmented sensory memories closer to the surface, so that teaching a simple
combination could suddenly be interrupted by the sound of metal in his head.

What finally brought Rodrigo to the clinic was a conversation with his wife, who told him gently
that she felt she had lost him — that he was present in the house but absent from their life
together. He arrived hoping, more than anything, to feel like himself again, and terrified that he
no longer knew who that was.
