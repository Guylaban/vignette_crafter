Priya Subramaniam is a 23-year-old South Asian woman who works as an auditor at a mid-sized
accounting firm in Chicago. She presented for psychological assessment approximately eight months
after witnessing a roadside explosion while visiting extended family in a region of South Asia
experiencing active civil conflict. She had been watching from a second-floor window when a bicycle
parked along the street below became the locus of a detonation. The blast was sudden and total — a
wall of sound that seemed to compress time — and in the seconds that followed, Priya saw things on
the ground that she has never been able to fully assemble into a coherent sequence: fragmented
images of what she understands, without being able to say aloud, were human body parts scattered
across the road. She was physically unharmed and was evacuated within hours, but the event lodged
itself in her mind not as a story she could tell, but as a series of frozen frames she cannot escape
and cannot complete.

Priya describes her memory of that afternoon as a collection of snapshots — four or five images,
each one vivid and static, with nothing connecting them. She knows there were sounds before the
blast, and she knows she eventually found herself on the floor of the room, but the middle is
missing. She has tried, in the months since, to reconstruct what happened, but the gaps resist her.
What she is left with is not a narrative but a set of images she cannot put into words, even when
she tries to write them down or speak to her closest friend. This fractured, wordless quality of the
memory means that whenever a trigger breaks through — a loud bang from a car backfiring on the
street outside her office building, a bicycle locked to a rack near the entrance she uses each
morning, an image of an injured limb glimpsed on a news website — the memory does not return as a
story but as a sudden reinvasion of those same frozen frames, immediate and context-free, as though
the event is happening again rather than being recalled. The triggers do not cue reflection; they
cue re-experiencing.

And because the memory arrives incomplete and without language, Priya cannot contain it, cannot
argue with it, cannot place it safely in the past. Each intrusion leaves her more convinced that
something essential in her has been permanently altered. She had come to believe, with quiet but
absolute certainty, that she was no longer capable of the focused, detail-oriented work that had
defined her since university — the same precision she once brought to audit files now felt
inaccessible, as though it belonged to a version of herself that no longer existed. More than that,
she described feeling like an object moving through her days rather than a person inhabiting them —
functional, observable, but interior in no meaningful sense. She feels dead inside, she said, not
dramatically but factually, the way one might report a temperature. These appraisals — of
incapacity, of dehumanisation, of emotional extinction — do not remain inert. They actively colour
how she encounters the world, and in particular they lower her threshold for perceiving threat: when
she feels already emptied out and no longer herself, the bicycle by the office door is not merely a
bicycle but evidence of a world that remains dangerous and indifferent, and the explosion that
follows in her mind is not a memory but a confirmation.

The loop has tightened steadily over months. Her auditing work requires her to commute into the city
each day past the building entrance where the bicycle rack sits, and on days when she has already
woken with the frozen images pressing close, she has begun taking a longer route, adding twenty
minutes to her morning, arriving late enough that her supervisor has noticed. She has stopped
attending team lunches because the open-plan restaurant near the office plays music loud enough to
occasionally produce sounds that startle her. Two colleagues have asked if she is all right. She has
said yes each time.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was not a single crisis but an accumulation: a quarterly
review she could not complete because she sat for forty minutes staring at figures she could not
process, followed by the realisation, riding home, that she could not remember the last time she had
felt like herself. She came in hoping, she said carefully, to feel like a person again. What she
feared most was that the person she had been before the window, before the bicycle, before the
sound, was simply gone.
