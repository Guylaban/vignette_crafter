Margot Voss is a sixty-three-year-old sculptor of Czech and German descent who lives with her
husband in a mid-sized city in the Pacific Northwest. She has spent most of her adult life working
in a converted garage studio, producing large-scale ceramic and bronze pieces that she described, in

78 places her in the severe range, and she presented as composed but exhausted, choosing her words
carefully in the way of someone who has learned that certain words can detonate things inside her.

Fourteen months ago, Margot was raped by a man she knew — a fellow artist who had been a peripheral
but consistent presence in her professional circle for years. The assault occurred in the late
evening after a gallery opening, in a back room she had entered without suspicion precisely because
she trusted him. She remembers the sound of sirens passing outside at some point during the assault
— she cannot say whether it was before or after — and she remembers that no one came, that the noise
simply faded. She reported the assault to the police and, subsequently, to the board of the arts
collective to which both she and her assailant belonged. The collective declined to remove him,
citing insufficient evidence. He continued to attend events. She stopped attending everything.

In the months that followed, the assault's sensory residue embedded itself in the texture of
ordinary life. Sirens — ubiquitous in her neighbourhood — began to carry the weight of that night:
the sound of help that did not arrive. Hearing one while working in her studio could stop her hands
mid-motion and return her, without warning, to the back room. Comments about sexual violence in the
news, in conversation, or overheard at the coffee shop she used to frequent became unbearable
triggers, each one reopening the question of what had happened to her and whether it had been, in
some way, her fault. Most acutely, encountering anyone connected to that former social world —
mutual acquaintances, former colleagues — could precipitate a full reactivation of the assault's
emotional register. When these triggers fired, they did not simply produce distress; they reliably
surfaced a cluster of beliefs Margot had been struggling to hold at bay. She had come to believe
that she had acted in ways that went against who she was — that she had been naive, compliant,
insufficiently alert — and the triggers seemed to confirm this reading of herself each time they
struck. The institutional failure she experienced, the collective's refusal to act, wove itself into
these appraisals: if those who should have protected her had judged her story unworthy of action,
perhaps the story itself reflected something about her.

These appraisals, once activated, collapsed the world around her. She had come to feel safe in
almost no place outside her own home, and even there, safety was conditional. Unexpected movement —
her husband rising suddenly from his chair, a cat crossing her peripheral vision — produced a
starlike startle response she found humiliating and exhausting. Strong fear, horror, and shame
arrived not as discrete emotions but as a weather system that could descend without warning, leaving
her unable to work, unable to speak, sometimes unable to move. The conviction that nothing in her
life held meaning anymore was not, she explained, a mood — it was a conclusion she had reached and
kept reaching. Her sculpture, which had always been her primary source of identity and purpose, now
felt unreachable; the studio where she had spent thirty years making things felt contaminated by
what she now knew about the world.

The threat she experienced daily — the hypervigilance, the constricted geography of safety, the
hair-trigger alarm to movement — did not remain contained. It reached backward, sharpening the
triggers she encountered and making encounters with sirens or related people feel not merely
distressing but genuinely dangerous, as though the assault were ongoing and proximity to its echoes
carried real risk. The loop between her appraisals and her threat responses had, over months, become
self-sustaining: the sense of danger confirmed that her judgment could not be trusted, and the
belief that she was fundamentally compromised kept her nervous system on permanent alert.

What finally brought Margot to seek help was her husband's quiet observation that she had not
entered the studio in four months. She had not realised, or had not let herself count. What she most
fears is that the person who made things — who found the world meaningful enough to make things — is
gone. What she most hopes, though she said this quietly and without much conviction, is that she is
wrong about that.
