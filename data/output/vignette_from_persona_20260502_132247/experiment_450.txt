Amara is a 25-year-old Ethiopian-American surgical resident who presented for psychological
assessment following a deployment-related trauma sustained during a medical humanitarian mission in
a conflict zone approximately fourteen months ago. She had volunteered as part of a forward surgical
team, and on a morning that began like any other — the smell of antiseptic, the weight of gloves
being pulled over her hands — a series of coordinated explosions struck the perimeter of the field
hospital. In the chaos that followed, she found herself working on casualties while aircraft circled
overhead and secondary blasts continued to detonate nearby. What she cannot speak about in any
coherent sequence — and what she has never been able to fully articulate to anyone, even in this
intake — is what she saw and touched in those hours. The memory does not come to her in words. It
comes in sensation: the particular give of damaged tissue beneath her gloved fingers, the vibration
in her sternum from the concussive blasts, the sound of rotors cutting the air above a structure
that no longer felt safe. She has told her supervising attending only that she "went through
something overseas." She has told no one more than that.

Since returning to her residency program at a large urban hospital, Amara has found that the memory
does not wait for her to summon it. It arrives unbidden, pulled forward by the world around her. The
sound of a helicopter — and in a trauma centre, helicopters are not rare — can collapse the distance
between then and now in an instant, flooding her with the same terror she felt pressed against that
operating table fourteen months ago. A construction crew detonating something nearby last autumn
sent her to the floor of a supply corridor before she had consciously registered what she was doing.
Most insidiously, the texture of certain surgical materials — a particular grade of packing gauze,
the specific resistance of a wound cavity — can return her without warning to a moment she cannot
narrate but cannot escape. The memory, fragmented and nonverbal as it is, remains exquisitely
sensitive to these sensory doorways, and the triggers it activates have begun to reshape her daily
life in ways that are becoming impossible to conceal.

What has developed in response to these intrusions is a set of habits Amara thinks of, privately, as
simply being careful. Before entering any room — a conference room, the hospital cafeteria, the OR
anteroom — she identifies where the exits are. She does not sit with her back to a doorway under any
circumstances; in the residents' lounge, she has quietly rearranged her routine so that she always
occupies the corner seat facing the entrance, and colleagues have begun to notice and comment. She
scans every environment she enters, reading faces, tracking movement, listening for anything that
does not belong. She understands, on some level, that a hospital cafeteria is not a forward
operating base. But the part of her that understands this is not the part that is in charge. What
she has not yet recognised — and what is clinically significant — is that this constant vigilance
keeps her perceptual system primed and sensitised, so that the very scanning she uses to feel safer
makes her more likely to detect the sounds and sensations that send her back. The helicopters she
listens for, she also hears first. The textures she braces against, she encounters with a nervous
system already at threshold. The strategies that were meant to contain the triggers have become,
over months, one of the mechanisms by which those triggers find her.

The consequence that finally brought her to seek help was not a flashback in the OR — though she
fears that above almost everything else — but a formal notation in her performance review citing
"difficulty with team communication and situational awareness during high-acuity cases." Amara, who
had imagined herself defined by precision and composure, read those words and understood that what
she was carrying had begun to cost her the thing she had built her life around. She came to this
intake hoping to be told there is something specific that can be done, and terrified that what will
be asked of her is to find words for something that, in her experience, has none.
