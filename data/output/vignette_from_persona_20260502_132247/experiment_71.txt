Rodrigo is a seventy-year-old widowed Salvadoran man who has worked as a welder for most of his
adult life. He was referred for psychological evaluation following a significant deterioration in
his functioning over the past year, and he presents with a PCL-5 score of 80, consistent with severe
post-traumatic stress disorder. The events that brought him to this point reach back decades, but
their hold on him has never loosened.

Approximately thirty years ago, Rodrigo was detained by paramilitaries during a period of political
violence in his home country. He was held in a windowless room — a concrete cell with no visible
exit, no way to call for help, and no indication of when or whether he would be released. Over
several days, he was subjected to repeated acts of physical violence. At one point, a guard
discharged a firearm inside the building — not at him, but close enough that the concussive sound
filled the room entirely. That sound, the absolute absence of options, and the reality of violence
as something that could be done to a human body without consequence: these became the architecture
of his terror. He survived. He emigrated. He built a life, married, worked with his hands, and for
years told himself it was behind him. His wife died three years ago, and the structure that had
helped him contain his past collapsed with her.

Rodrigo now works in a fabrication shop, and it is there that the present intrudes most forcefully
on the past. The sharp crack of metal striking metal, the occasional pneumatic discharge of
equipment nearby — any sudden percussive sound at the shop floor can stop him mid-weld, his body
flooding with certainty that the noise is a gunshot. In those moments the memory does not feel like
something that happened thirty years ago; it feels like it is happening now, the cell walls present,
the air close. When these sensory triggers arrive, Rodrigo finds himself detaching from the memory
almost involuntarily, pulling back from it as though from a flame, and simultaneously working hard
to suppress whatever images are surfacing. He has described trying to "push it back down" as a near-
constant mental effort during his shifts. Situations that feel constraining — being asked to stay
late when he hadn't planned on it, finding himself in a small equipment closet, or simply being told
what to do in a tone that carries any edge of authority — carry the same charge as the cell did, and
these moments of felt entrapment reliably pull the memory forward into the present, vivid and
unfinished.

What makes these intrusions so destabilising is not only their sensory intensity but the appraisals
that arrive with them. Rodrigo has come to believe, with a conviction that feels like fact, that you
can never truly know who will harm you — that violence is latent in ordinary people and ordinary
situations, and that his failure to detect it before his detention is evidence of a permanent
blindness in himself. This belief that threat is everywhere and unknowable keeps him hypervigilant
in ways that make the triggers more likely to fire: a stranger's raised voice, a coworker moving
quickly behind him, any scene of physical conflict on television — all of these are scanned for the
signature of what happened in that room. His appraisals and his triggers feed one another in a loop
he cannot see clearly enough to interrupt. Worse, he carries a crushing conviction that he bears
responsibility for what was done to him — that he acted in ways that brought it on — and that he
failed to stop it when he should have. When the memory surfaces and he feels the rage that
accompanies recall, that rage turns inward almost immediately, folding into guilt and self-
recrimination. In those states, he becomes outwardly deferential, almost placatory, agreeing with
whatever is asked of him — a fawn response he does not fully understand but which feels, in the
moment, like the only safe posture.

When he tries to explain any of this to his adult children, he loses the thread. The account
fragments; he cannot finish sentences. He has stopped trying. What finally brought him to seek help
was a moment at work when a younger colleague accidentally dropped a steel plate and Rodrigo,
without fully understanding what he was doing, pressed himself against the wall and could not move
for several minutes. He is most afraid that what happened to him has permanently changed who he is.
What he hopes, quietly and with some embarrassment, is that someone might help him find a way to
live in the present tense again.
