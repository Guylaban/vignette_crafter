Tama Faleolo is a 63-year-old Samoan-American man who has been a commercial airline pilot for nearly
three decades, a career he built in the years following his service in the Pacific theatre during a
period of regional conflict. He is married, the father of grown children, and by most outward
appearances a man of considerable professional accomplishment. He presented for psychological
assessment with a PCL-5 score of 75, describing himself in the intake session as someone who no
longer recognises the person he has become.

The event that organises Tama's current presentation occurred during a night combat operation when
he was in his mid-twenties. His unit had taken fire during a helicopter extraction; the aircraft
went down in partial darkness, and Tama spent hours in the wreckage before rescue, in sustained
physical contact with the bodies of men he had served alongside. The specific texture of the torn
canvas webbing and blood-soaked fabric he gripped throughout that night left an impression that has
never fully released him. The low thudding of rotor blades, which he could hear in the distance
during the wait, became inseparable in his nervous system from the terror of that vigil. And the
time — late evening, around 2100 hours — remains a threshold his body remembers before his mind
does.

In the decades that followed, these sensory details did not fade but sharpened. Tama reports that
the memory itself has a strange, dissociated quality — it feels, he says, like it belongs to someone
else, like watching footage of a stranger — and yet when it surfaces, the terror it carries is
entirely his own, immediate and total, as though no time has passed at all. This quality of the
memory — both alien and annihilating — has become a source of its own dread, because Tama cannot
predict when something in his surroundings will pull the full memory back without warning. The
memory, once activated, floods him with a sense of present danger that extends well beyond the
recollection itself, leaving him scanning rooms for exits, tracking the movements of people around
him, and unable to settle his nervous system for hours. The hypervigilant state that follows a
memory intrusion in turn makes him more susceptible to the next one — the heightened arousal lowers
his threshold, so that sounds and textures he might otherwise absorb now catch him and drag him
back.

His occupation places him in direct daily contact with his most potent trigger. The cockpit of a
commercial aircraft is filled with the sounds of engines and, on certain approaches or in certain
weather conditions, the unmistakable percussive rhythm of nearby rotor craft. When Tama hears a
helicopter during a flight — during a pre-departure hold, a coastal approach, or an air traffic
interaction — his body responds before any conscious appraisal occurs. His hands tighten, his chest
constricts, and the threshold for the memory collapses. That the trigger arrives within the
professional context he has spent his adult life building has deepened his sense that something is
fundamentally wrong with him. He has come to believe, with a quiet but entrenched conviction, that a
man who cannot hear a helicopter without trembling is defective in some essential way — broken at a
level that precedes circumstance. This appraisal of himself as damaged does not remain contained; it
bleeds into his reading of the world more broadly, confirming for him that danger is everywhere and
that his inability to feel safe is simply an accurate perception of reality rather than a symptom of
injury.

The nights are worst. Tama finds that after approximately 2100 hours, a generalised unease settles
over him that he cannot reason away, and his wife has noted that he becomes withdrawn and watchful
in the evenings in ways that have quietly eroded the intimacy between them. His belief that he is
fundamentally miserable now — that the happy man he once was is simply gone — has made it harder for
him to reach toward her or accept reassurance, because he has come to understand his unhappiness not
as a wound but as a verdict.

What finally brought Tama to seek help was a near-incident during a landing approach — not a
mechanical failure, but a moment in which a low-flying helicopter passed close enough that he froze
at the controls for several seconds. His co-pilot noticed. Tama is terrified of losing his licence,
but more than that, he is afraid of what it would mean to have nothing left to prove he is still the
man he once was.
