Rangi is a 71-year-old Māori man, single, who has worked as a painter for most of his adult life —
the kind of patient who arrives at the intake appointment with his hands still faintly stained with
pigment, as though the studio is the only place he still trusts. He was referred following a
terrorism-related incident approximately fourteen months ago, in which he was present at a public
venue when an attack occurred. He had been attending an outdoor cultural festival, one of the few
public events he still made a point of going to in retirement, when the explosion happened nearby.
He remembers the concussive pressure against his chest, the sudden tilt of the ground as he fell,
and the position his body locked into — arms raised, torso twisted — a posture that now returns to
him unbidden, reproduced in his muscles whenever the memory surfaces. The clarity of what he saw
that day has not faded. If anything, it has sharpened: the colours of the stalls, the specific
quality of the afternoon light, the faces of strangers in the seconds before and after. These are
not impressions but high-resolution records, and they force themselves into his awareness without
invitation — while he is mixing paint, while he is eating, while he is lying in the dark.

In the months that followed, those intrusive memories began to do something more than disturb him.
Each time the recollection arrived — vivid, embodied, insistent — it carried with it a conclusion
that felt less like interpretation than fact: that the world had always been this dangerous, and
that he had simply failed to see it. He had come to believe that his own judgment could not be
trusted, that he had walked into that festival with a naïve confidence that nearly cost him
everything. The sense that other people were equally unreliable settled in alongside this — no one
had warned him, no one had protected him, and the social fabric he had taken for granted had proven
itself hollow in a single afternoon. These convictions — that he could not rely on himself, that he
could not rely on others, that danger was the world's default condition — took root quickly and have
not loosened since.

It was from within this frame of threat and self-doubt that his coping strategies developed, and
they have proven to be the architecture of his current suffering. He began avoiding going out
entirely, first limiting his trips to necessities, then eliminating most of them. His painting
practice, which had always required occasional visits to suppliers, galleries, and the open-air
locations he favoured for landscape work, contracted to the interior of his flat. The occupation
that had once carried him outdoors now traps him inside it. He also started seeking reassurance
compulsively — calling a neighbour, texting his niece, asking whether they thought he was coping,
whether he seemed alright — and working hard to suppress any emotion that felt too large to contain,
holding himself rigid through moments of distress. The difficulty is that these strategies, rather
than quieting the system, keep feeding it. The avoidance and the emotional suppression prevent him
from processing what happened, so the memories return with undiminished force — bodily, vivid,
uncontrolled — and each intrusion refreshes his certainty that the world is dangerous and that he is
not equipped to navigate it. The reassurance-seeking, in turn, quietly confirms what he fears most:
that he cannot trust his own read on things, that he needs external verification just to feel
temporarily safe, which sits poorly alongside his belief that others cannot truly be relied upon
either.

What finally brought Rangi to seek help was a conversation with his niece, who told him gently that
she had not seen him leave his building in six weeks. He had not realised it had been that long. He
arrived at the first session saying very little about what he hoped for, only that he wanted to be
able to go back to painting outside — to stand in front of a landscape without scanning it for
exits. What he is most afraid of, though he has not said it in these words, is that the man who
walked into that festival no longer exists, and that the one who came out is all that remains.
