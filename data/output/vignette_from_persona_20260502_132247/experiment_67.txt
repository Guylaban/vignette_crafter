Aroha Ngata is a 46-year-old New Zealand-born veterinarian living in the Pacific Northwest with her
husband. She was referred for psychological assessment following a score of 78 on the PCL-5,
submitted through her employee assistance programme after her clinic manager noted she had become
increasingly withdrawn and was struggling to complete routine procedures. The events that brought
her to this point began approximately three years ago, when Aroha was volunteering with an
international animal welfare organisation in a region of active civil conflict. She had been
travelling in a convoy when shelling began without warning. The vehicle she was riding in swerved
and stopped abruptly; the driver grabbed her arm to pull her from the door, and in the chaos of that
moment — the concussive percussion of explosions, the smell of smoke, bodies pressing against her in
the scramble — she lost sight of two colleagues. She later learned they had not survived. She was
evacuated within hours but returned home carrying something she could not name for a long time.

In the months that followed, Aroha found that her memories of the event did not settle into a
coherent story. They came instead in disconnected pieces — a hand gripping her forearm, a sound
without context, a fragment of light through a cracked windscreen — that surfaced without sequence
or resolution. When she attempted to recall what had happened, the memories played on a loop she
could not pause, and they arrived alongside a wave of shame that confused and distressed her. She
had come to believe that she had failed her colleagues, that a more competent person would have
acted differently, and that what had happened to her was profoundly unjust with no accountability
ever offered. These appraisals did not stay abstract; they fed back into the memories themselves,
colouring each replay with a fresh charge of self-recrimination that made the act of remembering
feel punishing rather than clarifying.

The memories, in turn, kept her body in a state of permanent readiness. Aroha described being unable
to fall asleep without checking the locks twice, lying still but alert, certain that relaxation was
a form of negligence. She scanned every room she entered — her clinic, the car park, the waiting
area — for anything that might signal danger, and she could not let that surveillance lapse even in
settings she consciously recognised as safe. This persistent threat monitoring had, over time,
confirmed her worst appraisals of herself: she was certain that her inability to simply calm down
was evidence that she could not rely on her own judgement, and that her hypervigilance was proof,
rather than symptom, of how badly she had been broken.

Certain things in her daily life had become unbearable in ways she struggled to explain to others.
Loud noises — a dog kennel erupting, a metal instrument tray dropped on the clinic floor — sent her
immediately back into the convoy, the memories arriving before she had time to orient herself. Being
touched without warning was equally destabilising; during examinations requiring a colleague to hand
her instruments or guide her hands, an unexpected contact could collapse the present entirely. She
had begun driving to farm calls alone, refusing to take the veterinary technician who had previously
accompanied her, because being a passenger with another person at the wheel returned her,
viscerally, to the moment the driver had seized her arm. This had strained her working relationship
with her technician and slowed her caseload considerably. Each of these triggers not only pulled her
back into the fragmented memory but reinforced her conviction that she was helpless — that bad
things arrived without warning and that she possessed no reliable means of preventing them. That
belief, in turn, had made her more attuned to potential triggers, so that ordinary sensory events
were now pre-emptively threatening, and the world of her clinic had quietly contracted around her.

What finally brought Aroha to seek help was a morning when she was unable to enter the surgical
suite because a colleague had accidentally knocked over a tray of instruments. She stood in the
corridor for several minutes, unable to move. She told the intake clinician that she was not afraid
of dying — she was afraid that this was simply who she was now, and that nothing would ever be done
about what had happened to make it right.
