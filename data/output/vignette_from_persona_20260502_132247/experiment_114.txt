American city — a role he had pursued with fierce ambition, having entered the force through a
specialized youth pipeline program that fast-tracked exceptionally capable recruits. He is currently
in a relationship, though his partner has grown quietly worried about him over recent months. Kofi
presented for psychological assessment with a PCL-5 score of 63, describing a period of sustained
psychological deterioration following an incident of captivity that occurred approximately eight
months prior to intake.

The event itself unfolded during what should have been a routine surveillance operation. Kofi,
working alongside senior colleagues, was separated from his team in an industrial district at night
and was apprehended by a group connected to the investigation. He was held for several hours in a
confined space — a locked storage room with no windows — during which time he was subjected to
sustained verbal threats, physical restraint, and intermittent periods of complete darkness. What he
remembers most acutely are the sounds: a particular voice issuing instructions in a low, controlled
tone; the scrape of a metal door; the rhythm of footsteps on a concrete floor outside. These sounds
did not leave him when he was eventually found and released. They followed him home.

In the weeks after the incident, Kofi noticed something troubling about the nature of his memory. He
knew what had happened — the sequence of events was not lost to him — but when he tried to speak
about it, in debriefs or with his partner, the words would not come. He would sit, mouth open, with
full knowledge of the experience and no language to contain it. More disorienting still were the
moments when the emotional weight of the captivity would descend on him without any accompanying
image: a sudden wash of terror while sitting at his desk reviewing case files, no flashback, no
picture, just the feeling — raw and complete — as though his body had remembered something his mind
had not yet processed. And at other times, unprompted, he would hear the sounds. The voice. The
footsteps. The scrape of that door. These auditory intrusions were particularly potent during the
night hours, which meant that being outside after dark — a near-unavoidable feature of detective
work — became a site of acute distress.

It was the intrusive quality of these memories, the sounds and the wordless surges of emotion that
arrived without warning, that drove Kofi toward a set of strategies he understood, at some level,
were not helping him. He began working hard to suppress any thought connected to the captivity,
redirecting his attention whenever the edge of the memory approached, keeping himself occupied with
paperwork or his phone or conversation — anything that might hold the recollection at bay. He
stopped going out at night unless absolutely required, and when he was required to, he would
experience a tightening in his chest that made concentration nearly impossible. He also became
unable to tolerate physical contact. His partner's touch, which had once been a source of comfort,
now produced an immediate flinching withdrawal that he could not explain and could not control.
These were not choices he had deliberated over; they were habits that had formed in the shape of his
distress, each one a response to the unbearable quality of what the memories brought with them.

Over months, the cost accumulated. His supervisors had begun to note his reluctance to take night
assignments. His partner had stopped reaching for him. Kofi had come to feel that the captivity had
changed something fundamental in him — that the person who had walked into that storage room and the
person who came out were not the same, and that no one around him fully understood what it was to
carry sounds in your head that belonged to the worst hours of your life.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at work when a colleague's footsteps in the

afraid, he said quietly at intake, that this will never stop. What he is hoping for, just as
quietly, is that someone will be able to help him find the words.
