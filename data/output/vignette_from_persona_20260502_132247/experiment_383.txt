Malia Thongchanh is a fifty-one-year-old Laotian American dentist who presented for an initial
psychological evaluation reporting significant and persistent distress following a prolonged
stalking experience that began approximately three years ago. Married and working full-time in a
private dental practice in a mid-sized Midwestern city, she described herself before the stalking as
confident and professionally accomplished — someone who had built her career and family life with
deliberate care. That sense of safety collapsed over the eighteen months during which a former
patient tracked her movements, appeared outside her clinic, left packages at her home, and sent
messages that escalated from unsettling to explicitly threatening. What made the experience
particularly destabilizing was not any single incident but the cumulative erosion of her ability to
trust her own environment: she never knew when he would appear, and the spaces she had considered
most hers — her waiting room, her car, her front porch — became sites of potential danger. When
police were eventually involved, their response felt procedurally distant and practically
insufficient, and she was left with the enduring impression that no external authority could
actually guarantee her safety. A restraining order was obtained, the stalking eventually ceased, and
by most external measures the threat is over. Malia does not experience it that way.

In the period following the stalking, she noticed that certain things in her ordinary life had
become impossible to move through without a surge of alarm. A particular kind of slow, ambient pop
song — the type that had played in her clinic's waiting room during the months the stalker was a
patient — could stop her mid-task and flood her with a sense of immediate danger that felt entirely
real, regardless of where she was. Encountering media coverage of stalking cases, true-crime
content, or even news segments about harassment produced the same effect: a sudden, involuntary
shift in which the present moment seemed to collapse into the worst period of her life. Most
acutely, the sight of uniformed police officers — once a neutral or even reassuring presence — now
triggered the same visceral alarm, her body registering their presence as evidence that something
threatening was already happening. Each of these encounters reliably pulls Malia back into a state
of perceived threat, and once that state is activated, it does not resolve quickly. She finds it
essentially impossible to accept reassurance when she is in that condition; even her husband's calm,
measured insistence that she is safe cannot reach her, because she has come to believe, at a level
that feels more like knowledge than fear, that safety is not something others can verify on her
behalf.

That conviction now organizes much of her daily behavior. At her dental practice — where she spends
the majority of her waking hours — she has quietly restructured her environment around the
management of perceived threat. She no longer uses the break room when other staff are present,
preferring instead to eat at her desk with her back against the wall of her private office, from
which she can see the hallway and both entry points to her suite. In the reception area, she has
developed a habit of scanning every new patient's face before approaching, and she has repositioned
her chair at the front consultation desk so that she sits with solid cabinetry behind her. These
arrangements feel not like preferences but like necessities — the minimum conditions under which she
can function. When a song comes through the office speaker system that resembles the ones from that
period, or when a patient mentions something that touches on the media coverage she tries to avoid,
the threat response returns immediately, and no amount of rational self-talk or collegial
reassurance interrupts it.

What finally brought Malia to seek help was a moment several weeks ago when a police officer came to
her building to respond to an unrelated incident in an adjacent office. She saw him in the corridor,
felt the alarm cascade through her, and spent the remainder of the afternoon unable to complete
patient procedures without her hands trembling. She told her office manager she had a migraine. She
is most afraid that this will never change — that she has permanently lost the capacity to feel safe
anywhere — and most hoping, tentatively, that someone can help her understand why her mind continues
to insist the danger is still present.
