Kiet Phommasack is a forty-one-year-old Laotian-American contemporary dancer and choreographer who
lives with his wife in Portland, Oregon. He presented for treatment with a PCL-5 score of 77,
describing a presentation that had been quietly dismantling his life for the better part of two
years. Before seeking help, he had told almost no one what had happened to him during a six-month
deployment as a relief worker in a conflict zone in Southeast Asia, where he had been coordinating
medical supply distribution in a region under intermittent shelling.

The incident that broke something in Kiet occurred in the late afternoon — around four o'clock —
when a supply convoy he had helped organize was ambushed at a checkpoint. A vehicle ahead of his was
struck and caught fire. Kiet pulled two colleagues from the wreckage, but a third man, a local
driver named Boua, could not be reached in time. What Kiet remembers most viscerally is the smell:
the acrid, suffocating stench of burning material and flesh, and the sharp reek of alcohol from a
hand sanitizer container that had ruptured and soaked his sleeve as he worked. He remembers the
angle of the late-afternoon light, amber and low, cutting through the smoke. He did not stay with
Boua long enough. He has believed this ever since.

Back in Portland, Kiet returned to his dance company and attempted to resume teaching and
rehearsing. For a time, the effort held. But the late afternoon began to betray him. Around four
o'clock, something in the quality of the light — its warmth, its particular angle through the
studio's high windows — would act as an immediate portal, collapsing the distance between Portland
and the checkpoint entirely. The smell of something burning in a nearby restaurant kitchen, or the
faint trace of alcohol from a dancer's injury spray, could produce the same effect without a
moment's warning: the full scene would return, not as memory but as present reality, his body
responding as though he were still there, crouching beside the burning vehicle, the heat against his
face, Boua's voice gone silent. These intrusions did not feel like recollections. They felt like
facts happening now.

What made the intrusions unbearable was not only their vividness but what they carried with them.
Each time the memory broke through, it arrived wrapped in a wave of shame so acute it was almost
physical — the certainty that he had failed Boua, that he had made a choice that revealed something
irreparable about his character. This shame, in turn, deepened the memory's hold, giving it an
emotional charge that kept it close to the surface. He found that when he sat with the appraisals —
the conviction that he had violated everything he believed about who he was, that he had proven
himself unreliable under the conditions that mattered most — the memories became more accessible,
more easily triggered, as though his self-condemnation kept the neural pathways warm. The thought
that he could not rely on himself, that his responses since the event had confirmed him as a lousy
coper, seemed to prime him for the next intrusion; he would scan the studio for the smell of
something burning, or tense at the afternoon light, already anticipating the collapse.

The loop had consequences his wife and colleagues could see even if they could not name it. He
stopped scheduling rehearsals in the late afternoon, citing fatigue. He began keeping the studio
windows covered. When a colleague used hand sanitizer near him during a warm-up, he left the room
without explanation and did not return for an hour. His choreographic work, once fluid and
collaborative, became halting. His wife noticed he flinched at the smell from their neighbor's fire
pit on summer evenings, and that he had grown distant and self-critical in ways she did not
recognize.

What finally brought Kiet to seek help was a performance he had to abandon mid-rehearsal when the
smell of a candle the lighting designer had lit drifted across the stage at quarter past four on a
Tuesday. He walked out, sat in his car, and wept. What he is most afraid of is that the shame is
accurate — that what the memories keep showing him is simply true. What he is hoping, cautiously, is
that someone might help him find out whether it is.
