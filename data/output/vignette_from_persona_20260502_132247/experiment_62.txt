palm, his mother's muffled pleading, the way the silence afterward felt heavier than the noise. He
did not go out. He stayed behind that door, small and motionless, and that stillness has never
entirely left him. Over the years the violence continued, and so did his inaction, and he had come
to believe that his failure to intervene — even as a child, even when he had no power — was a moral
failure he could never undo. He will never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened.
That conviction is not abstract; it surfaces in the middle of the workday, while he is moving
irrigation lines alone between rows of cotton, replaying scenarios in which he imagines confronting

even, constructing and reconstructing acts of retribution that never arrive at resolution and leave
him more agitated than before.

These appraisals — that he is fundamentally guilty, that people cannot be trusted, that whatever
happiness he once had is permanently behind him — have quietly organized his experience of threat.
Because he has come to see himself as someone who failed to protect, and the world as a place where
