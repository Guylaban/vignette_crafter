Tane Aroha is a 46-year-old Māori man, widowed, who works as an auditor for a regional accounting
firm in the Pacific Northwest. He was referred for psychological assessment following a period of
increasing functional difficulty at work and a recent episode in which he was found sitting
motionless in a stairwell by a colleague, unable to explain why he had left a routine team meeting.
His PCL-5 score of 41 places him in the clinical range for PTSD, and his presentation is consistent
with a trauma response rooted in a catastrophic cyclone that struck his home island of Niue
approximately three years ago, during a visit to care for his ailing mother. His wife, Hina, had
accompanied him. She did not survive.

The cyclone struck in the early hours of the morning. Tane woke in darkness to a sound he has since
described as the world being unmade — a roar that swallowed every familiar frequency of the night.
The smell of split timber, wet earth, and something metallic he could not name filled the air as the
structure around them began to fail. He found Hina, reached her, and then lost her in a collapse he
could neither predict nor prevent. He was pulled from the debris hours later. The darkness, the
silence that followed the wind, the smell of destruction, the particular taste of salt and dust at
the back of his throat — these are not memories that have faded. They are, in his words, "still
happening."

In the months that followed, Tane returned to work in the United States, where the demands of
auditing — structured, predictable, numerical — offered a kind of scaffolding. But the world had
reorganised itself around threat. He had come to believe, with a quiet and settled certainty, that
bad things happen without warning and cannot be stopped; that the world is dangerous in ways that
cannot be audited or controlled; and that something essential in him had died alongside Hina,
leaving him feeling dead inside. These convictions did not sit passively. They actively shaped his
perception, so that any unfamiliar environment — a new client's office, a different floor of his
building — became a space he could not assess for danger, and he avoided them where possible. When
something at work took longer than expected, a delayed report or an unanswered email, he felt
certain it signalled something catastrophic unfolding just beyond his view. Unusual silence in the
open-plan office, the kind that falls when colleagues step away for a moment, produced a visceral
anticipatory dread.

The memory itself remains sensory and fragmented in a way Tane finds deeply disorienting. He can
smell the wet earth and splintered wood when the recollection surfaces uninvited, and there are
moments when the taste — salt, dust, something alkaline — arrives without warning during an ordinary
afternoon. The memory is so extreme, so outside the register of his previous life, that he sometimes
finds it hard to believe it happened to him at all; it has the quality of something witnessed rather
than lived. Yet this very quality of unreality offers no protection. When the sensory fragments
return, they do not arrive as past events but as present signals, and the threat monitoring that
follows is immediate and physical — his body scanning the room, his attention narrowing, the sense
that something is about to happen again reinforcing the memory's grip rather than loosening it.

Tane has developed a set of strategies that he understands, on some level, are not helping. He
smokes heavily, stepping outside the office several times a day — a habit that began in the weeks
after the cyclone and has since become the primary way he interrupts states of tension. He avoids
relaxation deliberately, because moments of ease feel like the silence before the wind; he had been
relaxed, lying beside Hina, when the cyclone struck. He does not speak to colleagues or friends
about what happened. But the avoidance of talking has left the trauma unprocessed and his isolation
total, and the smoking and withdrawal have, over time, deepened his sense that he is managing rather
than living, reinforcing his belief that he is helpless and that the world remains implacably
dangerous. Even the rare moments when he feels briefly happy or at ease have become triggers in
themselves — a flash of warmth that his nervous system reads as the calm before catastrophe, pulling
him back into vigilance, back into the memory, back into the conviction that nothing good holds.

What finally brought Tane to seek help was not the stairwell incident, though it frightened him. It
was that he realised he could no longer remember Hina's voice without the smell of wreckage arriving
with it. He is most afraid that this is permanent. What he is most hoping, though he struggles to
say it plainly, is that she might one day be allowed to exist in his memory without the disaster.
