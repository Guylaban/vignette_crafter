Mateo is a 28-year-old Latino man who works as a corrections officer at a state prison. He was
referred for psychological evaluation following a period of escalating interpersonal conflict at
work and at home. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 42 and describes a significant deterioration in
functioning over the past several months, tracing everything back to the sudden death of his younger
brother, Rodrigo, who died in a car accident without warning. There had been no illness, no goodbye.
Mateo had been the last family member to speak to him by phone that evening, and when the call
ended, he had thought nothing of it. He has not been able to stop thinking about it since.

In the weeks following Rodrigo's death, Mateo began to notice that ordinary moments could pull him
under without warning. A news segment about a family killed in a highway collision, a coworker
mentioning that his father had passed — these were enough to produce a creeping sense of unreality,
as though the floor beneath him had quietly disappeared. In those moments, Mateo would feel himself
beginning to detach, watching his own hands from somewhere slightly outside his body. When that
dissociative feeling came on, he found himself reaching up to cover his face with both hands,
pressing his palms hard against his eyes as though he could physically block the sensation from
taking hold. He also began steering sharply away from anything connected to grief or loss — he
stopped attending church, where the priest sometimes spoke about the dead, and he avoided the corner
of the break room where colleagues had placed a memorial photo for a recently deceased inmate. These
avoidances were not planned; they accumulated the way scar tissue does, quietly and without
announcement.

What made the triggers especially unbearable was what they confirmed for him. Mateo had come to
believe, with a certainty that felt almost physical, that he had failed Rodrigo. He had been the
older brother. He should have known something was wrong. He should have kept him on the phone
longer, driven to see him, done something. This conviction — that he had failed to protect someone
who depended on him — sat at the centre of his self-understanding and darkened everything around it.
He was certain he had been a happy person once, but that version of himself now seemed like someone
else entirely, a stranger he could no longer locate. Worse, he saw no path forward. When he tried to
imagine the future, he found nothing — not despair exactly, but a kind of blankness, as though the
years ahead had simply been removed.

These appraisals did not stay still. They fed back into the very triggers he was trying to escape.

way that made dissociative episodes more likely to surface — a low-grade fragility that meant almost
any reference to loss could tip him over. And the strategies he had adopted to manage the pain were
making things worse in ways he was only beginning to acknowledge. Picking arguments with his wife or
manufacturing tension with colleagues at the prison gave him a few minutes of sharp, present-tense
feeling that cut through the numbness — but when the drama subsided, he was left more depleted than
before, the grief sitting heavier, and the belief that he was broken and beyond repair slightly more
entrenched. Covering his face and walking away from reminders kept the worst moments at bay in the
short term, but they also meant he never processed anything, and the appraisals calcified further.

trigger.
