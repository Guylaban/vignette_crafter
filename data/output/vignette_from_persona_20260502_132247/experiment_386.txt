Kofi Mensah is a 62-year-old Ghanaian-American stockbroker, divorced, who presented for
psychological assessment following what he described as a prolonged and worsening deterioration in
his functioning since the sudden death of his adult son, Kwame, approximately fourteen months ago.
Kwame, aged 34, collapsed without warning at a family gathering — a Sunday afternoon cookout in
Kofi's backyard — and was pronounced dead at the scene from a previously undetected cardiac
arrhythmia. Kofi had been standing no more than ten feet away, mid-conversation, when he heard his
son fall. He turned to find Kwame on the ground, unresponsive. He performed CPR until paramedics
arrived. Kwame never regained consciousness. The sudden, physical intimacy of that moment — his
hands on his son's chest, the weight of him, the silence that followed — left impressions that have
since fractured into something Kofi can neither fully access nor fully escape. His PCL-5 score of 63
reflects a presentation of significant clinical severity.

Kofi's memory of that afternoon is deeply disordered. He cannot recall the sequence of events with
any coherence; when he tries to deliberately reconstruct what happened, the memory resists him,
surfacing only in fragments — the pattern of Kwame's shirt, the sound of a neighbor's laughter
stopping abruptly — that arrive without context and vanish before he can hold them. This fragmented,
incomplete picture has become a source of profound distress in itself. Because he cannot assemble a
coherent account, he has come to believe that he must have missed something, done something wrong,
failed to notice a sign. The gaps in his memory feed relentless rumination: he goes over and over
the afternoon, trying to reconstruct what he cannot access, searching for the moment he could have
intervened. The harder he tries to remember, the more the memory splinters, and the more he
ruminates, the more exhausted and disoriented his recall becomes — each effort at reconstruction
leaving the picture less complete than before.

The intrusive fragments, when they do arrive, carry enormous threat. On the trading floor, where
Kofi spends his days surrounded by noise, sudden movement, and the physical press of colleagues, a
flash of memory can arrive without warning — a sudden piece of that afternoon — and for a moment he
is back in the backyard, his son on the ground, certain something terrible is about to happen again.
These flashbacks leave him flooded with shame, horror, and a guilt he cannot metabolize. He has
become certain, in the aftermath of these episodes, that he is fundamentally damaged — that his mind
cannot be trusted, that he is closer to collapse than anyone around him realizes. This persistent
sense of threat has, over months, begun to drive behavior that alarms even him: he has taken on
reckless trades, made decisions on the floor that carry exposure he would once have considered
unconscionable, as though part of him has stopped believing the future is worth protecting.
Colleagues have noticed. One senior partner raised concerns about his judgment in a formal review —
a professional consequence that would have been unthinkable a year ago.

In an effort to manage the threat he feels constantly, Kofi has worked hard to suppress any thought
connected to Kwame. He avoids the backyard entirely. He has declined every family gathering since
the funeral, stopped attending the Ghanaian community events he once anchored his weekends around,
and removed photographs from his apartment. But the suppression does not hold — it seems instead to
sensitize him, so that when a fragment does break through, it arrives with greater force, triggering
the flashbacks and negative emotions he was trying to forestall. The avoidance has also sealed off
the very cues that might otherwise help him reconstruct a more coherent narrative of what happened,
leaving the memory more fragmented still, and the rumination with nothing resolved to rest on.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a moment on the trading floor last month when he froze
mid-call, certain — for several seconds — that he could hear Kwame's voice. He told no one. He drove
home and sat in his car for two hours. He is most afraid that he is losing his mind. What he hopes,
tentatively, is that someone can help him remember what actually happened — and that remembering it
might finally let him stop.
