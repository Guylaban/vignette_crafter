Arjun is a 24-year-old South Asian man currently employed as a junior diplomat, a role he accepted
with considerable pride less than two years before the events that brought him to seek clinical
help. He is in a relationship, though he describes it with a flatness that suggests the connection
has become more obligation than comfort. His PCL-5 score of 66 places him in the severe range, and
the picture that has emerged across intake and early sessions is one of a young man who has not so
much survived a war zone as been quietly dismantled by it.

Approximately eighteen months ago, Arjun was stationed at a diplomatic post in a city that came
under sustained aerial bombardment. During one afternoon that he returns to repeatedly in session, a
series of explosions struck within blocks of his building. He had been in a meeting room when the
first blast hit; the windows blew inward, and in the chaos that followed, armed men entered the
compound. What Arjun cannot fully articulate — and he is acutely aware of this failure — is what
happened in the minutes after. He knows, in the way one knows facts on a page, that he was held at
gunpoint, that a colleague was badly injured beside him, that there was the sharp, unmistakable
smell of alcohol on the breath of one of the men who stood over him. He knows all of this. But the
words, he says, dissolve before they reach his mouth. What remains instead is sensory and immediate:
the smell, the sound, the feeling of being utterly exposed.

Back in Washington, where he has been reassigned to a desk role, Arjun's working days are structured
and ostensibly safe — and yet the embassy corridors, the security briefings, the occasional
ceremonial gathering where strangers approach with extended hands have become a sustained ordeal.
When a door slams in an adjacent office, or when a delivery truck backfires on the street outside,
the explosion is not remembered so much as re-entered. His body floods with the certainty that it is
happening again — the flashback arriving not as image but as total re-immersion — and from that
state of hyperarousal, the vivid, sensory-laden memory reasserts itself with full force, the smell
of alcohol returning as though the man were standing beside him again. These triggers — the bang,
the smell, the subtler but equally destabilising feeling of emotional vulnerability that descends
without warning — feed directly into a sense of threat that Arjun finds impossible to reason away.
Even when colleagues or his partner tell him he is safe, something in him cannot receive it.
Strangers approaching his desk, couriers arriving at the building entrance, anyone moving toward him
in an unannounced way — all of it registers as danger. The activated memory, so vivid and
olfactorily alive, keeps the nervous system persuaded that the past is still present, and the felt
sense of threat in turn pulls the memory forward again, a current that runs in both directions.

What has calcified around this cycle is a set of beliefs that Arjun holds about himself and the
world with quiet, devastating conviction. He has come to believe that there is something about him —
some quality he cannot name but feels responsible for — that made the event happen. He has lost
whatever faith he once carried in people, in the structures of diplomacy he entered with idealism,
in any sense that the future holds something worth moving toward. He sees no future for himself in
any concrete sense. These appraisals do not sit passively; they sharpen his vigilance, making every
ambiguous social encounter feel confirming rather than neutral, and they pull triggers closer — the
feeling of vulnerability becomes more frequent, more unbearable, because he already believes himself
to be fundamentally exposed.

To manage what he cannot resolve, Arjun has constructed a set of routines that have gradually taken
over. He takes drugs — he is matter-of-fact about this — to blunt the evenings. He exercises
compulsively, pushing past pain in a way he describes as the only time he feels he has any control
over his body. He wears concealing clothing when he goes out, a deliberate effort to pass unnoticed
through public space. These strategies offer brief relief, but under intoxication the memory
surfaces with less resistance, and the physical depletion from excessive exercise leaves him more
emotionally raw, more easily triggered, more certain the threat is real.

What finally brought Arjun in was his partner's quiet statement that she no longer recognised him.
He came not with hope exactly, but with the exhausted acknowledgment that what he is doing is not
working — and beneath that, barely voiced, the fear that nothing will.
