Amara is a twenty-year-old African woman working as a pharmacist in a busy urban dispensary,
currently in a relationship, and presenting with a PCL-5 score of 69. She was referred for
psychological assessment after a colleague noticed she had been arriving late to morning shifts and
appearing visibly distressed whenever deliveries arrived at the back of the pharmacy — deliveries
that sometimes came during rainstorms, when water pooled against the loading dock doors.

The trauma that brought Amara to this point occurred approximately fourteen months ago, when she was
detained by a paramilitary group in her country of origin during a period of civil unrest. Over
several days, she was subjected to prolonged physical torture in a structure near a flooding river.
She was restrained in stress positions for extended periods, her captors issuing commands she was
forced to obey under threat of further violence. At one point, the room flooded partially at the
ankles. Others detained alongside her did not survive. She was eventually released, and within
months had emigrated to the United States to begin her pharmacy training.

What Amara finds most difficult to communicate — to her partner, to her supervisor, to herself — is
that the event resists language. She cannot seem to put what happened into words, and the memories
that do surface come not as narrative but as sensation: her body suddenly rigid, arms pulled back as
though still restrained, breath shallow, the physical posture of captivity returning without
warning. She has the distinct impression that whole portions of what happened are missing, that the
picture is incomplete, and this incompleteness frightens her as much as what she can recall. These
fragmentary, embodied memories are reliably activated by specific sensory encounters. At the
pharmacy, when she hears water running through the pipes above the dispensary ceiling during heavy
rain, or glimpses flooding on a patient's phone screen, the somatic memories surge before she has
time to prepare. Even a raised voice between colleagues — a brief argument over stock inventory — is
enough to pull her back. Being asked by her supervisor to cover an unplanned shift, the simple fact
of having no choice, can send the same current through her.

Amara has come to believe, with quiet but absolute conviction, that she must remain on guard at all
times, because the world has shown her it can become dangerous without warning. This belief — that
hypervigilance is not a symptom but a rational necessity — organises much of her daily life. Before
she enters any room, including the dispensary storeroom she visits dozens of times each shift, she
identifies where the exits are. She sleeps poorly, lying alert in the dark, certain that relaxing
her attention would be a mistake. She scans the faces of customers for any resemblance to the men
who held her, and on two occasions has left the counter mid-transaction because a man's build or
colouring triggered a wave of recognition that felt indistinguishable from real danger. This
hypervigilant state keeps her nervous system primed, which means that ordinary triggers — rain on
the roof, a colleague's sharp tone — land with the full force of the original event, reactivating
the incomplete, wordless memories and confirming to her that the threat is still present. The sense
of ongoing threat in turn deepens her certainty that she cannot afford to think about what happened,
because she has come to believe she would not be able to handle it if she did — a belief that
forecloses the very processing that might bring relief.

Her avoidance has shaped itself around these triggers in ways that have quietly accumulated. She
avoids the late shift, when the building is quieter and harder to monitor. She avoids sleep by
staying up until exhaustion overtakes her, having learned that certain hours of the evening reliably
bring nightmares. She has stopped visiting the break room when a particular male colleague — whose
height reminds her of one of her captors — is present. These strategies have reduced her exposure to
the worst moments, but they have also kept the memories raw and inaccessible, preventing any
coherent account from forming, and sustaining the sense that danger is everywhere and constant.
Underneath all of it runs a thread of guilt she rarely speaks aloud: she survived, and others did
not, and she has come to feel that her suffering is, in some private way, deserved.

What finally brought Amara to seek help was her partner's distress. He told her gently that he no
longer recognised her, that she flinched at kindness, that she seemed to be living somewhere he
could not reach. She is most afraid that he is right — and most hoping, though she would not quite
say this yet, that some part of what happened can eventually be put into words.
