Malee Siriwan is a 52-year-old Thai-American woman who came to the United States with her husband
nearly two decades ago and has worked the morning shift at a fast food restaurant in a mid-sized
Southern city for the past six years. She presented for treatment following a catastrophic flood
that struck her neighbourhood fourteen months ago, during which she was trapped alone in her home
for nearly eighteen hours as water rose to chest height in her living room. The event unfolded in
the early hours of the morning, and what she remembered most vividly — in the fragmented, non-
sequential way that had come to define her recall — was the sound: the deep, percussive crack of
something structural giving way outside, the water arriving not as a trickle but as a sudden cold
pressure against her legs, and then a long darkness broken only by the intermittent groan of the
house settling. She had screamed and no one had come. When rescuers finally reached her, she was
standing on her kitchen counter, shaking, the water just below her knees.

In the months that followed, Malee's memory of the flood did not consolidate into a coherent
narrative she could hold at a distance. Instead, it arrived in disconnected pieces — a flash of the
waterline against the wall, the weight of her wet clothing, the specific quality of silence between
sounds — and these fragments forced themselves into her awareness without warning or invitation. The
intrusions were not merely uncomfortable; when the memory returned, the emotional overwhelm was so
total that she had to stop whatever she was doing, gripping a counter or stepping outside until it
passed. This quality of the memory — its uncontrollability, its incompleteness — had become itself a
source of dread, and the anticipation of being overwhelmed again kept her in a state of chronic
alertness that she could not voluntarily lower.

That alertness had become the texture of her daily life. Malee slept poorly, lying awake long after
her husband had settled, listening. Nighttime felt categorically less safe to her than daylight, a
belief she could not reason herself out of even when she tried. At the restaurant, where the kitchen
environment produced a near-constant stream of unexpected sounds — the clatter of trays, the hiss of
the fryer, the sudden bang of a delivery door — she found herself flinching and scanning several
times each shift. A loud noise would arrest her mid-motion at the counter, her body bracing for
something she could not name. Her supervisor had noticed, and while nothing formal had been said,
Malee was aware that she had become slower, less reliable, and she felt this as a quiet accumulation
of shame.

What made the threat feel so inescapable was not only the memory itself but what Malee had come to
believe the flood had revealed about her life and her place in the world. She had come to feel, with
a certainty she could not shake, that she had no power to prevent catastrophe — that bad things
arrived without warning and that nothing she did would matter. This conviction of helplessness
coloured every moment of vigilance: because she was certain that disaster could come again at any
time, her nervous system remained perpetually mobilised, scanning for early signals that others
around her seemed not to notice. Equally corrosive was her sense that she no longer belonged
anywhere — not in this country, not in her neighbourhood, not even in her own home, which no longer
felt like shelter. These appraisals fed directly into how she held the memory of the flood: because
she believed herself permanently damaged and unmoored, the fragments of that night felt not like a
past event but like ongoing evidence of who she now was, making the intrusions harder to set aside
and easier to be overwhelmed by.

What finally brought Malee to seek help was a morning at work when she heard a loud metallic crash
from the back of the kitchen and found herself unable to stop crying for nearly twenty minutes in
the staff bathroom. She told her therapist that she did not expect to feel like herself again — she
was not sure that self still existed — but she hoped, quietly, that she might one day sleep through
the night without listening for the sound of water.
