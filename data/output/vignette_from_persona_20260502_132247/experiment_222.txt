Tariq is a 56-year-old married man of Lebanese descent who works as a social media manager for a
mid-sized marketing firm. He presented for intake reporting a PCL-5 score of 48, following an
incident of police violence that occurred approximately fourteen months ago. He had been attending a
community vigil in his neighborhood when officers moved in to disperse the crowd. In the chaos that
followed, Tariq was shoved to the ground, struck repeatedly, and held face-down on the pavement with
his arm wrenched behind his back. He recalls the specific, acrid taste of blood and asphalt in his
mouth as his face was pressed against the ground — a detail that returns to him unbidden and with
nauseating fidelity. He remembers screaming that he had done nothing wrong, and that no one
intervened. It is this last fact — the bystanders, the silence, the faces that looked away — that
has since become the organizing wound of his inner life.

In the weeks that followed, Tariq found that the world had rearranged itself around a single
conviction: that people are not what they seem, that those who appear neutral or friendly are
capable of indifference or complicity at the moment it matters most. He had also come to believe,
with a shame he could not fully articulate, that he had acted in ways that went against who he was —
that he had begged, that he had not fought back, that something essential had been surrendered on
that pavement. These appraisals — that the world is dangerous, that others cannot be trusted, that
he had violated his own values — did not remain abstract. They drove him toward a set of strategies
that, over months, quietly restructured his life. He began avoiding anything that might stir
feeling: he stopped watching television, refused to read news, and withdrew from the cultural
commentary that had once been the lifeblood of his professional enthusiasm. He had come to believe
that if he could keep his emotional register flat, he could remain safe.

But the sense of safety never came. At work, where his job requires him to monitor and curate social
media feeds, Tariq finds himself scanning not just for relevant content but for anything that might
signal threat — a raised voice in the open-plan office, a colleague approaching from behind, the
flash of a police uniform in a video thumbnail. Before he attends any meeting, he identifies the
exits. He cannot sit with his back to the door. He has told his wife he simply prefers certain seats
at restaurants; she has not yet understood that he is mapping escape routes. This persistent state
of threat monitoring — the inability to relax even in spaces he knows to be objectively safe — feeds
back directly into his worst appraisals. The fact that he cannot feel safe anywhere confirms, in his
private reasoning, that the world truly is dangerous and that his distrust of others is simply
realism rather than symptom.

The memories themselves have become a site of active struggle. When the recall surfaces — triggered
most often by the taste of something metallic, or by video footage of crowds on his work screen — it
arrives with an intensity of rage that frightens him. He has learned to push the memory away before
it can fully form, cutting it off before it reaches its conclusion. Yet this suppression, which
offers momentary relief, appears to be sustaining the very threat state it was meant to quiet: the
incomplete, repeatedly interrupted memory leaves him in a state of unresolved physiological arousal
that makes relaxation impossible and keeps his scanning behavior constant. The avoidance, in turn,
forecloses the kind of emotional processing that might soften his appraisals; because he never
allows himself to feel through the event, his beliefs about his own violated character and the
world's fundamental danger remain untested and intact. The loop has grown tighter over fourteen
months. He is, by his own account, exhausted.

What finally brought Tariq to seek help was a moment at work last month when he deleted an entire
week's worth of scheduled content — posts he himself had written — because a single image reminded
him of the vigil. His supervisor noticed. Tariq is most afraid that he will never trust another
person again. What he hopes for, quietly, is to be able to sit in a room and simply not be waiting
for something terrible to happen.
