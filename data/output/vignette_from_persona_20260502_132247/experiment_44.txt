seconds, close enough to hear the sounds Cody made and to hold pressure on wounds while waiting
forty minutes for emergency services to arrive in the remote site. Cody survived but lost the use of
his legs. Dale has not returned to active field supervision since.

In the weeks after the accident, Dale told himself the numbness would pass. It did not. What came
instead were memories that arrived not as recollections but as physical events — when the image of
Cody on the ground surfaced, Dale's body would fold forward, his hands pressing involuntarily
against his own thighs, replicating the posture he had held on the forest floor. Along with the
bodily reenactment came a wave of grief so total it felt like drowning, and beneath the grief, a

random: they were summoned. Any physical pain — a strained shoulder, a headache, even the ordinary
ache of middle age — would pull the scene back with sudden force, his nervous system apparently
treating his own body's distress as a signal that Cody's injuries were happening again. Objects or
locations that reminded him of the victim — a hard hat hanging in the garage, a photograph of the
crew — carried the same power, and the memories they triggered arrived with the same bodily weight
and emotional violence.

As the memories accumulated, they fed a set of convictions Dale had begun to form about himself. He
had come to believe, with the quiet certainty of a man reviewing evidence, that there was something

something intrinsic. The memory of his own confident voice giving the felling direction replayed as
proof. He was certain he could no longer rely on his own judgment in any domain, and the skills that
had defined him for thirty years felt not merely rusty but gone, stripped away by what had happened.
