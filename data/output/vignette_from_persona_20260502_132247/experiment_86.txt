Rosalinda is a 79-year-old widowed Latina woman who spent decades as a combat pilot, a career that
demanded precisely the kind of controlled vigilance she now finds impossible to switch off. She came
to the clinic after her daughter insisted, following an incident in which Rosalinda had pressed
herself against a grocery store wall when a police cruiser idled at the curb outside, her hands
shaking so badly she dropped her basket. She told the intake clinician she had "held it together for
long enough," and her PCL-5 score of 80 confirmed what her voice already communicated: she had not
been holding it together at all.

The event that brought her to this point occurred approximately fourteen months ago. Rosalinda had
been walking back to her car after an evening community meeting when she was stopped by two officers
who mistook her for a suspect. What followed was not brief. She was pushed against the hood of a
patrol car, her cheek pressed to the warm metal, her arms wrenched behind her. She remembers the
specific weight of a hand at the back of her neck, the radio crackling inches from her ear, and the
smell — gun oil and exhaust and something chemical she has never been able to name. A crowd gathered
on the sidewalk. She could hear them but not see them, and the sound of their shuffling and
murmuring merged with the officers' voices into a wall of noise that felt as though it would not
stop.

Rosalinda does not remember the event as a continuous narrative. She holds it instead as a sequence
of frozen images: the officer's belt at eye level, a bystander's sneakers on wet pavement, the angle
of a streetlight. These snapshot memories carry no temporal logic, which she finds deeply
disorienting — she is a woman trained to reconstruct sequences under pressure, and the fact that she
cannot reconstruct this one has itself become a source of dread. Fragments surface without warning:
a sudden picture of the hood of the car, or the smell of gun oil arriving without context while she
is reviewing a flight log. When these pieces appear, her body responds as though the event is
unfolding again — heart rate climbing, breathing shallow, muscles bracing — and the physical
reactivity of these intrusions keeps the memory charged, ensuring that it resurfaces with renewed
force and that the next intrusion is no less violent than the last.

The triggers Rosalinda encounters in her daily environment are not abstract. As a pilot still
consulting part-time at a regional airfield, she moves through spaces where authority figures in
uniform are common, where radios and intercoms produce sudden bursts of sound, and where ground
crews work in tight clusters. The sight of a uniformed officer near the terminal, or the wail of an
emergency vehicle on the approach road, is enough to activate the full weight of her threat system:
she cannot relax even in environments she rationally knows are safe, she scans every room for
objects that could become weapons, and her body floods with the same physical alarm that accompanied
the original event. This heightened threat state, once activated by a trigger, does not simply fade
— it pulls the fragmented memory back into awareness, so that the smell or the image arrives
alongside the alarm, and the cycle begins again.

Over the months since the incident, Rosalinda has built a set of strategies that feel to her like
reasonable precautions. She avoids wearing the dark jacket she had on that night, and has quietly
retired several similar garments. She stays awake until well past midnight, because lying in the
dark is when the fragments come most freely, and exhaustion at least delays them. She scans every
environment she enters — the airfield briefing room, the pharmacy, the church hall where her
daughter holds birthday parties — cataloguing exits and potential threats with the same systematic
attention she once gave to hostile airspace. But the scanning keeps her nervous system primed, and a
primed nervous system finds threats, and found threats pull the memory forward again. Her late
nights leave her cognitively depleted in ways that loosen her grip on the fragments further, so that
by morning the images feel closer and more vivid than they did the night before.

What finally broke through her resistance was not a single dramatic episode but an accumulation: she
had begun declining flights she was qualified to take, telling colleagues she was "stepping back,"
and her daughter had noticed she no longer sat facing away from the door in any room. Rosalinda
arrived at the clinic hoping, in her words, to "get the pictures in the right order." What she
feared most was that they never would be.
