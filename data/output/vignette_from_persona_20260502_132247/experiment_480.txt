Rajan is a 34-year-old South Asian man who works as a delivery driver and has been married for six
years. He presented for an initial assessment reporting a PCL-5 score of 46, describing persistent
and consuming guilt that has quietly restructured his daily life over the past several months.

The incident occurred on a grey Tuesday morning in late autumn. Rajan was running behind schedule —
a recurring pressure in his line of work — and was navigating a tight residential street when a
young boy on a bicycle appeared from between two parked cars. Rajan braked hard, but not hard
enough. The child sustained a fractured pelvis and a broken arm and was hospitalised for nearly
three weeks. He survived and, by all medical accounts, is expected to recover fully. Rajan was not
cited for reckless driving. None of that has mattered to him.

In the weeks following the accident, Rajan began to reconstruct the morning in exhaustive detail,
searching each decision for the precise moment he could have intervened differently. He had been
checking the dispatch app on his phone mount seconds before turning onto the street. He had been
moving at what he now insists was too fast for that particular road, regardless of whether it was
technically within the speed limit. He had, he believed, been careless in a way that was entirely
his own doing — not bad luck, not an unavoidable convergence of circumstances, but a direct
consequence of who he was and how he had chosen to act. He had come to believe, with quiet
certainty, that the event happened because of the way he acted, and that no framing of the situation
as an accident could change the essential truth of his culpability.

This belief has taken on a moral weight that extends beyond the crash itself. Rajan thinks of
himself as someone whose job it is to move through other people's neighbourhoods, past their homes
and schools and children, and he had failed the most basic obligation that responsibility carried.
He should have been watching more carefully. He should have slowed down on that street. He should
have protected that boy, and he did not. The word "should" appears constantly in his speech — not as
regret but as indictment.

What has followed is a form of internal trial that never reaches a verdict. Rajan reports that he
will never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened, and this is not a passing
feeling but a settled conviction. He does not believe forgiveness is something he has earned or
could earn. His wife has told him repeatedly that it was an accident, that the child recovered, that
he is a careful and conscientious person. He listens to her patiently and remains entirely unmoved.
At work, the guilt is most acute: every time he turns down a narrow residential street — which his
routes require him to do dozens of times each day — the memory of that morning reasserts itself. He
has begun taking longer routes to avoid certain roads near schools, which has added time to his
shifts and drawn comment from his dispatcher. He has not explained why.

At home, Rajan has become quieter and more withdrawn. His wife has noticed that he no longer seems
to take pleasure in things he once did — weekend cricket with friends, cooking together on Sunday
evenings — though he continues to function and has not taken time off work. He carries the weight of
what happened largely in silence, which he has rationalised as protecting his wife from worry,
though it has created a distance between them that neither has fully named.

What finally brought Rajan to seek help was a conversation with his wife in which she told him,
carefully and without accusation, that she felt she had lost him. He did not dispute it. He came to
the first session saying very little about what he hoped for, only that he was tired — tired of the
replaying, tired of the weight of it, and, most of all, afraid that she was right, and that the
person he had been before that Tuesday morning was simply gone.
