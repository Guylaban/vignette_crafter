Thanh is a 60-year-old Vietnamese-American man who has managed a mid-sized hotel in the Pacific
Northwest for nearly two decades. He lives with his long-term partner and was, by all accounts, a
steady and capable professional — the kind of man who prided himself on knowing every corner of his
building and keeping things running without incident. That self-image collapsed approximately
fourteen months ago when a contractor improperly stored and mixed industrial cleaning chemicals in a
poorly ventilated basement utility room. Thanh, conducting his customary morning walkthrough,
entered the room and within seconds felt a searing burn in his throat and nasal passages, his eyes
streaming, his chest tightening as if gripped from the inside. He stumbled backward into the
corridor, unable to call out, certain in those moments that his lungs were being destroyed.
Emergency services were called; he was hospitalised overnight for chemical inhalation injury. The
physical damage was assessed as moderate and largely resolved within weeks. What did not resolve was
everything else.

In the months that followed, the memory of those moments in the utility room began returning in a
form that felt nothing like ordinary recollection. Without warning — during a staff briefing, while
reviewing invoices, while simply walking the hotel's corridors — the memory would force itself into
Thanh's mind with an immediacy that erased the present. He would feel the burn again at the back of
his throat, and, most disturbingly, he could taste it: a sharp, acrid chemical taste that arrived
with the memory as though it were still happening. He has described these intrusions as not
remembering but returning — the sensation that fourteen months had collapsed and he was once again
in that room, unable to breathe, certain he was dying. These intrusive episodes, in their vividness
and bodily immediacy, reinforced his deepest fear: that his body had been permanently altered and
could no longer be trusted. The taste arriving unbidden felt like evidence that something was still
wrong inside him, and this conviction — that his body was unreliable — deepened each time the memory
broke through.

That belief became the lens through which Thanh interpreted almost every physical sensation. He had
come to feel that he could not stop bad things from happening to him, that the exposure had
confirmed a fundamental helplessness he could no longer argue against. These appraisals did not
remain abstract; they drove his behaviour in ways that accumulated into a recognisable pattern. He
began scanning his body constantly — attending to his breathing, monitoring the back of his throat
for any trace of that chemical taste, cataloguing every tightness in his chest. He also began
scanning his environment with the same vigilance, checking ventilation grates, noting the smell of
cleaning products, pausing at closed doors. At work, this meant that a job requiring confident,
fluid movement through the hotel had become an exercise in managed dread. He would hesitate before
entering storage rooms, sometimes sending junior staff ahead. Colleagues noticed. His partner
noticed. Thanh, who had once moved through the building with proprietary ease, now felt like a
stranger in it — a man who no longer belonged anywhere, not in his workplace, not in the body he had
inhabited for sixty years.

The vigilance itself fed the very processes it was meant to contain. When Thanh scanned his body and
found a sensation — a tightness, a mild throat-clearing — his nervous system treated it as
confirmation of threat, flooding him with the readiness he could not turn off. His body felt
perpetually braced. When a maintenance request took longer than expected to be fulfilled, he was
certain something had gone wrong, that another exposure was unfolding somewhere in the building.
This state of constant physiological alert made him more susceptible to the intrusive memories,
which arrived with greater force when his arousal was already elevated, and each intrusion renewed
his certainty that he was helpless and his body unreliable, closing the loop tightly around him.

What finally brought Thanh to seek help was a conversation with his partner, who told him quietly
one evening that she was frightened — not of anything external, but of watching him disappear into
himself. He came to the first session hoping, above everything, that someone could tell him his body
was not still poisoned. What he feared most was that it was.
