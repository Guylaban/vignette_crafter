Miriam, a 72-year-old married woman of North American background, has spent the better part of four
decades as a network engineer — a career that has always suited her methodical, systems-oriented
mind. She came to the current intake following months of quiet insistence from her husband, who had
watched her world contract steadily since the afternoon, roughly two years ago, when she witnessed a
violent assault on a city bus. She had been riding home from a downtown conference, seated near the
back, when a confrontation between two men escalated with terrifying speed into a brutal attack on a
third passenger — a younger man who had tried to intervene. The sounds, the confined space, the
press of frozen bystanders, the helplessness of watching someone be seriously harmed while the bus
lurched forward through traffic: all of it imprinted with a specificity that has not softened with
time.

In the weeks that followed, Miriam noticed that certain ordinary moments seemed to reach directly
into that afternoon and pull it forward. Boarding any bus, even briefly considering it, returned her
immediately to the vibration of the vehicle and the smell of the seats. Crowded places — shopping
centres, transit platforms, the open-plan floors of her own workplace — reproduced the sensation of
being hemmed in with no clear exit. Even certain conversation topics, colleagues casually discussing
crime or urban safety over lunch, could snap her back to the moment the first punch landed. Each
time one of these triggers fired, it did not merely recall the event; it confirmed something. She
had come to believe, with a quiet but absolute certainty, that the world was a genuinely dangerous
place, that violence could erupt without warning anywhere people gathered, and that she was required
to remain perpetually alert to survive it. The triggers, in this way, fed directly into her
appraisals — every intrusion became fresh evidence for the threat she was certain surrounded her.

Those appraisals, in turn, reshaped her behaviour entirely. Convinced that constant vigilance was
the only rational response to an unpredictable world, Miriam began avoiding the bus route she had
taken for years and eventually stopped using public transport altogether. She rerouted her commute
by car, added twenty minutes each way, and told herself this was simply prudent. She stopped
attending the quarterly industry conferences she had once enjoyed, because conference centres meant
crowds, and crowds meant exposure to exactly the kind of compressed, uncontrollable situation in
which harm had found her before. She avoided the intersection near the bus stop where the incident
had occurred, taking a longer walk to her parking garage each evening. At work, where the open
office floor sometimes filled with contractors and visiting teams, she began scheduling her most
demanding technical tasks for early mornings before others arrived, or dialling into meetings
remotely rather than walking to the conference room. A colleague who had been a lunch companion for
years remarked, gently, that Miriam seemed to have become a ghost.

What she had not told that colleague — or her husband, fully — was the internal strategy running
alongside the avoidance. When intrusive images surfaced, or when anxiety spiked before a work event
she could not escape, Miriam forced herself to think only positive thoughts: redirecting
deliberately toward gratitude, toward the safety of her home, toward the fact that she had not been
the one hurt. She had come to rely on this effortful reframing as a kind of mental hygiene. Yet the
strategy maintained the very architecture it was meant to dismantle. By suppressing the distress
rather than processing it, she remained sensitised; the avoided situations stayed charged. And
because the positive reframing kept her from tolerating the triggers long enough to disconfirm her
fears, her conviction that the world was dangerous — and that she was permanently set apart from the
easy, unguarded way other people moved through it — only deepened. She had come to feel, with a
sadness she could not quite articulate, that she was an outsider now, watching other people inhabit
a world she no longer fully trusted.

What finally brought her in was a family gathering she had declined to attend — her granddaughter's
birthday, held at a community centre. Her husband had gone alone. That evening, sitting in the quiet
house, Miriam decided she wanted her life back, though she was afraid, most of all, that it might
already be too late.
