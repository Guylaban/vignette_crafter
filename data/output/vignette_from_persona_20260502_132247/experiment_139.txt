Valentina Reyes is a 22-year-old Salvadoran American woman who works as a junior accountant at a
mid-sized firm in Houston. She was referred for psychological assessment following a conversation
with her primary care physician, who noted that she had been experiencing significant distress for
nearly eighteen months. The precipitating event occurred when Valentina was twenty years old and her
four-year-old nephew, Mateo, whom she had been babysitting alone for the first time, choked on a
small piece of hard candy and lost consciousness briefly before a neighbor with first-aid training
intervened. Mateo recovered fully, but in the minutes before help arrived, Valentina knelt on the
kitchen floor holding him, certain she was watching him die. She remembers the sweetness still
coating her own tongue from the same candy she had given him — a detail that returns to her unbidden
and without warning, arriving not as a thought but as a sensation, the taste of something that
should have been innocent flooding her mouth and pulling her back to that floor.

What makes the memory particularly difficult is that Valentina cannot seem to hold it whole. She has
never been able to describe the event in a way that feels adequate or complete; when asked to
recount it, she finds herself reaching for words that do not come, as though the experience exists
in a register that language cannot access. And when the fragments do begin to surface — the weight
of Mateo's small body, the sound of the neighbor's voice — she finds herself pushing them back down
before they can fully unfold. This pattern of suppression has left the memory unprocessed and poorly
integrated, and it is precisely this fragmented, wordless quality that feeds the darkest conclusions
she has drawn about herself and her life. Because she cannot make sense of what happened or place it
safely in the past, she has come to believe that she is fundamentally helpless — that bad things
will happen and she will be powerless to prevent them, as she was powerless that afternoon. That
belief has quietly colonized her sense of who she is. She used to describe herself as cheerful,
social, someone her family counted on for lightness. Now she says flatly that she is always
miserable, that the person she was before that day is simply gone, and that she feels set apart from
friends and colleagues in a way she cannot explain and they cannot see.

The threat monitoring that has developed since the event shapes her daily life in ways that have
become increasingly difficult to manage. Silence, in particular, has become charged with menace. In
her open-plan office, when the usual ambient noise of keyboards and conversation drops — during a
long lunch hour, or when colleagues step out for a meeting — Valentina feels a physical tightening
in her chest, a certainty that something dangerous is gathering just out of sight. Similarly, when a
spreadsheet takes longer to load than expected, or when a colleague does not respond to a message
within what she considers a reasonable window, she assumes something has gone wrong. These moments
of perceived delay or quiet function as triggers, and when they occur, her body responds with the
full force of the original emergency: her heart pounds, her hands sweat, and she finds herself
scanning the room. And it is in these moments of acute physical reactivity — when her nervous system

returning, the images pressing at the edges of suppression, making the threat feel not like a memory
but like a present reality.

she could articulate. What she hopes for, she said quietly at intake, is to feel like herself again.
