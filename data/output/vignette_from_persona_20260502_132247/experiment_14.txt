Valentina Reyes is a 54-year-old married Latina woman who has served as a combat engineer for the
better part of her adult life. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 62, seeking help for the first
time after what she describes as "finally running out of ways to hold it together." The incident
that fractured her sense of safety occurred during a forward operating mission several years ago,
when an improvised explosive device detonated beneath a vehicle in her unit. The blast ignited a
fuel line, and within seconds the air was thick with fire and the screaming of men she had trained
alongside for years. The heat pressed against her skin like a physical weight — a specific, searing
warmth she has never been able to forget — and the last sounds she registered before the ringing
overtook everything were the shouts of her team and then, abruptly, silence.

In the months that followed her return, Valentina noticed that certain moments in her environment
could collapse the distance between then and now without any warning. A candle lit at the dinner
table, a grill igniting in a neighbor's yard, the specific warmth of summer air against her forearms
— any of these could instantaneously restore the full weight of that afternoon, not as a
recollection but as a reliving. She would hear, internally, the shouting of her teammates, the
particular pitch of voices calling out in panic, and find herself gripping whatever surface was
nearest. The memory itself offered no coherent arc she could follow; when she tried to recount what
happened — even to herself, alone — the sequence dissolved into fragments, heat and noise and a
smell she cannot name. This incoherence frightened her almost as much as the intrusions themselves,
feeding a growing conviction that there was something fundamentally wrong with her, that a person
who had held her rank and her composure for decades should not be coming apart over a candle flame.
The belief that she was defective, combined with a deep and unresolved sense of injustice — that
what happened to her unit was never properly acknowledged, that no accountability followed —
sharpened her certainty that the world was a dangerous place indifferent to the people inside it.
These appraisals, in turn, kept her perceptual system permanently calibrated for threat: her body
remained tense and braced throughout the day, her attention drawn outward and scanning, and any
unusual silence — the kind that fell over the motor pool during a lull, or settled over her house in
the early morning — registered not as peace but as the moment before something terrible.

To manage all of this, Valentina built a careful architecture of avoidance. She began routing her
daily movements at the base to bypass areas where welding or controlled burns might occur. She
stopped attending unit social events where grills were lit and voices could rise with laughter that,
at a certain pitch, resembled shouting. She numbed herself emotionally with a deliberateness she
recognized but could not stop — telling herself that if she did not feel much, she could not be
overwhelmed. Over time, however, this numbing stripped her of the attentiveness her work required;
her difficulty concentrating became noticeable to a supervising officer who quietly recommended she
speak to someone. Her husband, a patient man, had begun to describe their evenings together as
"sitting in the same room alone." The avoidance that was meant to protect her had, by contracting
her world, removed the ordinary interruptions that might have gently disconfirmed her belief that
danger was everywhere — so her sense of threat only deepened. And because she was no longer exposing
herself to manageable reminders, even minor ones arrived with amplified force, her body already at
full alert before she had consciously registered the stimulus. The scanning that was meant to keep
her safe kept her nervous system perpetually mobilized, which in turn made the intrusive memories
easier to trigger, which returned her again to the conviction that she was broken and the world
could not be trusted.

What finally brought Valentina in was a morning at the base when a controlled burn exercise began
without warning nearby. The heat reached her skin before she saw the flames, and she was back in the
vehicle before she was back in the present. She sat in her car afterward for forty minutes, unable
to explain to herself what had happened. What she is most afraid of is that this is permanent. What
she is most hoping for, though she says it quietly, is that someone will tell her it is not.
