Miriam Holloway is a 76-year-old divorced woman who has served on her city's council for over a
decade, a role that has long given her a sense of order and civic purpose that her childhood home
never offered. She came to the current intake with a PCL-5 score of 48, describing a presentation
that, in her own words, she had "managed to outrun for most of my life" — until recently, she no
longer could.

The trauma Miriam witnessed unfolded across years rather than in a single incident, but the most
formative episode occurred when she was nine years old. She describes standing in the hallway of the
family home, frozen, as her father — a large man who smelled of cigarettes and something medicinal —
pinned her mother against the kitchen wall during a medical crisis her mother had tried to manage
quietly at home. A visiting nurse had arrived to examine her mother, and Miriam watched as her
father's rage at the intrusion became physical. The nurse's latex gloves, the clinical smell of
antiseptic, her mother's suppressed cry, and the particular way her father's voice dropped before he
struck — these details lodged somewhere that decades of adult competence could not fully reach.
Comments from extended family in the years that followed — "your father had his reasons," "don't air
what happens at home" — taught Miriam early that the event was unspeakable, and she became expert at
not speaking it, even to herself.

What brings her to treatment now is a convergence of exposures she can no longer avoid. As a council
member, Miriam attends community health forums and ribbon-cuttings at medical facilities. In recent
months, the smell of antiseptic in a hospital corridor during a site visit, or the sight of a nurse
snapping on latex gloves, has been enough to collapse the distance she has maintained for sixty-
seven years. These moments function as potent triggers precisely because they replicate the sensory
texture of that hallway: the medicinal smell, the clinical instruments, the atmosphere of
examination and helplessness. When triggered in this way, she does not always retrieve a coherent
image — sometimes what arrives is only the emotion, raw and sourceless, a wave of dread and shame
with no picture attached, as though the feeling has survived the memory intact. At other times the
memory feels more present and vivid than anything happening in the room around her, more real than
the council chamber or the faces of her colleagues — the past momentarily outweighing the present in
sensory weight.

This quality of the memory, in turn, makes her more vigilant in her daily environment. Because the
recollection can arrive so suddenly and feel so immediate, Miriam has become hyperattentive to
anything that might summon it. She scans for her estranged father — now in his late nineties and
occasionally mentioned by relatives — at family-adjacent civic events, and the mere possibility of
seeing him at a community gathering has caused her to pre-emptively decline two public appearances
this year. Comments about family, whether offered casually by a constituent or raised in a council
discussion about domestic policy, land on her with a force disproportionate to their intent; each
one becomes a potential trigger, and she has learned to steer conversations away from them with the
practiced efficiency of someone who has been doing so for most of her adult life.

She has also begun pushing the memories away before they can fully form — a habit she describes as
necessary but exhausting, like pressing a hand over a wound. She is aware, dimly, that this effort
prevents her from knowing exactly what she is afraid of, but she has come to believe that full
recall would be worse than the partial images she currently endures.

What finally brought Miriam to seek help was a scheduled cardiac examination — routine, her
physician assured her — that she postponed three times before admitting she could not enter the
clinic without shaking. She is most afraid, she says, that after seventy-six years of managing this
alone, she will not be able to stop it from undoing the professional life she has built. What she is
hoping for, quietly, is simply to be able to walk into a medical office without her childhood
following her through the door.
