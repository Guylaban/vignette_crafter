Harold is a 69-year-old widowed financial analyst who was referred for psychological assessment
following a prolonged period of functional decline and emotional dysregulation in the months after
his wife's death. His PCL-5 score of 74 places him in the severe range, and the clinical picture

intrusion, physiological alarm, and desperate suppression that has, by his own reckoning, made the
last year of his life nearly unlivable.

The traumatic event itself was not sudden. Harold watched his wife, Diane, deteriorate over fourteen
months from a diagnosis of aggressive pancreatic cancer to her death in the hospital room where he
sat holding her hand. What he has described most haltingly is not the death itself but a specific
afternoon three weeks before the end, when Diane, heavily medicated and barely conscious, suddenly
gripped his wrist and said his name with a clarity she had not shown in days. He understood in that
moment, with a certainty he has never been able to shake, that she was frightened, and that there
was nothing he could do. He left the hospital that evening and drove home alone through streets that
looked, he said, like they belonged to someone else's life.

In the months that followed, Harold found that certain moments in his environment carried that
afternoon back to him with brutal immediacy. A colleague mentioning a parent's illness during a team
call, a radio segment about hospice care, the word "loss" appearing in a client's estate planning
document — any of these could produce a sudden, unbidden return of the hospital room, the
fluorescent light, Diane's grip on his wrist. These triggers did not merely recall the memory; they
reliably produced a cascade of physical reactivity — his chest would tighten, his hands would
tremble over his keyboard, his heart would accelerate in a way he described as feeling like a
warning — and with that came waves of guilt, horror, and a shame he struggled to articulate, rooted
in his belief that he had somehow failed her in her fear. The physical alarm and the emotional
intensity of those moments felt to Harold like evidence that something was genuinely wrong with him,
which itself kept his attention narrowed toward anything that might signal another intrusion, so
that ordinary cues — a quiet office, a phone ringing unexpectedly — were scanned for threat and,
when they arrived, amplified what was already there.

Harold's response to this was to construct, methodically and with the discipline of a man who had
spent four decades managing risk, a life organized around avoidance. He declined invitations to a
colleague's retirement gathering because he knew there would be toasts, and toasts meant reflection
on time passing and people gone. He stopped attending his church, where grief and mortality were
subjects that arose without warning. He routed his morning commute away from the cemetery where
Diane was buried. At work, he began deflecting any conversation that touched on loss themes with a
brisk efficiency his younger colleagues have apparently taken for coldness. The numbing he employed
was deliberate — he described it as "turning a dial down" — and the suppression of intrusive
thoughts was effortful and constant. What Harold had not anticipated was that this architecture of
avoidance, rather than protecting him, seemed to make the triggers more potent when they did break
through; the suppressed material returned with greater force, and the physical reactivity that
followed was, if anything, worse for the silence around it. His irritability, which he had initially
attributed to grief, had become a source of rupture at work — a sharp exchange with a junior analyst
over a minor error had been escalated to HR, and it was this incident, more than anything, that
finally brought him to seek help.

Harold arrives at sessions punctual and formally dressed, a man who has held himself together

without his body turning against him.
