at close range, and Tariq, standing no more than fifteen feet away, watched one of the men collapse
onto the pavement. The sound of the shot — sudden, enormous, filling the air — was the axis around
which the entire experience seemed to turn. Tariq was not physically harmed, but he stood frozen for
what felt like minutes, certain in those moments that he was about to die, that the violence would
turn toward him next. The police arrived, there were more loud voices and radio static, and by the
time Tariq was driven home that evening, something fundamental had shifted in how the world felt to
him.

In the months that followed, Tariq found that the world had become a place that required constant
monitoring. He had come to believe, in a way that felt entirely rational to him, that safety was not
a general condition but a specific and fragile one — available only in a small number of places he
had carefully identified and tested. His apartment, a particular corner booth at a diner two blocks

his nervous system remained on a kind of standing alert. When he heard unexpected sounds — a car

undefined way. Similarly, mornings themselves have become laden. The pre-9 a.m. hours, when the
light looks a certain way and the shift is just beginning, carry a weight that Tariq cannot fully
explain to his coworkers. He simply knows that something bad happened at that hour, and his body has
not forgotten.
