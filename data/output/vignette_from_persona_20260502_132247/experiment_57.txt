Tama is a 42-year-old Samoan-American electrician currently living with his partner in a mid-sized
city. He presented for psychological assessment following a severe physical assault that occurred
approximately fourteen months ago, reporting a PCL-5 score of 78 and describing a daily existence
that has narrowed almost beyond recognition since the attack.

The assault took place in the early evening, just after dusk, when Tama had stayed late at a
commercial job site to finish a wiring panel. He was alone in a corridor when two men entered
through a heavy fire door — the bang of it slamming against the frame was the first thing he
registered — and attacked him without warning, striking him repeatedly and leaving him on the floor
before fleeing. The violence was sudden, wordless, and sustained. Tama did not lose consciousness,
but his memory of the event is fragmented: he carries it as a series of vivid, frozen images — a
fist, a ceiling tile, the particular quality of the light at that hour — rather than as a continuous
narrative. Whole stretches of what happened feel absent to him, and the incompleteness itself is
distressing, as though something important is perpetually just out of reach.

In the weeks that followed, Tama noticed that the late-afternoon light — the specific amber quality
of early evening — began to produce a crawling unease he could not initially name. At work, where he
moves through unfamiliar commercial buildings daily, the ordinary sounds of the trade became
treacherous. A door opening anywhere on a job site, or the percussive slam of a fire exit, would
send a bolt of alarm through his body before conscious thought could intervene. Unexpected movement
in his peripheral vision — a colleague rounding a corner, a supervisor approaching from behind —
caused him to spin and brace. If someone touched his shoulder without warning, his reaction was
immediate and visceral, his body responding as though the attack were resuming. These triggers did
not diminish with time; if anything, they accumulated force. When the triggers fired, they pulled
the fragmented memory with them — the frozen images surfacing without context, arriving in his body
as physical sensation before he could identify them as recollection. The incompleteness of the
memory made this worse: he was never sure what he was remembering, only that something terrible had
happened and that his body was certain it was happening again.

Tama has come to believe, with quiet but absolute conviction, that the assault happened because of
who he is — that something in his character, some weakness or carelessness, made him a person this
kind of thing would happen to. He is certain that another man, someone more capable or more alert,
would never have found himself in that corridor. These beliefs do not sit at a distance; they are
woven into how he interprets the present. Because he understands himself as fundamentally vulnerable
and culpable, ordinary environments feel like confirmation — every unfamiliar building he enters on
a job becomes a place where his inadequacy could again be exposed to violence, and so he has begun
declining work in locations he has not assessed in advance, a pattern that has already cost him two
contracts and created friction with his employer. The self-blame also returns most sharply when the
fragmented memories surface, the guilt flooding in alongside the images as though remembering and
condemning himself are the same act. And because the appraisals themselves carry an emotional charge
— the shame, the hopelessness, the sense that he has no real future — they lower his threshold, so
that even neutral stimuli at the wrong time of day can tip him into a state of threat-readiness.

To manage all of this, Tama has developed habits that feel like survival but function as
maintenance. By evening — the time of day that carries the heaviest associative weight — he is
typically on his phone or deep into an online game, the screen filling his attention so that the
quality of the light outside cannot reach him. He has been drinking most nights, enough to blunt the
edge of the hypervigilance before sleep. He avoids any television programme or news item that might
touch on violence. These strategies keep the acute distress at a bearable level in the short term,
but they also prevent him from ever fully processing what happened, and the alcohol and passive
hours leave him feeling worse about himself the following morning — reinforcing the belief that he
is someone who copes badly, which tightens his vigilance and lowers the threshold at which the
triggers fire again. His partner has noticed the withdrawal and the drinking. It was her concern,
finally, that brought him in. What Tama fears most is that the clinician will confirm what he
already suspects: that this is simply who he is now, and that there is no version of the future in
which that changes.
