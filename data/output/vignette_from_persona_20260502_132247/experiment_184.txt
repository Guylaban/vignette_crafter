Virak Sok is a 69-year-old Cambodian-American man who has worked as a plumber for nearly three
decades, a trade he took up after resettling in the United States following years of captivity under
the Khmer Rouge regime. During that period, as a young man in his late teens, Virak was held in a
labor camp where he was subjected to prolonged confinement, interrogation, and physical abuse by
male guards who controlled every movement of the prisoners. He was beaten across the back of the
neck on multiple occasions, forced to work alongside other men under constant surveillance, and
witnessed acts of violence he has never been able to fully describe to anyone. He eventually escaped
and, after years in a refugee camp, arrived in America, where he built a quiet life with his wife
and raised two children. For decades, he functioned — not without difficulty, but without crisis. It
was only in recent years, as he aged and his body slowed and the insulating demands of work began to
recede, that the past began pressing back in.

Virak first noticed something was wrong when the memories began arriving without invitation. He
describes them as a film that plays over and over — the same faces, the same sounds, the dim
interior of the camp, the weight of a hand on his neck. When he tries to explain what happened to
his wife or his doctor, he loses the thread mid-sentence, the words dissolving before he can reach
them, and he trails off with a look of frustration and shame. He knows what happened; he simply
cannot find language for it. These memory intrusions are themselves potent triggers: once the film
begins to play, the nausea rises and the pain at the back of his neck returns — a somatic echo so
specific and so reliable that he has come to associate any unexplained neck stiffness with the onset
of a replay. The memories do not stay abstract. They pull forward the original conditions of danger,
so that what began as recollection becomes, in his body, re-experience.

In his daily work as a plumber, Virak is regularly alone with male clients or colleagues in confined
spaces — crawl spaces, utility rooms, locked basements — and the proximity of men in enclosed
environments is among the most reliable triggers he faces. Before entering any new job site, he now
surveys exits first, mapping a way out before he allows himself to step fully inside. This escape
route planning is not something he chose consciously; it accumulated over months as a habit that
simply made the work feel survivable. Similarly, he avoids the anniversaries of the years he was
held, going quiet in ways his wife has learned not to question. When the memories are triggered by
these situations, he detaches — a practiced dissociation he has refined over years, a way of
watching the film from a distance rather than being inside it. The problem, he has come to
understand, is that detachment does not erase the memories; it suspends them, and they return with
renewed force when his guard drops. His avoidance of anniversaries and his detachment strategies
have, paradoxically, kept the memories fresh and unprocessed, so that ordinary moments — a sudden
movement from a co-worker reaching past him, the nausea that follows a heavy lunch — can reignite
the full somatic and emotional weight of the original experience.

Even inside his own home, Virak cannot fully relax. He feels on guard in rooms he has lived in for
twenty years, scanning for threat in the movements of visitors, tensing when someone crosses the
room quickly. Any sudden movement by another person puts him immediately on edge in a way he cannot
rationalize but cannot override. His maladaptive strategies, intended to manage threat, have instead
maintained a chronic state of hypervigilance: by never allowing the memories to be fully approached
or processed, he has preserved the nervous system's conviction that danger is still present.

What finally brought Virak to seek help was a conversation with his adult son, who told him gently
but directly that he seemed frightened all the time. Virak sat with that observation for several
weeks before making an appointment. What he fears most is that the memories will never become
something he can hold without being undone by them. What he hopes for, more quietly, is simply to
feel safe in his own house.
