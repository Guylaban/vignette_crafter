Arjun is a 68-year-old South Asian man, divorced, who works part-time for a small nonprofit
organization focused on immigrant resettlement services. He was referred for psychological
assessment following a disclosure he made to his primary care physician, and he presented to the
intake appointment with a PCL-5 score of 76, indicating severe posttraumatic symptomatology. What
follows is a clinical portrait drawn from his self-report and the first two sessions.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Arjun was sexually assaulted — raped — by an acquaintance in his
own apartment. He had invited the man in after a community event, someone he had known peripherally
for years through the resettlement network. The assault occurred in his living room, late at night.
He remembers the sound of the door being pushed shut, the particular creak of it, the sound of his
own voice failing him when he tried to speak. He remembers pain — specific, located pain — and a
silence afterward that felt absolute. He did not report the assault. He told no one for nearly a
year.

In the months that followed, the memory did not behave like a memory. It returned without
invitation, and when it did, Arjun heard things — the creak of that door, a low sound the man had
made — as though they were happening in the room with him. Simultaneously, he felt pain in his body
in the same places he had been hurt, a somatic replay that left him convinced, at times, that the
assault was recurring. He has found, consistently, that he cannot adequately describe what happened;
when he tries, the words dissolve before they form, and he sits in sessions with his hands open on
his knees, knowing what he wants to say and unable to say it. These intrusive, sensory, and
physically re-experienced memories have fed directly into his vigilance: after an episode of memory
return, Arjun finds himself scanning his apartment for sounds that might mean someone is entering,
listening at the threshold between sleep and waking for footsteps, for that specific creak. The
memory, in other words, does not stay in the past — it continuously restocks his sense of present
danger.

That sense of danger has become the architecture of his daily life. He startles violently when
touched unexpectedly — a colleague at the nonprofit placed a hand briefly on his shoulder during a
staff meeting, and Arjun stood up from his chair before he was conscious of doing so. He now
positions himself at every meeting with his back against the wall so that he can see the entire
room, and he has arranged his desk at the office accordingly. Loud or unexpected sounds — a door
slamming in the hallway, a car backfiring near the resettlement center — send him into a state of
high alert that can last for hours. This hypervigilance has, in turn, reinforced his belief that
something is deeply wrong with him, that a man his age responding this way to a slamming door is
beyond recovery, beyond dignity.

It is in the space of that belief that the most painful part of his presentation lives. Arjun has
come to understand his reactions as evidence of permanent, irreversible damage. He is certain he
will never feel normal emotions again — not warmth, not ease, not the quiet satisfaction he once
took in his work with newly arrived families. He experiences himself as permanently outside ordinary
human life, an outsider to the community he has spent decades serving. These appraisals — that he is
a lousy coper, that he is fundamentally altered — have driven him to avoid being alone, and yet his
divorce and his social withdrawal mean that he is alone most of the time, which intensifies both the
vigilance and the despair. When the feelings become unbearable, he has, on several occasions, hurt
himself physically — scratching or striking himself — because the physical sensation briefly
interrupts the internal noise. He recognizes, with shame, that this behavior confirms to him
everything he already believes about his inability to cope, and the shame itself sharpens his
hyperarousal and brings the memory closer to the surface again.

What finally brought Arjun to seek help was a moment at work when a young refugee woman asked him a
simple question about her resettlement paperwork and he could not respond — not because he didn't
know the answer, but because he had, in that instant, entirely left the room in his mind. He drove
home and sat in his car for two hours. He is most afraid, he says quietly, that he has become
someone unrecognizable. What he is hoping for, though he struggles to say it, is to find his way
back.
