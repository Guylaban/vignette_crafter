Raymond is a 74-year-old widowed combat pilot who spent decades navigating high-stakes environments
with precision and discipline. His military career demanded that he remain composed under conditions
most people will never encounter, and he carried that same controlled bearing into his civilian
life. What he could not carry intact, however, was the damage done by a relationship that ended only
in the past few years — a partnership marked by escalating physical abuse that Raymond, shaped by a
lifetime of stoicism and self-sufficiency, struggled for a long time to name as what it was. He has
presented for treatment now, reluctantly, because he can no longer manage alone what has quietly
overtaken his daily functioning.

The abuse unfolded over several years and intensified gradually, as such patterns often do. His
partner's violence was frequently preceded by drinking — the smell of alcohol on her breath became,
over time, an involuntary alarm signal his body learned before his mind could catch up. There were
episodes in which she would grab him without warning, her hands on his arms or face, and it was the
sudden, unpredictable nature of touch in those moments that his nervous system registered most
deeply. He had learned to read the room, to watch her movements, to track the particular quality of
her stillness before an outburst. That vigilance, once adaptive, has not left him.

In the period following the relationship's end, Raymond found that the world had become reorganised
around threat. He sleeps poorly, lying awake with a low-grade alertness that he describes as needing
to monitor the room, an old habit that no longer has a clear object but persists nonetheless. He is
easily startled, and when something brushes against his skin unexpectedly — a colleague's hand on
his shoulder during a briefing, a stranger passing too close — his body responds with a surge of
physiological arousal that he finds both alarming and humiliating. This state of sustained threat
perception, in turn, drives much of what Raymond now does to manage his days. Because he is always
braced for danger, he has built his routines around minimising exposure: he avoids any situation
that might require physical proximity, he will not enter spaces where alcohol is being consumed, and
he has constructed careful detours around any location or person associated with his former partner.
The threat itself, in other words, has become the engine of his avoidance.

Yet the avoidance does not bring relief so much as it reinforces the architecture of danger. By
withdrawing from touch, from social drinking occasions, from anything that carries the residue of
that relationship, Raymond never allows his nervous system the evidence that these things are now
safe. The smell of alcohol, the possibility of being touched, the sight of someone who reminds him
of her — each avoided cue remains charged, never defused, so that when an encounter does occur it
lands with full force. A colleague recently offered a handshake after a long debrief, and Raymond's
reaction — the flinch, the rapid heartbeat, the difficulty steadying himself afterward — left him
shaken in a way that felt disproportionate and inexplicable to those around him.

Raymond has also, over these same months, sealed off his anger entirely. He had come to believe that
feeling anger was dangerous — that it made him like her, or made him unpredictable to others, or
would open something he could not close again. And so he suppresses it, rigorously. But the
suppression keeps him tethered to the emotional landscape of the abuse; it prevents him from
processing what happened with any resolution and leaves him perpetually at the edge of a feeling he
refuses to approach. He has withdrawn from the few close friendships that survived his marriage,
finding emotional intimacy intolerable, the vulnerability it requires now feeling indistinguishable
from exposure to harm.

What finally brought Raymond to seek help was a near-incident at work — a moment during a training
exercise in which his hypervigilance caused him to misread a routine communication as threatening.
He is afraid, he says quietly, that he is becoming unreliable. What he hopes for, with some
difficulty articulating it, is to sleep through the night without standing guard.
