Valentina Reyes is a twenty-six-year-old Colombian-American woman who works as a school counselor at
a middle school in the same city where she grew up. She came to the clinic describing persistent
difficulties with sleep, concentration, and a pervasive sense of danger that she could not fully
explain to herself or to others. Her PCL-5 score of 40 places her in the clinically significant
range, and what emerged across the intake and early sessions was a presentation rooted in childhood
exposure to severe domestic violence between her parents — violence she witnessed repeatedly from
approximately age six through age eleven, when her mother finally left.

The most formative incident Valentina returns to, however incompletely, occurred when she was nine.
She had come downstairs during the night to find her father pinning her mother against the kitchen
wall by the throat. She froze at the bottom of the stairs, watching. What she remembers most vividly
— when she can remember it at all — is the sound of her mother's back hitting the cabinet, the
overhead light flickering, and her own absolute stillness. She had not screamed, had not run for
help, had not done anything. In the years since, she had come to believe, with quiet but corrosive
certainty, that her inaction that night had made things worse — that had she acted differently,
something might have changed. This self-blame sits at the center of her negative appraisals and
feeds directly into her current sense of threat: if she failed to protect her mother then, she
reasons, she cannot trust her own judgment about safety now. The belief that people cannot be
trusted — including the teachers, neighbors, and extended family members who saw and said nothing —
and a deep conviction that institutions charged with protection had failed her family, compound
this. These appraisals keep her nervous system in a state of chronic preparedness, as though the
danger were never truly over.

At home in the evenings, Valentina positions herself with her back against the wall whenever she
sits, whether at her kitchen table or on her couch. She reports feeling genuinely unsafe if she
cannot see the full room. She scans faces habitually — at the grocery store, in the school hallway,
in the staff lounge — reading them for signs of volatility, the way she once read her father's face
each morning before school. This hypervigilance is not something she chose; it evolved from the
conviction that people cannot be trusted and that threat is most dangerous when it goes undetected.
The sustained state of alertness, in turn, makes the intrusive memories more likely to break
through: when her body is already primed, the threshold for triggering a full re-experiencing drops
sharply. A raised voice in the school corridor, the particular quality of fluorescent light, an
unexpected hand on her shoulder from a colleague — any of these can instantly return her to the
kitchen at nine years old, without transition or warning. When the memory surfaces that way, the
emotional overwhelm is so complete that she loses the ability to narrate it coherently; in session
she trails off mid-sentence, her eyes going distant, unable to finish.

The intrusions themselves reinforce her sense that the world remains dangerous, and that she was —
and perhaps still is — at fault for not having stopped it. Each unwilled return of the memory feels
like fresh evidence that she has not recovered, which she interprets as confirmation of her own
inadequacy. This loop has shaped her behavior in concrete ways. She avoids the neighborhood where
she grew up entirely, rerouting her commute by fifteen minutes every day. At work, she is effective
with students in crisis — she is drawn to that role — but she has quietly declined every after-
school event held in the school gymnasium, whose layout and lighting remind her of the ground floor
of her childhood home. A supervisor has noticed her absence from those events and raised it twice;
Valentina offered logistical explanations, aware that the real reason would be difficult to explain
even if she could hold the thread of it long enough to try. Her avoidance preserves her in the short
term but sustains the sense of threat, and the sustained threat keeps the memory close.

What finally brought her to seek help was a session with a student disclosing family conflict at
home. She recognized, with some alarm, that she had dissociated briefly during the conversation —
that the student had been speaking and she had not been fully present. She is afraid, most of all,
of failing the children she works with the way she believes she failed her mother. She is hoping,
cautiously, to understand why she cannot simply move on.
