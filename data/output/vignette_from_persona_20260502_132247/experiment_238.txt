Gregor Halvorsen is a 49-year-old married man of Norwegian-American descent who serves as a city
council member in a mid-sized Midwestern municipality. He presented for psychological evaluation
approximately eighteen months after returning from a National Guard deployment to a combat zone,
reporting a PCL-5 score of 42 and describing a constellation of symptoms that had quietly dismantled
the life he had worked decades to build.

During his deployment, Gregor's unit was ambushed during a routine supply convoy. The vehicle ahead
of his was struck by an IED; the explosion was close enough that the concussive force threw him
sideways in his seat. In the chaos that followed — smoke, screaming, the hands of a fellow soldier
grabbing his shoulder to pull him from the wreckage — Gregor helped extract two injured men before
the unit was able to secure the area. One of the men died before evacuation arrived. Gregor has
never been able to reconstruct the full sequence of what happened between the initial blast and
finding himself outside the vehicle. He reports that the memory feels like a film with entire scenes
cut out, and when he tries to explain the event — to his wife, to his VA intake worker — he loses
the thread entirely, trails off, and sometimes finds himself staring at the wall, unable to say
where the words went. When recollection does come, it arrives not as narrative but as sensation: the
terror he felt in those minutes returns with the same physiological force as the original event, his
heart hammering, his breath shallow, as though the ambush is simply happening again.

It is this quality of his memory — fragmentary, unprocessed, and somatically charged — that has
become the engine of his current distress. Because the account remains incomplete and cannot be told
coherently, Gregor has come to believe there must be something catastrophically wrong with him, that
a mind intact would be able to remember, to explain, to put it behind him. He monitors his own
thoughts with exhausting vigilance, scanning for signs that he is, as he privately fears, losing his
grip on sanity. The fragmented memory also keeps his nervous system in a state of unresolved alarm:
without a clear beginning, middle, and end to the event, his body has never received the signal that
it is over. Sleep is nearly impossible. He lies awake for hours, and on the nights he does drift off
he wakes abruptly, certain something is wrong.

That certainty has hardened into a worldview. Gregor now operates from the conviction that the world
is genuinely dangerous, that safety is a story other people tell themselves, and that his body —
reacting as it does to every unexpected sound, every brush of contact — is simply telling the truth
that others are too naive to hear. When a constituent touched his arm during a town hall meeting
last spring to thank him for his service, Gregor lurched backward so visibly that it made the local
paper's social column. Even when his wife holds his hand and tells him he is home, he cannot absorb
it; the reassurance slides off something that has calcified inside him. These appraisals — that
danger is everywhere, that his body is a reliable threat-detector rather than a dysregulated one —
sustain his hypervigilance and keep his startle response on a hair trigger, which in turn pulls
fragments of the ambush back to the surface: the hands on his shoulder, the smoke, the half-
remembered faces. Each intrusion reinforces his certainty that he has not escaped anything, that the
threat is ongoing, and the appraisals tighten further.

To manage the noise inside his head, Gregor has developed several strategies that have quietly made
things worse. He fills every quiet moment with loud music or television, reasoning that if there is
no silence there is no space for the memories to surface. He avoids all physical contact, having
reorganized his public interactions and his marriage around a perimeter of untouchability. But the
distraction and withdrawal prevent him from ever sitting with the memory long enough to process it,
keeping it fragmented and therefore perpetually threatening. The avoidance, too, has fed his
appraisal of himself as broken and of the world as a place requiring constant management.

Gregor came to seek help after his wife told him quietly that she felt she was living with a
stranger. What he most fears is that she is right. What he most hopes is that someone can help him
find the missing pieces — and that finding them will not destroy what remains.
