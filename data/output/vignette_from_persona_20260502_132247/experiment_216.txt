Valentina Reyes is a 39-year-old Latina detective who grew up in a household where violence was a
nightly possibility. Her father was a large, physically imposing man whose rages arrived without
warning — one moment the kitchen smelled of sweat and beer, and the next her mother was against the
wall, her father's voice filling every room in the apartment. Valentina was seven the first time she
watched this happen in full, crouched behind the sofa, and she would go on watching for the next
decade: the particular smell of her father's body after he came in from the heat, the way a raised
hand or sudden movement across the room could mean everything was about to change, the shouting that
seemed to compress the air itself. These were the textures of her childhood, and they lodged
somewhere deeper than conscious memory.

She left home at eighteen, built a career in law enforcement through what she described as sheer
stubbornness, and for years managed to keep the past at a functional distance. But in the months
following a domestic violence callout two years ago — a scene that replicated her childhood
apartment with an accuracy that felt almost deliberate — the distance collapsed. The memories
returned not as narrative but as images: her mother's face at a particular angle, a hand raised in
silhouette, fragments that arrived without sequence or context and left her flooded with a grief she
could not locate or name. When she tried to deliberately recall what had happened in her childhood,
the memories became slippery and inaccessible, as though her mind refused to grant her the coherence
she sought. This quality of the memory — vivid in pieces, unreachable as a whole — left her feeling
that something was profoundly wrong with her.

She had come to believe, with quiet certainty, that the event had happened because of the kind of
person she was — that there was something in her nature that invited harm, that made her unworthy of
safety. This belief did not stay abstract. It fed directly into her sense that she could not trust
herself to act correctly, and that her current reactions — the hypervigilance, the intensity of her
distress — were evidence that she was losing her grip on reality. These appraisals sharpened the
grief that came with every memory fragment, making recall feel not just painful but confirmatory:
each image seemed to prove the worst things she thought about herself. And paradoxically, the
appraisals kept her sensitised to the very triggers she feared — she moved through the world already
certain that danger was her inheritance, which meant that sweat, sudden movement, a raised voice
felt not incidental but inevitable.

At work, this had become impossible to manage quietly. As a detective, Valentina regularly
encounters scenes of domestic disturbance, and the smell of a suspect's body odour or the sound of
shouting in a corridor would send her system into full alert — heart rate spiking, every muscle
braced, the threat response indistinguishable from what she had felt as a child. These moments of
activation led her to hurt herself physically, pressing her nails hard into her palm or later, at
home, more deliberately, because the sharp focus of physical pain was the only thing that
interrupted the cascade. She had also begun refusing all physical contact — colleagues who reached
for her shoulder in the break room were met with a step back and a tight smile — and she had
organised her life around emotional flatness, avoiding anything that might produce feeling in either
direction. She chose seats in every room — squad briefings, restaurants with her partner — based on
sightlines and exits, and she woke most nights to check her apartment, certain something had
shifted. Yet these strategies, rather than steadying her, had begun to erode the ground beneath her.
The self-harm and the emotional numbing kept her from processing what she carried, leaving the
memory fragments as raw as ever, and her partner had begun to name the distance between them. Her
avoidance confirmed, in her own mind, that she was not coping — which deepened her conviction that
she was going crazy, which in turn made the vigilance feel more necessary, the triggers more
unbearable.

What finally brought Valentina to seek help was a moment in the precinct parking lot: a male
colleague jogged past her unexpectedly, and she spun and had her hand on her weapon before she knew
what she was doing. She stood there afterward, shaking, and thought: I cannot keep doing this job
this way. What she most fears is that the therapy will confirm what she already suspects about
herself. What she most hopes, though she has not yet said it aloud, is that it will not.
