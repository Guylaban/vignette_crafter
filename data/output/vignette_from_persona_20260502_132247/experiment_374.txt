Aroha Tane is a 35-year-old Māori woman, married and working as an accountant at a mid-sized firm in
Auckland's financial district. She presented for treatment approximately eight months after being
mugged on her way home from a late evening at the office. The assault occurred in a poorly lit side
street she cut through regularly; a man came up quickly behind her, and it was the sound of his
footsteps — rapid, closing — that she registered a half-second before he grabbed her shoulder from
behind, spun her around, pressed something cold against her ribs, and demanded her bag. She
remembers the metallic taste of fear flooding her mouth as she stood frozen, her body bent slightly
backward the way he had wrenched her, her handbag stripped from her arm before he disappeared into
the dark. The whole event lasted under a minute, but it remade her world entirely.

In the weeks that followed, Aroha found that the memory did not behave like other memories. When it
returned — unbidden, at her desk, in the kitchen, during meetings — she did not merely recall the
mugging; she tasted that cold metallic wash on her tongue again, and felt her torso twist backward
into the same wrenched position she had been held in. The memory was so extreme, so total in its
physical return, that she sometimes found herself unable to believe it had actually happened to her
— it felt more like something she had witnessed than something she had lived. Yet the doubt offered
no relief, because the body kept insisting otherwise. These intrusive, viscerally embodied memories
reliably sharpened her sense of ongoing danger, leaving her in a state of physiological readiness
that she could not talk herself out of. Even when her husband held her hand and told her she was
safe, she found it impossible to believe him — the threat felt present and structural, not
situational, and no amount of reassurance could penetrate it.

What frightened Aroha most, in the quieter moments of reflection, was what she had come to believe
the mugging said about her. She was certain, at a level that felt like knowledge rather than
opinion, that the event had happened because of the sort of person she was and because of the way
she had acted — cutting through that street again, working late again, being careless again. This
conviction that she was, in some essential way, the author of her own harm meant that the world's
danger felt personally directed. She had come to believe the world was a dangerous place, and that
she moved through it as someone who attracted its worst. These appraisals did not stay abstract;
they fed directly back into her alertness, so that every environment she entered was scanned for
threat. When a delivery arrived late at the office, she did not think "traffic" — she assumed
something bad was unfolding. She could not relax even in her own home.

Aroha works in an open-plan office, and it is there that the tightest loops of her distress play
out. Colleagues pass behind her workstation constantly, and the sound of footsteps approaching from
behind — the very auditory signature of the assault — sends her rigid. In those moments, the threat
floods back as certainty rather than fear, and she immediately begins dwelling on what she had done
wrong that night, cycling through every decision that might have prevented it. If a colleague
touched her shoulder unexpectedly to get her attention, she would dissociate deliberately, trying to
blank out and leave her body, the only tool she had found that interrupted the sensation. But the
rumination and dissociation that she used to manage these moments kept the memory raw and close, and
when she emerged from a dissociative episode she often felt more convinced than before that she was
fundamentally unsafe and fundamentally at fault. Her maladaptive attempts to cope quietly reinforced
the very appraisals they were meant to quiet, and those appraisals in turn made her hypervigilant to
the triggers that started the cycle again.

Over months, she began arriving at work before her colleagues and leaving last, rearranging her
schedule to avoid the busy corridor. Relationships with her team became strained — she seemed
distant, startled easily, and declined after-work gatherings. What finally brought her to seek help
was her husband's quiet observation, one evening, that she no longer seemed to be living in their
home so much as guarding it. She came to the first session hoping, above all, to stop feeling like a
person to whom danger was inevitable — and afraid that she no longer knew how to believe she was
safe.
