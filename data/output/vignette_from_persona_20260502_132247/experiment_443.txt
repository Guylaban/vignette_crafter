Gerald is a 74-year-old man of German descent who lives with his long-term partner in a mid-sized
town in the Pacific Northwest, where he works part-time as a private tutor, helping high school
students with mathematics and history. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 48, seeking help
approximately fourteen months after surviving a severe wildfire that swept through the rural edge of
his community during a dry August.

Gerald had been at home when the fire crested the ridge faster than any forecast had predicted. The
air turned brown, then orange, and the smell — a dense, resinous burning that seemed to coat the
inside of his lungs — arrived before the flames were visible. He spent nearly two hours sheltering
in his car in a parking lot, watching the horizon pulse with light, uncertain whether emergency
services knew where he was, uncertain whether his partner, who had been out running errands, was
safe. It was the waiting, the helplessness inside that car, the acrid smell seeping through the
vents, that seemed to lodge itself most deeply in him. When his partner finally appeared through the
smoke-hazed lot, Gerald wept with a relief he could not afterward explain without returning,
involuntarily, to those two hours.

In the months that followed, the memory began to behave in ways Gerald found deeply unsettling. It
did not wait for him to choose to revisit it. During tutoring sessions — quiet afternoons at his
kitchen table with a teenager working through algebra — the memory would force itself forward
without warning, arriving not as a recollection he had sought but as an interruption he could not
refuse. More disturbing still, the smell would sometimes accompany it: that same thick, resinous
burning, arriving in a room where there was nothing burning at all. These intrusions left him shaken
and searching for ways to manage what he could not stop. He had begun telling himself, with
increasing deliberateness, that the fire had really happened to someone else — a kind of mental
stepping-back, a pretence of distance that allowed him to function through a session without visibly
falling apart. He also found himself spending long stretches of time turning the event over in his
mind, rehearsing the sequence of decisions made that morning, trying to identify the precise moment
at which things might have gone differently — as if the right answer, found late enough, might
retroactively make him safer.

These strategies, however, did not quiet the memory. If anything, the rumination kept the event
close, and the dissociative pretence seemed only to preserve its strangeness — when the memory did
break through, it sometimes arrived in that flattened, affectless register, as though it had
happened to a stranger, which frightened Gerald in its own way. The memory's intrusive quality fed
directly into how he now read his environment: he had become convinced that danger was always
proximate and that his own perception was the only reliable early-warning system. During tutoring
sessions, he now insisted on sitting with his back to the wall, positioned so he could see both the
door and the window simultaneously. When a student's parent was late to collect a child, Gerald's
chest would tighten with the certainty that something had gone wrong — an accident, another
emergency, something unfolding outside that he could not yet see. He scanned constantly: the quality
of light through the window, the smell of the air, the distant sound of sirens.

This vigilance in turn reinforced the strategies that had initially seemed protective. Because he
was always monitoring, always braced, the effort of getting through a tutoring afternoon had become
exhausting, and he had begun declining any engagement he judged potentially stressful — social
invitations, driving routes that passed near forested hillsides, any situation in which he could not
control his position in a room. His partner had noticed the narrowing. Gerald had noticed it too.

What finally brought him to seek help was a session in which a student asked, offhandedly, whether
Gerald could smell smoke. There was no smoke. Gerald had excused himself, gone to the bathroom, and
stood gripping the sink for several minutes. He came to the first appointment hoping, above
everything else, to stop being afraid of his own mind.
