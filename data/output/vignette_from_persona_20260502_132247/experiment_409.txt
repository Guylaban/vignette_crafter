not parse, could not hold onto, could not answer. What he does remember with terrible precision is
the taste in his mouth at that moment: the metallic bitterness of the coffee he had been drinking
when his daughter-in-law called, a taste that had followed him into the hospital and lodged itself
in the back of his throat as the scene unfolded. Kwame survived, but with significant cognitive
impairment, and Kofi has never fully left that room.

In the months that followed, Kofi found that the memory of what happened resisted the very tools his
profession had given him. He is a man who has spent four decades finding the precise word for
difficult things — conflict, grief, injustice — and yet when he attempts to describe what he
witnessed, the thread dissolves. He knows what happened. The knowledge sits in him like a stone. But
when he opens his mouth to give it shape, the language fails to arrive, and he trails into silence,
sometimes mid-sentence, in a way that disturbs him deeply. He has tried to write it down for
himself, privately, and the same thing happens on the page: a beginning, and then nothing. He has
come to believe that this failure is not incidental, that it means something irreparable occurred in
him that night, something that cannot be communicated and therefore cannot be shared or relieved.

conversations about loss — interviews with families who have experienced bereavement, features on
grief and ageing, panel discussions about mortality and legacy. When a source begins to talk about
losing a loved one, or when a colleague in an editorial meeting speaks about someone's death, Kofi
feels the room shift. The metallic taste returns first, rising at the back of his palate before the
images follow — the monitors, the voices, the particular angle of Kwame's face turned away from him.
What he finds most destabilising, however, is the sound of incomprehensible chatter: overlapping
voices, a crowded newsroom, a busy restaurant where multiple conversations merge into unintelligible
noise. That texture of sound — language that cannot be decoded — pulls him back with a force that
surprises him each time, flooding him with the sensory residue of that hospital room before he has
had time to prepare. The taste comes with it, vivid and unwanted, and for a few seconds he is no
longer at his desk or across from an interview subject but standing at the foot of Kwame's bed,
unable to understand what is being said and unable to do anything at all.

Conversations about the future carry their own weight. When his wife speaks hopefully about
retirement plans, or when friends discuss what the grandchildren will be like, Kofi goes quiet in a
way his wife has begun to notice and name. He cannot orient himself toward a future that once felt
continuous and now feels conditional in ways he cannot articulate.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a profile assignment — a piece about a family navigating

honestly. He is most afraid, he says, that the words will never come back. What he hopes, more
quietly, is that someone might be able to help him finish the sentence he has been trying to
complete for eighteen months.
