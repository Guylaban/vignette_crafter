Callum Delaney is a 35-year-old single man of Irish descent who works as a special education teacher
at a public middle school in a mid-sized Midwestern city. He presented for an initial intake
following a street robbery that occurred approximately eight months ago, and his PCL-5 score of 58
places him well within the severe range of post-traumatic stress. He described the incident with a
precision that was itself striking: walking home alone at dusk after a late parent-teacher
conference, he was approached from behind by two men who shoved him hard against a chain-link fence,
one pressing a forearm across the back of his neck while the other demanded his wallet and phone. He
remembered the specific pressure on his cervical spine, the metallic smell of the fence, the sound
of one man shouting inches from his ear. He did not fight back. A patrol car passed slowly at the
far end of the block and kept moving. Within minutes it was over, but for Callum, it has not been
over since.

In the weeks following the robbery, Callum found that certain experiences reached back into that
evening and pulled it forward with a vividness he described as photographic — not like remembering,
but like being shown. The sound of shouting, whether a colleague raising his voice across the school
hallway or a student having a meltdown in class, could snap him instantly back to that alley, the
scene assembling itself image by image with the same cold clarity. The dull ache that had settled
into his neck since the assault functioned in the same way: on difficult days in the classroom, when
tension accumulated in his shoulders and crept upward, the somatic reminder was enough to flood him
with the full sensory record of the attack. Even the sight of a police officer — a presence that
might reasonably signal safety — had become a trigger rather than a comfort, because the patrol car
that passed without stopping had lodged itself as proof that no one was coming. Each of these cues
reliably returned him to the memory, and the memory, when it arrived, brought with it not only fear
but a fierce, disorienting rage, followed almost immediately by guilt at feeling that rage at all.

That guilt had become one of the most corrosive features of his presentation. Callum had come to
believe, with a certainty he could not argue himself out of, that someone else — someone more alert,
more capable, more fundamentally adequate — would simply not have been in this situation. He had
stayed late at school. He had walked that route. He had frozen. These appraisals of personal
inadequacy were not abstract; they pressed directly on his sense of ongoing threat, convincing him
that he remained vulnerable precisely because of some deficiency in himself, and that the world
required constant vigilance as a result. He found himself unable to relax even in demonstrably safe
settings — in his own apartment, in the school staffroom — because the belief that he was inadequate
to protect himself made safety feel like an illusion. He had begun scanning for exits the moment he
entered any room, a habit so automatic now that he clocked the fire doors in his own classroom
before his students arrived each morning.

The hypervigilance fed back relentlessly into the memories it was meant to prevent. The more he
monitored his environment for threat, the more his nervous system remained primed, and the more
readily the photographic recall returned — the fence, the neck, the shouting, the passing car. Each
intrusion confirmed the appraisals that had generated the vigilance in the first place: he was
inadequate, he was not safe, he had to stay alert. The loop had tightened over months. He had
quietly stopped attending the after-school staff gatherings where voices sometimes rose in laughter
or argument, and a colleague he had considered a close friend had stopped reaching out after several
unanswered invitations.

What finally brought Callum in was a moment in his classroom when a student knocked over a chair
with a loud bang and Callum, in front of twenty-two children, dropped into a crouch behind his desk.
He described the shame of it with a flat, exhausted voice. He was not sure what he hoped for, only
that he could not keep living inside a body that treated every school day as a continuation of that
night.
