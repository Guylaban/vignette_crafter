Layla is a nineteen-year-old woman of Lebanese descent who works the early shift at a regional
distribution warehouse outside her hometown. She lives with her boyfriend and came to her first
intake session speaking quietly, choosing her words carefully, as though she had rehearsed how
little she was willing to reveal. Her PCL-5 score of 33 places her at the clinical threshold for
PTSD, and what emerged across the first two sessions was a picture of a young woman caught in a loop
she cannot name but cannot escape.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Layla's younger brother, then four years old, was admitted to the
pediatric intensive care unit following a rapid and severe asthma attack that progressed to
respiratory failure. Layla had been the only adult present when he stopped breathing. She had called
911, she had followed the dispatcher's instructions, she had done everything she was told to do —
and yet what she remembers most clearly is the colour of his lips and the sound the ambulance made
when it finally turned onto their street, which she had begun to believe came too late because of
something she had failed to notice earlier in the day. Her brother survived, but Layla did not come
away from the experience intact. In the weeks that followed, she found herself replaying the
afternoon with a precision that felt almost prosecutorial: every moment she had not checked on him,
every hour she had spent on her phone, every sign she now believed she should have read. She had
come to believe, with a certainty that resisted all counter-evidence, that she had failed to protect
him — that a more capable, more attentive person would have prevented the crisis entirely. Alongside
this was a quieter but equally corrosive conviction: that she was fundamentally weak, that her panic
in those minutes had confirmed something she had always suspected about herself.

These appraisals did not stay in the past. Because Layla understood herself as someone who failed to
protect and who could not prevent bad things from happening, the present felt perpetually dangerous.
She moved through her days in a state of low-grade vigilance, scanning for threats she could not yet
see. At the warehouse, where she spends hours moving through loud, physically demanding work, the
sudden hiss of a pneumatic pallet jack or the beeping of a reversing forklift would sometimes hit
her body before her mind had time to process it — her chest tightening, her hands going cold, her
heart rate spiking in a way that left her shaken and embarrassed in front of her coworkers. The
physical reactivity was not simply unpleasant; it fed directly back into her appraisal of herself.
Each time her body responded that way, she interpreted it as further evidence of her weakness, proof
that she was not managing the way a normal person would. The shame that followed the physical
reaction deepened her belief that she had been broken by something she should have been strong
enough to handle — and that deepened belief, in turn, kept her nervous system primed, so that the
next trigger arrived into a body already braced for catastrophe.

Concentration had become difficult in ways that affected her at work, where she had made small but
accumulating errors that her supervisor had begun to notice. She said little about this directly,
but it was clear the occupational consequences were adding to her sense of herself as someone who
could not be relied upon. Strong feelings — fear most often, but also a guilt that arrived without
warning and a shame she described as physical, like heat in her face — surfaced throughout the day
without obvious provocation, and she had no reliable way to interrupt them.

What finally brought Layla in was not a single crisis but a conversation with her boyfriend, who had
gently told her that she seemed to be disappearing. She had not argued with him. What she was most
afraid of, she said near the end of the first session, was that he was right — and that there was
nothing left to find.
