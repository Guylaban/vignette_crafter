Arjun is a 36-year-old South Asian man who works as a detective and presents with a PCL-5 score of
74, seeking help for what he describes, haltingly, as "falling apart from the inside." He is
married, though his wife has recently begun sleeping in the guest room, and it is this quiet
domestic rupture — more than any single symptom — that finally compelled him to make an appointment.

Arjun grew up in a household where his father's moods were the weather everyone else had to
forecast. The abuse was not random; it had its own cold logic. His father would return home late,
the front door slamming open against the hallway wall, and what followed was a reliable sequence:
raised voices, the particular chill of a house where the heating had been left off all evening, and
then contact — hands, a belt, the edge of a table. The cold was not incidental. His father would
sometimes force Arjun to stand outside in winter as a prelude, so that by the time he was brought
back in, the drop in skin temperature had become a kind of countdown. The slam of a door and the
sensation of cold air on his skin were not just contextual details of his childhood; they were the
architecture of anticipation.

Arjun did not seek help as a child, and for years he functioned — well, even. He was a good
detective, methodical and perceptive, someone who read rooms for a living. But in his mid-thirties,
without a single precipitating incident he can name, the past began to surface. Now, when a door
slams in the precinct bullpen — a colleague coming in from the cold, the wind catching a fire exit —
Arjun finds himself rigid at his desk, pulse spiking, the room narrowing. When the temperature drops
on an autumn evening, even the ordinary sensation of cold air on his forearms during a scene walk-
through can send him back somewhere he cannot fully name but cannot fully leave. These moments of
involuntary return do not feel to him like symptoms; they feel like evidence. Evidence that
something is fundamentally wrong with him. He has come to believe, with a detective's grim
certainty, that a man his age should not be undone by a draft or a door. His reactions, he is
convinced, reveal him as a lousy coper — worse, as someone defective at the level of character. He
no longer recognises himself in the mirror of his own responses, and this loss of self-recognition
frightens him as much as anything else.

It is this belief in his own defectiveness that drives the strategies he has assembled, month by
month, into a kind of fortress. He avoids going out after dark when he can help it, particularly to
social gatherings, where doors open and close freely and the temperature is unpredictable. He avoids
colleagues beyond what the job requires, and he has stopped accepting the after-shift drinks that
once made him feel like he belonged somewhere. He stays up until two or three in the morning —
watching procedurals with the volume low, anything to forestall the moment of lying alone in an
unfamiliar quiet — because sleeping alone, even in his own home, has become intolerable. His wife's
move to the guest room means he now faces that solitude nightly, and the dread of it begins building
around nine in the evening. But the strategies that were meant to protect him have begun to corrode
him further. The isolation confirms, in his mind, that he is someone people cannot be close to. The
late nights leave him depleted and raw, so that the next day's triggers land harder — a slammed
locker in the precinct hallway, the bite of November air as he steps out to a crime scene, and he is
back in that hallway, that cold, that countdown. Each triggered response renews his conviction that
he is broken; that conviction tightens the avoidance; the avoidance narrows his world until the
triggers are harder to escape and easier to find.

What Arjun most hopes for, he said at the end of the intake, is to recognise himself again. What he
most fears is that there is nothing left to recognise.
