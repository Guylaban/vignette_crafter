Priya Chandrasekaran was thirty-two years old and had spent the better part of her adult life in
public service, first as a community organizer in a mid-sized Midwestern city and then, after a
hard-fought election campaign, as one of its youngest city council members. Her work required her to
be present — at town halls, ribbon cuttings, late-night budget sessions, public hearings in
buildings she had never visited before. She had always understood that visibility was the price of
the job, and she had paid it willingly. That changed on the evening of a municipal infrastructure
summit fourteen months ago, when a gas explosion tore through the civic center where she and roughly
three hundred other attendees had gathered. The blast originated in a utility corridor adjacent to
the main hall. The lights failed immediately. In the darkness and smoke, the crowd surged toward
exits that were partially blocked by debris, and Priya was pressed against a wall for what felt like
several minutes, unable to move, unable to see, listening to the sounds of people screaming and the
groan of the building settling around her. She escaped with lacerations and a fractured wrist.
Eleven people did not escape at all.

In the weeks that followed, Priya told herself she was managing. She returned to council sessions
within a month, though she found herself arriving early to every meeting so she could walk the room
before it filled — locating the exits, noting which doors opened outward, positioning herself on the
aisle closest to the rear of the chamber. What had begun as a precaution hardened into compulsion.
By the time she sought help, she could not enter any space — a restaurant, a community center, a
colleague's office — without first conducting a systematic survey of its exits and identifying the
fastest path out. She avoided unfamiliar venues entirely when she could, and when her schedule
required her to attend events in buildings she did not know, the anticipatory dread could begin days
in advance. Her chief of staff had quietly begun routing her away from off-site meetings, telling
other officials that the councilwoman had scheduling conflicts. She had not told him why.

The explosion had left Priya with a settled and unshakeable conviction that the world was a
dangerous place and that safety, wherever it appeared, was provisional. She had come to believe that
vigilance was not a symptom but a rational response to reality — that the only people who felt safe
were people who had not yet been hurt. This belief was the engine driving nearly everything that had
organized itself around her fear. Because she was certain she had to be on guard at all times, she
scanned every room she entered for people whose behavior seemed off, tracked movement at the edges
of crowds, and sat with her back to walls when she could manage it. She avoided any situation that
might tax her physically — not because she feared injury in isolation, but because she understood,
with a logic that felt airtight, that a body already under stress could not respond fast enough if
something happened.

The behaviors she had built to manage her sense of threat were, in practice, sustaining it. Each
time Priya checked the exits, mapped the room, or positioned herself near a door, she left the
situation having confirmed only that danger was real enough to prepare for. The scanning never
produced reassurance; it produced more scanning. Nights were worse than days — something about
darkness returned her, without warning, to the civic center corridor — and she had largely stopped
accepting evening engagements, which in a city council role was professionally untenable. Her sleep
was poor, her concentration fractured, and a colleague had recently asked, with visible concern,
whether she was all right.

It was that question, more than anything, that brought her to the intake appointment. She was not
afraid of being seen as weak, she said carefully — she was afraid that if she let her guard down
long enough to accept help, something would happen that she had not prepared for. What she hoped
for, she admitted after a pause, was to stop living as though the explosion were still occurring.
She was not sure she believed that was possible.
