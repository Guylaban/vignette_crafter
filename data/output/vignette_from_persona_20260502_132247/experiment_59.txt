Aroha Tane is a 21-year-old New Zealand–born woman of Māori descent, currently working as a
registered nurse in a busy urban emergency department in the United States, where she relocated
after completing her training. She is in a relationship and presents with a PCL-5 score of 67,
seeking help for symptoms she has been carrying, largely in silence, since her deployment as a
military medical technician approximately fourteen months ago.

During her deployment, Aroha was stationed at a forward operating base when her unit came under
sustained mortar fire. She was mid-procedure on a wounded soldier when the first explosion hit. The
concussive sound tore through the medical tent, and in the seconds that followed she felt her own
heart hammering so violently against her ribs that she was momentarily uncertain whether she had
been hit. She continued working — chest tight, hands moving — while sirens and alert tones screamed
across the base and further blasts shook the ground. The soldier she was treating did not survive.
Aroha has described that afternoon as something that happened to her body before her mind could
follow.

In the months since returning, certain sensory experiences have become unbearable thresholds. When
the trauma-bay alarms go off at work, or when she hears sirens from an ambulance bay she passes a
dozen times each shift, the sound does not simply startle her — it pulls her back. Within seconds
she is no longer standing in a corridor in an American hospital; she is in the tent, the soldier is
bleeding, and she is certain something catastrophic is about to happen. Loud noises of any kind — a
dropped tray, a door slamming — produce the same telescoping effect. Her own heart racing, which she
notices constantly now, has become one of the most potent of all her triggers: the moment she feels
that pounding in her chest, her body interprets it as confirmation that danger is present and real,
and the threat alarm in her mind escalates accordingly. These triggers do not merely distress her;
they reliably tip her into a state of full physiological and perceptual threat, in which she is
irritable, prone to sharp outbursts with colleagues, and scanning every corner of the department for
signs of what might be coming next. Her supervisor has noted the change in her, and two junior
nurses have asked to be rostered away from her section — a consequence Aroha is aware of and ashamed
by.

What she is most afraid of, however, is the memory itself. When it surfaces — and it surfaces
without invitation — she does not experience grief or fear in any manageable form. She experiences
rage: a white, disorienting fury that she cannot explain and that frightens her more than the
sadness she expected. At other moments the memory arrives as pure overwhelm, an emotional wave so
total that she has had to leave patient cubicles mid-assessment, pressing herself against a supply-
room wall until it passes. She knows exactly what happened. She has told no one, partly because she
cannot find the language — not because she has forgotten, but because the words available to her
feel grotesquely inadequate, and attempting to speak it aloud produces only a kind of locked
silence. This wordlessness keeps the memory sealed and unprocessed, and the sealed memory keeps her
nervous system primed, so that even ordinary clinical sounds become capable of detonating it again.

The strategies Aroha has built around this have accumulated quietly over months. She stopped
exercising — running had once been restorative, but elevating her heart rate now mimics the somatic
signature of the base under fire, and she will not risk it. She avoids anything that might make her
feel genuinely happy or hopeful, having come to believe, without quite articulating it, that
allowing herself to feel good would be a form of dangerous inattention. She scans her environment
constantly, turning her head toward every sound, reading every face for threat — a habit that
exhausts her and keeps her body in a state of sustained arousal that makes the memory easier to
trigger and harder to escape. The avoidance that was meant to protect her has instead hollowed out
her daily life and kept the wound from closing.

What finally brought her in was her partner telling her, quietly and carefully, that they did not
know how to reach her anymore. Aroha wants, more than anything, to be able to do her job without
fearing herself.
