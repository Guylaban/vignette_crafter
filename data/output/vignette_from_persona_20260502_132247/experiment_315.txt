Douglas is a 65-year-old married prosecutor of North American heritage who has spent four decades
inside courtrooms, holding the state's power accountable to its citizens. He came to his first
session with the careful, measured cadence of a man accustomed to building arguments, yet something
in the way he paused before certain words — *police*, *crowd*, *shouting* — suggested that language
itself had become treacherous territory.

The incident occurred fourteen months ago at a public demonstration Douglas attended as a private
citizen, not as an officer of the court. What began as an orderly gathering deteriorated rapidly
when a line of officers in riot gear advanced without warning. Douglas was caught in the surge. He
was thrown against a barrier, and for what he estimates was four or five minutes — though he cannot
be certain, because time fractured — he was surrounded by officers shouting commands he could not
parse. The noise was overwhelming: overlapping voices, the crack of batons on shields, the static
burst of radios. He could not distinguish orders from threats. He could not tell whether compliance
or flight would keep him safe. By the time the crowd dispersed, he had sustained minor physical
injuries, but the experience of being entirely unable to read the intentions of uniformed men with
weapons — men whose authority he had spent his career respecting — had left something deeply
disorganized in him.

In the weeks that followed, Douglas noticed that specific things pulled him back. The sight of a
police officer — whether a colleague's escort in the courthouse corridor or a cruiser idling at an
intersection — triggered an immediate surge of alarm, his body mobilizing as though the riot line
were re-forming around him. Crowds producing incomprehensible overlapping voices had the same
effect; he could be in the courthouse cafeteria and the moment conversation blurred into
undifferentiated noise, the present moment thinned and the memory pressed through. Witnessing people
behave in ways that struck him as disorderly or morally wrong — a heated argument in a parking lot,
a witness losing composure on the stand — activated the same cascade, as though disorder of any kind
had become a signal that danger was imminent.

When these triggers fired, Douglas's entire threat-monitoring system engaged. He had come to
believe, in a way that felt irrefutable, that the world now required constant surveillance. He began
watching people closely — colleagues, strangers in the gallery, the officers who testified in his
cases — searching their posture and expressions for early signs of hostility. Sleep became nearly
impossible; he would lie awake constructing threat assessments of the following day. At work, where
he once moved through the building with the authority of long familiarity, he now found himself
superalert at every turn, tracking exits and reading the room before he would sit down.

What made this worse, in ways Douglas was only beginning to understand, was that the strategies he
had adopted to manage the threat monitoring were quietly feeding it. He had taken to playing loud
music or the television at high volume at home, filling the auditory space so that no ambient sound
could resolve into the chaotic chatter that undid him — yet the constant noise kept his nervous
system elevated, so that by evening he was more sensitized, not less. He had begun asking his wife
repeatedly whether she thought he seemed all right, whether his reactions were reasonable, whether
she was worried about him — and though she reassured him each time, the relief lasted minutes before
the doubt returned, and the need to check again grew stronger. His constant scanning of his
environment — cataloguing who was near, how they were positioned, whether any of them were police —
meant that he was never not looking for a threat, and the looking itself confirmed that a threat
must exist. These habits, taken together, kept him perpetually primed, so that the next trigger — an
officer in the corridor, a burst of indistinct voices in the gallery, a witness raising their voice
— landed with full force, renewing the alarm that the strategies were supposed to quiet.

The relational cost has been significant. His wife has described feeling as though she is living
with a security guard rather than a husband. His junior colleagues have noticed that he avoids pre-
trial briefings with law enforcement liaisons whenever possible, delegating tasks he once performed
without hesitation.

Douglas sought help after a morning when he could not enter the courtroom because two officers were
conferring just inside the door. He stood in the hallway for eleven minutes. He has not told anyone
at work. What he most fears is that he has become, in some permanent way, a man who cannot trust
authority — which is to say, a man who can no longer do his job.
