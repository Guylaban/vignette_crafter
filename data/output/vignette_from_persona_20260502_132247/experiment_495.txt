Priya Mehta is a 43-year-old South Asian woman who works as a licensed plumber, a trade she has
practiced for nearly fifteen years. She lives with her husband in a mid-sized city and, until
approximately eight months ago, described herself as someone who rarely worried about her health or
safety on the job. That changed on a Tuesday morning when she was dispatched to service a commercial
boiler room in an older industrial building. What she did not know when she descended into that
basement was that a slow leak in a corroded pipe had been saturating the space with a mixture of
industrial cleaning solvents and combustion byproducts. Within minutes of entering, her eyes were
burning, her throat was closing, and a thick, acrid smell — something between scorched plastic and
chemical smoke — filled her lungs before she could reach the exit. She lost consciousness briefly.
When paramedics arrived, the air around her still carried that same charred, chemical odor. She was
taken by ambulance to the emergency department, where she spent two days under observation for
respiratory injury. The smell of that basement — burnt, chemical, suffocating — became the sensory
signature of the worst moments of her life.

In the weeks that followed, Priya found that her nervous system had not forgotten what her mind was
trying to process. The first time she smelled a neighbor's bonfire from her back garden, her chest
seized and her heart hammered as though the basement had returned. Any smoke or fire smell — a
candle, a barbecue, a passing cigarette — triggered the same cascade of physical reactivity: racing
pulse, shortness of breath, a cold sweat that soaked through her work shirt. Even subtler related
smells, a colleague's solvent-based adhesive or a gas burner in the break room, were enough to send
her body into full alarm. These olfactory triggers, so ordinary in her trade and daily life, became
the primary mechanism through which the trauma reasserted itself, pulling her instantly back into
the sensory world of the boiler room. As the triggers fired, her body's threat response activated
with them — she would become superalert, scanning every room she entered, certain that danger was
present and imminent.

This sense of threat did not stay confined to triggered moments. Priya began waking in the night,
sometimes two or three times, getting out of bed to check the windows, the kitchen, the hallway —
convinced that something was wrong, that she would smell it before she saw it. The hypervigilance
that had begun as a response to specific smells gradually expanded into a constant background state,
and that sustained watchfulness made her more sensitive, not less. Because she was always scanning,
she detected smells she had never noticed before — a faint chemical trace in the plumbing supply
warehouse, exhaust from a passing van — and each detection reactivated her threat response,
confirming to her that danger was everywhere and that she had been right to keep watch. The
monitoring that was meant to protect her was, in practice, generating new triggers faster than she
could avoid them.

She began to manage this by restructuring her life around avoidance. She stopped accepting jobs in
commercial or industrial buildings where boiler rooms or chemical storage might be present. She
declined a significant contract — a school renovation that would have been her largest commission
that year — because the building had an old basement. She avoided hospitals and ambulances entirely,
refusing a routine physical examination her GP had requested, because the antiseptic smell of
medical settings was too close to what she had breathed that day. Over time, she also began
suppressing her emotional life more broadly, avoiding anything — a film, a conversation, even a
piece of good news — that might produce a strong feeling, because strong feelings, she had come to
believe, left her too open and too unguarded. Yet this emotional flattening kept her in a state of
low-grade vigilance that made her body more reactive, not less, so that when a smell did break
through her defenses, the physical response was overwhelming.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a morning last month when she smelled smoke from a house
fire two streets away and was found by her husband crouched in the corner of the kitchen, unable to
speak. She told the intake clinician that she was not sure she could keep working, and that she was
most afraid that this version of herself — frightened, contracted, always waiting — was now
permanent.
