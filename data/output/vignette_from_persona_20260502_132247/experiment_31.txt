was its bodily intimacy — her mother would grab her face to force eye contact, press her down onto
surfaces, handle her as though she were an object to be repositioned rather than a child to be
spoken to. Linh learned early that being seen — being looked at directly, being the focus of

mother's moods broke, were reliable warnings. Those two things, the sensation of being watched and
the sick feeling in her stomach, became the nervous system's shorthand for danger.

physical — her stomach turns, and with the nausea comes a fragmentary emotional surge, rage or
dread, that feels entirely out of proportion to the room she is standing in. She cannot always

intensity without an accompanying picture, which is itself disorienting. When she tries deliberately

narrate it cleanly, she has come to doubt the severity of it, which feeds directly into her belief
that she is simply a weak person who cannot manage what others manage without difficulty.

until it covers almost no territory at all; she feels genuinely at ease only in her own apartment,
and even there the ease is conditional.

redirecting or leaving the room, which means she has never told anyone close to her what happened.

it, and so the rage and the sense of injustice remain as raw as they were years ago. She has also

employer's health insurance renewal. She lay on the examination table, a stranger's hands moving
efficiently across her, and felt — not fear exactly, but a total, annihilating certainty that she
did not exist as a person in that room. She wept in her car afterward for an hour and could not
explain why. What she most hopes for, she says, is to stop feeling like something that happened to
her body rather than someone who lives in it.
