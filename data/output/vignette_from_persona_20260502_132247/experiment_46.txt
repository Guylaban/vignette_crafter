Rangi Taufa is a 63-year-old Tongan-American financial analyst who has been married for thirty-one
years and has worked for the same investment firm in Seattle for nearly two decades. He presents
with a PCL-5 score of 65 following a life-threatening road accident that occurred fourteen months
ago. Rangi was driving home late on a winter evening when a truck ran a red light and struck his
vehicle on the driver's side at high speed. The collision happened in near-total darkness, the
impact preceded by a shriek of brakes and the acrid smell of burning rubber that flooded the cabin
in the seconds before the truck connected. He was trapped for over an hour before emergency services
freed him, conscious throughout, the smell of hot metal and scorched material thick in the air, the
sound of passing traffic a continuous reminder that other vehicles were moving just feet from where
he lay pinned.

In the months that followed, Rangi found that the world had become a landscape of ambushes. The
first time he noticed it was in the underground parking garage beneath his office building — the
darkness closed around him as he stepped from the elevator, and before he had consciously registered
anything, his chest had tightened, his legs had locked, and his heart was hammering. His body, he
came to understand with grim certainty, reacted to danger before his mind had even had time to
think. The smell of a colleague's burned coffee in the break room, the sudden sound of a car
accelerating on the street outside — each of these could, without any warning, collapse the distance
between then and now and return him fully to the wreckage. The memory did not feel like something
that was over; it felt like something that was still happening, complete with the sounds he had
heard during those terrible minutes: the grinding metal, the muffled voices of bystanders, the
intermittent rush of passing cars. These sensory triggers pulled the memory forward with a force
that felt involuntary and total.

The triggers also fed directly into how Rangi understood his situation. He had come to believe, with
a conviction that felt entirely rational to him, that he had to be on guard at all times — that the
world was the kind of place where catastrophe arrived without announcement and that only sustained
vigilance stood between him and the next disaster. This belief that he had to be especially careful
because you never know what can happen next was not experienced as anxiety; it was experienced as
clear-eyed realism. And it was this appraisal, hardened over months, that kept his threat-monitoring
system in a state of permanent activation. He began scanning for exits the moment he entered any
room or building — his office, a restaurant, a conference room — mentally noting the nearest door
before he had taken his coat off. He woke repeatedly during the night to check whether he was safe,
sometimes lying still in the dark for twenty minutes, listening. These nocturnal checks, born from
the conviction that vigilance was the only reliable protection, in turn reactivated the memory —
lying awake in darkness, hearing the ambient sounds of the night, his mind would slip back into the
car, and the cycle would begin again.

What Rangi most feared was losing control of the memory itself. He was certain that if he allowed
himself to think about the accident, he would not be able to handle it — a belief that had led him
to suppress any deliberate engagement with what had happened. Yet the suppression offered no real
protection; the belief that the memory was unmanageable only seemed to make it more intrusive, more
urgent, surfacing precisely when his guard was lowered. At work, his concentration had deteriorated
significantly. Colleagues had noticed that he left meetings when the room felt insufficiently lit,
and he had stopped attending the firm's monthly dinners, which were held in a dimly lit restaurant
near the waterfront. His wife had grown quietly worried about the man who now slept with the bedside
lamp on and flinched at the sound of traffic from their bedroom window.

It was his wife who finally persuaded him to seek help, after a night in which he woke four times
and could not be consoled. What Rangi is most hoping for, tentatively and with some shame, is to be
able to drive again. What he is most afraid of is that the person he was before the accident is
simply gone.
