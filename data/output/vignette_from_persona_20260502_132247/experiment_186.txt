Kofi is a 25-year-old Ghanaian-American journalist, currently in a relationship, who presented for
psychological assessment following a traumatic encounter with police that occurred approximately
fourteen months ago. His PCL-5 score of 76 places him in the severe range, and the clinical picture
that has emerged across intake and initial sessions is one of a young man whose sense of self,
safety, and future have been comprehensively reorganised around a single night.

Kofi had been covering a community protest for an independent outlet when he was separated from the
press group, detained at gunpoint, forced face-down onto pavement, and held for several hours
without charge. Officers shouted commands inches from his face; boots struck the ground around his
head; at some point a radio crackled a song he recognised — a detail that, in retrospect, he finds
grotesque. He was eventually released without explanation. He drove home alone and told no one for
three days.

In the weeks that followed, the memories refused to settle into the past. They arrived with an
insistence that ordinary recollections never carry — more vivid, more present, more real than
anything else in his life, as though the event were still occurring somewhere just beneath the
surface of each day. When he tries to describe what happened — to his partner, to his editor, now to
his clinician — he loses the thread entirely, sentences dissolving before he can finish them, the
account collapsing in on itself. And embedded within those memories are sounds: the specific
percussion of boots on asphalt, a voice barking close to his ear, and that song. These auditory
fragments surface unbidden, and when they do, the memories flood back with full sensory force,
returning him instantly to the pavement.

It is this quality of the memories — their vividness, their auditory texture, their refusal to feel
historical — that keeps the threat system perpetually primed. Because the memories feel so
immediate, Kofi's nervous system reads the past as ongoing danger, and he moves through the world
accordingly. He scans constantly, checking behind him for anyone who might be following. He avoids
unfamiliar neighbourhoods, unable to assess their safety, which is professionally crippling for a
journalist whose work requires exactly that kind of mobility. Concentration has become unreliable;
he will be mid-sentence in a piece and find the words gone. And when the threat monitoring is high,
it sharpens his attention to anything that might signal recurrence — footsteps behind him on a
pavement, a song drifting from a passing car — which means the very vigilance designed to protect
him keeps delivering him back to the triggers that reactivate the memories.

Kofi now wears loose, concealing clothing when he goes out, a strategy that emerged quietly over
months from the conviction that being less visible means being less of a target. He avoids anything
that might remind him of the colleague he lost to gun violence two years prior — grief, he has
learned, opens a door he cannot close quickly enough. More broadly, he has constructed a near-total
embargo on emotional experience, steering away from anything that might generate feeling, positive
or negative, because feeling anything has come to feel like losing control of the perimeter. But
these strategies carry their own cost: the emotional flatness, the withdrawal, the concealment have
become further evidence to him that something essential has been permanently lost. The avoidance
that was meant to protect him instead confirms his worst appraisals of himself.

What frightens Kofi most is not the memories themselves but what he has come to believe they prove.
He was, by his own account, a happy person — curious, driven, defined by his work. He is now certain
that person is gone. He believes his life has been destroyed, that he is no longer capable of the
journalism that gave his life meaning, and these convictions are not idle thoughts: they are load-
bearing. They amplify the threat he reads into every unfamiliar street, every pair of footsteps,
every song. They make the memories feel not like wounds that might heal but like evidence of a
permanent verdict. And as he retreats further — from assignments, from feelings, from his partner's
attempts to reach him — the retreat itself becomes further proof.

What finally brought him to seek help was a panic response in the newsroom when a colleague's
footsteps came up fast behind him. He had shouted, shaken, and been unable to explain why. He told
his clinician he was not sure he hoped for much — but that he could not keep losing ground.
