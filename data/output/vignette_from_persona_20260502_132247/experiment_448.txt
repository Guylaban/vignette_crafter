Valentina Reyes is a 34-year-old married Latina woman who works as a corrections officer at a
medium-security state prison. She was referred for psychological evaluation following a mandatory
review after a workplace incident, and her PCL-5 score of 55 places her firmly in the severe range
for post-traumatic stress disorder. The trauma she carries originates not from her current
profession but from the years before she arrived in the United States — years spent fleeing
political violence in her country of origin, during which she and her family were detained at a
military checkpoint. Soldiers reeking of alcohol pulled her from a vehicle at gunpoint, shouting
orders inches from her face, their uniforms pressed and immaculate in a way that made the violence
feel bureaucratic and absolute. She was held for several hours, separated from her husband, and
witnessed beatings she has never fully described to anyone. It was the smell of alcohol on the lead
soldier's breath, and the cadence of his commands — clipped, authoritative, contemptuous — that her
nervous system encoded as the signature of mortal danger.

Valentina arrived in the United States and, in a decision that now strikes her as darkly ironic,
pursued a career in corrections. She had wanted to feel powerful rather than powerless, to be the
one with the keys. For a time, it worked. But the prison environment is saturated with the very
sensory material her trauma encoded as threat. When a supervising sergeant issues a directive in a
sharp tone, or when a colleague in uniform approaches her unexpectedly from the side, her body
responds before her mind can intervene — heart hammering, hands bracing, a surge of readiness that
has no legitimate outlet. The smell of alcohol on an inmate's breath, smuggled in on visiting days,
is perhaps the most destabilising trigger of all; on those occasions she has had to excuse herself
from the unit, which has drawn comment from supervisors and eroded her professional standing over
the past year.

These triggers do not simply startle her — they pull her back. When the smell hits or a command is
barked, she hears, with an almost auditory precision, the soldier's voice from the checkpoint: the
specific syllables, the contempt in them. The memories return with a vividness that she finds
bewildering and humiliating, and the emotional overwhelm is immediate enough that she has learned to
cut the recall short by any means available. At home she turns the television to its highest
tolerable volume; at work she retreats to a supply room and puts in earbuds. These strategies
interrupt the memory before it can fully surface, but they also prevent any processing from
occurring, so the memory remains sealed and potent, ready to be triggered again at the next sensory
encounter.

What Valentina has come to believe, slowly and with a certainty that now feels like bedrock, is that
the world is genuinely dangerous and that people — especially people in positions of authority — are
not what they appear to be. This conviction is not abstract; it shapes every room she enters. She
scans for exits, selects seats with her back to the wall, and reads the body language of uniformed
colleagues with an intensity that exhausts her. These beliefs, in turn, keep her sensitised to the
very triggers that confirm them: a supervisor's tone, a colleague's unexpected movement, the
institutional smell of a corrections facility that, on bad days, seems to carry the ghost of that
checkpoint. Her distrust of authority figures has created a painful paradox, because she is herself
an authority figure, and the dissonance between who she is now and who she was — a woman who once
trusted institutions, who believed in safety — has left her feeling that she no longer knows
herself.

The rumination is relentless. She replays the night of the detention and searches it for the
decision she should have taken, the route they should not have used, the moment she could have
changed the outcome. This dwelling does not bring resolution; it sustains the sense of a world in
which danger is everywhere and always preventable if one is only vigilant enough, which tightens her
threat monitoring further and makes every false alarm feel like evidence rather than error.

Valentina sought help after the workplace incident — she startled so violently at an unexpected
movement from a colleague that she shoved him into a wall. She was not disciplined, but the look on
his face, and on the faces of those who witnessed it, told her something had to change. What she is
most afraid of is that nothing will.
