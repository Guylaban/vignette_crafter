Tariq is a 52-year-old Lebanese-American actor, widowed, who presents with a PCL-5 score of 66
following the traumatic death of his young daughter three years ago. She was killed in a crowd crush
at a public festival — a community event Tariq had encouraged the family to attend. He had been
separated from her in the surge of bodies, and by the time he reached her, emergency responders were
already present. He could do nothing. The sounds he heard in those final moments — screaming, the
particular pitch of children crying in panic — are sounds he has never been able to leave behind.

The memories themselves do not come as a coherent narrative. Instead, Tariq experiences them as
photographic fragments: a freeze-frame of her face in the crowd, the colour of her jacket, his own
hand reaching. These disconnected pieces resist assembly into anything that could be processed or
mourned cleanly, and when they surface — which they do without warning — they bring with them a wave
of intense guilt so consuming that Tariq has described it as a physical weight pressing on his
chest. The fragmented, guilt-saturated quality of these recollections has made him terrified of
stillness, because stillness is when the images arrive most forcefully. That fear has quietly
reorganised his life: he avoids being alone in a room or building for any length of time, filling
his schedule and his space with other people not out of connection but out of a desperate need to
keep the memories at a manageable distance. Yet paradoxically, the avoidance of solitude has kept
him from ever sitting with the memories long enough to integrate them, so they remain raw and
ungoverned, ready to flood him at the next trigger.

His work as an actor presents particular difficulty. Rehearsal rooms, green rooms, and sets are full
of ambient sound — and on more than one occasion, a child's crying from somewhere in the building,
or a piece of incidental music resembling sounds from that day, has stopped him mid-scene. When
these auditory triggers land, the threat system activates immediately: his concentration dissolves,
he is flooded with fear, horror, and shame simultaneously, and he finds it impossible to believe he
is safe even when colleagues reassure him directly. He has come to believe that safety is simply not
a category that applies to him anymore — that the world demonstrated its true nature that day, and
no amount of reassurance can overwrite that knowledge. The triggered state of high arousal then
makes him hypervigilant to further stimuli, so that ordinary sounds on set become potential threats,
and his perceptual threshold lowers until the environment feels saturated with danger. In this
state, related media — news coverage of crowd incidents, documentary footage, even fictional
depictions — becomes unbearable, and he has learned to leave rooms where screens are on, to scroll
past anything that might carry him back.

The appraisals Tariq holds about the event are among the most corrosive features of his
presentation. He is certain that the festival organisers failed in their duty of care, that the
systems meant to protect attendees were negligent, and that no one has been held accountable in any
meaningful way. These beliefs — which are not without factual basis — feed a constant sense of
threat and injustice that keeps his nervous system in a state of low-grade emergency. But the most
painful appraisal is directed inward: he will never forgive himself for not doing more. This
conviction of inaction guilt is reinforced every time the memories return, and the guilt itself
deepens the sense that the world is dangerous and that he, specifically, cannot be trusted to
protect those he loves. The people-pleasing behaviour that has developed — going along with whatever
others want, never asserting a need, making himself agreeable and undemanding — feels to him like a
form of penance, but it also sustains a posture of helplessness that confirms his worst beliefs
about his own agency.

What finally brought Tariq to seek help was a rehearsal last month in which a child actor on set
began crying during a break. He walked off the production without explanation and sat in his car for
two hours, unable to move. He has not returned to that job. He comes to therapy hoping, in his
words, to be able to work again — but what he is most afraid of is that the guilt will turn out to
be deserved.
