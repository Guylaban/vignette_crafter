Rangi is a 56-year-old Māori man, divorced, who has worked as a detective for nearly two decades. He
presented for assessment with a PCL-5 score of 62, describing a clinical picture that has quietly
consumed him for most of his adult life before finally, in recent months, becoming impossible to
contain.

The trauma at the centre of Rangi's presentation is not a single incident but a sustained
architecture of violence. Throughout his childhood, his primary caregiver — a man he describes only
as "the old man" — subjected him to systematic physical abuse that was unpredictable in its timing
but consistent in its cruelty. What Rangi remembers most viscerally is the smell: burnt food, singed
fabric, the acrid haze that preceded the worst episodes, when the old man's rage would find its
focus on him. He remembers believing, even then, that there was something about him that invited it
— that he was, in some essential way, the kind of boy this happened to. That belief did not leave
him when he grew up. It calcified.

For years, Rangi managed. He entered law enforcement, drawn perhaps by a need to impose order on the
chaotic, and rose to detective. But the work placed him in proximity to the very material his mind
had never processed. On scene visits, interviewing victims, reviewing case files — he encountered
people whose faces, whose body language, whose visible suffering mirrored something he had never let
himself fully name. When he saw a victim who resembled the children he had once been, or walked into
a space where something had been burning, the full memory returned without transition or warning —
not as recollection but as reinstatement, as if fifty years had not passed. He would feel it in his
body first: his chest tightening, his jaw locking, a surge of physical reactivity so intense that
colleagues had twice asked if he was having a cardiac event. He told them he was fine.

The rage that accompanied these returns was something Rangi found almost more distressing than the
fear. When the memories came, they brought with them a fury so consuming he could not finish
sentences — he would lose the thread of what he was trying to explain and go silent, which he read
as further evidence that he was broken, that his reactions since the event proved he was a lousy
coper who had never managed to become someone capable of handling his own history. This self-
condemnation deepened the memories' grip: the more he believed the abuse had happened because of who
he was, the more the memories returned saturated with shame and rage, and the more those memories
confirmed, in his mind, that his beliefs about how the world works — that people can be safe, that
suffering is not deserved — had been permanently destroyed.

To manage, Rangi built a careful life of avoidance. He stopped wearing certain colours and cuts of
clothing that resembled what he had worn as a child, a habit so automatic he could no longer fully
explain it. He withdrew from colleagues socially, declining invitations, eating alone, gradually
becoming someone his team described as "private." He learned to detach himself from the memories
when they surfaced — to observe them from a remove, as if watching footage of someone else —
believing this was control. But detachment kept the memories unprocessed and structurally intact, so
that the next trigger — a burnt smell drifting from the break room at the precinct, a victim's face
in a photograph — could instantly reinstate the full experience again. The avoidance, which was
meant to reduce exposure to triggers, had instead preserved the sensitivity to them; and the social
withdrawal had removed the relationships that might have offered any corrective experience, leaving
him only with the conclusion that he was someone others were right to keep at a distance.

At night, Rangi could not surrender to sleep. He lay alert, monitoring, certain that lowering his
guard was a risk he could not afford — a vigilance that had protected a child and now exhausted a
man. Positive emotions, when they arose, confused and unsettled him; he had no framework for them,
and their presence felt wrong in a way he could not articulate.

What finally brought him in was a case he could not close — not professionally, but internally. He
had interviewed a child victim three months ago and had not slept properly since. He came to the
intake session hoping, he said quietly, to find out whether any of this could be different. What he
feared most was that it could not.
