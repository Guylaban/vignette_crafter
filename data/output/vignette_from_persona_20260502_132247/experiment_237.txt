Malia Suwan is a 61-year-old Thai American sculptor, married and living in a quiet residential
neighbourhood outside Portland, Oregon. She came to the clinic after her husband noticed she had not
entered her studio in nearly three months — the longest interruption to her practice in over two
decades. Her PCL-5 score of 41 places her in a clinically significant range, and what emerges across
the intake and early sessions is a presentation shaped by a single, devastating afternoon witnessed
roughly eighteen months ago.

Malia had been attending an opening at a gallery where her work was on display. The evening was
celebratory until, near the end of the night, she witnessed an assault in the corridor outside the
main hall — a man, heavy-set and wearing a strong, sweet-musky cologne, pursuing a younger woman
down the passage. Malia heard the footsteps first: irregular, accelerating, the sound bouncing off
polished concrete. By the time she understood what was happening, she had seen enough that she
cannot now reconstruct the sequence with any coherence. When she tries to tell the story — even to
herself, lying awake at night — she loses the thread. The memory does not behave like memory. It
arrives in fragments that feel more immediate and more real than anything else in her life, vivid
and disorganised in equal measure, resistant to the beginning-middle-end structure that she has
always used to make sense of experience. She cannot produce a clear account of what happened, and
this failure of narrative has itself become frightening to her.

That fragmentation has made the memory unusually dangerous. Because it carries no temporal frame —
no sense of then and there — it does not stay in the past. Instead, the disordered quality of the
recollection keeps her system primed, as though the event might still be unresolved. This chronic
activation feeds directly into her sense of threat: she feels jumpy throughout the day, startles at
small sounds, and finds that darkness specifically amplifies her unease — night feels categorically
less safe than day, and she often sits by the bedroom window until her husband coaxes her back to
bed. When something triggers a physical echo of that evening, her body responds with full force:
heart rate surging, hands trembling, breath shortened — reactions that feel to her entirely
disproportionate and deeply shaming.

The triggers themselves are precise and potent. The cologne is the most disabling: a sweet, musky
scent she has now encountered on strangers in grocery stores, at her husband's workplace gatherings,
and — crucially — at the arts supply warehouse she used to visit monthly for casting materials. She
stopped going. Footsteps behind her, particularly on hard floors, produce an immediate and
overwhelming physical response that pulls the fragmented images back with startling force, and the
combination of sensory intrusion and memory flooding has made any unpredictable environment feel
genuinely dangerous. Her studio, where visiting collectors and assistants came and went without
fixed schedules, became intolerable. The footsteps on the concrete floor, the unpredictability of
who might arrive — each element was woven into what she had witnessed, and the studio began to feel
like a corridor she could not control.

What has settled over Malia in the months since is a set of beliefs she experiences as hard-won
conclusions rather than distortions. She has come to believe that people cannot be trusted — that
the social world she once moved through with relative ease is populated by threats she failed to
read. More painfully, she has come to believe there is something fundamentally wrong with her: that
her inability to tell the story clearly, her physical reactions, her vigilance, all point to a
defect in her character rather than the ordinary consequences of what she witnessed. The world, she
is now certain, is dangerous in ways she had naively overlooked. These appraisals do not sit quietly
— they sharpen her threat sensitivity, so that neutral stimuli are scanned for menace, and they
return her again and again to the fragmented memory, as though she might finally locate the flaw in
herself that explains why she could not prevent what she saw, or why she cannot now recover.

What finally brought her in was her husband finding her, one morning, sitting outside the locked
studio door. She was not sure whether she had been trying to go in or trying to stay out. She is
most afraid that the person who made things with her hands — who moved through space with purpose —
is gone. She is most hoping to be able to finish a sentence about that night.
