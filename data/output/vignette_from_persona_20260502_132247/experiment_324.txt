Raymond is a 71-year-old married man who has worked as a security guard for the better part of two
decades, a role he took on after retirement from an earlier career and one that, until recently,
gave him a clear sense of purpose. Approximately eight months ago, Raymond was on duty at a public
venue when a terrorist attack unfolded in the space he was responsible for protecting. The attack
was sudden and chaotic — there were people running, screaming, the smell of smoke, and Raymond found
himself unable to reach several individuals who were injured in the initial blast. He was not
physically wounded, but he was close enough to witness the immediate aftermath in visceral detail:
faces, sounds, the particular quality of the light through the haze. He survived. Others did not
fare as well. And it is that gap — between what he was there to do and what he was able to do — that
has sat at the centre of his suffering ever since.

In the weeks following the attack, Raymond began to notice that his memories of the event were both
overwhelming and strangely fractured. He can recall specific images with almost painful clarity — a
particular face, the angle of a doorway, the sound of an alarm — yet when he tries to assemble these
fragments into a coherent account, even for himself, the story falls apart. He cannot tell it from
beginning to end. The sequence dissolves. On other occasions, he finds himself recounting what
happened with a flat, detached quality, as though narrating something that happened to a stranger
rather than to him — a dissociation that leaves him unsettled in its own way, as though even his own
experience has become inaccessible.

What has come to dominate Raymond's waking life, however, is a set of beliefs about himself that
have hardened into convictions. He is certain that another man — a more competent man, a real
security professional — would not have found himself in that position, or would have responded
differently. He has failed, he believes, in the role that mattered most to him: the role of
protector. These appraisals have not remained passive. They have driven him toward a vigilance that
is now relentless. He arrives at any room — the break room at work, a restaurant with his wife, a
waiting area at a clinic — and immediately positions himself with his back against a wall, scanning
every face, every entrance, cataloguing who is present and who might be dangerous. It is not a
choice he makes consciously anymore; it is what his body does before his mind catches up. And
alongside this physical hypervigilance, he spends hours in a kind of grinding mental rehearsal — not
processing the event, but turning over fantasies of retribution, imagining confrontations with those
responsible, calculating what he should have done and what he might yet do. He has come to believe
that if he can just think it through completely enough, he will find the answer that restores
something of what he has lost.

The tragedy of this pattern is that it does the opposite. The constant scanning and the rumination
keep Raymond's nervous system primed and his attention fixed on threat, and this sustained state of
arousal makes it harder, not easier, to process what happened. The fragmented, non-sequential
quality of his memories persists — perhaps deepens — because the mental space required to integrate
experience is crowded out by the endless cycle of vigilance and revenge fantasy. And the very
strategies he uses to manage his sense of failure end up confirming it: the wall-sitting, the
scanning, the inability to relax in any public space, all of it reads to him as evidence that he is
broken in a way that cannot be repaired. His wife has noticed the change. Dinners out have stopped.
Conversations have grown shorter. She has told him, gently and then less gently, that she no longer
recognises the man she married.
