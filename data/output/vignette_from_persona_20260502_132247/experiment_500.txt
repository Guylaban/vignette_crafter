Margit is a 60-year-old midwife of Hungarian descent who has worked in a busy urban hospital for
nearly three decades. She lives with her long-term partner and, until recently, described her
professional life as the central source of purpose in her existence. That sense of purpose collapsed
approximately fourteen months ago when Margit was exposed to an unidentified toxic chemical during a
routine shift on the maternity ward. A cleaning agent had been incorrectly stored and mixed with
another substance in a supply room she entered without protective equipment. Within minutes she felt
a burning sensation in her throat and chest, her eyes streamed, and she became acutely nauseated and
dizzy. She was found collapsed in the corridor by a colleague, her vision blurring, convinced in
those moments that she was dying. The incident lasted less than ten minutes in real time, but it
left a mark that has not lifted.

In the weeks that followed, Margit returned to work too quickly, reassuring everyone — her partner,
her supervisors, her GP — that she was fine. She had always been the capable one, the steady
presence in the room when deliveries went wrong, and she could not reconcile being a patient with
who she understood herself to be. Yet the ward, which had once felt like home, had become
treacherous. The smell of cleaning products, the sight of blood during a complicated delivery, the
ordinary physical sensations of a long shift — fatigue, a racing heart, mild nausea — all began to
carry the texture of that afternoon in the supply room. When she felt physically unwell during a
shift, even from something as mundane as skipping lunch, her body interpreted it as the beginning of
something catastrophic, and the memory of the chemical burning in her lungs would rise up with
startling immediacy.

It was these intrusive moments that began to shape her behaviour in ways she did not fully recognise
at first. When triggered — by blood, by a colleague describing a violent or traumatic birth, by any
flicker of physical unwellness — Margit found herself going very quiet, agreeing to whatever was
asked of her, deferring to junior colleagues rather than leading, as though making herself small
might keep the feeling from cresting. She stopped making plans: no holiday bookings, no professional
development courses, nothing that required imagining a future self intact enough to show up. She
told herself this was practical. It did not feel like avoidance. It felt like survival.

When she eventually attended two sessions of trauma-focused therapy at her partner's urging, she
found the content intolerable — not because it was badly handled, but because the act of thinking
carefully about any of it seemed to make the triggers more accessible, not less. She began actively
suppressing anything that had been touched on in those sessions, pushing it beneath the surface of
each working day. The suppression cost her. She arrived home exhausted in a way that sleep did not
repair.

Underneath all of this, Margit had formed a set of beliefs about herself that had become load-
bearing. She had come to believe that her inability to simply recover — to be the professional she
had always been — was evidence of fundamental weakness. A strong person, she reasoned, would not be
undone by a brief exposure that left no lasting physical damage. This conviction of weakness fed
directly into her tendency to defer and please, to never assert a need or decline a request, because
asserting herself felt like claiming a status she no longer deserved. And the belief that she no
longer knew who she was — that the woman who had guided thousands of births with confidence was
simply gone — made the triggers more devastating when they came. Each episode of blood or violence
on the ward did not merely startle her; it confirmed the appraisal. It proved, again, that she was
not herself, that nothing meant what it once had.

Margit presented for assessment after a delivery in which a postpartum haemorrhage required
emergency intervention. She managed the clinical situation competently, but wept in the car park
afterwards for forty minutes and could not explain why. Her partner had gently noted that she seemed
to have disappeared from her own life. What Margit said she feared most was that there was no
version of herself left to recover. What she hoped, more quietly, was that she was wrong.
