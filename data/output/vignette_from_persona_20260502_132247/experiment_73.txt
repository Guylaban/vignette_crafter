intrusive somatic memories. Married and professionally prominent, she described her current distress

threshold she had spent years refusing to acknowledge.

Ingrid grew up in a household where her father's violence toward her mother was a recurring and
unpredictable feature of domestic life. She was seven the first time she witnessed a serious
assault: her father had cornered her mother in the kitchen doorway, and Ingrid, frozen at the top of
the stairs, watched what unfolded below. She could not now recall the sequence of events with any
coherence. Certain images remained searingly vivid — the angle of her mother's body against the door
frame, the quality of the kitchen light — while other moments had been swallowed entirely, leaving
gaps she could not fill no matter how deliberately she tried to reconstruct them. She described
sometimes finding herself in the posture she had held that night: shoulders drawn in, one hand
gripping a railing or the edge of a desk, her body apparently carrying what her mind could not fully
hold. These bodily reenactments were not voluntary. They arrived without warning and left her
disoriented and ashamed.

The fragmentary nature of these memories had, over time, become a source of considerable dread.
Because she could not reconstruct what had happened in its entirety, Ingrid had come to believe that
the gaps themselves were dangerous — that what she could not remember must be worse than what she
could, and that her inability to contain the memory meant she was fundamentally unprepared for
threat. This conviction had gradually organized her experience of the present. She found herself
watching other people closely in meetings, in corridors, at community events — scanning faces and
postures for the earliest signs of hostility or escalation. She had narrowed the geography of her
felt safety to a very limited number of places: her home office, a particular seat in the council
chamber, a corner table at one café she had visited for years. Anywhere outside these anchored
locations carried a low but persistent hum of menace she could not rationalize away.

That vigilance, in turn, fed back into the memories themselves. When Ingrid was in a heightened
state of alertness — which was often, given the demands of her public role — the somatic intrusions
became more frequent and more disruptive. A raised voice during a council session, a colleague who
stood too close, a sudden shift in the room's atmosphere: any of these could pull her body back
toward the staircase. The sense of being watched or threatened in the present appeared to prime the
fragmented memories forward, so that the two states — past and present danger — became difficult to
disentangle. She was certain, on some level she found hard to articulate, that her body knew
something her conscious mind did not, and that this knowledge demanded constant vigilance. The loop
between unresolved memory and present threat perception had tightened steadily over the past two
years, each reinforcing the other in ways she had only recently begun to name.

Sleep had become increasingly difficult. She lay awake parsing the sounds of the house, unable to
relinquish the watchfulness that the day had required. By morning she was depleted, which made the
intrusions harder to manage and the monitoring harder to suspend.

What finally brought Ingrid to seek help was a public moment: during a heated council debate, she
had gripped the edge of the table, gone briefly unreachable, and a colleague had noticed. The
prospect of her private history becoming legible to others in a professional context frightened her
more than the symptoms themselves had. She arrived at the intake hoping, above all, to regain some
sense of control — over her memory, over her body in public spaces, over the vigilance that had
quietly restructured her life around a childhood she had long believed she had left behind.
