Phong Nguyen is a sixty-eight-year-old Vietnamese American public defender, widowed, who presents
with a PCL-5 score of 70 following a traumatic event involving his adult son. Approximately fourteen
months ago, Phong received a call informing him that his son Minh had been the victim of a violent
assault outside a convenience store not far from the neighborhood where Phong has practiced law for
over three decades. Minh survived, but the injuries were severe — a fractured skull, a week in the
ICU — and Phong had arrived at the hospital in time to see his son unconscious, surrounded by
monitors, his face barely recognizable beneath the swelling. What Phong cannot escape, even now, is
the sound: the low, continuous tone of the cardiac monitor, the wet, labored quality of Minh's
breathing through the oxygen mask. Those sounds have lodged in him like splinters.

In the months that followed, Phong found that his memories of that night did not cohere into a
narrative he could hold at a distance. They arrived instead in disconnected pieces — a flash of the
fluorescent corridor, the weight of the plastic chair beneath him, a nurse's hand on his shoulder —
fragments that surfaced without warning and refused to assemble themselves into something he could
process or put away. When those pieces surfaced, what he heard most vividly was the cardiac
monitor's tone, replaying with a fidelity that made his chest tighten as though he were back in that
waiting room. And with the memories came grief so overwhelming that he sometimes had to stop mid-
sentence during client consultations, pressing his fingers to the bridge of his nose and waiting for
it to pass.

It was not long before those intrusive memories began feeding directly into a state of near-constant
vigilance. Phong had come to believe, with a certainty that felt entirely rational to him, that the
world contained dangerous people who moved through it undetected — and that he, of all people,
should have known how to see them coming. He is a public defender; he has spent his career inside
the machinery of violence and its consequences, and still he had not protected his son. Now, walking
the courthouse hallways or sitting in the holding rooms with clients, he scans faces with an
alertness that exhausts him. He reads posture, watches hands, notes who stands too close to whom.
Several colleagues have remarked on a new guardedness in him, and at least one junior attorney has
stopped seeking his mentorship, interpreting his hypervigilance as coldness.

At home each night, Phong checks the deadbolts on his front and back doors multiple times before he
can sit down, and still he finds himself returning an hour later to check again. He reacts to
ordinary sounds — a car door, the building's elevator — as though they signal something worse, his
body flooding with fear or anger before his mind can catch up. These false alarms leave him shaken
and ashamed, and the shame itself seems to sharpen his watchfulness, as though relaxing his guard
even briefly would be a moral failure. That heightened threat state, in turn, pulls the fragmented
memories back to the surface — the monitor tone, the corridor light, the grief — so that vigilance
and recollection feed each other in a cycle Phong has not been able to interrupt.

To manage this, he has stopped watching television in the evenings and avoids any news that might
carry stories of assault or street violence, a significant restriction given that his work keeps him
adjacent to exactly such material. He has also begun eating heavily after court, working through
whatever is in the kitchen with a focused, joyless efficiency that he recognizes, in his clearer
moments, as distraction rather than hunger. These strategies bring brief relief, but they also
narrow his world and keep him indoors and isolated — conditions in which the memories, when they
come, feel louder. The avoidance sustains the threat he is trying to escape, and the threat sustains
the memories he cannot yet face.

What finally brought Phong to seek help was a morning last month when he sat in his car outside the
courthouse for forty minutes, unable to go in. He is afraid, he says quietly, that he is becoming
someone his son would not recognize. What he hopes for, more than anything, is to stop hearing that
sound.
