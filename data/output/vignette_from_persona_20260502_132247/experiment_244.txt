Priya Nair, a 32-year-old South Asian woman working as a registered nurse in a busy urban hospital,
presented for her initial psychological consultation approximately eight months after surviving a
catastrophic industrial gas leak that swept through the medical complex where she was on shift. The
event unfolded without warning on an otherwise unremarkable Tuesday evening: a pressurised chemical
line ruptured in the building's maintenance corridor, releasing toxic fumes that moved rapidly
through the ventilation system and into the wards. Priya was triaging patients when the alarms
sounded — and then, almost immediately, they stopped. What followed was a period of confusion, mass
panic, colleagues collapsing around her, and the particular horror of being a trained nurse unable
to distinguish who needed help from who was already beyond it. She herself was evacuated by
emergency responders and treated for exposure. Seven of her patients and one colleague did not
survive.

What brought Priya to the clinic was not, she explained carefully, distress in any form she could
easily name. It was more that she had begun to feel she could not trust her own mind. Her memory of
that evening existed in a way that felt fundamentally broken — not painful in the way she had
expected trauma to feel, but strange and disorienting in a manner that unsettled her more deeply
than she could articulate. The event, she said, did not feel entirely real to her. Even now, eight
months on, there were moments when she would catch herself thinking that something so extreme simply
could not have happened to her — not to her specifically, not in that hospital, not on a Tuesday.
The memory carried a quality of unreality that she found both relieving and deeply frightening, as
though the night existed behind glass she could press against but not break through.

What she could access came in fragments. She might be checking a patient's IV line and find herself
holding a brief, vivid image — the particular blue of a colleague's scrubs on the floor, the sound
of a trolley handle hitting tile — and then nothing. No before, no after. The pieces did not connect
into anything she could call a story. She had tried, in the early weeks, to sit with the memories
and reconstruct the sequence of events, partly out of professional habit and partly because she
believed, as a nurse, that understanding what had happened was the responsible thing to do. But she
found that she could not sustain it. Before any recollection reached its natural conclusion,
something in her pulled away — a kind of internal flinching that she had come to recognise as
deliberate even when it did not feel like a choice. She would push the memory back before it could
complete itself, and then find herself standing in the present moment, slightly breathless,
uncertain how much time had passed.

This pattern had consequences that extended well beyond her private distress. On the ward, Priya had
become quietly hypervigilant about ventilation sounds — the low hum of the hospital's air handling
units, which she had never consciously registered before, now occupied a portion of her attention on
every shift. She had not told her supervisor. She had not told anyone, in fact, which was part of
why she had come. She was a person who prided herself on competence and clarity, and the fragmented,
unreal quality of what she carried felt like a professional liability she could not afford to
acknowledge aloud. She worried that if she began to speak about it, the pieces would rearrange
themselves into something she would not be able to manage on a ward full of patients who needed her
present.

What Priya hoped for, she said at the end of the intake session, was to be able to remember the
night as something that had actually happened to her — coherently, completely, and in the past. What
she feared most was that the memory would always remain this way: broken, unfinished, and not quite
believable, like a story told about someone else that she had somehow been assigned to carry.
