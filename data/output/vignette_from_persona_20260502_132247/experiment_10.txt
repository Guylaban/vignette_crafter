Aroha is a thirty-year-old Māori woman who works as a plumber and lives with her husband in a mid-
sized Pacific Northwest city. She came to the clinic describing a persistent sense that something
inside her had never fully settled, a feeling she had carried so long she had almost stopped
noticing it — until recently, when it began interfering with her marriage and her ability to work.

Aroha grew up in a household where her primary caregiver was volatile and physically abusive. The
abuse was not chaotic in the way outsiders might imagine; it was often preceded by a particular
quality of stillness — a moment of being watched from across a room, the caregiver's eyes tracking
her before the shouting began. The shouting itself was not simply loud; it was a signal that
something physical was imminent. By the time she was an adolescent, the sound of raised voices and
the sensation of being observed had become inseparable from the anticipation of harm. Even calm
expressions of anger — a tightened jaw, a clipped tone — had come to feel like the first syllable of
something worse.

Her memories of the abuse are fragmented in ways that trouble her. Some moments are unnervingly
vivid: the texture of a particular wall, a specific phrase her caregiver used. Others are entirely
absent, and she is aware of these gaps as gaps — a sense of an incomplete picture, as though she is
only holding part of the story. The memory itself feels strangely impersonal, as though it belongs
to someone she once read about rather than to herself. This dissociated quality, rather than
providing relief, feeds her hypervigilance: because the memory feels incomplete and alien, her body
seems to compensate by staying perpetually alert, scanning for the pieces the mind cannot supply.
The felt sense of threat remains constant even when the remembered narrative is thin, and that
bodily alarm in turn keeps pulling her attention back toward the traumatic past, as though her
nervous system is still trying to assemble a coherent account.

At work, these dynamics become concrete and unavoidable. On job sites, Aroha is frequently
supervised or watched by contractors and project managers, and the sensation of being observed
triggers an immediate physical response — her body tenses, her concentration fractures, and she
finds herself startling at unexpected movements from colleagues working nearby. When a site foreman
raises his voice to be heard over machinery, even without any anger directed at her, she feels the
old alarm flood through her and her capacity to focus on the task in front of her collapses. These
triggered states of threat reinforce themselves: once her body is on alert, ordinary ambient stimuli
— a raised voice two rooms away, someone stepping into her peripheral vision — register as potential
danger, and the cycle of scanning and reactivity intensifies.

Over the months following a period of particular stress in her marriage, Aroha developed a set of
strategies she understood, at some level, as management. She began restricting her eating, finding
that hunger gave her something concrete to monitor and control when everything else felt
uncontrollable. She worked hard to keep her mind occupied at all times — podcasts during commutes,
television through the evening — because any unstructured mental space seemed to invite the
fragments of memory to surface. She tried forcefully not to think about what had happened to her.
What she did not anticipate was that these strategies would tighten rather than loosen the knot. The
constant mental occupation and thought suppression meant she never processed the incomplete memory,
and the gaps remained, keeping her threat system primed. The undereating left her physically
depleted, which made her startle responses more pronounced and her concentration worse. The
avoidance kept the triggers close, so that shouting or being watched or witnessing someone else's
anger landed with the same raw force as ever, and each fresh trigger sent her back into the coping
loop she had constructed.

What finally brought Aroha to seek help was a conversation with her husband in which she could not
explain why she had flinched when he raised his voice during an argument — not at her, simply in
frustration. She saw his confusion and her own inability to account for herself. She came to the
clinic hoping, cautiously, that it might be possible to feel less like a person perpetually braced
for impact. What she feared most was that looking directly at the incomplete memory would confirm
that the missing pieces contained something she was not equipped to survive.
