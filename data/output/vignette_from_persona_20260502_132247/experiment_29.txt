Caleb, a 19-year-old single man of North American background, works the morning shift at a fast food
restaurant in a mid-sized Midwestern town. He was referred for psychological assessment following a
brief conversation with his shift supervisor, who had noticed a marked change in his demeanour over
the preceding months. Caleb arrived at the intake session quiet and visibly tense, choosing his
words with unusual care — pausing before each answer as though rehearsing it first.

Approximately eight months prior to the intake, Caleb had been at home alone when a severe tornado
struck his neighbourhood without adequate warning. He had no time to reach a shelter. He described
pressing himself into the corner of a bathroom as the roof above him partially gave way, the air
pressure dropping sickeningly, the smell of wet earth and splintered wood filling the space around
him. The sound was what he remembered most viscerally — a roaring that he said felt alive, like
something was trying to swallow the house whole. He stayed there for what he later learned was
eleven minutes but experienced as something without duration. When it was over, the neighbourhood
was largely unrecognisable. He walked out into a street strewn with insulation and shattered glass,
still in his socks.

In the weeks that followed, the memories returned not as coherent narratives but as fragments —
predominantly images: the wall bowing inward, the light fixture swinging violently, the shadow of
debris crossing the window. These visual memories arrived without warning and were accompanied by
the same terror he had felt in the moment, a physiological flooding that left him shaking and
breathless. On several occasions, particularly during outdoor shifts when the wind picked up or when
a customer left the restaurant door open and the smell of rain drifted in, the olfactory memory
returned with startling precision — wet earth and wood — and Caleb would find himself momentarily
back inside that bathroom. The intrusive quality of these memories, their sensory immediacy and the
terror they carried, began to convince him that something fundamental had broken inside him. He had
come to believe that the person he had been before the tornado — easy-going, sociable, someone his
coworkers had described as reliably cheerful — was simply gone, replaced by someone he did not
recognise and did not like.

These appraisals of permanent, irreversible change shaped the strategies Caleb began building around
himself. Certain that he was damaged in ways that could not be repaired, and that any additional
stress might confirm or deepen that damage, he began systematically avoiding anything that felt even
mildly threatening. At work, this meant he stopped volunteering for tasks that required him to go
outside to collect trolleys or take out bins — situations where weather was unpredictable. He also
began rehearsing conversations before approaching a manager or answering a customer complaint,
terrified that he would say the wrong thing or appear visibly distressed. He tried hard not to think
about the tornado at all, suppressing the memories whenever they surfaced, which had the effect of
keeping him in a state of constant low-level vigilance, braced against the next intrusion.

The suppression did not work. The memories returned with the same intensity, and each time they did,
the terror they carried felt like fresh evidence that he was right — that the damage was real and
permanent. The more the memories confirmed his worst fears about himself, the more rigidly he
applied his avoidance strategies; and the more he avoided, the more his world contracted, and the
more miserable and unrecognisable he became to himself. His social life had effectively ceased. He
had stopped responding to messages from friends he had known since high school. His mother had
commented that he seemed like a different person. He agreed with her, privately, and found no
comfort in the agreement.

What finally brought Caleb to seek help was a shift at work during which a sudden thunderstorm sent
him into the walk-in freezer to avoid the sound of the rain on the metal roof — and a coworker found
him there, standing alone in the cold, unable to explain himself. He was ashamed, but more than
that, he was frightened. What he most feared, he said quietly at the end of the intake session, was
that this was simply who he was now. What he most hoped was that someone could tell him otherwise.
