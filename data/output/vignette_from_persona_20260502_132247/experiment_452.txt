Aleksander Nowak is a 25-year-old Polish-American salesperson currently living with his partner in a
mid-sized Midwestern city. He was referred for psychological assessment following a period of
mounting distress that had begun to affect both his relationship and his performance at work. His
PCL-5 score of 76 places him well within the severe range, and his presentation centres on a
persistent, consuming sense of threat that has reorganised much of his inner life since his time as
a civilian caught in an active war zone approximately eighteen months ago.

Aleksander had been visiting extended family in Eastern Europe when the conflict escalated with
little warning. What had begun as a tense but seemingly manageable political situation deteriorated
over the course of several days into sustained shelling, street-level violence, and the collapse of
basic infrastructure in the area where he was staying. He witnessed destruction at close range —
burning buildings, the bodies of civilians, the sound of ordnance close enough to feel in the chest
— and spent nearly two weeks sheltering in a basement with his relatives before a humanitarian
corridor allowed them to evacuate. He returned to the United States physically unharmed, but
something in him had not come back the same way.

In the months that followed, Aleksander found that the world no longer felt like a place where
safety could be assumed. He had come to believe, in a way he could not fully articulate but could
not shake, that catastrophe was not an exception but a permanent background condition — that the
basement in Eastern Europe had simply shown him what was always true. This conviction settled into
his nervous system rather than his reasoning mind, which made it resistant to reassurance. His
partner would point out that they were safe, that the apartment was secure, and Aleksander would
agree intellectually while his body continued its quiet, exhausting argument to the contrary.

The sense of threat intensifies markedly after dark. Nights have become a separate country with
different rules. Aleksander lies awake long after his partner has fallen asleep, listening — to
traffic outside, to the building settling, to sounds he cannot immediately identify. He does not
frame this to himself as irrational; it feels, rather, like the only honest response to what he now
knows. During the day, the ordinariness of his environment offers some counterweight. At night, that
counterweight disappears, and the vigilance that kept him alive in that basement reasserts itself as
the dominant mode.

This carries into his working life in ways that have become increasingly difficult to manage.
Aleksander works in inside sales, spending long hours on the phone and at a desk in an open-plan
office. The concentration required to track client conversations, manage his pipeline, and meet
targets has become genuinely difficult to sustain. He finds his attention drifting — not toward
distraction in the ordinary sense, but toward a kind of low-level scanning, as though part of his
mind refuses to fully commit to the task in front of him and insists on remaining available for
something else. His supervisor has noticed. There have been two conversations about his numbers in
the past quarter, and Aleksander has not been able to explain what is happening without risking a
disclosure he is not ready to make.

Alongside the vigilance and the difficulty concentrating, Aleksander carries an emotional weight
that is harder to name precisely. Strong feelings move through him unpredictably — fear that arrives
without a clear object, moments of anger that feel disproportionate to whatever prompted them, and a
low current of shame that he associates with having left while others stayed. These emotions do not
follow a logic he can track or prepare for.

What finally brought him to seek help was a conversation with his partner, who told him quietly that
she was frightened — not of him, but for him, and for what was happening to them as a couple. He had
not realised how visible his state had become to someone who loved him. He arrived at the intake
appointment hoping, above everything else, to feel safe again — and most afraid that he no longer
remembered what that had felt like.
