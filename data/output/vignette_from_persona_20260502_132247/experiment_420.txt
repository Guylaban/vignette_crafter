Marcus is a 57-year-old single man of North American background who works as a private tutor, a role
that has brought him into close contact with students and parents in domestic and semi-domestic
settings for the better part of two decades. He presented for psychological assessment following a
sexual assault that occurred approximately fourteen months ago, when he was overpowered and
assaulted by a male acquaintance in a private residence. During the assault, his arms were
restrained and he was physically forced into compliance, unable to move or call out. The experience
of being held down — his body controlled entirely by another man's strength — lasted long enough
that he became acutely aware of every sensation: the pressure of hands on his shoulders, the weight
bearing down, the complete removal of any choice about what happened to his own body. He described
the event, haltingly and with long pauses, as something he had not yet found adequate words for. He
knows what happened. He simply cannot locate the language.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus noticed that his memories of the assault were not fading in the
way he had expected them to. They were, if anything, sharper — vivid and detailed in ways that felt
almost punitive, arriving without invitation when a student's parent placed a hand briefly on his
shoulder in greeting, or when a male colleague walked into the room and closed the door behind him.
These sensory triggers — touch, the proximity of men, any moment in which his freedom of movement
felt subtly constrained — would pull the memory forward with an immediacy that collapsed the
distance between then and now. Yet the memory also arrived with gaps, sections he could not fully
reconstruct, which paradoxically amplified his distress: the incomplete picture left his mind
searching, rehearsing, trying to fill in what was missing, and in doing so returning again and again
to the fragments that were most vivid and most disturbing.

The memories began to shape how Marcus understood himself and the world around him. He had come to
believe that his body was fundamentally unreliable — it had failed to protect him, had been used
against his will, and could not be trusted to signal safety accurately. He felt profoundly isolated,
as though the assault had installed a glass wall between him and other people, one invisible to them
but impermeable to him. And he had arrived at a settled conviction that danger was not exceptional
but ambient: you never really knew what could happen next, and the only reasonable response was
perpetual alertness. These appraisals — of a treacherous body, a separating isolation, and a world
requiring constant vigilance — fed directly back into how threatening ordinary situations felt, and
kept the memory close to the surface, never fully receding because his mind interpreted even neutral
cues as potentially confirming what he now believed to be true.

This heightened sense of threat had become the organizing principle of Marcus's daily life. He
scanned other people constantly — in waiting rooms, in the homes of students' families, anywhere he
could not immediately assess the exits — watching for microexpressions, shifts in posture, any sign
that someone nearby might mean him harm. He chose his seat in every room according to how protected
it made him feel: back to the wall, facing the door. He avoided unfamiliar places entirely if he
could manage it, because an environment he hadn't mapped could not be assessed for danger. This
vigilance, exhausting as it was, felt not like a symptom but like rational self-protection — and it
kept him in a state of arousal that made the memories more accessible and the triggers more potent.

Over the months, Marcus's strategies for managing all of this had narrowed his life considerably. He
had stopped accepting new tutoring clients whose homes he hadn't visited before. He refused any form
of physical contact — handshakes, incidental brushing in a hallway — and had begun scheduling
sessions only with female students and their mothers present, quietly restructuring his professional
life around the avoidance of being alone with men. He smoked more than he ever had, using cigarettes
to blunt the edge of emotional surges he could not otherwise tolerate. He avoided situations likely
to produce strong feelings of any kind. One long-standing tutoring family — a father and his teenage
son he had worked with for three years — eventually ended the arrangement after Marcus cancelled
four consecutive sessions without adequate explanation.

What finally brought him to seek help was a session last month in which a teenage male student,
frustrated during a lesson, slammed a textbook on the table. The sudden noise and the flash of the
boy's physical force — however minor — sent Marcus into a state of such acute distress that he ended
the session early and sat in his car for forty minutes unable to drive. He came to the intake
appointment hoping, above all, to be able to work again without dread. What he feared most, he said
quietly, was that the person he had been before the assault — competent, calm, trusted by families —
was simply gone.
