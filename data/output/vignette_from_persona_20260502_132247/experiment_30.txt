Viktor Hale is a 56-year-old married musician of European descent who presents with a PCL-5 score of
71 following a sexual assault that occurred approximately eighteen months ago. He was assaulted by a
male authority figure — a well-established producer who had invited him to a private studio session
under professional pretenses. The assault took place in a confined space, and Viktor recalls with
terrible clarity the man's physical weight, the sight of blood from where his hand struck a surface,
and the overwhelming presence of a particular cologne — heavy, cedar-based — that the producer wore.
What he cannot reconstruct is the sequence of events that connected those fragments: there are
stretches of the encounter that remain entirely blank to him, and he has never been able to tell
himself a coherent story of what happened that night. He knows what he knows in pieces, and the
pieces do not form a whole.

In the months that followed, Viktor found that certain sensory moments could collapse the distance
between then and now without warning. The sight of any physical altercation — even a shove between
strangers on the street — would drop him immediately into a state of raw vigilance. Blood,
encountered during a minor kitchen accident or glimpsed on a television screen, produced the same
effect. Most destabilising of all was scent: on one occasion a fellow musician at a rehearsal wore a
cologne with a similar cedar note, and Viktor left the room without explanation and did not return.
These triggers fed directly into a sustained state of threat — his nervous system, already primed,
would escalate rapidly into irritability, angry outbursts that frightened him as much as those
around him, and a hypervigilance to sudden movements by others that made ensemble playing feel
genuinely dangerous.

The fragmented, incoherent quality of his memory has become one of the central engines of his
distress. Because he cannot narrate what happened — even privately, even to himself — he cannot
locate the danger in the past. The memory exists not as a story with a beginning and an end but as a
set of live sensory charges, and this means that his triggers carry the full weight of the original
threat rather than the weight of something remembered. The incompleteness of the memory also drives
the threat appraisal directly: Viktor is certain, in a way he cannot fully articulate, that what he
cannot remember is worse than what he can, and this certainty keeps his body in a permanent state of
preparation for something unspecified and therefore inescapable.

In response to this sustained threat, Viktor began organising his life around containment. He
identified the places where he felt safe — his home studio, the back garden, one particular corner
of a café two blocks from his house — and he has not meaningfully left that geography in months. He
stopped attending the open sessions and informal gatherings that had been the social fabric of his
professional life, avoiding the musicians, producers, and industry contacts he had known before the
assault. He has never allowed himself to feel or express anger, suppressing it the moment it rises,
convinced that if he lets it surface he will become something he cannot control. He avoids any
situation involving authority figures — managers, venue owners, anyone in a position of
institutional power — which has effectively ended his ability to negotiate his own career. His wife
has noticed that he is no longer the person she married, and the distance between them has grown in
proportion to the territory he has ceded to avoidance.

What is particularly insidious is the way his strategies have closed off the possibility of
resolution. By pushing the memory away each time it begins to surface before it can complete itself,
Viktor ensures that the fragments remain raw and unprocessed. The suppression of anger and the
avoidance of social contact mean that he never encounters the triggers in a context that might allow
them to lose their charge; instead, when they do break through — a sudden movement in his peripheral
vision, a scent on the air — they arrive with full force and confirm his belief that nowhere, and no
one, is safe.

What finally brought Viktor to seek help was a session he had been trying to complete for weeks: a
recording he had promised a close friend, done in his own home studio, that he has been unable to
finish because he cannot remain in the room long enough. He is most afraid that whatever he cannot
remember will eventually surface and destroy him. What he is hoping for, more quietly, is that
someone might help him find a way to carry this that does not require surrendering everything he has
built.
