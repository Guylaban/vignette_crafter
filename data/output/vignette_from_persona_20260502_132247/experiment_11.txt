Miriam is a 43-year-old European-American chef who has worked the line at a mid-sized restaurant in
Portland for over a decade. She came to the clinic after her partner insisted she speak to someone,
telling her that the woman she had been before was barely recognisable anymore. Her PCL-5 score of
80 places her presentation in the severe range, and the clinical picture that emerged across intake
and early sessions is one of interlocking cycles that have quietly dismantled her life over the past
two years.

Approximately twenty-six months ago, Miriam was admitted to the intensive care unit following what
began as what she dismissed as fatigue and a mild fever. Within forty-eight hours she was in septic
shock, intubated, and her medical team was telling her partner that the next twelve hours would be
decisive. Miriam later learned she had been sedated for nine days. What she carries from that period
is not a clean narrative but a series of overwhelming sensory fragments — the cold weight of the
ventilator tube she felt herself pulling at during a moment of partial consciousness, the particular
antiseptic smell of the unit that still reaches her before any visual memory does, the sound of an
alarm she could not locate or silence. The memory is so extreme, she has said more than once, that
it is genuinely hard to believe it happened to her own body — it carries the quality of something
witnessed rather than survived. And yet when it returns, it returns completely: not as image alone
but as smell, weight, cold, and a grief so total it leaves her unable to move for minutes at a time.

It is that grief on recall, and the physical totality of the reliving, that first pushed Miriam
toward the belief that something in her had been permanently altered. She had come to believe, with
a quiet but unshakeable certainty, that her body had been damaged in ways that medicine had not
fully measured — that the organ that had nearly failed her would fail her again, and that the
emotions she had felt before the illness were simply no longer available to her. These appraisals —
of physical vulnerability, of lost emotional life — became the lens through which she interpreted
everything that followed. Because she understood her body as fundamentally unreliable, she read
every moment of physiological arousal as confirmation of ongoing danger, and this kept her nervous
system in a state of near-constant alert. She cannot relax even in settings she knows to be safe;
her body, she says, simply does not receive the message. And because she had concluded she would
never feel normal emotions again, she stopped trying — gradually withdrawing from situations that
might produce hope or pleasure, as though protecting herself from the disappointment of feeling
nothing in response to them.

In the kitchen, where Miriam once found genuine satisfaction, the high-heat environment and the
sudden shriek of a timer or a dropped pan now trigger an immediate, full-body startle — her heart
rate surging before she has consciously registered the sound. Her body reacts before her mind has
time to catch up, and in those moments the ICU is briefly, viscerally present. These intrusions pull
the memory back with sensory force, and the grief that accompanies it is so destabilising that she
has reorganised her working life around preventing them. She leaves service early when she can,
delegates tasks that require sustained attention, and has told no one at the restaurant why. The
colleagues who once knew her as exacting and warm now find her remote.

At home, Miriam stays up until two or three in the morning — not because she is not exhausted, but
because sleep feels like a surrender of vigilance. Sleeping means her body might do something she
cannot monitor. Over months, this has deepened her exhaustion and narrowed her emotional range
further, which in turn has reinforced her conviction that she is no longer capable of feeling, and
that her body remains a source of threat rather than a home. The numbing that began as a way of
managing unbearable appraisals has itself become evidence for them. The loop, though she would not
name it that way, is one she feels closing around her.

What finally brought Miriam in was a moment at dinner when her partner laughed at something on
television and she felt, she said, absolutely nothing — and realised she had been waiting for that
feeling to come back for two years. What she is most afraid of is that it never will. What she is
most hoping for, tentatively, is to be told that her body is not her enemy.
