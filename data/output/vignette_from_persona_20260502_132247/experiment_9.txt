Marcus had always been someone who prided himself on control — over his systems, his schedules, his
carefully ordered life. At thirty-four, he was a competent database administrator at a mid-sized
logistics firm in Columbus, Ohio, the kind of man who kept redundant backups and never missed a
deadline. Then, during a routine afternoon at his desk, he felt a tightening across his chest that
he initially dismissed as stress. By evening he could not draw a full breath. His wife drove him to
the emergency room, where the next several hours dissolved into a blur of oxygen masks, IV lines
threaded into his arm, the cold press of electrodes, and a physician's careful voice explaining that
a pulmonary embolism had nearly killed him. He lay in that hospital bed for four days, watching
nurses approach with syringes, listening to the rhythmic beeping of monitors, and feeling — for the
first time in his adult life — that his own body was a place he could not trust.

In the months that followed, Marcus's recovery was technically successful. Medically, he was
cleared. Psychologically, something had not resolved. His body, he had come to believe, was still in
the grip of that emergency room — still braced for the moment the air would stop coming. He noticed
this most acutely at his workstation, where long hours of sedentary concentration would sometimes
produce a faint pressure in his chest. Immediately his attention would narrow onto his own
breathing, and within seconds what had been a mild sensation would become a terrifying one: his
chest tight, his heart loud, his breath short and ragged. These episodes left him shaking, convinced
each time that the embolism had returned. His body felt permanently tensed, as though standing
sentry against a threat that might announce itself at any moment through a skipped heartbeat or an
unusual tightness in his lungs. The fear, the horror, the physical alarm — these were not occasional
visitors. They were the background hum of his daily existence.

It was from within this state of chronic physiological vigilance that Marcus began building his own
system of management — one that made complete sense to him even as it quietly dismantled his life.
He stopped exercising entirely. Running, he reasoned, would accelerate his heart and constrict his
breathing, and he could not afford to confuse those sensations with another clot. He began
controlling his food intake with rigid precision, eating small, carefully measured portions, because
he had decided that weight and exertion placed demands on a cardiovascular system he no longer
trusted. He cancelled a follow-up cardiology appointment, then another, and eventually stopped
engaging with any medical setting altogether — no examinations, no blood draws, no waiting rooms
with their antiseptic smell and the soft squeak of nurses' shoes. These strategies felt like
protection. What he did not see, for a long time, was that they were also a cage.

Because his avoidance was so thorough, Marcus never had the opportunity to learn that his body could
tolerate movement, that a racing heart from a brisk walk was not a harbinger of catastrophe.
Instead, the absence of any corrective experience left his nervous system exactly where it had been
the night of the embolism. And the avoidance itself became a source of triggers: on the rare
occasions when something slipped through — a colleague mentioning a hospital visit, a needle
glimpsed in a television drama, a moment of breathlessness when he climbed the stairs too quickly —
the reaction was immediate and overwhelming. His body flooded with the same alarm it had felt in the
emergency room, which only confirmed his belief that the danger was real and ongoing, and that the
avoidance had to be maintained, even intensified. His restricted eating and sedentary routine, meant
to protect him, kept his threat system perpetually primed.

His divorce was finalised eight months ago. His ex-wife had grown exhausted by the restrictions that
had gradually colonised their shared life. Marcus came to his first session quiet and precise,
describing his symptoms the way he might describe a corrupted database — methodically, with a faint
undertone of shame. What finally brought him was a performance review at work, where his manager had
noted increasing withdrawal from the team. What he feared most was that he had already passed the
point of return. What he hoped, though he said it carefully, was that he had not.
