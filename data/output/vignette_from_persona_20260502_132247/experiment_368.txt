Kwame is a 72-year-old African American man who served for decades with the United States Coast
Guard and who presented for psychological assessment following a period of escalating distress that
his wife of forty-one years described as having "stolen the man I knew." He is a broad-shouldered,
deliberate speaker who chose his words carefully during intake, pausing often, as though testing the
ground before each step. His PCL-5 score of 76 places him in the severe range, and the picture that
emerged across the first several sessions was one of a man whose inner life has been reorganised
almost entirely around a single catastrophic afternoon.

Approximately three years ago, Kwame was part of a Coast Guard response team dispatched to a
commercial vessel that had reported a chemical emergency in a coastal industrial channel. What began
as a routine boarding became something else within minutes. The hold of the vessel contained
ruptured drums of an industrial solvent, and the air inside was thick and acrid in a way that
registered as wrong before anyone had named it. Kwame recalled the silence first — the eerie absence
of the normal sounds of a working ship, no engine hum, no voices from below — and then the burning
in his throat, the sudden weight in his chest, the way his vision began to blur at the edges. Two of
his crew members collapsed before anyone could reach them. He was pulled from the hold by a
colleague and evacuated by helicopter, semi-conscious. He spent eleven days in hospital, and the two
men who had gone below before him did not survive.

In the months that followed, Kwame's memories of that afternoon refused to remain in the past. They
returned not as vague impressions but with a photographic precision that disturbed him — he could
see the exact pattern of condensation on the drum nearest to him, the particular angle of the light
through a porthole, as though his mind had taken still images and then strung them into a film that
ran on a loop without his permission. When the memory surfaced, it arrived with such emotional force
that he would have to stop whatever he was doing — put down his coffee, leave the room, grip the
edge of a counter — because the feeling of being overwhelmed was total and immediate. These
intrusive replays began to reshape how Kwame understood himself and his world. He had come to
believe, with quiet certainty, that he could not manage even minor disturbances — a raised voice on
television, a neighbour's car alarm, anything unexpected — because the memory had shown him what he
was capable of feeling, and he was certain he could not survive that feeling again. The world, he
told his clinician, was not the place he had once thought it was; it was a place where invisible
things could kill you before you understood what was happening.

This conviction — that danger is always present and always silent — has become the lens through
which Kwame reads his daily environment. He remains on active administrative duty with the Coast
Guard, and it is at work, reviewing incident logs and sitting in briefings about maritime hazards,
that the threat monitoring becomes most acute. A room that grows suddenly quiet, a pause in a
colleague's sentence, the particular stillness before someone delivers bad news — these silences
carry the same sensory signature as the hold of that vessel, and his body responds accordingly:
heart rate climbing, jaw tightening, a metallic taste at the back of his throat. This physical
reactivity then drags the memory forward again, the film resuming mid-frame, and the cycle closes
around him. Even at home, where his wife has worked to create calm, Kwame cannot fully lower his
guard. He moves through his own house as though something may yet be found in it.

He has not spoken to his wife about the content of the memories, partly because he believes that
engaging with them directly — even in words — will breach some internal containment he is barely
maintaining. What frightened him most, he admitted in their third session, was not the memory itself
but what it proved: that the world offers no real warning, and that he, a man who had spent a career
reading the sea, had not seen it coming.

It was his wife who finally made the appointment. Kwame came because he was tired of standing guard
inside his own life, and because some part of him, however small, was hoping to be told there was
still a way back.
