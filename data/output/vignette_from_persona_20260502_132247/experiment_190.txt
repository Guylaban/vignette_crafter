Rangi Tane is a 74-year-old Māori man, divorced, who works as a freelance editor from his apartment
in Portland. He came to the clinic after scoring 77 on the PCL-5, referred by his GP following a
series of appointments in which he had difficulty explaining what was wrong. He described himself,
with careful understatement, as "not coping well."

The abuse was perpetrated by his former partner over a period of several years, ending approximately
eighteen months ago when Rangi finally left. The physical violence was rarely random; it was almost
always preceded by criticism — a contemptuous correction of something Rangi had said at dinner, a
dismissive remark about his work, a cold inventory of his failings delivered in a measured voice
before the first blow. The pattern had been so consistent that correction and violence became fused
in his nervous system. By the time he left, he had come to understand himself as someone who invited
harm — not through anything he had done, but through what he fundamentally was. He was certain, in a
way that felt like fact rather than belief, that the event had happened to him because of the sort
of person he is.

His memory of the worst incidents does not come back as narrative. Instead, it arrives as a series
of still images — a raised arm caught in lamplight, the corner of a kitchen counter, his own hands
flat on a table — each one vivid and isolated, with no before or after. These snapshots feel, he
says, as though they happened to someone else entirely, as though he is looking at photographs of a
stranger. Yet when he tries to examine them, what comes is not grief or fear but a surge of intense
rage that frightens him almost as much as the original events. This rage, rising unbidden from the
images, has itself become a trigger: the anger feels like evidence that something is broken in him,
and the broken feeling sends him back into the images, cycling through the same frozen frames.

Rangi's editorial work requires him to receive and give corrections daily — to mark manuscripts, to
respond to queries from authors, to accept tracked changes on his own writing. Each act of being
corrected, however gentle, lands in his body as something closer to threat than information. When a
junior colleague emailed him last spring with a politely worded note about a formatting
inconsistency, Rangi found himself unable to respond for three days. The email had pulled up the
snapshots, and the snapshots had pulled up the rage, and the rage had confirmed, once again, that he
was not a person who could be trusted to feel things normally. Any scene involving violence — a news
segment, a film, a raised voice in the corridor outside his apartment — produces the same
telescoping effect: the present moment collapses into the frozen images, and the images bring the
rage, and the rage must be managed.

Managing it, for Rangi, has meant constructing a life with as few sharp edges as possible. He
distracts himself compulsively — with podcasts running through every waking hour, with crossword
puzzles stacked beside his bed — and he avoids anything that carries the texture of his former
partner: a particular cologne, certain restaurants, the neighbourhood where they lived. Most
consequentially, he has learned to suppress any strong emotion before it can fully form, on the
grounds that feeling too much leads to the rage, and the rage leads back into the memories. But this
suppression has had a cost he did not anticipate. By flattening his emotional life, he has
inadvertently kept the memories close — they surface more readily now, as though the absence of
ordinary feeling creates a vacuum the images rush to fill. And the avoidance itself has narrowed his
world so completely that he rarely encounters anything that might contradict his conviction that he
is incapable of normal feeling. The belief that he will never feel emotions the way other people do
has become self-confirming: the more he avoids, the more alien his inner life becomes, and the more
alien it becomes, the more certain he is that the abuse revealed something true and permanent about
him.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a moment at work. An author whose manuscript he had
edited sent back his corrections with a single dismissive line: "I don't think you understood what I
was trying to do." Rangi sat at his desk for an hour, unable to move, certain that the author was
right — not about the editing, but about everything.

He is most afraid that the rage will never leave. He is most hoping to feel like a person again.
