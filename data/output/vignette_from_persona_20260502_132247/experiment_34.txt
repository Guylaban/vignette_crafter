Raymond is a 68-year-old married school counselor who has spent the better part of four decades
helping adolescents navigate crisis. He presents now as someone in crisis himself, arriving at the
intake appointment with the careful composure of a man who has spent years teaching others to name
their feelings while remaining, in his own estimation, entirely unable to do so. His PCL-5 score of
62 places him in the severe range, and the clinical picture that emerges across the first few
sessions is one of a man whose professional instincts toward connection have been systematically
dismantled by what happened to him fourteen months ago.

The assault occurred late on a Friday evening in the parking lot of a bar Raymond had visited with a
former colleague. He had not been drinking heavily — a detail he returns to with some bitterness —
but the man who attacked him had been. Raymond remembers the smell before anything else: a sharp,
sour wave of alcohol on the man's breath as he was shoved against a car door, then struck
repeatedly. At some point he lost consciousness. He came around in an emergency room, the antiseptic
sting of the air hitting him before he could open his eyes, a nurse leaning close and speaking in
the careful, lowered voice people use when they are trying not to frighten you. He was badly
concussed, had two broken ribs, and remained in hospital overnight. His wife held his hand. He does
not remember much of that part.

What Raymond does remember, he remembers in pieces. He has described the assault not as a continuous
narrative but as a sequence of still photographs: the yellow light of the parking lot, the man's
face at close range, the specific angle of a fist. Each image arrives complete and discrete, and
between them there is nothing — just the sensation of one frame cutting to the next. When a fragment
surfaces without warning, as they frequently do, it carries with it the full physiological terror of
the original moment: his heart rate spikes, his chest tightens, and for several seconds he is
entirely certain he is about to be killed. The memory does not feel like recollection. It feels like
recurrence.

The triggers that reliably summon these intrusions have shaped Raymond's daily life in ways that
have become increasingly difficult to conceal. The smell of alcohol — encountered at faculty
gatherings, at the occasional dinner where wine is poured nearby — sends a fragment crashing forward
without warning, and Raymond has responded by withdrawing from most social occasions. He stopped
attending the monthly staff dinners at his school, offering scheduling conflicts as explanation.
More consequentially, the antiseptic smell of the school nurse's office, which sits two doors from
his own, has become nearly intolerable; he now routes himself through a longer corridor to avoid
passing it, and a colleague has noticed and asked, with genuine concern, whether he is all right.
What troubles Raymond most acutely, however, is the trigger he finds hardest to explain: emotional
closeness itself. When a student leans in to confide something painful, or when his wife reaches for
him in the particular way she has when she senses he is struggling, something in him recoils. The
proximity — the lowered voice, the attentive face — maps onto something in the assault he has not
yet been able to articulate, and the memory surfaces.

In response, Raymond has developed a set of strategies that he recognises, with the rueful clarity
of a trained counselor, as counterproductive. He avoids people where he can manage it, keeping
sessions with students brief and professionally bounded. He has pulled back from his wife in ways
that have produced a new and painful distance between them. And when avoidance is not possible —
when someone gets emotionally close in a moment he cannot exit — he has learned to dissociate
deliberately, to blank out behind attentive eyes, to be present in body while retreating entirely
inward. The trouble is that this deliberate withdrawal keeps him in a state of vigilant watchfulness
for the very cues he is trying to escape, so that the triggers remain perpetually salient,
perpetually capable of pulling the next fragment forward.

What finally brought Raymond to seek help was a session three weeks ago in which a student described
being attacked, and Raymond sat across from him, blank and unreachable, aware that he was failing
the boy entirely. He came to his first appointment hoping, above all, to be able to do his job
again. What he fears most is that the person who was good at it is gone.
