Liam Hartley is a twenty-one-year-old European-American man who works for a faith-based social
ministry organisation and has been married for just under a year. He presented for psychological
assessment reporting a PCL-5 score of 47, following a deployment to a conflict zone in the Middle
East where he served as a relief worker distributing humanitarian aid in an active war zone. What he
encountered there has not left him.

Approximately eight months before seeking help, Liam was part of a small convoy delivering supplies
to a displaced-persons settlement when the convoy was stopped at an improvised checkpoint. Armed men
surrounded the vehicles, shouting commands in Arabic, and one of them pulled Liam from the cab by
his collar and held a weapon against his face while screaming inches from him. The episode lasted
perhaps four minutes, though Liam has never been able to account for the time reliably. The convoy
was eventually waved through, and no one was physically harmed. What Liam was left with, however,
was the absolute conviction, held in his body long after the mind had processed the facts, that he
had been seconds from death.

In the months that followed, Liam found that the memory resisted ordinary containment. It does not
feel to him like something that is over. When it surfaces — and it surfaces often — it carries the
same terror he felt pressed against that door, so complete and physical that his hands shake and his
chest tightens as though the weapon is still there. The memory is so extreme, he has told his
therapist, that he sometimes struggles to believe it actually happened to him, and yet it feels more
present than anything in his current life. This quality of felt-present-ness means that certain
ordinary moments become dangerous. When colleagues at the ministry raise military topics in
conversation — discussing foreign policy, veterans' services, the news from overseas — the memory
surges forward, not as recollection but as re-experience, and with it comes the same overwhelming
terror. Hearing shouting in Arabic, even at a distance in a crowded neighbourhood, stops Liam
entirely. Even talking about the event in session requires careful pacing, because the act of
narrating it can itself become a trigger that floods him back into the checkpoint.

Because the memory arrives with such force, Liam has come to monitor his environment with exhausting
vigilance. He is certain that strangers approaching him in ordinary situations represent a genuine
threat — a man walking toward him on the pavement outside the ministry building, a client entering
the waiting room too quickly — and sudden movements by others put him immediately on guard in a way
he cannot override by reasoning. He wakes repeatedly during the night to check whether he is safe,
scanning the room before he can return to anything resembling sleep. This hypervigilance is not
merely reactive; it actively keeps him close to the material he is trying to escape, because a body
in permanent threat-detection is a body that keeps finding things that confirm the danger. The state
of alert itself resurfaces the memory, and the memory sustains the alert.

To manage this, Liam has built a set of strategies that have, over months, made things considerably
worse. He stays up until very late each night — two, sometimes three in the morning — because he has
learned that sleep brings the memory back in forms he cannot control, and exhaustion at least blunts
the edges. But the sleep deprivation that results keeps his nervous system raw, which means the
memory, when it does break through, hits harder. He ruminates at length about how the checkpoint
incident could have been prevented: a different route, an earlier departure, a different decision at
headquarters. This prevention-focused rumination does not resolve anything; it keeps the event
perpetually alive and rehearsed. He also avoids situations that might make him feel happy or hopeful
— social gatherings with his wife's family, small celebrations at the ministry — because he has come
to believe, without quite articulating it, that allowing himself any positive feeling would be a
kind of betrayal of what he knows the world to actually be.

What finally brought Liam to seek help was his wife. She told him, quietly and without accusation,
that she felt she had lost him. He is most afraid that she is right. He is most hoping, tentatively,
that she is wrong.
