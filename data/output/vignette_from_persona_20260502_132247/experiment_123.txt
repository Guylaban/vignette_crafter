Arjun is a 32-year-old South Asian man who works as a housekeeper at a mid-sized hotel in the city.
He has been married for four years and, until approximately eight months ago, described his life as
quiet and manageable. He presented for assessment with a PCL-5 score of 61, reporting significant
distress that he said had been building since a mugging he experienced on his way home from a late
shift.

The incident occurred on a winter evening, shortly after eleven o'clock. Arjun had just exited the
hotel's service entrance, still wearing his work uniform, when two men approached him from a
recessed doorway. One pressed close behind him while the other demanded his wallet and phone. When
Arjun hesitated — not out of defiance, he later explained, but out of sheer incomprehension that
this was actually happening — the man in front struck him twice across the ribs and once across the
face. He remembered the cold of the pavement through his knees when he fell, the particular smell of
the alley, and the sound of footsteps retreating. What stayed with him most viscerally, however, was
the moment just before the first blow: the realisation that the man smiling at him had never
intended anything but harm.

In the weeks that followed, the memory returned with a quality that Arjun struggled to articulate.
He knew what had happened — the sequence was clear in his mind — but when he tried to describe it to
his wife or later to the intake clinician, he found himself stopping mid-sentence, reaching for
words that would not come, as though the experience existed in a register that language could not
access. The memories themselves were not vague. They were, if anything, too vivid: the yellow light
of the streetlamp, the exact angle of the man's jaw, the pressure against his ribs. And when they
returned — uninvited, during the ordinary course of his day — Arjun reported feeling physical pain
in his side and face, the same sites where the blows had landed. He was not metaphorically reliving
the event; his body appeared to be re-enacting it.

These intrusive returns of the memory fed directly into a darkening interpretation of the world
around him. The men had looked ordinary. They had approached like strangers asking for directions.
This became, for Arjun, the central lesson of the event: people are not what they seem. He had come
to believe, with a certainty that felt less like a conclusion than a discovered fact, that trust was
a form of naivety he could no longer afford. The world, he was now sure, was dangerous in a way he
had simply failed to appreciate before. Every time the memory surged back — in the hotel corridor,
in the car park, on his walk to the bus — it confirmed and deepened this conviction, as though each
intrusion were fresh evidence for a case already closed.

The relationship ran in both directions. When Arjun moved through environments that reminded him,
even faintly, of that night — a dimly lit service hallway, the smell of cold air when he took out
the bins, a stranger lingering near the hotel's side entrance — his hypervigilance spiked and his
appraisals of threat sharpened. These moments of perceived danger then pulled the memory forward
with renewed force, the physical pain briefly returning in his side, the image of the man's
expression arriving unbidden. The loop had become self-sustaining: his belief that the world was
dangerous made the memory more accessible, and the memory's return made the belief feel more
irrefutable.

At work, the consequences had become impossible to ignore. Arjun had begun refusing to clean the
rooms on the ground floor that faced the alley, telling his supervisor the assignment had been
covered. He avoided the service entrance entirely, adding nearly twenty minutes to his commute by
using the main lobby. His supervisor had noticed and commented. His wife had noticed the silence at
dinner, the way he sat with his back to the wall in their own kitchen.

What finally brought him to seek help was not a single event but an accumulation: the exhaustion of
vigilance, the loneliness of being unable to explain what he carried, and a quiet fear that the man
he was becoming — watchful, closed, unable to trust even familiar faces — was not a man he
recognised.
