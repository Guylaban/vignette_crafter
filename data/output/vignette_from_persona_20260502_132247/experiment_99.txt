Mere Tuilagi is a 71-year-old Fijian-American woman who has served as a police officer for nearly
three decades, a career she chose, she has always said, because she believed in protecting people.
It is that belief — and what she now sees as its catastrophic failure — that brought her to the
clinic eighteen months after the night her son was struck by a drunk driver outside a bar in the
city where she works her beat.

The accident happened on a Friday evening in late autumn. Mere had been off duty and had driven her
son, Daniel, to meet friends, dropping him at the curb and telling him she would be back by eleven.
When she returned, she turned the corner to find his body on the road, a crumpled car against a
lamppost, and the smell — sharp, unmistakable — of alcohol already thick in the cold air. She
remembers the sound of the engine still running, a radio playing something she could not name, and
Daniel's voice, thin and frightened, calling her name before the paramedics arrived. She knelt
beside him on the asphalt and could do nothing. He survived, but he sustained a traumatic brain
injury that has left him with lasting cognitive impairment, and Mere has not forgiven herself for
any of it.

Her memories of that night are extraordinarily vivid and sensory. She reports that they arrive as
clear, detailed pictures — the angle of the crumpled hood, the spread of broken glass catching the
streetlight — and that layered beneath the images she can hear the engine, the radio, Daniel's
voice. These memories do not feel like recollections; they feel like re-entry. And because they are
so fully formed and so immediate, they have made the world around her into a minefield of re-entry
points. Working patrol, Mere encounters the triggers that map directly back to that night with a
frequency that would be almost impossible to avoid in any other occupation. A car with a damaged
front end in a collision report, the smell of alcohol drifting from a bar she passes on a welfare
check, a colleague in the break room describing a traffic fatality — each of these sensory or
contextual details acts as a doorway. The memories flood back through it: the images, the sounds,
the cold of the pavement. Her nervous system does not distinguish between then and now.

What makes this particularly crushing is what follows each intrusion. The moment a trigger pulls her
back into that night, Mere is also pulled back into the conclusions she drew there. She had come to
believe, with the certainty of someone who has examined the evidence exhaustively, that she caused
this. She dropped him at that curb. She chose that time, that corner. She was a police officer —
someone trained to read danger, to act — and she had driven away. The thought that she should have
protected him and failed is not a fleeting worry; it is a verdict she returns to every time the
memories surface. The smell of alcohol on a suspect's clothing during a routine stop is enough to
send her, within seconds, from professional composure to the silent conviction that she is the
reason her son will never fully recover. Alongside this she carries a broader helplessness — a sense
that no matter what she does, she cannot prevent harm from reaching the people she loves — which has
quietly eroded the professional identity that once anchored her.

Over the months since the accident, Mere began rerouting her patrol to avoid the street where it
happened, declined to attend the departmental debrief on a fatal traffic collision, and stopped
joining colleagues when conversations turned to accident calls. Her husband has noticed that she
leaves the room when the television carries news of road accidents. These adjustments accumulated
without her quite deciding on them, until her sergeant, a man she trusts, told her gently that she
seemed to be disappearing. It was that conversation, more than anything, that brought her in. What
she fears most is that she will be told she is right — that she is responsible, that she failed —
and what she hopes, though she can barely articulate it, is that someone will help her understand
how to live with what she remembers without it destroying what remains of who she is.
