Rajan is a 75-year-old South Asian man who came to the United States as a refugee decades ago,
carrying with him a history that had never fully resolved into the past. He is married, soft-spoken,
and has spent the better part of his adult life in America as a potter — a craft he brought from his
homeland, one that once gave his days both structure and meaning. He presented for treatment with a
PCL-5 score of 50, describing a constellation of symptoms that had worsened quietly over recent
years until they could no longer be managed in silence.

The events that fractured Rajan's sense of safety occurred during the violence and forced
displacement of his early life. He witnessed the deaths of people he loved — bodies carried past
him, makeshift burials in disturbed earth, the sounds of vehicles and shouting that preceded raids
on his village. Police and soldiers moved through his community with authority that meant terror
rather than protection. The chaos was not a single event but an accumulated season of loss, and much
of what Rajan experienced during that time has never organized itself into a coherent narrative. He
reports that his memories of the period come in disconnected pieces that do not fit together —
sensory fragments, images without context, a feeling of cold ground beneath his feet that arrives
without any story attached. What he experienced was so overwhelming that he cannot seem to put it
into words even now, though the memories themselves feel more real and vivid than anything from his
ordinary life since.

It is precisely this quality of his memory — raw, wordless, and fragmented — that keeps the past so
close to the surface. When a memory intrudes, it does not arrive as a recollection but as a
reliving, and in its wake Rajan finds himself scanning his surroundings compulsively, reading every
doorway and parking lot for signs of threat. This hypervigilant checking, which has become a near-
constant feature of his daily life, reinforces his deepest fear: that he is no longer capable of
being safe, that the world remains fundamentally dangerous, and that he lacks the inner resources to
cope with even minor disturbances. His belief that he must be especially careful because you never
know what can happen next is not an abstract worry but a felt certainty, one the fragmentary
memories keep renewing.

These negative appraisals, in turn, make the memories harder to bear and easier to trigger. When
Rajan believes he is incapable and perpetually at risk, any sensory echo of the original violence
lands with amplified force. A car engine revving in the street outside his pottery studio is enough
— the sound pulls him back before he can orient himself, his hands going still over the clay. Police
cars, even parked ones he passes on his walk from the parking lot, send a current of alarm through
him that takes hours to subside. Funerals are perhaps the most destabilizing: a neighbor's
bereavement gathering last spring, with its gathering of dark-clothed figures and its proximity to a
cemetery, left him unable to work for several days. The triggers, fed by the appraisals that have
settled into his thinking like sediment, arrive with a frequency that exhausts him.

Over the years, Rajan has organized his life around avoidance in ways that have quietly narrowed it.
He steers away from any situation that might involve disagreement or confrontation, which has meant
withdrawing from community organizations he once valued. More painfully, he has begun avoiding
situations that might make him feel happy or hopeful — celebrations, his grandchildren's recitals —
because he has come to believe that positive feeling is a kind of exposure, a lowering of the guard
that something terrible will punish. These avoidances have not brought relief; instead, they have
deepened his conviction that he is fragile and diminished, that the man who once shaped vessels with
steady hands is gone. The avoidance itself becomes a trigger for new intrusions, each retreat
confirming that the world outside is as dangerous as he fears.

What finally brought Rajan to seek help was a conversation with his wife, who told him gently that
she was losing him — not to death, but to disappearance. He arrived at intake hoping, tentatively,
that some part of what he had been might still be recoverable. What he fears most is that it cannot
be.
