Gerald Mast, a 73-year-old divorced journalist of Canadian-American descent, presented for an intake
assessment with a PCL-5 score of 74, indicating severe posttraumatic stress. Gerald spent the better
part of four decades covering industrial and environmental disasters across North America, and it
was during the coverage of a catastrophic chemical plant explosion in the Gulf Coast region — one he
witnessed at close range — that his current difficulties began. He had been embedded with an
emergency response team when a secondary detonation tore through a processing unit without warning.
The concussive force, the acrid chemical taste that coated his mouth and throat for hours afterward,
and the sight of bodies and wreckage captured in his own camera's viewfinder before the lens
shattered — these sensory fragments became the fault lines of what followed. The explosion's
defining quality was its absolute unpredictability: no alarm, no escalation, simply ordinary
industrial noise and then annihilation.

In the months that followed, Gerald found that the world had reorganised itself around potential
detonations. Unpredictable situations — a car backfiring, a fire drill at the newspaper office, a
colleague appearing suddenly in a doorway — sent him into a state of hyperscanning that he could not
consciously override. He began listening for sounds of entry at his apartment door each night,
cataloguing the weight of objects on nearby tables, noting in restaurants and newsrooms alike which
items could be wielded as weapons. He could not relax even in demonstrably safe environments; his
nervous system, he felt, had simply stopped believing in safety as a category. These triggers fed
directly into his hypervigilant monitoring, so that the moment something unpredictable occurred, his
threat system was already primed and his body locked — he would freeze completely, unable to speak
or move, sometimes for a full minute.

Gerald had come to believe that thinking about the explosion would undo him entirely. He was certain
that if he allowed the memory full purchase, something in him would simply collapse and not recover.
This appraisal — that the event was too extreme to be metabolised — drove him toward a set of
strategies that provided momentary relief but slowly narrowed his life. He began sitting with his
back against the wall in every room he entered, a habit that began as vigilance and calcified into
compulsion. He spent money impulsively: books he would not read, equipment he did not need, online
purchases made at two in the morning when the listening for sounds at the door became unbearable.
These strategies, however, kept his attention perpetually organised around threat. The very act of
scanning the room from his wall-backed position meant he was always looking for what might go wrong,
which meant the triggers — a photograph of industrial wreckage in a magazine, an unexpected change
in plan — arrived with his nervous system already at full alert.

The memory itself had a quality Gerald described as cinematic and unreal simultaneously: it played
on a loop, yet he could barely believe it had happened to him. When it surfaced — and the
hypervigilant state reliably surfaced it, as did the chemical taste that occasionally returned when
he ate certain preserved foods — he did not feel as though he were remembering; he felt as though he
were still inside the moment, still tasting the chemical air, still watching through a cracked
viewfinder. The reliving experience in turn deepened his certainty that the event was beyond
processing, reinforcing his belief that engagement with the memory would destroy him, which sent him
back toward spending, freezing, and wall-sitting — all of which kept the threat system running hot,
which kept the memory accessible, which confirmed the appraisal.

Gerald had stopped pitching stories. He had stopped attending the editorial meetings he once led. He
described his emotional life as flat and foreign — he was certain he would never feel genuine
engagement again, that nothing in his remaining years would carry meaning. It was his editor, a
woman who had known him for twenty years, who told him plainly that she was frightened for him. That
conversation, and the quiet shame of recognising how much had been lost, finally brought him to seek
help. What he feared most was that the clinician would confirm what he already suspected: that he
had waited too long, and that the man who had once run toward disaster to bear witness was now
simply one of its casualties.
