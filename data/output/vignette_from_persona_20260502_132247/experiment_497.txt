Aroha Tane is a twenty-year-old auditor of Māori and Pacific Islander descent who was referred for
psychological assessment following a prolonged stalking ordeal that began eighteen months ago and
left her, in her own words, "a completely different person." Her PCL-5 score of 64 places her firmly
in the severe range, and the clinical picture that has emerged across intake and early sessions is
one of a young woman whose sense of safety, identity, and future have all collapsed inward around a
single, sustained threat.

The stalking began during Aroha's first year at the accounting firm where she still works. A former
acquaintance — someone she had known briefly through a shared social circle — began appearing
outside her office building, sending messages through colleagues, and leaving items at her
workstation. For several months, Aroha had no formal language for what was happening; she only knew
that she could not predict when he would appear, that the boundary between her professional life and
her personal safety had dissolved entirely, and that nowhere felt closed off to him. The ordeal
ended only after a restraining order was obtained, but by then the damage to her internal world was
profound. The office building where she had once felt competent and purposeful had become, in her
nervous system, a site of exposure. Even now, arriving at work each morning, scanning the lobby
before stepping through the door, she feels the same low-grade dread she felt during the worst
months — her eyes moving to faces, exits, anyone standing still too long.

That hypervigilance is not experienced by Aroha as a symptom but as rational necessity. She has come
to believe, with a certainty that feels unshakeable, that the world is genuinely dangerous and that
her continued safety depends entirely on her own alertness. This conviction that the world is an
unsafe place, and that her life has been fundamentally and perhaps irreparably destroyed by what
happened, has shaped the strategies she now uses to manage daily life in ways that have quietly
narrowed it to almost nothing. Because she is certain the threat is ongoing — not necessarily from
him, but from the world as it now presents itself to her — she has stopped going out beyond what is
absolutely required. Weekends pass inside her apartment. Invitations from colleagues are declined.
The few friendships she had when she started the job have thinned to almost nothing, partly because
she no longer appears, and partly because she no longer tries. Her appraisal that her life has been
destroyed makes any effort to rebuild it feel not just futile but somehow dishonest — as though
reaching for something good would be a kind of lie about what she is now.

This is where the picture becomes most painful. Aroha does not simply avoid danger; she avoids
anything that might make her feel hopeful. A colleague's birthday lunch, a message from an old
friend, a film she might once have enjoyed — these are things she turns away from, not because they
frighten her in the way a stranger's gaze does, but because she has come to believe she does not
deserve them. She tells herself, quietly and with a kind of bleak consistency, that she deserves to
suffer for what happened — as though the stalking were something she had invited or failed to
prevent, and as though happiness now would be a form of cheating some just sentence. These patterns
of self-punishment and withdrawal did not arrive all at once; they accumulated over months, each
small refusal reinforcing the belief that preceded it, each belief making the next refusal feel more
reasonable. The result is a young woman who goes to work, performs her auditing duties with
technical competence, returns home, and otherwise inhabits a life that has been stripped of almost
everything that once gave it texture.

What finally brought Aroha to seek help was a conversation with her supervisor, who gently noted
that she had not taken a single day of leave in a year and that she looked, in his words, exhausted
in a way that sleep did not seem to fix. She came to the first session saying very little about what
she hoped for, and a great deal about what she feared — specifically, that she had already become
the person the stalking made her, and that there was no version of herself left on the other side of
it.
