Miriam Calloway is a 73-year-old widowed painter who presented for her initial assessment carrying a
canvas tote bag and speaking carefully, as though she had rehearsed what she was willing to say. She
lives alone in a small house on the outskirts of a mid-sized Ohio town, where she has kept a studio
for decades. She was referred by her primary care physician after her sleep difficulties and what
she described as "losing herself" had persisted for over a year following a gas explosion at a
community arts centre she had attended regularly. The blast, caused by a ruptured industrial line
that building management had been warned about months earlier, killed two people and injured several
others. Miriam was in a back hallway when the explosion occurred. She was thrown against a wall and
lay on the concrete floor in the dark, unable to move, surrounded by the acrid smell of gas and
burning material, while a man nearby wept loudly and uncontrollably. She was eventually pulled out
by emergency responders, but for what she described as an indeterminate stretch of time, she was
certain she was going to die there, alone, on a cold floor, in a building that should have been
safe.

In the weeks that followed, Miriam found that certain things returned her immediately to the
hallway. The smell of alcohol-based solvents — unavoidable in her own studio — would stop her at the
door, her chest tightening, her body bracing as though the floor might give way. When someone cried
near her, even on television, she would feel her pulse spike and her attention narrow. Physical
touch, even gentle contact from her daughter during visits, could send a wave of alarm through her
that she could not explain and could not stop. These triggers did not simply unsettle her — they
pulled her directly into memory, vivid and unmoving, as though she were looking at a series of
photographs she had not consented to keep: the angle of the hallway ceiling, the particular
darkness, her own hand flat against the concrete. With those images came a bodily certainty that her
limbs were pressed against the floor again, her back against the wall, her body arranged in the
posture of someone waiting to die. And with the memories came guilt — a persistent, irrational
conviction that she had failed to warn others, that she had not acted, that she had simply lain
there.

That guilt had hardened, over months, into something more structural. Miriam had come to believe
that the world did not operate the way she had spent seventy-three years assuming it did — that
institutions could not be trusted, that safety was a story people told themselves, that she herself
was somehow changed in a way that could not be undone. She was not sure, she said quietly, that her
life was worth living anymore. She no longer believed she would feel ordinary emotions again; what
she experienced instead was a kind of flatness interrupted by alarm. These appraisals had a way of
sharpening her vigilance — she lay awake at night listening for sounds at the doors and windows,
certain that her sense of threat was now permanently recalibrated. She was frequently irritable with
the few people she saw, and her daughter had begun visiting less often, a loss Miriam registered
with a grief she could not fully access. The negative beliefs also seemed to lower her threshold for
being triggered — a world perceived as fundamentally unsafe meant that the smell of solvent or the
sound of crying carried a weight they might not otherwise have borne. And when memories surfaced,
they did not soften her appraisals but confirmed them, cycling back into the conviction that she was
permanently altered and permanently at risk, which in turn kept her body in a state of readiness
that made sleep almost impossible.

To manage all of this, Miriam had stopped watching the news entirely and kept her television tuned
only to gardening programmes. She worked to keep her mind occupied at all hours — listening to
audiobooks while she painted, filling silence aggressively. She tried hard not to think about the
explosion, pushing images away the moment they surfaced. Her studio output, once prolific, had
slowed considerably; she could rarely tolerate the smell of the solvents long enough to work for
more than an hour.

What finally brought her in was her daughter's tearful plea during a recent visit — the crying
itself had triggered an episode, and Miriam had asked her to leave. She arrived at the clinic
hoping, she said, to feel like herself again, and most afraid that she no longer knew who that was.
