Aroha Ngata is a twenty-nine-year-old New Zealand Māori woman who relocated to the United States
several years ago to pursue a professional dance career. She presented for psychological assessment
following a terrorist attack she survived at a crowded public venue approximately fourteen months
ago. Her PCL-5 score of 69 places her in the severe range, and her presentation reflects a tightly
interlocking set of cognitive and behavioural patterns that have progressively narrowed her world.

The attack unfolded without warning. Aroha had been performing at an outdoor cultural festival when
an armed assailant opened fire into the crowd. She was caught in a corridor between two stage
barriers — hemmed in on both sides, unable to move, watching people fall around her. The sensation
of having no options, of being physically trapped with no exit, became seared into the fabric of the
experience. She survived by remaining motionless for nearly forty minutes until the scene was
secured. In the weeks that followed, she found she could not construct a coherent account of what
had happened. Her memories arrived in disconnected pieces — a flash of a particular colour, the
texture of the ground beneath her palms — without narrative sequence or verbal form. She could not
put the event into words in any way that felt true to it, and sometimes the full emotional weight of
those forty minutes would descend on her without any accompanying image at all, as though the fear
had become autonomous. These fractured, nonverbal memory qualities made it impossible to process the
event as something past and bounded, and they fed directly into her conviction that the world
remained dangerous: if she could not understand what had happened, she could not be sure it would
not happen again.

Aroha now works as a dance instructor at a studio in the city, and it is there that the architecture
of her symptoms becomes most visible. When a door to the practice room closes behind a group of
students, she feels the walls contract. Enclosed or confined spaces — the changing room, the
elevator, even a full studio with the blinds drawn — have become intolerable, and she has begun
teaching with the studio door propped open regardless of weather or noise complaints, a habit her
employer has started to notice. When a student enters mid-session and the door swings shut with a
bang, Aroha's body responds before her mind does: her attention locks onto the sound, scanning for
what it might mean, and within seconds she is listening for further sounds that might indicate
someone entering from another direction. This hypervigilance toward unexpected sounds and toward
strangers approaching her — even a parent walking across the floor to collect a child — is not
experienced as irrational. She is certain, in those moments, that the threat is real. The heightened
state of alert then pulls her back toward the sensory fragments of the attack itself, so that
trigger and memory feed one another in a loop she cannot interrupt from the outside.

She has also come to believe that the people responsible for the attack have faced no consequences,
and this absence of accountability has hollowed out whatever meaning she once drew from her work and
her cultural identity. Dance was, for Aroha, inseparable from her connection to her Māori community
and her sense of self; she has lost that thread entirely, and the loss feels permanent. When certain
conversation topics arise — anything touching on public safety, political violence, or even the name
of the venue — the appraisal that nothing matters and no one is held responsible surges forward, and
with it comes a fresh wave of fragmented sensory memory and the feeling of entrapment all over
again. To manage this, she has adopted a rigid practice of forcing herself to think only positive
thoughts whenever distress rises, and she spends long hours trying to find a reason why this
happened to her specifically. But these strategies prevent her from sitting with the memory long
enough to integrate it, keeping it raw and unprocessed, which sustains the sense of ongoing threat
and confirms, in her mind, that the world is as dangerous as her body insists it is. The positive
reframing, in particular, has left her feeling fraudulent and further cut off from her community,
deepening the appraisal that she has lost something irretrievable.

What finally brought Aroha to seek help was a session last month in which she found herself unable
to enter the studio at all, standing outside for twenty minutes before leaving. She is most afraid
that she will never dance again — and most hoping, tentatively, that someone can help her find words
for what she cannot yet say.
