are vivid — startlingly so. His memories of that morning are exceptionally clear and detailed, he
says, more like a film he is forced to watch than something that happened to a child sixty-six years
ago. That quality of vividness is itself part of the burden: the memory forces its way into his mind
without invitation, arriving whole and uninstructed. And yet, in the same breath, he will say that
it feels like it happened to someone else — that the boy in the memory cannot be him, cannot be
continuous with the man sitting in the chair across from the clinician. This dissociative quality
does not diminish the intrusions; it makes them stranger and harder to metabolize.

In his work as a firefighter, the triggers are unavoidable. When an ambulance passes the station

blast — is enough to stop him mid-sentence. These sensory encounters do not merely recall the
memory; they seem to unlock it, and the unlocking arrives alongside a particular shame. He had come
to believe, in the months since symptoms intensified, that a stronger man — a smarter man — would
