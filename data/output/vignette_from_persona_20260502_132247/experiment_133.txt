Kwame Asante is a fifty-nine-year-old Ghanaian-American professor of environmental studies at a mid-
sized university in the Pacific Northwest, single, and presenting with a PCL-5 score of 54 following
a traumatic event involving his younger sister. Approximately fourteen months ago, Kwame received a
call informing him that his sister Abena had been caught in catastrophic flash flooding during a
visit to relatives in a rural part of Ghana. She survived, but only narrowly — pulled from debris-
choked water after nearly twenty minutes submerged, resuscitated on a riverbank, and hospitalized
for weeks with complications. Kwame had not been present, but in the days that followed he was sent
photographs, video clips, and detailed accounts from family members, and he flew to Accra within
forty-eight hours. What he found when he arrived — Abena pale and swollen in a hospital bed, the
smell of river mud still faintly clinging to her hair and clothing, the waterlogged landscape still
visible through the ward window — lodged itself in him with a force that time has done nothing to
diminish.

The triggers that now govern Kwame's daily life are tightly organized around the sensory texture of
that experience. Water, in almost any form that carries scale or force, is the most reliable one.
This matters enormously given his occupation: his research concerns watershed management and
climate-driven flooding, and his office is lined with maps of river systems, his lectures populated
with satellite imagery of inundated plains. When he stands at the front of a lecture hall and
advances a slide showing aerial footage of a flooded delta, something in his body responds before he
has had time to register what he is seeing — his chest tightens, his hands go cold, his breathing
shallows. The physiological alarm is already sounding by the time his conscious mind catches up and
identifies the image for what it is. A particular smell — the organic, silty odor of standing water
or wet earth — produces the same effect, and the campus grounds in autumn, after heavy rain, carry
just enough of that quality to make the walk from his car to his building an exercise in bracing.
Even stress itself, the ordinary accumulative pressure of departmental deadlines and student
demands, has become a trigger in its own right: when tension rises, the threshold lowers, and the
body's readiness to detect catastrophe sharpens to a point where almost anything can set it off.

What these triggers reliably produce is a state of threat that Kwame finds both overwhelming and
humiliating. He is a man who has spent his career thinking carefully about environmental risk, and
yet he cannot sit beside a campus fountain without his body declaring an emergency. The strong
negative feelings that accompany these moments — fear, horror, a guilt he cannot entirely account
for given that he was not there and could not have prevented anything — arrive with a physiological
intensity that feels entirely out of proportion to his circumstances. And crucially, the threat
state does not resolve when the trigger passes. Kwame cannot relax even in settings he knows to be
safe; the activation lingers, his nervous system apparently unconvinced by his rational assessments.
This sustained arousal, in turn, lowers the threshold at which the next trigger registers. The smell
of rain through an open window, a student's offhand mention of a flood in the news, the sound of
water running in a bathroom down the hall — things that would have meant nothing to him two years
ago now arrive with the weight of warning. Threat keeps the sensory world primed, and the primed
sensory world keeps feeding threat, and the loop has been running, largely unbroken, for over a
year.

The consequence most concrete to Kwame is professional. He has begun restructuring his courses to
minimize flood-related imagery, a compromise he despises in himself. A colleague recently asked why
his syllabus had shifted away from case studies in inundation events. He gave an academic answer.
What finally brought him to seek help was simpler: he stood in the shower last month and realized he
had been holding his breath.
