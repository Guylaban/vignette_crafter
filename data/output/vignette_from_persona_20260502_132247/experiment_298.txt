Valentina is a 37-year-old Colombian-American woman who has worked her way up over fifteen years to
manage a mid-sized hotel in downtown Houston. She is described by colleagues as composed, exacting,
and tireless — qualities that, in the months since her assault, have calcified into something she
can no longer entirely distinguish from survival. She came to the clinic after her partner of three
years told her, quietly and with evident pain, that he no longer knew how to reach her.

The assault occurred approximately eight months ago, following a late-night industry event at a
hotel conference center — a setting almost indistinguishable from the one where Valentina spends the
majority of her waking hours. A male colleague whom she had trusted, someone whose face she still
occasionally sees at regional management meetings, followed her into a service corridor and
assaulted her. The fluorescent hum of the corridor lighting, the particular smell of industrial
cleaning product, the sound of a key-card reader clicking — these details lodged themselves in her
nervous system with a precision her conscious mind cannot match. Back in her own hotel, those same
sounds and smells are everywhere, and her body responds to them as though the corridor is still
happening: her chest tightens, her hands go cold, her heartbeat accelerates in the elevator bank.
She had come to believe that this was evidence of something broken in her specifically — that a
functioning person would have recovered by now, that her body's continued alarm was proof of a
fundamental defect rather than an injury. And because she was certain, on some level, that the
assault had happened because of who she was — too ambitious, too trusting, too willing to be in that
corridor at that hour — the shame of remembering felt indistinguishable from confirmation.

It is partly this shame, and the deeper conviction that something in her character had invited what
happened, that has made the memory itself so resistant to form. Valentina knows the sequence of
events in the way one knows a fact learned by rote, but when she attempts to tell it — to herself,
in the dark, or once haltingly to her partner — she finds the words simply are not there. The story
will not cohere. There is no beginning that leads to a middle; there is only a series of sensory
fragments that arrive without permission and depart without resolution. The shame that floods her on
those moments of partial recall makes sustained engagement with the memory feel unbearable, and so
she does not stay with it long enough to build the narrative that might, in time, begin to settle.

Instead, she works. Valentina now arrives at the hotel before six in the morning and rarely leaves
before ten at night. The busyness is not incidental — it is engineered. As long as there are
staffing rotas to review, guest complaints to resolve, and suppliers to negotiate with, she does not
have to sit alone with the fragments. But the overwork, which began as relief, has gradually
deepened the problem it was meant to solve: the memory remains unprocessed, its edges no more
defined than they were in the first weeks, and the exhaustion has stripped away whatever emotional
resilience might have helped her begin to examine it. She has also begun avoiding a senior events
coordinator who bears a passing physical resemblance to her assailant — rerouting her own movements
through a hotel she has managed for years, restructuring meeting schedules under the cover of
operational rationale. Her partner notices her absence. A deputy manager has begun quietly absorbing
responsibilities that are formally Valentina's. The shape of her professional life is changing
around an absence she has not named to anyone at work.

The rumination tends to arrive at night, when the overwork finally fails her: elaborate, circular
thoughts about exposure, about what she might say or do to ensure her assailant faces consequences.
These thoughts feel like agency but leave her more agitated than before, and by morning she is more
convinced than ever that she is not the kind of person who recovers — that the event has revealed
something that was always true about her. What finally brought her to seek help was her partner's
words, spoken without accusation: that he missed her, and that he was frightened she had decided,
somewhere she could not access, that she deserved to disappear. She is not sure he is wrong. What
she is most hoping for, she said at intake, is to find the words — not to forgive, not yet, but
simply to be able to tell herself what happened.
