Ernesto is a 70-year-old widowed dentist of Salvadoran descent who has practiced in the same modest
clinic in a mid-sized American city for nearly three decades. He presented for psychological
assessment after his daughter, alarmed by his increasing withdrawal and a recent incident at his
clinic, persuaded him to seek help. His PCL-5 score of 42 places him in the moderate-to-severe
range, and the picture that emerged across the intake and early sessions is one of a man quietly
held hostage by an event that occurred more than forty years ago — and that, in his nervous system,
has never fully ended.

Ernesto was in his late twenties, working as a young dental student in San Salvador, when a military
patrol entered the university campus during a period of acute political violence. Without warning,
shots were fired in the courtyard below his classroom window. He saw a fellow student fall.
Uniformed soldiers moved through the corridor outside his door, and for what he describes as the
longest minutes of his life, he pressed himself against the wall and did not move or call out. His
friend died. Ernesto survived. He has never, in forty years, been able to construct a coherent
verbal account of what happened — the event resists language entirely, existing instead as a set of
sensory fragments: the crack of gunfire, the particular olive-green of the uniforms, the smell of
concrete and blood. When the memory does surface, it does not feel like remembering. It feels like
being there again, with full sensory immediacy, and the emotional force of it is so overwhelming
that he has to physically stop whatever he is doing, grip the edge of his dental chair, and wait for
it to pass. Because the memory is so resistant to narrative form, he has never been able to process
or contextualize it, and this incompleteness has kept it perpetually raw.

In his daily work, Ernesto encounters his triggers with a regularity that has made the clinic itself
a site of chronic low-grade dread. The clinic sits near a police substation, and officers
occasionally walk past or enter the waiting room for routine dental appointments. The moment Ernesto
sees a uniform — a police badge, a security guard's jacket, a patient who mentions military service
— the memory fragments erupt without warning, dragging him back to that corridor. A car backfiring
in the parking lot, or a patient dropping a metal instrument tray, produces the same effect: his
body floods with alarm before his mind can intervene. These triggers do not merely distress him;
they activate a state of acute threat, in which he begins scanning every room for exits the moment
he enters it, watches patients' hands and postures for signs of danger, and holds himself in a state
of sustained hypervigilance that leaves him exhausted by the end of each working day. The threat
monitoring, in turn, keeps him in a physiological state in which almost anything can become a
trigger — a raised voice in the waiting room, a patient who arrives in military fatigues — and the
threshold for activation grows lower with each passing month.

To manage this, Ernesto has built a set of strategies that feel to him like reasonable precautions
but that have steadily narrowed his life. He avoids all news coverage of Central American violence
or anything involving armed conflict, and has stopped watching television almost entirely. He
carries a licensed handgun in his coat pocket at all times, telling himself this is simply prudent
for a man who works alone in a clinic. He ruminates extensively, returning again and again to the
question of whether he could have done something to save his friend — whether his silence was
cowardice, whether he is, at his core, a man who fails others when it matters. This self-blame,
rather than resolving anything, keeps the memory activated and close to the surface, so that the
next trigger finds him already primed. The weapon, the avoidance, the rumination — each of these
strategies sustains the sense that the world remains dangerous, which sharpens his scanning and
watchfulness, which ensures that triggers are encountered more frequently and responded to more
intensely.

What finally brought him to the clinic was an afternoon when a patient arrived wearing a National
Guard uniform. Ernesto excused himself, went to the back room, and sat on the floor for twenty
minutes, unable to speak. He is afraid, he says quietly, that he is losing his mind. What he hopes
for — though he does not quite believe it is possible — is a single day in which he does not feel
like that young man still pressed against the wall, waiting for the door to open.
