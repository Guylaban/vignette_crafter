the past eleven years, caring for young children in private homes in the greater Houston area. He

Approximately fourteen months ago, Boon-Nam was the sole adult present when the four-year-old boy in
his care collapsed without warning in the kitchen, his lips turning blue, his small body convulsing
on the tile floor. The ambulance took what felt to Boon-Nam like an eternity to arrive. He had held
the child, spoken to the dispatcher, and watched the boy's eyes roll back — all while being entirely
alone and entirely certain, in that moment, that he was watching a child die in his arms. The boy
survived, diagnosed with a previously undetected cardiac arrhythmia, but Boon-Nam has never fully
registered that the story ended there. The memory does not feel finished. It feels, when it
surfaces, more vivid and present than anything in his waking life — more real, he has said, than the
room he is sitting in. It does not arrive as a recollection. It arrives as the kitchen floor, the
blue lips, the dispatcher's voice, the unbearable smallness of the child's body. Because the memory
has never cohered into a narrative with a clear beginning, middle, and end — because he cannot tell
himself or anyone else what happened in a way that feels complete — it retains this raw, unprocessed
quality, erupting without context and without resolution. The fragmented, present-tense nature of
the memory in turn deepens his sense that something has been permanently broken: that the man who
knelt on that kitchen floor is not the man who woke up that morning, and that the distance between
those two people cannot be crossed.

Boon-Nam has come to believe several things about himself and the world that feel, to him, like
hard-won truths rather than distortions. He is certain he has been permanently damaged — not just
shaken, but altered at some structural level that cannot be repaired. He no longer trusts the
families he works for, suspecting that what they present to him is a managed surface concealing

hidden in the children he cares for. These appraisals — of irreversible self-change and of a world

available moment with activity — podcasts, errands, the relentless small tasks of childcare — so
that no silence opens up in which the memory might find him.

Boon-Nam never allows the memory to be processed or narrated, it remains incoherent and present-
tense, and each intrusion reinforces his conviction that he is permanently changed and that the

hopes for. What he fears most is being told, clearly and finally, that he is right — that what he
lost on that kitchen floor is gone for good.
