reorganized her inner life over the past two years.

The incident occurred on a Saturday afternoon when Valentina and her husband, Marco, were returning
from a farmers' market. An argument had broken out between two men in the parking lot, and
Valentina, who had taken a self-defense course years earlier, moved toward the confrontation when
she saw one man shove the other toward the ground. She had a folding knife in her bag — something
she had carried for years without ever using — and drew it reflexively when the larger man turned on
her. In the struggle that followed, the blade caught the other bystander, the man she had moved to
help, opening a deep laceration across his forearm and severing a tendon. He survived, but lost
significant function in his hand. Marco died of a cardiac event unrelated to the incident six months
later, and the two losses — the guilt and the grief — became entangled in ways Valentina has
struggled to separate ever since.

The memory of that afternoon is not intact. Valentina reports distinct gaps: she has no clear
recollection of the moment the knife made contact, only the sound the man made and the image of
blood spreading across a gray sleeve. She cannot reconstruct the seconds before or after, no matter
how deliberately she tries. What she is left with instead is something worse — an emotional residue
that arrives without any accompanying image, a sudden wash of horror and shame that overtakes her
without warning and without the anchor of a specific memory to explain or contain it. She describes
sitting at her workstation, deep in a routine audit, when the feeling simply arrives: the full
weight of what she did, pressing down without a face or a scene attached to it.

sidearms. The first time she saw a holstered weapon on a colleague's escort in the corridor, the

clear image, but the grief and the guilt arriving together, sudden and disorganizing. She left the
building early and told her supervisor she had a migraine. Since then, she has rerouted her morning
path through the office to avoid the corridor where those briefings are held, and she has declined
two project assignments that would have required her to attend on-site meetings at the federal
building. A promotion she was tracking has quietly moved to a colleague instead.

Confrontations of any kind — a raised voice in a meeting, a terse exchange between coworkers — act
as a similar portal. The sound of conflict pulls her back toward the parking lot in a way she cannot
fully articulate, and the emotional intensity that follows is not a flashback so much as a flooding:
grief for Marco, guilt about the injured man, and a formless dread that she is, at her core, someone
capable of causing serious harm. She had come to believe this about herself in the months after the
incident, and that belief has only deepened. What she finds most unbearable is the grief that
surfaces whenever memory — however partial — is activated; it is not clean mourning but something
compound and contaminated, the loss of Marco folded into the loss of her image of herself.

What finally brought Valentina to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when she sat in her car for
forty minutes after a minor disagreement with a coworker, unable to move, feeling the full emotional
weight of the parking lot without any image to attach it to. She is most afraid that the gaps in her
memory mean she will never fully understand what she did. She is most hoping, she said quietly, to
feel like herself again — or to find out whether that is still possible.
