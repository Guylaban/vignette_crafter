Bunthan is a 42-year-old Cambodian-American farmer who lives with his wife on a small vegetable
operation in the Central Valley of California. He presented for psychological assessment with a
PCL-5 score of 76, describing a constellation of symptoms that have persisted for nearly two years
following what he calls, with characteristic understatement, "the night we almost lost Sophea."

Sophea, his seven-year-old daughter, had developed a severe bacterial infection that progressed with
terrifying speed. By the time Bunthan carried her to the car, her small body was limp and her skin
had taken on a mottled, dusky colour that he had never seen before and has never been able to unsee.
At the hospital, nurses cut away her clothing and attached monitors to her chest, her arms, the soft
crook of her elbow. Bunthan stood at the bedside in the fluorescent glare while a physician pressed
a mask over his daughter's face and called for additional staff. A song had been playing softly from
a portable speaker someone had left on a counter — something with a slow, descending melody he could
not name — and it played on, indifferently, while the room filled with people and his wife collapsed
against him sobbing. Sophea survived. But Bunthan did not return from that room the way he entered
it.

In the months that followed, the memory refused to settle into the past. Rather than receding, it
retained an eerie presentness — when it surfaced, Bunthan did not experience it as recollection but
as recurrence, as if he were once again standing at that bedside with his heart hammering and his
daughter's arm going slack. Fragments arrived without invitation: the mottled skin, the mask, the
indifferent song. These intrusions were not orderly. A piece of the memory would flash through him —
the particular colour of Sophea's forearm against the white sheet — and vanish before he could
orient himself, leaving him shaking in the cab of his truck or frozen at the edge of a field.
Because the memory felt so insistently like something still happening rather than something already
survived, Bunthan's nervous system had never received the signal that the danger was over.

It was the nighttime that became his most treacherous terrain. Darkness itself had become a trigger
— he associated it viscerally with the hours he had spent in that hospital corridor, not knowing
whether his daughter would live — and waking in the dark could send him immediately back into the
full sensory texture of that night. The memory, in turn, sharpened his sensitivity to anything that
had been present during the event: a slow, minor-key song heard from a passing car, or the sight of
a child's arm or leg in a certain light, could detonate the same intrusive reliving. Each trigger
fed the memory's vividness, and the memory's vividness fed the potency of the triggers, a circuit
that had been running without interruption.

To interrupt it, Bunthan had constructed a set of habits that made a kind of desperate sense to him.
He began staying up until two or three in the morning — exhausting himself with farm paperwork, with
checking irrigation lines by flashlight — so that when he finally lay down, unconsciousness came
quickly and the dark had less time to work on him. He had largely stopped going to sleep in any
ordinary way. He had also begun avoiding other people, partly because social gatherings sometimes
involved music, and partly because he had come to feel that no one around him understood the
particular texture of what he carried. These strategies, however, had their own consequences: the
chronic sleep deprivation left him hyperreactive, so that ordinary sensory details on the farm — the
bruised skin of an overripe squash, a radio left on in a neighbour's truck — registered as threats
and triggered intrusions that he was too depleted to manage. His avoidance had also quietly hollowed
out his marriage; his wife, who had shared the same night, now found herself living with a man who
came to bed after she was asleep and left before she woke.

What finally brought Bunthan to seek help was a morning three weeks ago when he heard a song
drifting from his wife's phone in the kitchen — the same slow, descending melody from the hospital —
and found himself on his knees on the kitchen floor, certain, for a long and terrible moment, that
Sophea was dying again. He is not hopeful, exactly. What he wants, he said quietly, is to be able to
lie down in the dark beside his wife without going back to that room.
