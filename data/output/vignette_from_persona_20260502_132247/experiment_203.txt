Leilani Fono is a 61-year-old Samoan-American woman who has worked as a security guard at a large
regional shopping mall for the past eleven years. She is single, lives alone in a small apartment,
and describes herself as someone who has always taken her responsibilities seriously — someone who
believed, before that evening, that careful attention could prevent most harm.

Fourteen months ago, during a late-night shift, Leilani was responding to a reported disturbance
near the mall's east loading dock. Moving quickly through a poorly lit corridor, she pushed open a
heavy fire door without knowing that a young maintenance worker was standing directly on the other
side. The door struck him with considerable force, and he fell, sustaining a fractured skull and a
serious traumatic brain injury. Leilani was the first to reach him, kneeling on the concrete floor
and pressing her hands to his head while calling for help, watching blood spread beneath her palms.
The sounds of his laboured breathing and the particular quality of the light in that corridor — the
sickly yellow of the emergency strip lighting — were the last things she registered with full
clarity before something in her memory seems to have simply stopped recording.

She knows she stayed until the paramedics arrived. She knows she gave a statement. But those minutes
are gone, and no amount of effort has recovered them. The gaps in her memory have become a source of
profound distress in themselves. Leilani cannot accept that she does not know exactly what she did
or failed to do in those missing moments, and the incompleteness of what she can recall drives her
into long cycles of review — going over and over the sequence of events, turning each detail she
does retain in her hands like a fragment of broken glass, searching for the shape of the whole. She
has spent entire nights at her kitchen table doing this, reconstructing timelines in a notebook,
convinced that if she can just find the right angle of approach, the memory will finally cohere and
she will understand what she is responsible for.

Yet the more she dwells, the stranger the memory becomes. When she does manage to call up the scene
— the corridor, the door, the sound — it arrives with a disturbing flatness, as though she is
reading a report about a woman she has never met. She can narrate what happened without feeling that
it happened to her, and this emotional distance, rather than offering relief, frightens her. She has
come to believe that her numbness is itself evidence of something broken in her character, that a
person who truly felt remorse would not experience her own memory as belonging to a stranger. This
interpretation feeds her compulsion to keep analysing, to keep searching the record of events for a
version she can actually inhabit and grieve.

Her rumination is relentless and takes several forms simultaneously. She dwells on what she did
wrong — the speed of her approach, her failure to check the door, her inadequate knowledge of who
was working in that corridor that night. She searches for reasons the event happened at all, cycling
between punishment narratives and random-universe narratives with equal exhaustion. And she
overanalyses the details with a persistence that has colonised her waking hours. This sustained
cognitive effort does not produce resolution; instead, it keeps the event perpetually present and
perpetually unprocessed, so that the memory remains fragmented and alien, which in turn compels
further analysis. At work — the very environment where the accident occurred — she finds herself
pausing at every fire door, rehearsing the moment, checking and rechecking, her mind still trying to
close a loop that will not close.

Over the past year she has become increasingly isolated. Colleagues have noticed her distraction.
Her supervisors have raised concerns about her concentration on shift. She has not told anyone what
she is doing in her own mind during those long quiet hours on patrol.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was a conversation with the maintenance worker's sister,
who contacted her to say that he had survived but would never fully recover. Leilani had hoped, she
realised, that certainty about his outcome would end the rumination. It did not. What she is most
afraid of is that nothing ever will.
