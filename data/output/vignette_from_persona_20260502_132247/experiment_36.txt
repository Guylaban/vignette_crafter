Marlene Kowalski is a sixty-five-year-old widowed military medic who spent the better part of four
decades working in field medicine and emergency trauma care. She is a composed, practically minded
woman who has seen more than her share of catastrophic injury, and she had long prided herself on
her capacity to remain steady under pressure. That self-understanding was fundamentally disrupted
eighteen months ago when, driving home from a late shift on a rain-slicked highway, a truck drifted
across the centre line and struck her vehicle at speed. The impact spun her car into the median
barrier; when she finally came to rest, the cab was filled with smoke, the windshield had collapsed
inward, and her door would not open. She remembers the sound most vividly — a percussive, tearing
crash that seemed to arrive before she had time to register the truck's headlights — and then the
smell of something burning, and the particular quality of stillness that followed. She was extracted
by first responders and sustained serious but survivable injuries. Physically, she has recovered.
Psychologically, she has not.

In the months that followed, Marlene noticed that certain things in her ordinary environment had
become unbearable. Seeing a car with a crushed quarter panel in a parking lot, or passing a vehicle
of the same dark blue colour as the truck that hit her, could stop her mid-stride. Discomfort that
she initially dismissed as nerves would rise sharply and without warning when these images
registered. These triggers function as a direct pathway into a state of acute threat: her body
shifts immediately to high alert, muscles tensing across her shoulders and jaw, her attention
narrowing as though the danger were present and ongoing. Loud or unexpected sounds — a door slamming
in the hospital corridor, a backfire in the parking lot — produce the same response, and on several
occasions she has experienced full flashbacks in which the moment of impact replays with a sensory
immediacy that makes the present moment temporarily inaccessible. She is not simply remembering the
accident; she is, in those moments, inside it.

The quality of the memory itself compounds this. When Marlene attempts to recall what happened, she
is flooded by emotion before any coherent narrative can form — a wave of overwhelm that forces her
to stop. She knows what occurred, in the way one knows a fact, but she cannot find language adequate
to the experience; when she has tried to describe it to her daughter, the words have failed her
entirely. This fragmented, wordless quality means that the memory arrives not as a story with a
beginning and end, but as sensation and image — and when it does, it carries the same charge as the
external triggers, returning her body to that same state of readiness and alarm.

To manage the persistent tension and the intrusive material, Marlene has developed a set of
strategies that have gradually restructured her daily life. She distracts herself heavily with work
schedules, paperwork, and procedural tasks, and when distraction is insufficient she tries to
suppress the thoughts directly — pushing them down through force of will, a technique that works
briefly and then fails. In the evenings, alone in the house she shared with her late husband, she
eats — not from hunger but from a need to quiet something inside her that will not settle. These
strategies do not resolve the underlying state of threat; they sustain it. The suppression and
distraction keep her vigilance elevated, so that her body remains primed and reactive, and the
overeating, far from providing relief, leaves her feeling ashamed and physically uncomfortable — a
state that lowers her threshold for encountering the very triggers she is trying to avoid. The cycle
has tightened steadily: avoidance narrows her world, the strategies maintain her arousal, and the
arousal makes the next trigger more potent.

Occupationally, the consequences have become impossible to ignore. Marlene has begun arriving at
work via a longer route to avoid a particular intersection, and she has twice left a shift early
after a flashback rendered her unable to focus on patient care — something that, for a woman who
defined herself through professional competence, she finds deeply shameful. It was a supervising
colleague who finally named what she was observing and urged Marlene to seek help. Marlene came to
the intake session guarded but exhausted. What she fears most is that she has become permanently
unreliable — to her patients, to her colleagues, to herself. What she hopes, though she expressed it
only once and quietly, is that she might find a way to remember what happened without it happening
again.
