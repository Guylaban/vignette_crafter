Rangi Parata is a 53-year-old Māori-New Zealander photographer who has lived in the United States
for nearly two decades, building a respected career documenting conflict zones and humanitarian
crises for international news outlets. He is married, and his wife has described watching him
become, over the past three years, a man she barely recognizes — someone who cannot sit in a
restaurant without first walking its perimeter, who calls her from assignment locations simply to
hear her say that he is going to be fine. Rangi himself came to the intake appointment with his back
against the wall of the waiting room, eyes moving steadily across the other patients.

Three years ago, while on assignment in a politically unstable region, Rangi was detained by a
paramilitary faction and held for eleven days in a series of small rooms with other captives. The
conditions were unpredictable in the way that made them most psychologically corrosive: guards
rotated without pattern, meals arrived or did not, and violence was administered without discernible
reason. What Rangi remembers most vividly is the way a guard would cross the room with a sudden,
purposeful stride — the shift in posture that preceded something terrible — and the complete
impossibility of knowing, when a door opened, whether it meant food or a beating. He was eventually
released without explanation. He flew home. He told his wife he was fine and went back to work
within a week.

The symptoms did not announce themselves dramatically. They accumulated. Within months of his
return, Rangi found that he could not enter a location — a gallery opening, a press briefing, a
coffee shop — without first identifying the exits and positioning himself where no one could
approach from behind. In his professional life, this meant he began declining certain assignments:
crowded press conferences, indoor events with poor sightlines. He told his editors it was a
scheduling conflict. What he could not tell them was that he had stood outside a venue in downtown
Chicago for twenty minutes, unable to make himself walk through the door, because he had not yet
worked out where he would run if he needed to. His body, he said, simply would not cooperate — a
persistent, low-grade muscular tension that he described as being permanently braced for impact, as
though his nervous system had never received the signal that the detention had ended.

The constant sense that danger was present and imminent — that any person moving quickly nearby
might be the beginning of something — drove Rangi toward a set of behaviors that felt, to him,
entirely rational. He began choosing seats at the periphery of every room, back to the wall, facing
the entrance; in restaurants, he would ask to be reseated if the host placed him elsewhere, and on
one occasion left without eating because no suitable position was available. He scanned faces in
public for signs of hostile intent, reading the body language of strangers with the same
professional attention he had once reserved for the edges of conflict zones. And he sought
reassurance — from his wife, from colleagues, from his physician — asking repeatedly whether they
thought he seemed all right, whether they believed he was coping. He had come to believe that if he
asked enough people and received enough confirmation, the feeling of threat might subside. It did
not. In fact, the reassurance-seeking and the escape planning and the hypervigilant scanning had the
effect of keeping the alarm alive: each time he mapped an exit or repositioned himself for safety,
his body registered the action as evidence that danger was real and present, the tension never
releasing, the monitoring never switching off. The loop had become self-sustaining — the strategies
that were meant to lower the threat signal instead kept amplifying it, and the amplified threat made
the strategies feel more necessary than ever.

What finally brought Rangi to seek help was a conversation with his wife after he refused to attend
their daughter's university graduation ceremony because he could not assess the auditorium in
advance. He had sat in the car outside and wept. He told the intake clinician that he did not want
to lose any more of his life to this. What he feared most was that the person who had walked into
that room three years ago — curious, willing, unafraid — was simply gone.
