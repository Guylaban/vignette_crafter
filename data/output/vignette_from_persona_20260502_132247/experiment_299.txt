Linh is a 57-year-old Vietnamese American woman, married and the owner of a small convenience store
in a mid-sized city. She was referred for psychological assessment following a conversation with her
primary care physician, who noted that she had been avoiding routine blood tests for nearly three
years and had become visibly distressed during a recent examination. Her PCL-5 score of 33 places
her in the clinically significant range, and what follows is a portrait of how a single, devastating
period in her life has quietly reorganised the architecture of her daily existence.

Approximately four years ago, Linh's youngest daughter, then nineteen, was admitted to hospital with
a rapidly progressing infection that within forty-eight hours became life-threatening. Linh was
present for much of it: the IV drips threading into her daughter's arms, the nurses drawing blood at
intervals, the monitors, the smell of antiseptic, the particular quality of fluorescent light in a
paediatric intensive care unit. Her daughter survived, but the weeks that followed the discharge
were not relief so much as a delayed collapse. Linh found herself unable to account for what had
happened in any language she trusted. She knew the sequence of events, but when she tried to reach
for it deliberately, the memory seemed to dissolve — she could feel the weight of those days without
being able to reconstruct them clearly, and sometimes the full emotional force of the crisis would
arrive without any accompanying image, a grief with no picture attached. This fragmented quality of
the memory meant that it never felt finished, never felt survivable in the way that a coherent story
might.

The incompleteness of the memory fed directly into her interpretation of what had happened. Linh had
come to believe, with a quiet but consuming certainty, that the hospital had not acted quickly
enough in those first hours — that the people responsible for protecting her daughter had failed,
and that nothing had been done to acknowledge this. She felt the injustice of it as a physical
weight. And because the memory surfaced not as narrative but as sensation, the appraisals it
generated were correspondingly raw: she had been there, she had not insisted loudly enough, she had
failed to protect her own child. These beliefs, once formed, made the triggers she encountered all
the more unbearable. When a customer at the store paid for bandages and she caught sight of blood on
their hand, or when a pharmaceutical supplier delivered a box containing syringes, the sight alone
was enough to pull her back into the ward — not into a clear memory, but into the emotional core of
it, the helplessness and the terror.

The hypervigilance that developed in the months after her daughter's discharge further tightened
this pattern. Linh found she could not relax even in the back room of her own shop, scanning
customers and the street outside as though danger were always imminent. This constant state of alert
made her body exquisitely sensitive to any reminder, so that a routine physical examination at her
GP became almost unbearable, her heart rate spiking the moment the doctor reached for the blood
pressure cuff. The physical reactivity reinforced her sense that something was still fundamentally
wrong — which in turn reactivated the appraisals of institutional failure and personal culpability,
and sharpened her attention to anything that might signal a new threat.

To manage this, Linh developed a set of strategies that brought short-term relief but gradually
narrowed her world. She began asking her husband repeatedly whether she seemed well, whether he
thought she was coping — reassurance that satisfied her for minutes before the doubt returned. She
tried actively to suppress the memory, to push it away whenever it approached, though this effort
paradoxically left her more sensitised to the triggers that might summon it. She began avoiding not
only medical settings but anything that might produce strong feeling of any kind, withdrawing from
family celebrations and the small pleasures of her shop. This emotional blunting meant she was less
equipped to process what had happened, which kept the appraisals of injustice and failure alive and
unresolved, and left her body in a state of readiness that no amount of scanning ever resolved.

What finally brought her to seek help was a missed appointment for a mammogram — the third
consecutive year she had cancelled it. Her husband, who had watched the slow contraction of her life
with increasing worry, sat with her one evening and said, quietly, that he was frightened for her.
Linh told the clinician she did not expect to feel better. What she hoped for, she said, was to be
able to take care of herself without it feeling like walking back into the ward.
