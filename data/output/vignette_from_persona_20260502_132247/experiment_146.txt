Tariq is a 30-year-old Middle Eastern man who works as a tax inspector, recently divorced, and who
presented to the clinic several months after surviving a life-threatening motor vehicle collision.
He was a passenger in a car driven by a friend who, Tariq later learned, had been drinking. The
crash occurred at an intersection when the other driver ran a red light; the impact was sudden, the
sound of crumpling metal and shattering glass immediate and total. Tariq remembers the sharp smell
of alcohol in the enclosed car just before impact, the way the airbag struck his face, and the
particular make and colour of the vehicle that hit them — a dark sedan not unlike dozens he passes
each week. He survived with physical injuries that have since healed, but the psychological
aftermath has not followed the same trajectory.

In the weeks following the collision, Tariq began noticing that certain sensory details from that
evening had not stayed in the past where he felt they belonged. The smell of alcohol — encountered
at a colleague's retirement gathering, or drifting from a restaurant he passed on his lunch break —
would pull him back to the moment before impact with a vividness that felt less like memory and more
like recurrence. Seeing cars with similar damage at the roadside, or vehicles resembling the one
that struck them, produced the same effect. These triggers arrived without warning and, when they
did, Tariq found himself scanning the environment with an intensity he could not switch off —
hyperalert to every sudden sound, braced for impacts that did not come. He began startling badly at
ordinary noises: a door slamming in the open-plan office where he reviews tax files, a colleague
dropping a folder, a car horn from the street below. The threat felt omnipresent, and the triggers
that reactivated his fear only confirmed to him that danger could arrive from any direction at any
moment.

What compounded everything was what Tariq had come to believe about himself in the aftermath. He was
certain, with a quiet but devastating conviction, that he was no longer capable of the things that
had defined him — the focused, methodical competence he had built his professional identity around.
He had once prided himself on reliability and self-sufficiency; now he could not trust his own
reactions, his own steadiness, his own mind. He felt his emotional life had gone somewhere
unreachable, flattened into a numbness that frightened him almost as much as the flashbacks. These
appraisals did not remain passive — they actively sharpened his vigilance, as though his belief that
he could no longer rely on himself demanded that he compensate by watching everything more
carefully. And they made the triggers feel more potent: when a dark sedan pulled alongside him at a
pedestrian crossing, the internal voice that said *you cannot handle this* amplified the sensory
jolt into something close to paralysis.

Avoidance became the architecture of his days. He stopped driving entirely and refused to be a
passenger in any vehicle, which in practical terms meant he began arriving late to field audits,
offering implausible explanations to supervisors, and quietly withdrawing from cases that required
travel. He had not told anyone at work what had happened. To fill the hours when he was not working,
and to keep the images from surfacing, he spent long stretches on his phone — games, forums,
streaming — anything that occupied enough of his attention to forestall the intrusions. He ate less
and less, not from nausea but from a kind of punishing indifference to his own needs. Over time,
these strategies, which had initially offered brief relief, began feeding back into the very beliefs
they were meant to quiet. Each hour lost to a screen, each meal skipped, each journey refused,
confirmed to Tariq that he was diminished — less capable, less present, less himself — and that
confirmation kept him hyperalert, braced, scanning.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at work when a colleague knocked over a metal
wastepaper bin and Tariq, in front of three people, dropped to a crouch behind his desk. The shame
of it was acute. He came to the clinic saying he was not sure he could be fixed, but that he could
not continue living in a body that treated every ordinary afternoon as a crash site.
