Gerald is a 62-year-old married man of European descent who has worked as a bank teller for nearly
two decades. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 58, seeking help approximately fourteen months after
the incident that, in his own words, "broke something in me that hasn't healed."

The event occurred on a busy Friday afternoon at the bank. A long-standing customer — a man Gerald
had greeted by name for years, whose children's savings accounts Gerald had helped open — became
agitated during a transaction dispute. As Gerald leaned across the counter to pass documents, the
man stumbled backward, lost his footing on a recently mopped floor, and struck his head against a
marble pillar. The sound was immediate and terrible. Gerald had reached out instinctively, his hand
grazing the man's shoulder a fraction of a second too late. The customer sustained a serious
traumatic brain injury and spent weeks in intensive care. He survived, but Gerald was told he would
never fully recover. Gerald has replayed that moment of contact — his fingers brushing fabric, the
inadequate reach — thousands of times since.

In the weeks that followed, fragments of the memory began surfacing without warning: the wet sound
of impact, the particular stillness of the man's body on the marble floor, the faces of other
customers frozen in shock. These intrusive pieces arrived suddenly and then vanished, leaving Gerald
shaking at his teller window or gripping the edge of the bathroom sink at home. Each time the memory
returned, the terror he had felt in that moment returned with it — not as recollection but as re-
experience — and alongside the terror came a wave of guilt so acute it felt physical. This
combination of terror and guilt kept Gerald's nervous system in a state of chronic arousal. He found
himself unable to fall asleep without scanning the house, unable to sit quietly in the evenings,
hypervigilant to sounds and movement in a way that exhausted his wife and bewildered him. The
sustained threat state, in turn, seemed to lower the threshold for intrusion: the more alert he
remained, the more readily the memory broke through, and the more it broke through, the more alert
he became.

Gerald returned to work after three weeks, which he now recognises was too soon. The bank
environment — where he stands behind the same counter, handles the same transactions, and sees many
of the same familiar faces — became saturated with cues. Customers he recognised, people connected
even loosely to the man who was injured, triggered a cascade of self-accusation. He had come to
believe, with quiet certainty, that he was inadequate — that a competent person, a person of sound
reflexes and good judgment, would have caught the man in time. When he saw related customers at his
window, this belief crystallised instantly: he was the teller who had failed, and everyone in the
bank knew it. These encounters with known people fed directly into his appraisals of permanent
damage, and those appraisals in turn made him more sensitised to the triggers themselves — he began
scanning the lobby for familiar faces before his shift, dreading the moment of recognition. Even
stress at the counter, the ordinary pressure of a queue or a disputed balance, now carried the
weight of that afternoon, pulling him back toward the memory and the guilt embedded in it.

An unexpected touch became its own particular ordeal. When a colleague placed a hand on his arm to
get his attention one morning, Gerald flinched so violently that the woman stepped back in alarm. He
was unable to explain it to her. He had come to see his reactions — the flinching, the
sleeplessness, the inability to relax even at home on a Sunday — as further evidence of how
thoroughly he had been damaged. He was, he believed, a lousy coper, and this belief had begun to
isolate him from his wife, who could not reach him in the evenings, and from colleagues who had
quietly stopped confiding in him.

What finally brought Gerald to seek help was his wife's tearful observation that she felt she had
lost him. He arrived at the intake appointment hoping, more than anything, to stop feeling like a
man who had irreparably broken both another person and himself.
