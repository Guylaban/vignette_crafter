Rajan is a 42-year-old South Asian man who works as a tax inspector, married with two children, and
presents with a PCL-5 score of 72 following an incident approximately fourteen months ago in which
he accidentally struck a cyclist while pulling out of a car park adjacent to his district office.
The cyclist, a young man, sustained a fractured pelvis and severe head trauma and spent several
weeks in intensive care. He survived, but with lasting neurological impairment. Rajan was not
charged, and colleagues at the revenue office told him repeatedly that it was an accident, that the
sun had been in his eyes, that anyone might have done the same. None of this reached him. From the
moment he climbed out of his car and saw the man lying in the road, something in Rajan's
understanding of himself and his world broke open in a way that has not closed since.

The event itself left a fractured imprint. Rajan can describe certain moments with painful, almost
photographic clarity — the sound of the impact, a low thud followed by a scrape he still hears in
quiet rooms — but other stretches of those minutes are simply gone, as though the film reel was cut.
He does not know exactly how long he stood before calling for help. He is not sure what he said when
he knelt beside the man. These gaps are not a relief to him; they are, he says, a source of dread.
He had come to believe that the missing pieces must contain something worse than what he can
remember — some hesitation, some failure of instinct — and this conviction feeds directly into his
sense of himself as a man who, when it mattered, failed to protect another human being. The
fragmented memory does not soften the horror; it amplifies his appraisals of his own culpability,
and those appraisals in turn seem to sharpen the clarity of the worst moments while leaving the
exculpatory details buried. The auditory echo of the impact, in particular, surfaces without warning
and makes the scene feel immediate and present, pulling him back before he has chosen to return.

When the intrusions come — triggered most acutely by the sight of cars in collision, even minor
fender-benders he passes on the road to his office, or by any encounter with funerals, cemeteries,
or the language of grief — Rajan reaches immediately for a set of mental strategies that have become
as automatic as flinching. He tells himself he deserves to suffer, that self-punishment is the only
honest response to what he has done. He reminds himself, with a kind of grim accounting, that the
man lived, that it could have been a fatality, that he should be grateful. He pushes the thoughts
down with effort, sometimes spending entire audit reviews mentally suppressing the image of the
cyclist's body. These strategies feel, to Rajan, like moral seriousness. In practice, they sustain
and deepen his sense of permanent humiliation — the feeling that he is marked, that everyone who
knows what happened sees the same thing he sees when he looks in the mirror. The self-punishment
thinking in particular forecloses any revision of his beliefs about himself, and the downward
comparisons, rather than providing relief, seem to circle back and reactivate the imagery he is
trying to contain, so that the very act of reminding himself it could have been worse conjures again
the crash, the sound, the road.

His beliefs about how the world works — that care and attention keep others safe, that a decent man
does not destroy a stranger's life on an ordinary Tuesday — have not recovered. He avoids any
conversation about the event at work, and when a colleague once mentioned a road accident in
passing, Rajan excused himself and did not return for the rest of the afternoon. Over the months
this withdrawal has become noticeable; his manager has commented on his distraction during fieldwork
that requires him to drive between client sites, and his wife has described him as absent even when
present.

What finally brought Rajan to seek help was an invitation to a colleague's father's funeral — a
routine, expected social obligation — which he could not bring himself to attend. He told his wife
he was unwell. He has come to therapy not because he believes he can be helped, but because he is
afraid that if he continues as he is, he will lose what remains of the life he had before that
Tuesday. What he most fears, he said quietly in the first session, is that he already has.
