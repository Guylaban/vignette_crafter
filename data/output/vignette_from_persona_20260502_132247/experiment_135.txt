Kwame is a 65-year-old married man of African descent who works at a large home-improvement retail
store on the outskirts of his city. He was referred for psychological assessment following a PCL-5
score of 60, and he arrived at the intake appointment with the measured, deliberate manner of
someone who had spent months trying to hold himself together through sheer discipline.

Approximately eighteen months ago, Kwame was at work when a severe tornado struck without adequate
warning. The storm tore through the building's roof in a matter of seconds, collapsing a section of
shelving in the hardware aisle where he had been restocking inventory. He described the sound — a
low, pressurised roar that seemed to come from everywhere at once — and the sudden darkness as the
power failed, the air filling with debris and the screaming of colleagues he could not see. He
pressed himself against a concrete support pillar for what felt like an eternity, certain he was
about to die. When the storm passed, he emerged to find two coworkers seriously injured and the
building partially destroyed. He was physically unharmed, but something in him, he said quietly, did
not survive that afternoon.

In the weeks that followed, the memory of those minutes inside the store refused to settle into the
past. Kwame described it as a film that plays over and over without his permission — the roar, the
darkness, the debris — and crucially, when the memory surfaces, it carries no temporal distance. He
does not remember the tornado; he is inside it again, his body bracing against the pillar, his heart
hammering as though the roof is still coming down. This quality of the memory as present rather than
past has become one of his most distressing symptoms, and it feeds directly into his sense of
ongoing danger. Because the event feels perpetually current rather than concluded, Kwame has come to
believe with quiet certainty that the world is genuinely and permanently dangerous — not that it was
dangerous on one terrible afternoon, but that threat is the natural condition of existence. He also
believes he has been permanently changed for the worse, that the man who walked into that store
before the storm is simply gone.

These convictions shape almost everything about how Kwame now moves through daily life. Believing he
must be on guard at all times, he enters every room — including the retail floor where he still
works — scanning immediately for exits. He has reorganised his working patterns so that he stations
himself near the garden-centre doors at the far end of the store, where he can see an exit at all
moments. His manager initially accommodated this, but over recent months colleagues have noticed his
reluctance to work in the interior aisles, and he has been passed over for a supervisory role he had
been informally promised. When the hypervigilance becomes overwhelming on the shop floor, he
distracts himself — counting ceiling tiles, focusing on a customer's list — anything to interrupt
the flood of sensation. He also struggles to fall asleep each night, lying awake in the belief that
alertness is the only thing standing between him and catastrophe.

The tragedy of Kwame's situation is that none of these strategies provide relief; they deepen the
problem. Remaining near exits and scanning constantly keeps his nervous system primed, so that
ordinary sounds — a trolley rattling, a door slamming — register as potential signals of disaster
and pull the memory forward again, vivid and immediate. And when the memory surges back with that
quality of happening right now, it confirms for Kwame everything he already fears: that the danger
is real, that he was right to stay alert, that the world cannot be trusted. His maladaptive
strategies, intended to protect him, have become evidence that protection is necessary, reinforcing
the very appraisals that drive them. His wife has noticed that he no longer sits with his back to
the room at family gatherings, and that he leaves restaurants if he cannot see the door. He has not
told her the full extent of what he experiences, because he is afraid that describing it will make
it worse — or that she will confirm his deepest fear, that he is not the man he was.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was a moment at work three weeks ago when a sudden rainstorm
produced a sound on the metal roof that sent him to his knees in the aisle, hands over his head,
before he had any conscious awareness of what he was doing. A young colleague helped him up without
a word, and the shame of that moment was, he said, the first thing that had felt stronger than the
fear. He is not certain therapy can help him. What he most hopes for, in his own careful words, is
simply to feel that what happened is over.
