Dorothy is a 68-year-old retired woman of North American background who presented for psychological
assessment approximately eight months after the sudden death of her adult son, Michael, who
collapsed from an undetected cardiac arrhythmia while visiting her home for a Sunday dinner. Dorothy
had been the one to find him on the kitchen floor, had called 911, and had attempted CPR until
paramedics arrived. He was pronounced dead at the scene. Her PCL-5 score of 66 places her firmly in
the severe range, and the clinical picture that has emerged across the intake and early sessions is
one of a woman whose grief has been overtaken by a pervasive, tightening sense of danger and self-
condemnation.

In the weeks that followed Michael's death, Dorothy found herself returning again and again to the
same question: why hadn't she noticed something was wrong sooner? She had spoken to him only minutes
before he collapsed. She had poured him a glass of water. She had been in the next room. She had
come to believe, with a certainty that resisted all reassurance, that a more attentive mother would
have seen the signs — that she had failed to protect her own child when it mattered most. This
conviction of culpable failure did not remain contained. It spread outward into a broader sense that
she no longer fit anywhere in the world she had previously inhabited — that the Dorothy who attended
neighbourhood dinners and hosted family gatherings had ceased to exist, replaced by someone who had
done something unforgivable and therefore had no right to belong. She described feeling as though
she were watching her old life through glass, close enough to see it but permanently excluded.

These appraisals — the belief in her own failure, the felt sense of exile — drove Dorothy toward a
pattern of withdrawal that has structured her days ever since. She began declining invitations from
the couples she and her husband had socialised with for decades, finding the prospect of sitting
across from people who knew Michael, who might ask how she was doing or offer condolences,
intolerable. She stopped attending her church group, where several members had known him since
childhood. In their place, she filled her hours with relentless activity — reorganising closets,
managing household accounts, researching nothing in particular online late into the night — anything
that would prevent her mind from settling. Her husband, Robert, has grown quietly worried; he told
her recently that they haven't had a real conversation in months, and Dorothy acknowledged this with
a flatness that suggested she had already accepted it as the cost of getting through each day.

What Dorothy had not anticipated was that the busyness and isolation, rather than quieting her
nervous system, seemed to keep it perpetually primed. Her body remained on alert — muscles braced,
jaw tight, a low-grade vigilance that never fully lifted even in the evenings when she sat alone in
a house that was, by any objective measure, safe. She was unable to relax, and the inability to
relax in turn confirmed her fear that something terrible was still imminent, that the world had
revealed itself as a place where catastrophe arrives without warning and she, specifically, is not
someone capable of preventing it. Strong waves of fear, horror, and guilt moved through her without
apparent trigger, and each time they did, they seemed to reinforce the conclusion she had already
reached about herself — that she was set apart, that she belonged nowhere, that her failure was not
a single moment but a defining truth. The loop between her physical state of alarm and her self-
condemning beliefs had become self-sustaining: the threat her body registered fed the appraisals,
and the appraisals kept the threat alive.

What finally brought Dorothy to seek help was a conversation with her daughter, who told her quietly
that she was frightened of losing her mother as well — not to death, but to disappearance. Dorothy
wept for the first time in months. She came to the first session saying she did not expect to feel
better, but that she was afraid, if she continued as she had been, there would be nothing left of
her worth saving. That fear, fragile and honest, is where the clinical work begins.
