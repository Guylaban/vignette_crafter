Loretta is a 69-year-old married woman of Indigenous North American heritage who has worked the land
most of her adult life, employed seasonally on a large agricultural operation in the rural Midwest.
She presents with a PCL-5 score of 67, indicating severe posttraumatic stress, following a sexual
assault that occurred approximately fourteen months ago. She was assaulted by a male acquaintance
during a shared ride on a rural bus route she had taken regularly for years to reach the fields
where she worked. The assault happened in the back of an almost-empty bus on an overcast Tuesday
morning, the smell of diesel and damp upholstery fixed in her memory alongside the particular angle
of pale light through smeared windows. Since that morning, the bus — any bus — has become unbearable
to her. She stopped taking the route within days of the assault, and within a few weeks had arranged
for her husband to drive her to work, a change that strained their schedule and quietly reshaped
their mornings into something tense and unspoken.

The intrusive memories began almost immediately. Without warning, the scene returns to her — not as
a narrative she can observe, but as something happening again, pressing in with the full weight of
the original terror. These intrusions arrive most violently when she is on or near public transport,
but they are not confined there. Certain conversation topics — any discussion of safety, of
strangers, of what women do or should do alone — can pull the memory forward with the same sudden
force. When the memory surfaces, Loretta's body responds as though the threat is present, and she
has come to rely on alcohol in the evenings to dull the edges of a day that has left her raw. She
also takes over-the-counter sleep medication more often than directed. She is aware, in a quiet and
ashamed way, that these habits have grown, but she reasons that without them she would not sleep at
all, and so she continues.

What she has come to believe about herself in the aftermath of the assault is perhaps the most
corrosive part of her presentation. Loretta is certain that she can no longer rely on her own
judgment — the assault happened because she trusted someone she should not have, and that failure of
perception feels to her like evidence of something permanently broken in her. She experiences
herself as an outsider now, cut off from the women she works alongside, from her husband, from the
ordinary fabric of community life. This conviction that she is set apart and cannot trust herself
feeds directly back into her vigilance: because she believes she cannot read danger accurately, she
must compensate by monitoring everything. She checks behind her constantly while walking between the
farm buildings, scanning for anyone approaching from behind, and she has reduced the places where
she feels safe to a narrow circuit — her kitchen, one corner of the farmyard visible from the back
window, nowhere else. That constricted sense of safety in turn sharpens the triggers, so that even
an overheard conversation about a bus route at work can bring the memory flooding back.

The threat monitoring and the maladaptive strategies have woven together in ways that maintain
rather than relieve her distress. When she rehearses worst-case scenarios — something she does
deliberately, telling herself it is preparation — the mental rehearsal keeps her nervous system
primed, so that ordinary stimuli register as warnings and the intrusions find fertile ground. The
alcohol that was meant to quieten the evenings has begun to lower her threshold for distress the
following day, making her more reactive, more easily startled, more likely to be ambushed by memory.
She avoids people beyond the minimum required by work, and that avoidance has meant that the
conversations which might have interrupted her isolation — with her husband, with a longtime
coworker — never happen.

Loretta came to seek help after her husband found her sitting in the dark kitchen at 3 a.m., unable
to explain herself. She told her primary care physician the following week, in flat, careful
language, that she was not doing well. What she is most afraid of is that the woman she was before
the assault — competent, rooted, known to herself — is simply gone. What she hopes, though she does
not yet say it plainly, is that someone might tell her otherwise.
