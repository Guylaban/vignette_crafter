Kwame is a 78-year-old Ghanaian-born man who has lived in the United States for the better part of
four decades, working for the last eighteen years as a librarian at a mid-sized public library in a
quiet midwestern city. He is married, soft-spoken, and widely regarded by colleagues as a man of
careful habits and deep reserves of patience. He presented for psychological assessment with a PCL-5
score of 69, describing himself, with characteristic understatement, as "no longer quite right
inside."

The event that reorganised Kwame's inner life occurred several years ago when he was visiting family
in Accra. He had gone with his nephew to a crowded open-air market on a Saturday morning — the kind
of place he had known since childhood, loud and warm and dense with movement. A bomb detonated in
the market's eastern corridor. Kwame was close enough to be thrown from his feet by the pressure
wave. When he rose, the scene around him was one of smoke, screaming, and bodies. He helped carry a
woman whose legs were no longer intact. He held her hand until she died. The market, the press of
people, the ordinary Saturday noise — all of it had been the context of catastrophe, and his nervous
system encoded every detail with terrible precision.

His memories of that morning are, as he describes them, almost unbearably clear — not faded with
time but sharpened, as though the event is still in the process of happening somewhere inside him.
The images replay like footage he cannot stop: the smoke thickening, the woman's face, the
particular quality of the screaming. Because the memory retains this unprocessed, present-tense
quality, it does not wait for Kwame to invite it. Something in his surroundings — a sound, a smell,
a flash of movement at the edge of his vision — can collapse the distance between then and now
without warning, returning him fully to the market corridor. This quality of the memory, its
immediacy and its hair-trigger accessibility, means that a wide range of ordinary stimuli have
become potent cues.

At work in the library, this is most visible. Certain conversation topics — violence, political
extremism, suffering abroad — can arrive across the reference desk without warning, and when they
do, the memory surfaces instantly and completely. Witnessing someone behave badly, even in the minor
register of a patron being rude or a child knocking over a display, carries for Kwame a moral charge
that connects, through some associative pathway he cannot fully articulate, to the senseless cruelty
of that morning. The anniversary of the attack arrives each year as a period of particular dread.
When these triggers activate the memory, Kwame has learned — or rather, his body has learned on his
behalf — to withdraw. He avoids crowded places now: the shopping centres his wife frequents, the
public events his colleagues invite him to, the busy parts of the library during peak hours. He
drifts, sometimes, into a private interior world, a kind of willed dissociation that offers
temporary relief from the present. And he has come to refuse situations that might generate
happiness or hope, as though good feeling is a surface too thin to trust.

What Kwame has not fully recognised is the way his strategies have begun to sustain the very problem
they were built to manage. By withdrawing from crowds and from positive engagement, he has narrowed
his life to a set of controlled, low-stimulus environments — and within those environments, the
absence of competing experience means the memory occupies more of the available space. The avoidance
itself seems to keep the memory close, rehearsed, ready. And because he is always managing his
exposure, always scanning for what might need to be escaped, the conditions that once triggered the
memory now include almost any deviation from his carefully maintained routine. Ordinary disruptions
— a patron raising their voice, an unexpected crowd in the periodicals section — have become
triggers in part because avoidance has made the world feel perpetually unsafe.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was his wife's quiet, sustained grief over what she
described as losing him to a room she could not enter. He arrived hoping, above all, to be less
afraid of his own mind — and afraid, most of all, that it may already be too late.
