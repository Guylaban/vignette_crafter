Rosario is a 59-year-old divorced Latina woman who has worked as a physical therapist for over two
decades, a career she chose, she once told a colleague, because she understood bodies that had been
hurt and needed careful handling. She came to her first intake appointment neatly dressed, her chair
pulled deliberately close to the wall, her eyes moving to the door each time someone passed in the
corridor outside. She had a PCL-5 score of 46, and she described herself, with some embarrassment,
as a woman who had managed fine for most of her adult life and was only now, at 59, beginning to
feel as though something had caught up with her.

The trauma that shapes Rosario's current presentation is not a single event but a sustained one:
years of physical abuse at the hands of her mother, beginning in early childhood and continuing
through adolescence. Her mother was unpredictable and explosive, and the household was one in which
Rosario learned to read the room constantly — the sound of footsteps, the smell of alcohol, the
particular quality of silence that preceded violence. She was hit with hands, belts, and whatever
was nearby, often without warning, and she learned that safety was provisional and that other
people's bodies moving toward her meant danger. These lessons, absorbed before she had language for
them, have never fully left her.

For most of her adult life, Rosario managed by staying busy and staying in control. Her memory of
the abuse has always been strange to her — she can recount certain incidents with clinical
detachment, as though describing something that happened to a woman she read about, the emotional
content drained away entirely. At other times, fragments arrive without invitation: the particular
crack of a belt, the sensation of a wall at her back, a child's high-pitched crying that may or may
not have been her own. These sudden intrusive pieces feel disconnected from any coherent narrative,
and the whole of it carries a quality of unreality — the memories are so extreme, she has said, that
part of her still cannot fully accept they belong to her life. When these fragments surface, they do
not resolve into understanding; instead they leave her raw and disoriented, and it is in those
moments that she finds herself reaching for the behaviours that have long served as her regulation:
cleaning the counters until her hands ache, eating past fullness, cancelling plans that might carry
any emotional weight. The memory, in other words, drives her directly toward the strategies she has
built to manage what the memory stirs.

The memory also feeds her sense of threat in the present. When a fragment surfaces — unbidden,
without context — it seems to confirm for Rosario that danger is not historical but ongoing, that
the world remains the kind of place where harm arrives without warning. She becomes hypervigilant in
its wake, scanning her environment with the same exhausting attentiveness she practised as a child.
She wakes in the night to check her apartment, moving through rooms she has already locked. At her
clinic, where patients approach her from behind and strangers sometimes enter the waiting room, she
has rearranged her schedule so that she works only in rooms where she can position herself with her
back to the wall — a habit her supervisor has noticed and gently questioned. The threat she
perceives, once activated, loops back into memory: the state of high alert reactivates the sensory
residue of childhood, and fragments that might otherwise have stayed dormant are shaken loose again.

The threat itself also sustains her avoidance. When she feels unsafe — in a crowded grocery store,
in a staff meeting where she cannot see the door — she withdraws, cancels, cleans. The compulsive
ordering of her apartment, which she does for hours after a difficult shift, gives her a sense of
control that her nervous system cannot otherwise locate. But this avoidance keeps her from the
ordinary exposures that might gradually disconfirm the threat, and the cleaning and overeating that
follow a bad night leave her feeling ashamed and out of control, which in turn brings the memories
closer — the self-contempt, the bodily shame, the sense that she is still that child who could not
make herself safe.

What finally brought Rosario to seek help was a moment at work three months ago when a patient
reached across her unexpectedly and she flinched so violently that she dropped her equipment and had
to leave the room. She stood in the hallway, heart pounding, and understood that she could no longer
manage alone. What she fears most is that the memories are not the past at all. What she hopes,
though she has not yet said it plainly, is that someone might help her believe otherwise.
