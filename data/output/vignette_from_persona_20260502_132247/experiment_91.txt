Viktor Hale, a 56-year-old married combat pilot with over three decades of military aviation
experience, presented for psychological assessment following a life-threatening accident that
occurred approximately fourteen months prior. During a routine training sortie, Viktor's aircraft
suffered a catastrophic engine failure at low altitude. The cockpit filled with acrid smoke, warning
alarms fired in rapid succession, and the aircraft entered an uncontrolled descent. Viktor ejected
seconds before impact. The explosion that followed — a concussive wall of sound and heat that he
felt in his chest before he had fully registered what was happening — left him suspended under a
parachute, watching his aircraft burn on the ground below. He landed hard, sustaining spinal
compression injuries, and lay in the field for nearly twenty minutes before rescue personnel reached
him. It is from that specific constellation of sensory experience — the dizziness of the ejection
sequence, the physical nausea of freefall, the detonation below him — that his current symptom
profile has grown.

In the months following the accident, Viktor began noticing that certain experiences would return
him, without warning, to that field. A sudden loud bang — a car backfiring near the base, a door
slamming in a hangar — would collapse the distance between present and past, and his body would
respond as though the explosion had just occurred: heart hammering, muscles braced, vision
narrowing. Equally destabilising was dizziness, which he encountered regularly given the vestibular
demands of his profession. A moment of lightheadedness during a flight simulator session would
ignite the full physiological alarm of the ejection, and he would find himself gripping the controls
not as a pilot but as a man falling. Feeling physically unwell in any form — nausea, fatigue,
general malaise — carried the same freight, because those sensations had been the texture of those
twenty minutes on the ground, certain he might not survive. These triggers drove his body into a
state of relentless readiness: muscles perpetually tensed, attention scanning every peripheral
movement, a startle response so hair-triggered that colleagues had begun to notice. Viktor was
certain the danger was not over, that his nervous system was simply reading the environment
correctly, and that relaxing his vigilance would be the mistake that finally killed him.

It was the appraisals that had formed around the accident, however, that gave his avoidance its
particular shape. Viktor had come to believe that the world was fundamentally and irreversibly
dangerous — not in the abstract way a risk-briefing describes danger, but in a visceral, cellular
way that had dismantled everything he had previously understood about control, competence, and
survival. His beliefs about his own mastery, about the reliability of machines and bodies, had been
destroyed in those seconds of freefall. What frightened him most was the accompanying sense that he
felt nothing — or rather, that what he felt was a kind of interior deadness, as though the man who
had once loved flying had been incinerated along with the aircraft. These appraisals pressed him
toward avoidance with a logic that felt unassailable: if the world was dangerous and his body an
unreliable instrument, then any encounter with medical settings — hospitals, ambulances, even a
routine physical examination — was an invitation to confirm the worst. He declined a mandatory
flight medical, citing scheduling conflicts, and had not seen a physician since his discharge from
acute care. He had also quietly given up the activities that had once anchored his off-duty life:
long trail runs, weekend flying with civilian clubs, dinners with his wife's extended family. What
remained was a stripped-down daily existence, and he had begun rigidly controlling his eating as one
of the few domains where he felt any agency at all. Yet this withdrawal, over time, had not quieted
the system — it had starved him of the corrective experiences that might have begun to loosen the
threat appraisals, and the absence of normal life had itself become evidence that something was
permanently wrong with him. The avoidance kept the triggers potent and the threat state elevated;
the constricted life confirmed his belief that he was already, in some meaningful sense, dead
inside.

Viktor sought help after his wife told him she no longer recognised him. He came to the first
session guarded and precise, a man accustomed to debriefs rather than disclosures. What he most
feared was that treatment would confirm the deadness was permanent. What he most hoped — though he
offered this only at the end of the session, quietly — was that he might one day sit in a cockpit
again and feel something other than terror.
