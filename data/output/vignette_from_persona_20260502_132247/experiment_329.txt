Linh is a 74-year-old Vietnamese American woman, married and working as a software engineer, who was
referred for psychological assessment following a prolonged period of sleep disturbance,
hypervigilance, and intrusive distress. Her presenting PCL-5 score of 75 reflects a severe and
pervasive symptom profile rooted in an event that occurred approximately three years ago, when her
youngest son was admitted to the intensive care unit following a sudden cardiac event. He survived,
but the weeks Linh spent at his bedside left a mark she has not been able to shake.

The crisis began without warning on an ordinary evening. Linh had been in the kitchen when she heard
her son go quiet — an absence of sound where there had been movement — and when she found him
unresponsive on the floor, the silence of that moment became the silence of everything that
followed: the ICU corridor at 3 a.m., the hush between the beeping of monitors, the held breath of
waiting for news. The smell of antiseptic that saturated her clothes and hair for weeks became
inseparable from those hours of not knowing whether her child would live. Even the sound of cars in
the hospital parking lot — the muffled arrivals and departures of other families in crisis — wove
itself into the sensory fabric of that period.

In the years since, these sensory traces have become the primary doorways back into the trauma. When
Linh is working late and the office grows quiet around her, or when she steps into a clinic for a
routine appointment and the antiseptic smell reaches her before anything else, she is pulled without
transition into the full terror of those ICU nights. The emotional intensity returns completely —
her heart rate surges, her hands go cold — and with it comes a wave of shame so acute it feels like
a verdict. She had come to believe, in the months after her son's crisis, that she had missed signs
she should have caught, that a more attentive mother would have noticed his fatigue sooner, that the
event had happened because of how she had acted. These convictions surface with particular force in
the moments after a trigger, as though the sensory cue does not merely recall the memory but
reinstates the entire moral weight of it.

That weight, in turn, has reshaped how Linh reads the world. She has come to feel with quiet
certainty that the world is a dangerous place and that she has no reliable power to prevent harm
from reaching the people she loves. This sense of helplessness does not stay abstract — it animates
her body. She lies awake at night certain that something is happening that she is not yet aware of,
that the silence of the house means something is wrong. She has not slept through the night in over
two years. At work, she keeps her office door open so she can hear movement in the corridor; she
checks her phone compulsively during meetings, scanning for messages from her son or husband. The
hypervigilance that began as a response to perceived danger has itself become a source of re-
exposure: her constant scanning of the environment means she is rarely more than a moment away from
noticing a silence, catching a clinical smell on a colleague's coat, or hearing a car door close in
the parking structure below her office window — and each of these sends her back.

The loop has tightened in ways that have cost her concretely. She has begun declining work travel,
partly because airports carry the antiseptic smell of medical facilities and partly because being
away from home intensifies her certainty that something catastrophic is imminent. Her manager has
noticed her distraction. Her husband, who does not fully understand what is happening, has started
treating her with a careful gentleness that she experiences as confirmation that she is not herself.
Occasionally, without any clear image or narrative, the full emotional weight of her son's
hospitalization descends on her without warning — a wave of grief and terror that arrives without a
face.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a moment at her desk when she realised she had been
staring at a blank screen for forty minutes, unable to remember what she had been working on, her
body still braced for something she could not name. She came in hoping, she said quietly, that she
might one day be able to sit in a silent room without being afraid.
