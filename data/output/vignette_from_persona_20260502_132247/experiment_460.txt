Tane is a 48-year-old Māori man who works as a roofer, married for over two decades, and until
recently considered himself someone who had long since made peace with the harder parts of his life.
He presented for psychological assessment with a PCL-5 score of 47, describing a clinical picture
that had been quietly consolidating for years before finally becoming impossible to manage.

The assault occurred several years ago. Tane had been at a work function — a late evening gathering
at a colleague's home — and had drunk more than usual. He remembers the smell of alcohol on the man
who cornered him in a back room, the sound of a motorcycle idling outside that seemed to stretch
time into something unbearable, and the particular helplessness of a body that would not do what he
told it to. He did not tell anyone. He drove home, showered, and returned to work the following
Monday. He had convinced himself, with the kind of discipline that had served him well on rooftops
and in hard weather, that it was something he could simply not think about.

What he did not anticipate was the way the world kept finding him. A news segment about sexual
violence — the kind that appears without warning between weather and sport — would stop him cold in
his living room, and within seconds he would be somewhere else entirely, the memory arriving not as
recollection but as presence, vivid and immediate in a way that ordinary memories simply were not.
He described it as the event being more real than anything that had actually happened that day.
Sometimes there was no image at all, only a wave of emotion — grief, shame, something that felt like
drowning — arriving without context or warning. And when he did remember clearly, what followed was
an overwhelming grief that he could neither explain to his wife nor outrun. These memory intrusions,
in turn, sharpened his vigilance to the very cues that had preceded them: the smell of alcohol at a
family barbecue, the sound of a motorcycle passing on the street outside a job site, a headline
glimpsed on a phone screen. Each trigger fed the memory, and each memory primed him to find the next
trigger faster.

In response to this, Tane built a life structured around prevention. He began refusing jobs that
required prolonged physical exertion in heat — the kind of full-body exhaustion that reminded him of
that night, of a body beyond his control — telling his crew he had a back problem. He started
monitoring his food intake with a precision that surprised even him, eating by strict rule, finding
in that control something the rest of his life no longer offered. And he worked. He scheduled
himself into every available hour, took on extra contracts, stayed on rooftops until the light
failed, because busyness was the only reliable way to keep the memories from surfacing. But these
strategies, over time, began to work against him in ways he had not foreseen. The rigid avoidance of
physical stress meant he never processed the somatic dimension of what had happened; the controlled
eating, though it gave momentary relief, left him feeling brittle and oddly exposed when routines
were disrupted; and the relentless busyness, while suppressing intrusions during the day, seemed to
make the evenings — when the work stopped and the house went quiet — into ambushes. His strategies
had become their own triggers, the moments when control slipped revealing the very memories the
control was meant to contain.

His marriage had become quieter in ways neither he nor his wife had named directly. She had noticed
his distance, his refusal of certain social events where alcohol was present, the way he sometimes
left the room when the television news ran certain stories. He had told her nothing. What finally
brought him to seek help was his eldest son's twenty-first birthday party, where the smell of beer
and the sound of motorcycles arriving in the driveway converged at once, and Tane found himself
sitting in his car for forty minutes unable to go back inside. He wants, more than anything, to stop
being ambushed by his own life. What he fears most is that there is no version of himself that gets
to come home intact.
