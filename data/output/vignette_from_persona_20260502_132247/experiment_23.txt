Aroha Tane is a 58-year-old Māori woman, divorced, working as a practicing psychiatrist in a mid-
sized American city. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 37 following a life-threatening road traffic
accident that occurred approximately fourteen months ago. She was driving home from a late hospital
shift when another vehicle ran a red light and struck her car on the driver's side at speed. The
impact was sudden and total — the sound of metal folding, the sensation of the car spinning, glass
across her face, and then a period she cannot account for, during which she was apparently conscious
but has no continuous memory. She remembers fragments: the smell of something burning, a voice she
couldn't locate, her own hands on the steering wheel that was no longer where the steering wheel
should have been. What she cannot do, even now, is tell herself a coherent story of what happened.
The memories have no reliable sequence — she cannot say with certainty what came before the impact
and what came after, and the gaps between fragments feel, to her, more frightening than the
fragments themselves.

In the weeks that followed, Aroha noticed that the memory did not behave like other memories. It did
not stay in the past. Something in her surroundings — the angle of headlights in her rearview
mirror, a news segment showing a crumpled vehicle, an unwanted thought surfacing without invitation
— could instantly return her to the inside of that car with a completeness that felt less like
remembering and more like re-entering. These intrusions reinforced what she had quietly begun to
believe: that she had been permanently altered. She had spent thirty years helping patients
understand the relationship between mind and body under stress, and now she found herself unable to
apply that knowledge to herself — which she read not as a normal human limitation but as evidence of
something broken. She came to doubt whether she could trust her own judgment, professionally and
personally. The world, which she had always navigated with a clinician's careful rationalism, now
felt structurally unsafe.

Those appraisals — of permanent damage, of a dangerous world, of a self that could no longer be
relied upon — did not remain abstract. They drove her behavior in ways that accumulated over months.
Because she was certain she could not trust herself to react correctly under pressure, she began
suppressing her emotional responses with deliberate effort, particularly at work, where she felt the
stakes of any visible disturbance were highest. She stopped approaching sleep willingly, associating
lying in the dark with the loss of control she had experienced in the car; she would delay going to
bed for hours, and when she did lie down, she would wake repeatedly during the night to check that
she was safe, positioning herself with her back against the headboard so she could see the room. She
withdrew from physical intimacy entirely — the vulnerability of closeness had become intolerable.

The effort to suppress her emotions and avoid sleep, however, did not quieten the memory. If
anything, the strategies kept her in a state of chronic physiological arousal that made the
fragmented recollections more accessible, not less. Hypervigilance — the constant scanning of rooms,
the startle response that made her colleagues glance at her when a door slammed — kept her nervous
system primed in a way that lowered the threshold for intrusion. Lying awake in the night, alert and
checking, she was more likely to encounter the unbidden images of cars hitting by accident, the
confused sequence of that evening returning in pieces. And each time the memory broke through, it
confirmed what she feared: that she was not recovering, that the damage was real, that the world in
which she drove to work each morning was one in which catastrophe could arrive without warning. That
confirmation, in turn, made the triggers more potent — trauma-related events that might once have
registered as background noise now carried the full weight of her appraisals, and the cycle
tightened.

Aroha finally sought help after a session with one of her own patients — a young man describing a
road accident — during which she recognized, with a psychiatrist's precision and a survivor's dread,
exactly what was happening to her. What she fears most is that she is too far inside the process to
be helped. What she hopes, more quietly, is that she is wrong.
