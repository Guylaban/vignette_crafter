Rodrigo is a 35-year-old Latin American musician, married, who presents with a PCL-5 score of 44
following a life-threatening accident that occurred approximately fourteen months ago. He was
driving home late after a recording session when a driver ran a red light and struck his vehicle at
high speed on the passenger side. Rodrigo was conscious throughout, trapped in the wreckage for
nearly forty minutes, aware of the smell of fuel and the sound of metal settling around him. The
other driver was cited but faced no meaningful legal consequence, a fact that has never left
Rodrigo's mind. When he was finally extracted and taken to hospital, he was told he had come within
inches of a more catastrophic injury. He was discharged within days, and for a time he told himself
he was fine.

The months that followed told a different story. Rodrigo found that when he sat down and
deliberately tried to reconstruct what had happened — to make sense of it, to place it in order — he
could not. The memory refused to cohere. What came instead were fragments: the white glare of
headlights, a sensation of weightlessness, the voice of a paramedic whose face he cannot picture.
These pieces arrived without sequence or context, and the gaps between them felt enormous. He had
the persistent, unsettling sense that he was only remembering parts of what happened and that the
rest had been taken from him. This fractured quality of the memory fed directly into his
interpretation of events: if he could not hold the full picture, then perhaps what had been done to
him was even worse than he could consciously access. The incompleteness of the memory deepened his
conviction that something profoundly unjust had occurred and that no one had been held accountable
for it.

These appraisals — that his life had been destroyed, that a terrible wrong had gone unanswered —
became the lens through which he viewed almost everything. He had come to believe that the rage he
felt toward the other driver was not a normal response to injustice but evidence of something
dangerous inside him, a force he was certain he could not control if it were ever fully released.
This fear of his own anger became a reason, in his mind, to keep the accident sealed away. Thinking
about the event, or allowing others to draw him into conversation about it, felt like opening a door
he could not close again. So he stopped. He deflected questions from his wife with brief
reassurances. He declined to discuss the accident with anyone. And he began filling every available
hour with other people — bandmates, friends, extended family — so that he was never alone long
enough for his own thoughts to find him. The constant socialising was not pleasure; it was
management.

The irony was that keeping the memory at arm's length through avoidance and relentless company only
ensured it stayed broken. Because Rodrigo never sat with the fragments long enough to work through
them, they remained disconnected and unprocessed, and the gaps in the memory continued to feel
threatening rather than simply incomplete. The fragmented, inaccessible quality of what he did
remember, in turn, renewed his sense that his life had been altered in ways he could not fully see
or name — that something irreplaceable had been taken from him and never returned. In his work as a
musician, where solitude and introspection had once been essential to his creative process, he now
found he could not be alone in a studio without the anxiety rising. Sessions that used to last hours
were cut short. Collaborations he once welcomed now served mainly as cover. His wife had noticed the
change and had said so, more than once, and the distance between them had grown in ways neither had
named directly.

What finally brought Rodrigo to seek help was a moment at a family gathering when a relative,
meaning no harm, asked him casually how he was doing after the accident. He felt the anger rise so
fast and so completely that he left the room without speaking. Standing outside, he was shaken — not
by what he had done, but by how close it had felt. He came to the first session hoping, above all,
that he was not the person he was afraid he had become.
