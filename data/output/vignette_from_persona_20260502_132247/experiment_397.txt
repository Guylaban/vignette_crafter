Linh Nguyen is a 28-year-old Vietnamese American tax consultant who presented for psychological
assessment approximately seven months after an experience of captivity that left her PCL-5 score at
74, placing her firmly in the severe range of post-traumatic stress. She is single, lives alone in a
one-bedroom apartment, and described herself, before the trauma, as someone who prided herself on
competence and self-sufficiency.

The incident occurred when Linh was returning to her car in a parking structure after working late
at her firm. Two men forced her into a vehicle at gunpoint, and she was held for nearly eighteen
hours in a residential basement before being released. During that time she was physically
restrained, moved between rooms without warning, and subjected to repeated unpredictable intrusions
— men entering the space at irregular intervals, sometimes silently, sometimes loudly. She was
released without explanation and without any of the men ever facing legal consequences, a fact the
police confirmed to her weeks later when the investigation stalled.

Since her release, Linh has developed a vigilance that organises her entire day. At her office,
where she sits in an open-plan workspace, she has quietly maneuvered her desk chair so that her back
is always against a partition wall and she can see the full length of the floor. Colleagues have
noticed but said nothing. When a coworker rounds a corner quickly or a door swings open
unexpectedly, her body responds before her mind can intervene — a sharp flinch, a surge of
adrenaline, her hands going still over the keyboard. At home, her evenings are spent in a particular
corner of her couch, her back to the wall, listening for sounds at the front door — a creak, a
footfall on the landing, anything that might mean someone is entering. She has begun sleeping with a
chair wedged under her door handle.

What the heightened threat monitoring does, in ways Linh finds harder to articulate but no less
real, is feed a corrosive internal narrative about herself. Each time she flinches at a sudden
movement in the office, she reads her own reaction as evidence — proof that she is broken, that
something essential in her has been permanently altered. She had come to believe, with a quiet and
terrible certainty, that the captivity had happened because of the way she had acted: that she had
been too trusting walking to her car alone, too slow to react, too compliant once restrained. In her
mind, her body's ongoing failure to feel safe confirmed this logic — if she had been stronger,
smarter, more capable, none of it would have happened, and she would not now be sitting at a desk
flinching at shadows. The shame she carries about what was done to her body during those eighteen
hours sits alongside this self-blame without displacing it; they occupy the same interior space,
reinforcing each other in silence. And the knowledge that the men responsible face no consequences —
that they are, as far as she knows, entirely free — makes the self-blame worse, because if no
external accounting exists, then the only explanation that remains is her own culpability.

The vigilance and the self-reproach have together narrowed her world considerably. She has declined
two client dinners, avoided the parking structure her firm uses, and stopped answering her door
without first checking the peephole twice. A friendship with a colleague has cooled because Linh
cancelled plans repeatedly without explanation, and she has noticed her supervisor commenting on
errors in her work — errors she attributes to the hours she spends listening, scanning, bracing.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a moment at the office three weeks ago when a fire drill
sent the building into sudden noise and movement and she found herself pressed against the wall in
the stairwell, unable to move, certain something terrible was happening. A colleague stayed with her
until she could breathe again. She said, quietly, that she was most afraid the clinician would
confirm what she already suspected — that this was her fault, and that she would feel this way
forever.
