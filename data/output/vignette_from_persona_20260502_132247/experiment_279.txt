Minh Tran is a fifty-one-year-old Vietnamese American policy analyst, married and living in the
suburbs of Sacramento, California. He was referred for psychological assessment following a visit to
his primary care physician, who noted that Minh had been presenting with persistent somatic
complaints and what Minh himself described, with some embarrassment, as "losing my grip." His PCL-5
score of 55 placed him firmly in the clinical range for PTSD, and the picture that emerged across
the intake and early sessions was one of a man whose inner world had been quietly reorganising
itself around a single catastrophic night for the better part of two years.

The event occurred in the late summer, when a fast-moving wildfire swept through the foothills east
of Sacramento with almost no warning. Minh and his wife had been asleep when a neighbor pounded on
their door just after 2 a.m. The sky, he recalled, was already orange. There was almost no time —
they grabbed documents, a bag, the dog — and as they drove down the hill, the smoke was so thick and
acrid that Minh could barely see the road. At one point, a tree fell across the lane ahead and he
had to reverse in near-zero visibility, his wife screaming, the dog howling, certain that they were
going to die. They survived. Their home survived, by a margin of perhaps two streets. Many of their
neighbors were not so fortunate.

In the months that followed, Minh found that certain experiences would pull him back into that night
without warning. The smell of smoke — from a neighbor's firepit, from a passing car with engine
trouble, even from a coworker's jacket on a cold morning — could instantly return him to the
sensation of driving blind down that hill. Because the disaster had arrived without any signal he
had been able to read, unpredictable situations had become acutely threatening: a sudden change in a
meeting agenda, an unexpected detour on his commute, any moment in which he could not anticipate
what came next would produce a surge of dread that felt entirely disproportionate. And when he felt
physically unwell — a headache, fatigue, the faint nausea that had become a near-daily companion —
his body seemed to interpret the sensation as a warning, as if illness were the precursor to
something worse.

Each time these triggers fired, they fed directly into a set of beliefs that had taken root after
the fire and that Minh found deeply distressing to articulate. He had come to believe, with a quiet
but persistent certainty, that he could not rely on himself — that the man who had frozen for
several seconds at the wheel that night, who had needed his wife to shout at him to move, was not
someone capable of protecting his family. He was increasingly convinced that his reactions since the
fire — the startle responses, the difficulty concentrating during policy briefings, the moments of
dissociation he struggled to describe — meant that something had broken inside him, that he was, as
he put it haltingly, "going crazy." And because he could not trust himself and could not trust the
world to behave predictably, he had come to feel that constant vigilance was not a symptom but a
rational response — that he had to monitor everything, always, because catastrophe could arrive
again without warning.

What made Minh's presentation particularly self-reinforcing was the way these appraisals looped back
to amplify his sensitivity to triggers. His conviction that he must remain perpetually alert meant
that he scanned his environment continuously — and in doing so, he encountered smoke smells,
unpredictable moments, and physical sensations more frequently and with greater intensity than he
might otherwise have. At work, where policy discussions regularly involved emergency preparedness
scenarios and where the open-plan office offered no warning before a colleague appeared at his
shoulder, this hypervigilance was constant. His inability to predict when a trigger would arrive
only deepened his belief that he could not rely on himself to manage when it did.

What finally brought Minh to seek help was a presentation he gave to a state legislative committee —
a room that smelled faintly of a burning candle — in which he lost his train of thought entirely for
nearly a minute. He stood there, in front of people whose professional respect he valued, and could
not speak. He told his therapist he was not afraid of the fire anymore. What he was afraid of was
himself.
