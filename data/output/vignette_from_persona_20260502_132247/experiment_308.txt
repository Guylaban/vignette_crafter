center to complete documentation. A man she did not recognise entered through a staff entrance she

burnt, like scorched wiring or a cigarette ground out on metal — and the sensation of being watched
before she understood what was happening. She had looked up from her desk and met his eyes, and in
that fraction of a second she had searched his face for something that would tell her she was
misreading the situation. There was nothing reassuring there. The assault that followed lasted
perhaps twenty minutes, though she cannot account for the time coherently. When it was over she sat
alone in that room for nearly an hour before she was able to move.

In the weeks that followed, Linh found that her body had reorganised itself around the memory of
that night in ways she could neither predict nor control. The smell of anything burnt — a
colleague's forgotten lunch in the break room microwave, the faint char from a candle at her
daughter's dinner table — would send a wave of physical alarm through her so sudden and total that
she had to grip whatever surface was nearest. Her heart would pound, her mouth would go dry, and she
would be, for a moment, entirely back in that room. These sensory intrusions did not feel like
memories to Linh; they felt like warnings. She had come to believe that her body was detecting
something real, that the discomfort and the smell and the horrible self-consciousness of being
observed were signals that danger was present again. And so whenever those sensations arose — the
prickling awareness of being seen, the vague bodily unease that had become her constant companion —
they sharpened immediately into a state of active threat monitoring. She would scan the room, study
the faces around her, listen for sounds that might mean someone was moving toward her from a
direction she couldn't see.

That vigilance, in turn, made everything feel more dangerous. Because Linh was always watching,
always listening for the creak of a door or the particular quality of a footfall, ordinary sensory
information was perpetually being screened for threat. A colleague rounding a corner too quickly, a
patient's husband whose expression she could not immediately read, the distant smell of something
burning in the hospital corridor — each of these fed back into the same loop, reactivating the
bodily alarm that had originally been triggered by the assault itself. At home she had begun
listening for sounds at the front door before she could sleep, and her husband had noticed that she
startled badly when he entered a room without announcing himself. He had adapted, calling her name
from the hallway, but the accommodation itself had become a source of quiet grief between them — a
daily reminder of what had changed.

Her work as a midwife had been affected in ways she found deeply shameful. The birth center — the
same building — had become almost unbearable. She had requested a transfer of shifts to avoid the
late evening hours, and she had stopped using the staff entrance entirely, a change her colleagues
had noticed without fully understanding. The smell of anything burnt anywhere in the building could
undo an entire shift. She had not told her supervisor why.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when she froze completely during
a delivery — not from clinical uncertainty, but because a family member in the room had looked at
her in a way she could not immediately categorise as safe. She had stood motionless for several
seconds while a patient needed her. She described this as the moment she understood she could no
longer manage alone. What she fears most is that she will never feel safe in her own workplace

let her forget.
