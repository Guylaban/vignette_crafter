Kwame is a 58-year-old Ghanaian-born hairdresser who has been married for over two decades and runs
a small barbershop in a mid-sized American city. He came to the clinic after his wife issued what
she described as an ultimatum: either he sought help, or she could no longer watch him disappear
into himself. His PCL-5 score of 72 reflects a presentation of considerable severity, and even in
the intake session he sat with his hands pressed flat against his thighs, as though bracing for
something.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Kwame was closing his shop alone on a Thursday evening when two
men approached him in the narrow alleyway behind the building. One pressed close enough that Kwame
could smell the alcohol on his breath — sharp, sour, unmistakable — while the other demanded his
phone and the cash float from the till. When Kwame hesitated, one of the men grabbed him by the
collar and shoved him hard against the brick wall, correcting him with a snarl: "Don't make me tell
you again." They took what they wanted and left him there. He was not seriously physically injured,
but he stood in that alleyway for a long time afterward, unable to find any words for what had just
happened — not to call his wife, not to call the police. He stood, and he breathed, and he could not
speak.

What has followed is a life increasingly organised around the residue of those few minutes. The
memories themselves have a particular quality that Kwame struggles to articulate even now: they come
as images rather than as narrative — the man's face too close to his, the wall's texture, the
particular orange of the alley light — and they replay with a compulsive, film-like momentum that he
cannot pause or redirect. Because the memories carry such vivid sensory force, they also carry their
own triggers back into the world: the smell of alcohol on a client's breath, a man loitering too
long near the shop entrance, or any moment in which someone corrects or criticises him — however
gently — can be enough to pull the images forward again, sudden and unbidden, the film restarting.
When that happens, Kwame's first instinct is to suppress it, to press down hard on the thought and
refuse it entry, and he has also begun quietly withdrawing from people — clients he once chatted
with easily, a brother-in-law who visits on weekends — as though reducing human contact will reduce
the chances of being caught off-guard.

The shop itself has become the central theatre of his difficulty. Hairdressing is close work:
clients sit inches from him, and some come in smelling of alcohol, particularly on Friday
afternoons. When that smell reaches him, the images flood back and Kwame finds himself dwelling
inward, rehearsing what he did wrong that evening — why he hesitated, why he was alone, why he
hadn't locked the back gate. This self-blame rumination, which he cannot seem to stop once it
starts, keeps the memory vivid and close to the surface, so that the next trigger — a client who
jokes that he's cut it too short, a stranger who pauses outside the window — lands with even greater
force. The loop has tightened over months: his attempts to suppress the memories and avoid people
have, if anything, left him more sensitised, more alert to anything that resembles a threat, so that
the triggers themselves now seem to multiply.

His wife has noticed that he flinches when she corrects him about small domestic things, that he has
stopped attending their church's Saturday morning gatherings, that he sometimes sits at the kitchen
table in silence for long stretches. She cannot reach him in those moments, and he cannot explain
them to her — the words still fail him, just as they did in the alley.

What finally brought him in was not the flinching or the silence but a morning when he turned away a
long-standing client mid-appointment, simply because the man had arrived smelling of last night's
drink. Kwame had lost business before, but losing it this way — through fear, through flight — felt
like a line he had not meant to cross. He is most afraid that this will not get better. He is most
hoping, quietly, that someone can help him find the words.
