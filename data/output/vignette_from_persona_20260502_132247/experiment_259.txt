Hinerangi is a 77-year-old New Zealand Māori woman, divorced, who has practiced as a cardiothoracic
surgeon for over four decades. She was referred for psychological assessment following a protracted
and life-threatening illness — a severe cardiac event that required emergency intervention
approximately fourteen months ago. The cruel irony of the referral was not lost on her: she had
spent her career on the other side of the sternum, and she arrived at the intake appointment with
the particular disorientation of someone who had become, without warning, the patient.

The event itself unfolded in fragments she has never been able to reassemble into a coherent whole.
Hinerangi recalls the sudden pressure in her chest during a morning ward round, the ceiling tiles of
her own hospital tilting as she lost her footing, and then — nothing. There are hours she simply
cannot account for. She knows from colleagues that she was resuscitated in the corridor, that she
was intubated, that she spent eleven days in the ICU. But her memory of that period arrives in
disconnected pieces that refuse to cohere: a brief, extraordinarily vivid image of a nurse's face
close to hers, the sensation of restraints, a fragment of conversation she cannot place. Other
stretches are entirely blank, as though excised. Some moments are rendered in near-hallucinatory
clarity while adjacent hours have vanished entirely. She describes it as trying to read a document
with whole paragraphs burned away.

It is precisely this fractured, unresolvable quality of the memory that has driven Hinerangi toward
the coping strategies she now relies upon — strategies that, over the past year, have quietly
worsened her position. Unable to construct a coherent narrative of what happened to her, she has
turned to relentless meaning-seeking, spending hours in a kind of ruminative loop asking why this
happened to her specifically, at this point in her life, after decades of disciplined self-care.
This search for causation has found no satisfying answer, and the unanswered question has become a
wound she keeps reopening. Alongside this, she has imposed on herself a rigid regime of forced
positive thinking — she will not permit herself to acknowledge fear or grief, insisting internally
that she is fortunate, that she survived, that she must be grateful. The effect of this suppression
is that the fragmented memories are never processed, never contextualised, never allowed to settle
into a stable narrative. The gaps remain gaps. The pieces do not fit together any better for being
refused.

What has emerged from the interaction of these unresolved memories and the strategies deployed to
manage them is a profound and frightening transformation in how Hinerangi understands herself. The
person she was — certain, technically gifted, grounded in a vocation she had inhabited since her
twenties — is no longer recognisable to her. She had built her identity around competence in the
face of mortal crisis, and having been the body in crisis, she no longer knows who she is. She has
lost faith not only in the reliability of her own body but in something larger: the sense that life
holds a comprehensible order, that decades of service and discipline confer some protection, that
the world is fair in any meaningful way. Forced positive reframing, applied as a daily discipline,
has not restored this faith — it has, if anything, deepened the sense of inauthenticity, because she
knows she does not believe the reassuring thoughts she is manufacturing.

The occupational consequences have been severe. Hinerangi continues to operate, but she now avoids
all contact with the medical environment that is not strictly required by her surgical duties. She
declines to attend clinical case reviews held in the ICU, refuses her own scheduled physical
examinations, and has not set foot in the cardiac ward where she collapsed. Her colleagues have
noticed. She has offered no explanation. The avoidance keeps the fragmented memories at a safe
distance, but it also keeps her from the very professional context that once anchored her sense of
self.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment in the scrub room three weeks ago when she looked
at her own hands preparing for surgery and did not recognise them as belonging to her. She is most
afraid that the person she was before is simply gone. What she is hoping for, though she says it
with some difficulty, is to understand what happened to her — all of it — and to find out whether
there is still someone coherent on the other side.
