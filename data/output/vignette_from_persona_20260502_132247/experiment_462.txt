Layla Mansour is a forty-four-year-old married woman of Lebanese origin who works as a prosecutor in
a mid-sized American city. She was referred for psychological assessment following a period of
escalating distress that she had, until recently, managed to conceal behind the disciplined
composure her courtroom career demanded. Her presenting difficulties trace back to a period
approximately three years ago when she returned to Beirut to care for her elderly mother during a
stretch of renewed urban conflict. What she encountered there — the sudden percussion of shelling,
the nights spent in a basement corridor listening to boots moving on the street above, the sensation
of being tracked through a neighbourhood that had become a grid of checkpoints and watchful
strangers — left marks she did not immediately recognise as injury.

During those weeks in Beirut, Layla had learned to read sound as information about survival. A voice
carrying through a stairwell, the cadence of footsteps behind her on a narrow street, the feeling
that someone at a checkpoint was following her progress with more than routine interest — each of
these had, at one point or another, preceded a moment of genuine danger. She returned to the United
States believing she had simply endured something difficult and moved on. It took months before she
noticed that the learning had not reversed.

The triggers that now govern her daily life are precisely those that were forged in Beirut. When a
male voice in an adjacent office rises unexpectedly, or carries a particular gravel quality she
cannot fully articulate, Layla's body responds before her mind catches up. The sensation of being
followed — which she encounters almost daily during the walk from the parking structure to the
courthouse — arrives without warning and feels entirely credible, even when she can see with her own
eyes that the street is ordinary. The feeling of being watched is perhaps the most disruptive,
because the courtroom is, by design, a place where she is observed. When these triggers fire, they
do not stay contained. They open immediately into a state of threat that reorganises her entire
attention: she wakes during the night to check the locks and listen at the bedroom door, she has
identified only her home office and one corner of her living room as places where she can breathe
without scanning, and she remains on high alert whenever an unexpected sound — a door slamming in
the courthouse corridor, a car backfiring outside — reaches her before she has had time to prepare
for it. The trigger and the threat response have become so tightly coupled that each feeds the
other: the hypervigilant scanning she maintains throughout the day makes her more likely to register
ambiguous stimuli as threatening, which in turn produces new surges of alarm that confirm, to her,
that the scanning is necessary.

This sustained state of threat has quietly eroded the way Layla understands herself and the world
she inhabits. She had come to believe, with a flatness that frightened her more than the fear
itself, that the person she used to be — someone her husband described as warm, even buoyant — was
simply gone. She was certain that she could no longer manage the ordinary friction of life; a minor
scheduling conflict at work, a raised voice in a meeting, a small domestic disagreement with her
husband would leave her undone in ways that felt humiliating for a woman who prosecuted violent
felonies without flinching. And undergirding all of it was a conviction, now almost axiomatic, that
the world is a place where danger is the baseline condition and safety is always provisional. The
heightened threat monitoring that had begun as a response to specific triggers had, over time,
supplied her with daily evidence for this belief, making it feel not like a symptom but like
accurate perception.

What finally brought Layla to seek help was a morning in the courthouse when she stepped into an
elevator, heard a man behind her speaking in Arabic on his phone, and had to exit before the doors
closed. She stood in the lobby, professionally dressed and entirely unable to re-enter the building
for eleven minutes. She told no one. But that evening she made the referral call herself, and what
she said she most wanted — quietly, without much hope — was to be able to do her job without being
afraid of a voice in a hallway.
