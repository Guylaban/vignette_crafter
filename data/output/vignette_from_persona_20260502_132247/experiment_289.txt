Valentina Reyes is a 26-year-old Colombian-American graduate student who presented for psychological
assessment approximately eight months after sustaining accidental toxic chemical exposure in a
university laboratory. She had been working late into the evening, alone in the research facility,
when a storage container of industrial solvent ruptured. The room filled rapidly with fumes in the
dark corridor adjacent to the lab — she had stepped out to retrieve equipment when the lights on
that wing failed simultaneously — and she spent what she later estimated was nearly twenty minutes
disoriented in near-total darkness, unable to locate the exit, her eyes burning, her chest
tightening with each breath. By the time emergency responders reached her, she had collapsed. She
spent four days in hospital, during which she underwent repeated respiratory assessments, the
antiseptic smell of the ward threading itself into every waking hour of her recovery.

In the weeks that followed, Valentina found that the memory of the event resisted the kind of
coherent telling that might have made it feel manageable. She knew, in some abstract sense, what had
happened — the sequence of events was documented in the incident report — but when she tried to
recall it herself, the images came out of order, fragmentary and lurching, and she found herself
unable to find the words to describe what it had actually been like. What made this more distressing
was that the memory did not stay past-tense: when fragments returned, she felt a tightening across
her chest and a burning sensation along her throat, the same physical pain she had experienced
during the exposure itself. These somatic intrusions reinforced her growing conviction that
something was fundamentally wrong with her — that her body had been damaged in ways that were not
fully visible, or that she was constitutionally too fragile to have recovered normally. She had come
to believe, with quiet certainty, that there was something defective in her as a person, that other
people did not fall apart the way she did.

This sense of bodily betrayal — the feeling that her body was an enemy operating outside her control
— fed directly back into how she experienced her own memories. When she tried to recall the event in
order to make sense of it, the physical sensations that arose confirmed, to her, that the danger was
not truly over. Her body reacting as though she were still in that dark corridor seemed like
evidence that she was still, in some meaningful way, there. This meant that the memories themselves
became triggers for renewed physical alarm, and the physical alarm in turn made the memories feel
more vivid and more disordered, a loop she could not step outside of.

Certain external cues had become unbearable. Darkness — any dark space, including her own bedroom
before she reached the light switch — produced immediate physical reactivity: racing heart, shallow
breathing, the full-body bracing of a person expecting impact. Waking in the night, disoriented in
the dark, reliably produced the most severe episodes. The smell of antiseptic, encountered in a
campus health clinic she visited for an unrelated matter, stopped her at the door. These triggers,
once established, began to structure her life. Because she had come to believe her body was
unreliable and that she herself was somehow defective, she interpreted each triggered response not
as a normal trauma reaction but as further proof of her own fragility — and this appraisal made the
triggers feel more threatening, more inevitable, and harder to approach.

Valentina responded by building an elaborate architecture of avoidance. She had not returned to the
laboratory building since the incident, arranging with her supervisor to conduct all her work
remotely — an accommodation that had begun to strain that relationship. She worked to push the
memory away whenever it surfaced, trying to erase it rather than process it, which left it
unintegrated and prone to intrusion. She kept lights on throughout her apartment at all times. She
also scanned her surroundings constantly when in unfamiliar spaces, her body perpetually tensed for
the next sign of danger — and this hypervigilance, exhausting as it was, kept her nervous system
primed so that even minor stimuli, including an unexpected touch on the shoulder from a classmate,
made her react with alarm. The vigilance itself became a source of physiological arousal that made
triggers more potent and the sense of threat feel more real.

Valentina sought help after a panic episode in a campus stairwell during a power fluctuation dimmed
the lights for less than a minute. She sat on the steps for an hour afterward, unable to move. What
frightened her most was not the episode itself but what she believed it meant: that she was getting
worse, not better, and that her body could no longer be trusted to tell the difference between
memory and reality. What she most hoped for, she said quietly at intake, was to be able to sleep
with the lights off again.
