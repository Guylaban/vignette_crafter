assessment approximately four months after being mugged at knifepoint outside a convenience store
near their apartment. It was a Tuesday evening in late autumn; Parisa had stopped to buy supplies on
the way home from a long clinic shift. A man approached from behind, pressed something cold and
sharp against their ribs, and demanded their phone and wallet. What Parisa remembers most viscerally
is not the knife but the smell — cigarette smoke and something chemical, like cleaning fluid — and
the sound of their own voice coming out thin and compliant, saying "okay, okay, take it, please."
Within seconds the man was gone. Parisa stood on the pavement for a long moment, then walked home,
and told themselves, as they unlocked the door, that it had not really been that serious. Nobody had
been hurt. It was over.

quietly prevented Parisa from processing what had happened. Over the following weeks, they noticed
that any time they tried to explain the mugging to their partner or a colleague, the thread of the
account dissolved; they would lose the sequence, trail off, and change the subject. The memory had
not been laid down as a coherent narrative but as fragments: the pressure against their ribs, the
smell, the sound of their own compliance. Because the memory remained disorganised and unresolved,
it was easily ambushed by the environment. At the veterinary clinic where Parisa works, the sharp
smell of antiseptic solution — a routine and unavoidable feature of the job — could instantly
collapse the distance between then and now, flooding them with the full sensory weight of that
evening. In those moments, still in scrubs and mid-procedure, Parisa would feel their heart
hammering and their eyes begin to sweep the room.

That reflexive scanning had become near-constant. Parisa now checked behind themselves when walking
between the parking lot and the clinic entrance, and remained alert even inside their own home,
sitting with their back to the wall and listening to sounds in the hallway. They had come to
believe, with quiet but firm conviction, that the world was fundamentally unpredictable and
threatening — that you simply could not know what was coming next, and that being caught unguarded
once was proof that vigilance was the only rational response. This heightened threat monitoring,
however, kept the nervous system in a state of perpetual readiness that made intrusive recall far
more likely: a shadow in the peripheral vision, a stranger's footstep behind them on the pavement,
and the memory would surge back unbidden, sometimes with the smell arriving before any visual image.

The appraisals that had settled around the event were bleak and generalising. Parisa had come to
feel that the person who had once moved through the world with relative ease and warmth was simply
gone — that they used to be happy, and now were not, and that this was a permanent condition rather
than a symptom. Alongside this was a corrosive sense that other people could not be relied upon: no
one had intervened that evening, and their attempts to explain what happened had been met with what
felt like inadequate understanding. These beliefs fed directly into Parisa's avoidance of going out,
which had narrowed their life considerably. They drove to work and drove home; they no longer walked
anywhere after dark. Their partner had begun to notice, and the resulting conversations — gentle but
persistent — had started to feel like another demand for compliance, another situation in which
Parisa found themselves agreeing and accommodating in order to reduce tension rather than from
genuine desire.

The magical thinking that the event had not really been serious remained a recurring retreat, but it
carried a cost: each time Parisa minimised what had happened, the memory stayed unintegrated and the
threat appraisals went unchallenged, leaving the whole system intact and tightening. The belief that
the world was dangerous kept Parisa scanning; the scanning kept triggering memory fragments; the
memory fragments reinforced the belief that danger was everywhere and that they were permanently
changed by it.

What finally brought Parisa to seek help was a moment at the clinic when, smelling the antiseptic as
a colleague cleaned an instrument tray, they froze completely in front of a client and could not
speak for nearly a minute. They were afraid, they said quietly at intake, that they were losing
their ability to do the one thing that had always felt like solid ground. What they most hoped for
was to stop feeling like a stranger in their own life.
