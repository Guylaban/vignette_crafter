Tane Havili is a fifty-five-year-old Tongan-American man, divorced, who works as a live-in nanny for
a family in suburban Portland. He was referred for psychological assessment following a disclosure
to his employer that he had not slept properly in months and had been found, on more than one
occasion, sitting motionless in the kitchen in the early hours of the morning, unable to account for
how long he had been there. His PCL-5 score of 65 places him in a range consistent with severe
posttraumatic stress, and what emerges across intake and early sessions is a picture of a man whose
grief and self-condemnation have become so thoroughly entangled that he can no longer tell them
apart.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Tane's younger sister Lani was killed in a car accident in which
he was the driver. They had been returning from a family celebration when another vehicle crossed
the median. Tane survived with minor injuries. Lani, seated beside him, sustained catastrophic
trauma to her head and upper body. Tane held her in the minutes before the ambulance arrived. He saw
things — the specific configuration of her injuries, the stillness of her hands in his — that have
since become inextricably lodged in his perceptual world. He remembers hearing her make a sound, a
single exhalation, and then nothing. He has never been able to describe this sequence to anyone in
full. When he tries, he says, the thread simply dissolves — he loses the order of events, loses the
words, and finds himself stranded somewhere inside the memory without a way out. What he does not
lose is the sound. It returns unbidden, most often at night, as a precise auditory echo, and when it
does, the grief that follows is not gradual but total, arriving like pressure behind the sternum and
staying for hours.

The triggers that now govern Tane's daily life are tightly organised around what he witnessed.
Working as a nanny, he is embedded in a domestic world full of children's bodies — small arms lifted
to be carried, bruised knees examined, the ordinary physical vulnerability of young lives in his
care. On more than one occasion, the sight of a child's limb at an unexpected angle has stopped him
mid-task, his vision narrowing to that single point of contact with the past. Conversations among
the parents in the household about loss — a neighbour's bereavement, a news story about a family
separated by tragedy — land on him with disproportionate force, pulling him back toward Lani before
he has time to orient himself. These triggers do not merely recall the event; they confirm, in his
experience, what he has come to believe about himself. He is certain he failed to protect his
sister. He was the driver. He should have seen it coming, should have reacted faster, should have
done something in those final seconds that he did not do. This belief — settled and immovable —
colours every intrusion. When a memory surfaces and he hears that sound again, the grief it carries
is inseparable from the verdict it seems to deliver: that he is weak, that he is less than a person,
that he has been reduced by what happened to something functional but not fully human. He moves
through the children's home performing care while feeling, underneath it, like an object executing
tasks.

What makes the cycle particularly relentless is the way the appraisals and the memories feed each
other. When Tane rehearses his failure — and he does, repeatedly, in the hollow hours before dawn —
the memories become more vivid and more auditorily present, the sound sharpening, the grief
intensifying. And when the grief arrives in full, it does not soften the self-judgment; it deepens
it, as though the magnitude of his pain is simply further evidence of how badly he failed. The
triggers, saturated now with this meaning, have multiplied rather than diminished over time.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when the youngest child in his
care asked him why he was crying. He had not known he was. He is most afraid, he says, that he will
never be able to explain to Lani's children — her two daughters — what happened that night. He is
most hoping, though he says this quietly and without much conviction, that someone might help him
find a way to finish the sentence.
