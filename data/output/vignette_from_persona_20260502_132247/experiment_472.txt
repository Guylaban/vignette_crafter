Nadia Khalil is a 38-year-old divorced woman of Lebanese-American heritage who has worked for the
past four years as a principal at a mid-sized public elementary school in suburban Michigan. She
presented for psychological evaluation reporting a PCL-5 score of 43 and a cluster of symptoms that
had persisted, and in some respects worsened, over the eighteen months since her return from a
civilian humanitarian coordination role embedded with a military unit in a conflict zone. What
brought her finally to seek help was a morning at school when she found herself crouched behind her
own desk during a fire drill, hands over her ears, unable to stand until a teacher knocked and
called her name.

The incident that anchors her current difficulties occurred during a mortar attack on a forward
operating base where Nadia had been coordinating supply logistics. She had been in a briefing tent
when the first shell struck approximately forty metres away. The sequence of what followed — the
second and third impacts, the shouting, the smell of burning, the sound of a colleague's voice
cutting off mid-sentence — remains fractured in her memory. She cannot reconstruct the order in
which things happened. What surfaces instead are auditory fragments: a specific low whistle
preceding impact, the particular pitch of someone screaming a name she still cannot identify. When
those sounds return to her, they arrive not as recollection but as presence, and with them comes a
surge of rage so intense it frightens her — rage at the commanders who chose that location, at the
systems that sent civilians into that proximity, at herself for having agreed to go. That rage,
arriving unbidden, has come to feel like evidence. She has concluded that her inability to process
what happened — the fragmented sequence, the uncontrolled fury on recall — proves she is a lousy
coper, someone constitutionally ill-equipped to manage what others in similar roles apparently
survived intact. That belief has calcified over months into something broader and darker: that
nothing in her life carries meaning anymore, that the work she once found genuinely purposeful now
feels hollow, and that other people — colleagues, her ex-husband, the parents of her students —
cannot fundamentally be trusted to be who they present themselves to be.

Those appraisals, in turn, have made the world feel continuously dangerous. Nadia has come to
believe that because she cannot trust her own judgment or the intentions of those around her, she
must remain perpetually vigilant. Inside her own home she feels on guard — she checks locks multiple
times before sleeping, positions herself facing doorways, and has rearranged furniture so no chair
sits with its back to a window. At school, before she can settle into any room, she locates the
exits: the side door of the gymnasium, the fire-escape access off the second-floor corridor, the gap
in the perimeter fence she mentally catalogued during her first week back. This hypervigilance is
not experienced as irrational; it feels like the only competent response available to someone who
has already been caught unprepared once. And that sustained state of alert, that constant scanning
of the environment for threat, reliably pulls the traumatic material back to the surface. The low
drone of a maintenance vehicle in the school car park, the muffled thud of a door slamming at the
far end of a hallway — these sounds, filtered through a nervous system already primed for danger,
become the whistle and the impact, and the memories return with their disordered fragments and their
fury, reinforcing once more her conviction that she is broken, that people around her are strangers
she cannot afford to rely on, and that the life she is living has been emptied of whatever made it
worth protecting.

The concrete costs have accumulated quietly. She has withdrawn from the district leadership team she
once chaired. Three staff members have remarked to the deputy principal that she seems unreachable.
Her younger sister, who lives twenty minutes away, has stopped calling after Nadia cancelled four
consecutive dinners without explanation. What Nadia fears most, she said in the intake session, is
that she will never feel safe anywhere again — not even in the building she runs, not even in her
own house. What she hopes for, more tentatively, is that the rage might someday stop feeling like
the truest thing she knows about herself.
