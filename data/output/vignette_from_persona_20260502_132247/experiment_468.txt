Kofi Mensah is a twenty-year-old Ghanaian-American man who, by any conventional measure, had already
achieved something extraordinary: he had completed an accelerated pre-medical programme and entered
a fast-track clinical placement, working as a junior doctor in a trauma and emergency unit before
most of his peers had finished their undergraduate degrees. He had grown up believing, with the
particular certainty of someone who had studied hard and sacrificed much, that competence and
preparation were sufficient armour against the worst the world could offer. That belief did not
survive his deployment as a medical volunteer to a conflict zone in East Africa eighteen months ago,
when his unit came under sustained attack during what was supposed to be a routine medical
evacuation.

The assault lasted less than four minutes. Kofi has since counted this — four minutes — as though
the brevity should make it more manageable. A convoy vehicle ahead of his was struck, and in the
seconds that followed, the air pressure changed before the sound did. He remembers the sudden,
absolute silence that swallowed the world just after the blast, a silence so complete it felt like
being pressed underwater, and then the chaos that replaced it: smoke, the sounds of men screaming,
and the sight of violence so concentrated and specific that he has never found adequate language for
it. He was not physically injured. He triaged and treated others under fire. He did what he had
trained to do. By the time the silence returned — the real silence, after it was over — something
had already shifted in him that he could not name.

His memories of the event do not behave like memories. They arrive in disconnected pieces that
refuse to cohere into a sequence he can hold: a hand, a colour, the smell of something burning, a
face he cannot place. There are gaps he cannot account for — stretches of those four minutes that
simply do not exist for him no matter how deliberately he tries to retrieve them. And when the
fragments do surface, he finds himself pushing them away before they can fully form, as though
completing the memory would confirm something he is not ready to know. This fractured, effortful
quality of the memory has itself become a source of dread: he has come to believe that the gaps mean
something terrible about what he witnessed or failed to do, and that if he could remember clearly,
he would find evidence of his own failure. The incompleteness of the memory feeds a corrosive
uncertainty that has steadily eroded his sense of who he is.

That erosion is now pervasive. Kofi reports feeling like a stranger to himself — the young man who
was certain the world rewarded preparation and moral seriousness seems to belong to someone else's
life. His beliefs about how the world operates have been, in his own words, completely destroyed. He
no longer trusts the assumption that care and competence protect people. This loss of foundational
belief keeps him in a state of constant vigilance; he is certain, at some level he cannot fully
articulate, that catastrophe is always proximate and that he must remain alert to it at all times.
This hypervigilance in turn makes him more sensitive to the very sensations that carry the residue
of the event. In the emergency department where he now works, the occasional moments of eerie quiet
between resuscitations — that particular suspended silence — stop him cold. His breathing tightens,
becomes laboured, and the breathlessness itself pulls him back toward the fragments: the pressure
change, the void before the noise. And when, as happens in trauma medicine, he witnesses violence or
its immediate aftermath, the appraisal that follows is not clinical but existential — a confirmation
that the world is exactly as dangerous and arbitrary as he now believes it to be, which in turn
makes the memory fragments surge with renewed force.

He came to seek help after a shift three weeks ago during which he froze for several seconds while
managing a patient who had sustained a penetrating injury. A colleague covered for him. No one was
harmed. But Kofi understood, standing in the corridor afterward, that he could not continue to
practise medicine in this state without eventually causing harm. What he is most afraid of is that
the person he was before the convoy — the one who believed in something — is gone for good. What he
is hoping, more tentatively, is that someone can help him find out what actually happened in those
four minutes, and whether he can live with it.
