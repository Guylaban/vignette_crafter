Rodrigo is a 75-year-old married man of Latin American descent who works as a QA engineer, a role
that has anchored his sense of precision and control for decades. He presented for psychological
evaluation following a wildfire that swept through his rural community approximately fourteen months
ago, during a stretch of dry, windless heat that he now finds almost impossible to describe without
his voice faltering. The fire moved faster than anyone had anticipated. Rodrigo was at home when a
neighbor's frantic knock brought him to the front door, and what he saw — a wall of orange and black
consuming the tree line less than half a mile away — remains, in his words, "burned into the back of
my eyes." He had perhaps twenty minutes to gather what he could before the evacuation order came.
Several homes on his street were lost. His own survived, but only barely, and the smell of smoke
hung in the air for days afterward, seeping through every window seal and settling into furniture,
clothing, the walls themselves.

In the weeks that followed, Rodrigo found himself unable to move through ordinary life without the
disaster pressing in. The triggers that now hold the most power over him are precisely those that
were most present in those twenty minutes: the smell of smoke, the sight of open flame, and weather
conditions that resemble that afternoon's dry, still heat. He works from home as a QA engineer,
reviewing software systems for failure points — a job that requires sustained concentration — and on
days when a neighbor burns yard debris or the summer air sits heavy and motionless, the smell drifts
through his window and his focus collapses entirely. His body responds before his mind can
intervene: his heart pounds, his chest tightens, his hands begin to shake. These strong physical
reactions feel, to Rodrigo, like evidence that something is genuinely wrong, that the threat is real
and present, and so his vigilance sharpens further — he finds himself scanning the horizon from his
desk window, watching the sky for smoke columns, checking weather forecasts multiple times a day.
That state of sustained alertness, in turn, lowers his threshold for surprise; ordinary sounds — a
door closing, a car backfiring — send a jolt through him that leaves him embarrassed and exhausted.

What makes the cycle particularly tenacious is the way his heightened threat state feeds back into
the very sensory world he is trying to monitor. Because he is already scanning, he notices the faint
smell of a distant barbecue, the flicker of a candle across the room, the particular quality of a
dry afternoon, in ways he never would have before. These perceptions, once registered, pull him
immediately back into the disaster. When that happens, Rodrigo does not simply re-experience the
fear — he begins to interrogate himself. He dwells on decisions he made that day: why hadn't he
cleared the brush along the fence line that spring, why hadn't he insisted his wife leave earlier,
why hadn't he been more prepared. He rehearses alternative timelines in which a different version of
himself had acted with the foresight the situation demanded. This rumination, though it feels to him
like problem-solving, keeps the emotional intensity of the event alive and close, which means the
next encounter with smoke or flame or still heat arrives with the full weight of unresolved distress
already loaded. When the rumination becomes unbearable, he tries to push the thoughts away entirely
— to concentrate on a work task, to redirect his attention — but the suppression is unreliable, and
the thoughts return with renewed force, often accompanied by the physical arousal he was trying to
escape, which only confirms to him that he remains in danger.

Over the past year, Rodrigo has become quieter at home. His wife has noticed that he avoids sitting
on the back porch in summer, that he leaves rooms where candles are lit, that he is short-tempered
in ways that are new to him. He has not told her the full extent of what he experiences, partly
because he believes, with quiet conviction, that a man his age should have been able to prevent this
— or at least to recover from it by now. What finally brought him to seek help was a review cycle at
work in which he missed a significant software defect, something he attributes directly to the days
of fragmented concentration that followed a particularly smoky afternoon. For a man whose
professional identity rests on catching what others miss, that failure felt intolerable. He arrives
at treatment hoping, above all, to be able to work again without the past ambushing him — and
afraid, beneath that, that it never will.
