Tariq is a 37-year-old Lebanese-American firefighter who has been married for nine years. He
presents with a PCL-5 score of 62 following a prolonged stalking experience that began approximately
fourteen months ago and lasted nearly six months before legal intervention. What made the experience
particularly destabilising was not a single violent incident but the slow, grinding accumulation of
surveillance: a man Tariq had briefly encountered through a neighbourhood dispute began appearing
wherever Tariq went — outside the fire station at shift changes, parked across the street from his
home, following him on a bicycle through residential streets when Tariq rode to work. The stalker
never spoke directly to him. He simply appeared, and watched. It was the bicycle that became the
centre of it — the quiet, almost casual way the man would pedal into view, unhurried, as though
Tariq's movements were a schedule he had memorised. By the time a restraining order was obtained,
Tariq had internalised the certainty that any public space could become a trap and that no distance
was truly safe.

In the months that followed the legal resolution, Tariq found that his nervous system had not
received the news. Entering any room — a grocery store, the apparatus bay at the station, a
restaurant with his wife — he would immediately map the exits, his eyes moving before his conscious
mind caught up. He watched other people closely, reading their posture and gaze for signs of hostile
intent, and unexpected sounds — a door slamming, a radio burst from dispatch, the scrape of a chair
— sent a jolt of alarm through him that he could not talk himself out of. These responses were not
occasional; they were constant, exhausting, and entirely beyond his control.

The most acute difficulty came with memory. Certain things in his environment could collapse the
distance between then and now without any warning — most powerfully, the sight of a bicycle. Tariq
works in a neighbourhood where cyclists are common, and on several occasions during street responses
he has rounded a corner and seen a man on a bicycle and felt the full weight of those six months
land on him in an instant, the memory returning not as recollection but as presence. When the memory
came back this way, Tariq was so overwhelmed by the emotion that accompanied it — a flooding dread,
a physical constriction in his chest — that he had to stop what he was doing. He told his
supervising lieutenant he had "something in his eye." He could not explain further. When he tried to
speak about what had happened, even in the intake session, the words would not come; he knew what he
had lived through but found it inaccessible to language, as though the experience existed in a
register that speech could not reach.

It was these failures of language and composure that fed the most corrosive part of Tariq's current
presentation: what he had come to believe about himself. He was certain he had been permanently
changed for the worse — that the person who had entered those six months was gone and what remained
was diminished. A firefighter, he reasoned, was supposed to be someone who moved toward danger
without flinching; the fact that a bicycle, a sound, a feeling of being watched could undo him
meant, in his private accounting, that he was weak. These beliefs did not stay contained. When Tariq
appraised himself as weak and broken, the threat monitoring intensified — as though his damaged
state made him more vulnerable, less capable of protecting himself, and therefore more exposed to
harm. The scanning of exits, the watching of strangers, the vigilance around sounds all sharpened in
the wake of these self-condemning thoughts. And the appraisals reached backward into memory as well:
when he thought of himself as permanently changed, the memories returned with renewed force,
confirming the verdict. The loop was self-sealing. The memories, when they broke through, renewed
his conviction that something was fundamentally wrong with him, and that conviction kept him primed
for threat, which kept the triggers live, which kept the memories close.

Tariq has begun to withdraw from the social dimensions of his shift — the shared meals, the
conversations in the common room — and his wife has noticed a distance she cannot name. What finally
brought him to seek help was a moment at the station when a colleague arrived on a bicycle and Tariq
had to leave the bay. He stood outside in the cold for several minutes, unable to re-enter. He is
most afraid, he said quietly at intake, that this is simply who he is now. What he is hoping for,
though he offered it almost as an apology, is to be wrong about that.
