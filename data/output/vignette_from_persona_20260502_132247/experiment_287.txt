Rodrigo is a 64-year-old divorced Latin American man who works alone at a small jewelry repair bench
in a strip mall in the outer suburbs of Houston. He came to his first intake appointment wearing a
collared shirt buttoned to the throat, sitting with his back to the wall and his eyes moving
steadily around the room. He spoke carefully, in measured sentences, as though each word had been
inspected before release.

The events Rodrigo carries began before he had language for them. Growing up in a small household in
San Antonio, he watched his father beat his mother across a span of years that felt, in memory, like
a single unbroken night. What he remembers most precisely are not the long stretches of dread but
the moments themselves: his father's face when his voice first rose, the particular sound a kitchen
chair makes when it is thrown against linoleum, the shadow of a man's arm raised in a narrow
hallway. These images are fixed and photographic — each one a still frame rather than a scene — and
they remain, more than five decades later, with the clarity of something that happened this morning.
The memories carry with them an immediate and crushing guilt. He had been the eldest child. He had
not stopped it. He had hidden.

That guilt has calcified, over the decades, into a settled conviction: that he is a weak man who
failed at the most fundamental thing a person can be asked to do, and that this failure placed him
permanently outside the circle of his own community. He grew up in a culture where the protection of
family was the measure of a man, and he had come to believe — quietly, without ever quite saying it
aloud — that he had disqualified himself from that belonging long ago. This belief does not remain
abstract. It surfaces most forcefully when the memories intrude, and the memories do not require
much invitation. The sound of raised voices in the strip mall parking lot, a confrontation between
customers at the counter next door, or simply finding himself alone in a room with another man — any
of these can return him, without warning, to that hallway. When the triggers fire, the conviction of
his own weakness arrives with them, and the shame of that conviction makes the memories feel even
more present and inescapable, as though they are evidence he is compelled to keep reviewing.

His body has organized itself around the anticipation of danger in a way that has become
indistinguishable from his ordinary attention. At his jeweler's bench, where he handles small tools
all day, he is aware at every moment of what in his immediate environment could be used as a weapon
— a loupe, a pair of pliers, the glass display case edge. He watches the few customers who enter
with the focused assessment of someone who has learned that harm comes from people who appear
ordinary. He cannot be told he is safe; the information does not reach whatever part of him is
making that determination. This hypervigilance is itself a source of shame: a man who flinches at a
raised voice, who scans a room like a frightened animal, is confirming everything he already
believes about himself. And that belief — that he is weak, unmoored from his culture, a failed
protector — keeps him primed, so that ordinary social friction reads as threat, and the threat keeps
the memories close.

To manage all of this, Rodrigo has constructed a life of careful suppression. He tries, with
considerable effort, not to think about his childhood, but the effort of not thinking has made the
memories more intrusive, not less, and has kept them from ever settling into something integrated or
distant. He monitors his own thoughts with the vigilance of a man watching for symptoms of a
disease, frightened that if he lets his guard down he will lose his mind entirely. He does not allow
himself to rest — relaxation feels like an open door — and this chronic tension has left him
exhausted in a way that has slowly narrowed his world. He no longer attends the community events he
once found meaningful. His divorce, several years ago, removed the last daily relationship that had
given him structure. He has few friends now, and he does not pursue them.

What finally brought Rodrigo to seek help was a moment at his workbench three months ago when a
delivery man raised his voice on a phone call just outside the shop door, and Rodrigo found himself
crouched behind the counter, heart hammering, before he had any conscious awareness of moving. He
sat on the floor for several minutes, and what he felt most was not fear but a profound and
exhausted grief. He is not sure what he hopes for. What he is most afraid of is that there is
nothing left to recover — that the boy who hid in the hallway is all he has ever been.
