Gerald is a 51-year-old widowed school principal of Canadian descent who was referred for
psychological assessment following a PCL-5 score of 56. He presents as a composed, deliberate man
whose composure, on closer observation, costs him considerable effort to maintain. Approximately
fourteen months ago, Gerald was admitted to hospital following a cardiac event that required
emergency intervention. He had been alone when it happened — a Sunday evening in his kitchen, a
glass of wine half-poured on the counter — and by the time paramedics reached him he had lost
consciousness briefly. He spent eleven days in the ICU, surrounded by the persistent antiseptic
smell of the ward, the cold pressure of oxygen masks, the routine indignities of blood draws and
catheter changes. His wife had died of cancer three years prior, and there was no one at his bedside
for the first four days. He survived. He has not, in any meaningful sense, felt that survival as a
good thing.

The smell of alcohol — wine, hand sanitiser, the rubbing alcohol a school nurse uses — now functions
as an almost instantaneous portal back to that kitchen, to the moment before the world went dark.
When Gerald encounters that smell, whether passing a colleague's office holiday party or walking
through his own school's medical room, the sensory cue does not retrieve a coherent narrative so
much as it detonates something somatic: his chest tightens, his breathing shortens, and he finds
himself sitting with the full emotional weight of that night without being able to locate a clear
image to attach it to. The same occurs with the antiseptic smell of hospitals. He describes it as
drowning without knowing where the water is. These triggers also pull forward his most destabilising
beliefs — that his body is a thing that failed him without warning, that a man who collapsed alone
in his kitchen cannot be trusted to run a school, to make decisions, to be someone others rely on.
The smell alone is enough to make him feel, briefly but completely, that he is that person again:
helpless, unrecognised even to himself.

Because the triggers arrive through the senses before he can prepare, Gerald has built elaborate
systems of anticipatory control. He rehearses conversations before any interaction — with staff,
with parents, with his GP — scripting responses so that nothing catches him off guard, nothing
forces him to feel his way through a moment in real time. He has declined two routine health check-
ups since discharge, citing scheduling conflicts, and has not returned to his cardiologist. He has
gradually withdrawn from friends, stopped answering calls from his sister in Vancouver, and now
spends most evenings alone in a house that has become, he admits quietly, very quiet. These
strategies have not made him feel safer. Instead, the avoidance of medical settings and the severing
of relationships have left him with fewer anchors to a self he recognises, deepening his conviction
that the person who once ran a school of four hundred students with confidence and warmth no longer
exists. The isolation, in particular, has removed the relational mirrors through which he might have
tested whether his self-perception was accurate, leaving his negative appraisals unchallenged and
entrenched.

The memory itself is fragmentary in a way that compounds everything. When Gerald tries deliberately
to recall what happened — to construct a timeline, to make sense of it — he finds the memory
strangely inaccessible, as though it belongs to someone else. Yet his body has not forgotten. When
fragments do surface, he notices he is holding himself in the position he was in when he lost
consciousness — slightly slumped, right hand braced against the counter — and the physical
reenactment confirms, to him, that his body is still living there. This bodily knowledge, arriving
without narrative context, feeds directly into his belief that he cannot trust himself, that the
ground beneath his identity has shifted permanently. And because he believes this, he avoids the
very situations — medical follow-up, honest conversation with people who knew him before — that
might interrupt the loop.

What finally brought Gerald to seek help was a moment at school last month. A student fainted during
a fire drill. Gerald froze. A teacher managed the situation. Gerald went to his office, closed the
door, and sat for forty minutes unable to move. He is most afraid that what happened to him has made
him unfit for the work that has defined him. What he hopes, though he struggles to say it directly,
is that some version of the man he was might still be recoverable.
