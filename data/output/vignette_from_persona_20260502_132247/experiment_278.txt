Raymond is a 71-year-old single insurance agent who came to therapy after decades of managing alone
what he now acknowledges he can no longer contain. He grew up in a household where his father, a
heavy drinker, would return home unpredictably violent. The abuse was not occasional — it was a
sustained pattern across Raymond's childhood, characterised by sudden physical attacks that arrived
without warning, often announced only by the creak of the front door, the sharp smell of whiskey, or
the particular silence that preceded his father's rages. Raymond learned early to read the air of a
room for danger. He is still reading it now, sixty years later.

The memories do not come to Raymond as coherent narratives. They arrive in fragments — a flash of a
raised arm, the particular quality of light in that kitchen, and then gone, as suddenly as they
appeared. What disturbs him most is that these pieces feel more vivid and present than anything in
his ordinary life, more real than his morning coffee or the view from his office window. And when a
memory surfaces, it almost always carries the smell of alcohol with it — a sensory residue so
immediate that Raymond has described it as the event happening inside his body rather than inside
his mind. These intrusive fragments have, over time, deepened his conviction that the past has not
truly passed: that he was betrayed by the one person who should have protected him, that his father
faced no meaningful consequences for what he did, and that the world remains a place where harm can
arrive from any direction without warning. The memories, in other words, do not only haunt him —
they instruct him, reinforcing a set of beliefs about danger and injustice that shape how he moves
through every day.

Those beliefs, in turn, keep Raymond's threat system perpetually armed. He has come to feel
genuinely threatened by strangers approaching him in ordinary situations — at the grocery store, in
the elevator at his office building — and he frequently reacts as though he is in physical danger
when nothing is actually wrong. He listens at night for sounds that might mean someone is entering
his home, lying awake cataloguing the building's noises. This hypervigilance is not something
Raymond experiences as irrational; he is certain that his body's alarm system has simply learned the
truth about the world, even if others cannot see it. Yet the sustained state of threat also pulls
memories closer to the surface — a raised heartbeat or a sudden noise is enough to dislodge a
fragment — which then renews and intensifies his sense that the danger is real and ongoing.

Raymond's work as an insurance agent requires him to speak with strangers daily, and it is here that
the clinical picture becomes most concrete. When a new client approaches his desk or calls without
warning, the encounter carries an edge that Raymond cannot entirely suppress. Talking to strangers
activates the same appraisals the memories carry — of betrayal, of unpredictability, of a world
where trusted figures harm you — and those appraisals sharpen his threat monitoring further, so that
ordinary professional interactions become exercises in vigilance. He has begun avoiding in-person
client meetings where possible, redirecting them to phone or email, and a colleague recently
commented on how withdrawn he has become. The smell of alcohol at a holiday office gathering sent
him out of the room and into the parking lot, where he sat alone for twenty minutes. Sirens passing
his building can stop him mid-sentence.

To manage all of this, Raymond has built an elaborate internal architecture of prevention. He
performs quiet mental rituals — specific sequences of thought he runs through when he feels danger
approaching — convinced that these acts of mental effort are what stand between him and catastrophe.
He keeps his mind occupied at almost every moment, filling silences with tasks or radio, because
stillness allows the fragments in. He avoids television programmes or news stories involving abuse
or violence entirely. These strategies feel necessary to Raymond, but they have also quietly
confirmed his deepest fear: that without constant vigilance and effort, he is not safe. The rituals
and distractions have become their own evidence that the threat is real, keeping his body locked in
a state of readiness that exhausts him and from which he cannot seem to step down.

What finally brought Raymond to seek help was a conversation with his physician, who noted that his
sleep had deteriorated significantly and asked, gently, whether something was troubling him. Raymond
is most afraid that nothing can actually change — that at 71, the patterns are simply who he is.
What he hopes for, tentatively and almost reluctantly, is some relief from the smell that follows
him.
