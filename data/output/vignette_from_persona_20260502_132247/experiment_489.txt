Aroha Tane is a 63-year-old Māori woman, married and working as a project manager for a regional
infrastructure firm in Auckland-adjacent New Zealand before relocating with her husband to the
Pacific Northwest of the United States several years ago. She presented for psychological assessment
with a PCL-5 score of 62, describing a clinical picture that has progressively narrowed her world
since a life-threatening illness eighteen months prior.

The precipitating event occurred during what Aroha had initially dismissed as fatigue and mild chest
tightness. She collapsed at her desk during a site planning call, and was rushed to hospital, where
she spent eleven days in intensive care following a severe cardiac event complicated by sepsis. She
remembers, with a clarity that horrifies her, the sensation of being intubated — the cold metal of
the laryngoscope, the pressure against her throat, the ceiling tiles above her blurring as
consciousness slipped. She recalls lying immobilised, lines running into both arms, a drip stand in
her peripheral vision, unable to speak or call for her husband. It is precisely this helplessness —
the body becoming a site of emergency rather than agency — that has made the aftermath so
relentless.

The memories do not return to Aroha as recollections. When they come, she is back in the ICU bed:
she feels the restraint of the sheets across her chest, the weight of her own limbs as they were
then, heavy and unresponsive. The experience is fully sensory — the antiseptic smell, the beeping
monitors, the fluorescent cold — and she cannot translate any of it into language. She has tried to
explain it to her husband and found herself mute, gesturing helplessly at her sternum. This
nonverbal, bodily quality of the memory is itself destabilising: because she cannot narrate what
happened, she cannot distance herself from it, and the intrusions leave her convinced, in those
moments, that her body is still failing. What frightened her most was not the illness itself but
what the memories seemed to confirm — that her body had betrayed her without warning, that it could
do so again at any moment, and that the life she had built was no longer structurally sound. She had
come to believe that the trauma had destroyed something irreparable in her, and the shame she felt
about what her body had become — scarred, monitored, dependent — sat beneath nearly everything else
she reported.

These appraisals, in turn, have made the memories harder to escape. Because she understands what
happened as evidence of permanent bodily unreliability, every intrusion feels confirmatory rather
than historical, and she finds herself scanning her body constantly for new signals of failure. That
vigilance keeps the traumatic material close to the surface, so that the memories recur more
frequently and with greater force — and each recurrence deepens her certainty that she is not the
person she was. The loop is self-sealing.

In her daily work as a project manager, Aroha now struggles to concentrate through meetings she once
led with ease. She becomes irritable with colleagues over minor scheduling issues, and has twice
reacted with visible alarm when a coworker touched her shoulder to get her attention — responses she
finds humiliating and that reinforce her sense that her body is no longer her own. This state of
chronic alertness is not passive: it feeds back into the traumatic memories themselves, so that a
moment of startle or a flash of anger seems to summon the ICU back into her body. The threat state
and the memories have become mutually sustaining.

Certain conversation topics — colleagues mentioning hospital stays, news items about cardiac events
— are enough to trigger the full physiological alarm response, as is any medical context. A routine
blood draw at her GP's office last spring, the sight of the needle and the drip stand in the corner
of the room, sent her into a dissociative episode that lasted the remainder of the day. She has
since cancelled two follow-up cardiology appointments. Her appraisals of her body as untrustworthy
have made medical settings feel not like sites of safety but of renewed threat, and so the very care
she needs has become something she avoids.

Aroha came to therapy at her husband's urging, after he found her sitting in the dark in their
kitchen at 3 a.m., unable to explain why. She hopes, cautiously, to be able to return to her
cardiologist. What she fears most is that she is right — that the person who walked into that
hospital has not come back.
