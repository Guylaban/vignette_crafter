Gerald is a 66-year-old married software engineer of European descent who presented for
psychological assessment reporting a cluster of distressing symptoms that, on careful inquiry,
traced their roots to a childhood marked by repeated physical abuse at the hands of a primary
caregiver. He described years of unpredictable violence — being grabbed without warning, struck with
open hands or household objects, and held down in ways that left him acutely sensitised to the
physical world around him. Cold air against his skin in winter, the particular chill of an air-
conditioned room, the sudden warmth of a hand reaching toward him — these were the textures of
danger in his earliest years, and they have never fully lost that meaning.

Gerald spent decades constructing a life that kept those memories at arm's length. A career in
software engineering suited him: solitary, logical, mediated through screens rather than bodies. For
many years the strategy worked tolerably well. But in his early sixties, a minor cardiac event left
him hyperaware of his own physiology, and it was as though a door he had kept shut for forty years
swung open. When his heart pounds now — during a brisk walk, after a second cup of coffee, in a
moment of mild exertion — the sensation arrives not as a neutral fact but as an alarm. The racing
pulse pulls a sudden, unbidden memory forward: the specific weight of fear in his chest as a child,
the sense of the body bracing before the mind has caught up. That intrusive memory, arriving whole
and without context, immediately sharpens his perception of the environment around him. He finds
himself scanning his home office for objects that could serve as weapons — a heavy stapler, a letter
opener, the corner of a monitor stand — and his body has already tensed before he has consciously
registered why. The link between the somatic trigger and the threat response is nearly
instantaneous, as though his nervous system has never updated its threat model from childhood.

What has hardened over time is not merely the fear itself but the conclusions Gerald has drawn from
it. He has come to believe, with quiet certainty, that people cannot be trusted — that warmth is a
prelude to harm, that closeness is a form of exposure. He experiences himself as permanently set
apart: a man who can function among others but never truly belong to them. These convictions feel to
him not like symptoms but like hard-won realism, the sensible conclusions of a man who paid
attention. And it is precisely because he holds them as truths that they govern his behaviour so
completely. He avoids physical contact with near-totality — he has not embraced his wife
spontaneously in longer than either of them can comfortably name. He has gradually withdrawn from
the small circle of colleagues and neighbours he once socialised with, finding reasons not to attend
gatherings, not to answer invitations. He will not look at or attend to his own body beyond what
hygiene requires, as though the body itself is a site too dangerous to examine.

These strategies, which feel to Gerald like prudent self-protection, have in practice tightened the
very conditions they were meant to relieve. His social withdrawal has confirmed, in his experience,
that he is an outsider — because he is now, functionally, outside. His avoidance of touch has made
any accidental contact — a colleague brushing past him in a hallway, his wife's hand on his shoulder
— land with startling force, his whole system reacting with alarm before he has time to think. The
avoidance keeps the threat system primed and the appraisals unchallenged, so that a cool breeze
through an open window or a sudden flutter in his chest is enough to reinstate the full vigilance of
a frightened child. His negative beliefs about the world, never tested against disconfirming
experience, continue to generate the hypervigilance that makes every temperature change and every
heartbeat feel like a signal worth attending to.

What finally brought Gerald to seek help was his wife's quiet statement, delivered without
accusation, that she felt she had lost him. He came to the first session uncertain whether he could
be helped and more uncertain still whether he deserved to be — but beneath that, something that
might cautiously be called hope.
