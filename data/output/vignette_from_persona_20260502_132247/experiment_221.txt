Liam Kowalski is a 29-year-old Polish-American salesperson who presented for psychological
assessment approximately eight months after surviving a catastrophic flash flood that struck a rural
campsite in the Pacific Northwest where he had been vacationing alone. He had been sleeping in a
tent near a riverbank when the water rose with almost no warning in the early hours of the morning.
He described clawing his way out of the tent as it collapsed around him, being swept downstream in
near-total darkness, colliding with debris, and clinging to a fallen tree for what he estimated was
close to two hours before rescuers reached him. Throughout that time he had no certainty he would
survive, and the roaring of the water, the cold, and the absolute isolation of those hours appear to
have constituted the core of his traumatic experience.

In the weeks following the flood, Liam noticed that certain sensory encounters could pull him back
into the terror of that night with startling immediacy. Driving past a river on a sales route
through the Central Valley, catching a particular smell of damp earth on a client's property, or
simply finding himself in any wooded or natural setting would produce a visceral re-experiencing —
suddenly he was not in his car or on a doorstep but back in the dark current, certain he was going
to die. These flashbacks arrived without warning and carried the same terror he had felt clinging to
that tree, not as a recollection but as a present event. On nights following such encounters he
would wake repeatedly, sitting upright in the dark to listen for sounds he could not quite name, his
body convinced that danger was still close. He had come to move through the world in a state of
near-constant watchfulness, scanning his environment in ways he recognised as excessive but could
not suppress.

What complicated Liam's recovery most profoundly was the fragmentary and emotionally volatile nature
of his memories of the flood itself. There are portions of those two hours he simply cannot retrieve
— gaps that feel less like forgetting and more like an absence he cannot see around. At other times,
with no clear image attached, the full emotional weight of the event would descend on him without
context: a wave of cold dread and helplessness that seemed to come from nowhere. When he did try to
recall the event deliberately, the terror returned at its original intensity, and this made memory
itself feel dangerous. These intrusive emotional states fed directly into his sense that something
had permanently broken in him, reinforcing his belief that he was no longer the person he had been
before the flood. The fragmented, uncontrolled quality of his remembering also meant that any cue
resembling the flood — a trail map on a colleague's desk, a nature documentary playing in a waiting
room — could trigger a renewed flood of distressing emotion, as though the incomplete memory were
perpetually searching for a way back in.

Over the months since the event, Liam's appraisals of himself and his world had darkened
considerably. He had come to believe that he could not rely on other people — that the isolation of
those two hours had revealed something true about his fundamental aloneness — and this conviction
had led him to withdraw from the modest social network he had maintained. He was certain that
nothing in his life held meaning anymore, and this pervasive emptiness made it harder to resist the
pull of threat monitoring: if nothing mattered and no one could be counted on, then vigilance was
the only rational response. His negative appraisals also amplified the potency of his triggers;
because he now saw himself as permanently damaged and the world as fundamentally unsafe, even mild
emotional vulnerability — a moment of loneliness during a long sales drive — could itself become a
trigger, flooding him with the helplessness he had felt in the water. This loop had tightened
steadily. His hypervigilance and fragmented memories confirmed his worst beliefs about himself, and
those beliefs in turn kept him scanning, kept him waking at night, kept him reading ordinary
situations as harbingers of catastrophe.

Liam sought help after declining a regional sales promotion that would have required travel through
mountainous terrain. He told the intake clinician that he had once considered himself adventurous
and self-sufficient, and that turning down that opportunity was the moment he could no longer
pretend he was managing. What he most feared, he said quietly, was that the person he used to be was
simply gone. What he most hoped was that someone could tell him otherwise.
