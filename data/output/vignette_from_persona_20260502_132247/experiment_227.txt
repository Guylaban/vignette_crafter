anything other than ordinary.

Marguerite had been walking home from a city council meeting, passing through a plaza she crossed
several times a week, when a protest nearby drew a police response that escalated rapidly. She was
not part of the demonstration. She was caught in the perimeter, and in the confusion, she was shoved
to the ground by an officer, struck twice with a baton, and held face-down on the pavement for
several minutes while she called out that she was not involved. No one intervened. She was
eventually released without charge, without apology, without acknowledgment. She drove herself home,
hands shaking, and did not sleep that night.

In the weeks that followed, the memory of the pavement — the cold concrete against her cheek, the
weight pressing her down, the sound of her own voice unanswered — began to surface without
invitation. Marguerite found that almost anything could bring it back instantly and completely: an
unwanted thought drifting in during a quiet moment at the front desk, the anniversary of the evening

during a conference weekend. When the crowds thickened at check-in, the sensory density — bodies,
noise, the feeling of being hemmed in — would collapse the distance between the present and the
plaza, and she would find herself reliving the event with the same terror she had felt on the
ground. The memory, when triggered this way, arrived not as recollection but as reinstatement:
vivid, complete, and terrifying in exactly the proportion it had been the first time.

What those triggers also brought with them — arriving alongside the memory, sometimes preceding it —

could not argue herself out of, that the people and institutions she had trusted to protect citizens
like her had failed her utterly and deliberately. The police, the bystanders, the city itself — none
of them had seen her as someone worth protecting. That conviction, arriving on the heels of each
trigger, settled into something fixed and cold.

had happened was not an aberration but a revelation: she had been shown something true about her
place in the world. And that belief, once formed, made the memories harder to bear, not easier —
because now they carried not only fear but meaning. Remembering brought back the terror, and the
terror confirmed the conclusion, and the conclusion made the next remembering more freighted than
the last.

Over these fourteen months, Marguerite has grown increasingly uncertain of who she is. The woman who
