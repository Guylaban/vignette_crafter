Kawin Srisuk is a fifty-year-old divorced Thai-American musician who presents for an initial
psychological assessment following an extended period of stalking that lasted nearly two years
before culminating in a violent confrontation at his apartment building eighteen months ago. Kawin
had built a modest but meaningful career performing and teaching traditional and contemporary
Southeast Asian fusion music in the Pacific Northwest, and it was through that professional world
that his stalker first made contact — initially as an admiring audience member, then as someone who
began appearing at every venue, every workshop, and eventually outside his home. Kawin had reported
the escalating harassment to local police on three separate occasions and had filed a formal
complaint with the musicians' union, but nothing substantive was done. The night the stalker forced
entry into the lobby of his building and cornered him in the stairwell, screaming and brandishing a
knife before a neighbor intervened, Kawin was entirely alone in his terror. He had trusted that the
system would protect him. It had not.

In the months that followed, Kawin found that his memory of the event was not a single coherent
thing but a fractured, unreliable presence. There are stretches of that night he cannot recover at
all — blank spaces where he knows something happened but can retrieve nothing — and then, without
warning, a fragment surfaces: the sound of the stairwell door slamming, the smell of the confined
space, the quality of the overhead light. These sudden pieces arrive unbidden during rehearsals,
while he is tuning an instrument or in the middle of a lesson, and when they come with full sensory
force — which they sometimes do — he is no longer in the practice room but back in that stairwell,
the experience indistinguishable from the original. These intrusive episodes leave him shaken and
hyperalert, scanning the faces of students and passersby for any sign of hostile intent, and they
have reinforced his growing conviction that the world is fundamentally and unpredictably dangerous.
The memory, in its fragmentary and overwhelming form, feeds directly into his sense of ongoing
threat, as though the event has never fully ended.

That sense of threat, in turn, shapes the memories themselves. When Kawin is already on edge —
watching others closely in a studio waiting room, or lying awake at night listening to sounds in the
corridor — the intrusions come more easily and more violently, the sensory reliving more complete.
He has come to dread nighttime most of all; darkness feels like an environment in which danger
cannot be assessed and in which he is most exposed. He avoids unfamiliar venues entirely now, which
has cost him significantly: he has declined two touring opportunities and stopped accepting
engagements at clubs he has not previously visited, and his income has contracted accordingly. His
students notice that he seems distracted, sometimes stopping mid-sentence and staring toward the
door.

What has perhaps done the most damage is what Kawin has made of all of this. He had come to believe,
with quiet but firm certainty, that his inability to protect himself that night — and his failure to
make the authorities act before it came to violence — revealed something fundamental about who he
is. He feels like a weak person. He describes feeling dead inside, as though the emotional range
that once made him a sensitive and expressive musician has simply gone quiet. These beliefs about
himself as inadequate and abandoned by institutions deepen his perception of threat: if he is weak
and unprotected, then danger is everywhere and he alone cannot manage it. And the numbness and self-
contempt make the intrusive memories harder to integrate — when a fragment surfaces, he meets it not
with curiosity or self-compassion but with shame, which keeps the material raw and unprocessed,
cycling back through his days without resolution.

Kawin came to seek help after his ex-wife, with whom he remains on cordial terms, told him she was
frightened by how diminished he seemed. What he most fears is that this is now simply who he is.
What he most hopes, though he said it quietly and without much conviction, is that he might one day
feel safe enough to play music the way he used to — fully present, unguarded, and alive to it.
