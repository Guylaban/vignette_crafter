Amara is a 42-year-old Congolese-born woman who came to the United States following a period of
political detention in her home country. She is divorced, lives alone in a small apartment near the
factory where she works the early morning shift, and presented for psychological evaluation with a
PCL-5 score of 73. She described her reason for seeking help with characteristic understatement: she
said she was "not functioning the way a person should."

The events that brought her to this point occurred approximately four years ago, when Amara was
detained by security forces and held for eleven days. During that time she was subjected to
prolonged physical torture in a concrete room that smelled of rust and damp earth. Her captors
referred to her repeatedly by a shortened form of her name — a diminutive she had once loved — and
the sessions often took place in the early morning hours during the region's heavy rainy season, the
sound of downpours audible through a narrow window above her head. She was eventually released
without explanation and made her way, over many months, to the United States.

Amara's memory of those eleven days carries a quality she struggles to articulate. She says the
events were so extreme that she sometimes cannot believe they happened to her — they feel, she says,
like something she read about rather than something she survived. The memory feels alien, as though
it belongs to another woman entirely. And yet it is not distant: when the memory surfaces, she can
smell the rust and the wet concrete as clearly as if she were standing in that room, and this
sensory return is what she finds most destabilising. The vividness of that olfactory recall pulls
her back into the memory with a force that overrides her sense of being safe in the present, and in
the wake of such intrusions she finds herself scanning her apartment for sounds — listening at the
door, tracking every creak of the building — certain that the danger the memory carries is somehow
still real and proximate.

Her body, she has come to believe, cannot be trusted. It reacts before she has had time to think:
she startles violently at ordinary sounds on the factory floor — a dropped metal casing, a
compressed-air hiss — and her heart is already pounding before her mind has registered what she
heard. This hypervigilant state, in which her body seems to operate on a threat logic of its own,
has led her to a dark conclusion: that she is no longer capable of the things that once defined her.
Before her detention, she was known among her family as someone physically strong, emotionally
steady, dependable under pressure. Now she feels the world to be fundamentally dangerous, her body
unreliable, and herself diminished. These appraisals are not abstract — they press on her daily, and
they keep her oriented toward threat in a way that makes even ordinary moments feel precarious.

Certain things in her daily life function as doorways back into the room. Physical closeness — a
supervisor leaning over her workstation, a colleague brushing past her in the corridor — produces an
immediate somatic alarm. Hearing a name that resembles the diminutive her captors used causes her
chest to tighten before she has consciously registered the sound. And when the weather turns heavy
and humid, the way it does in late summer in this part of the Midwest, she finds herself unable to
leave her apartment without effort, the air itself a trigger. When these moments arrive, the memory
floods back with its smells and its weight, and the threat monitoring that was already running
intensifies further — she listens harder, startles more readily, and her conviction that the world
is dangerous and her body unreliable deepens.

To manage this, Amara has built a careful architecture of control. She applies heavy makeup each
morning, covering the visible scars on her face and neck — wearing them exposed, she says, makes her
feel like the woman in that room, not the woman at the machine. She avoids the clothes she was
wearing during her detention, choosing her wardrobe with a precision her coworkers do not
understand. She keeps her mind occupied at all times — podcasts during the commute, mental
arithmetic during breaks — because stillness allows the memory to surface. But these strategies, she
has begun to notice, are not working. The distraction keeps the memory just below the threshold of
full recall without ever resolving it, and the avoidances have gradually narrowed her life: she has
declined two promotions that would have required her to supervise others, certain she is no longer
the capable person such a role would demand. What finally brought her to seek help was a morning
last month when she heard a coworker's name called across the factory floor, smelled the industrial
coolant that had pooled near her station, and found herself frozen at her machine for nearly four
minutes, unable to move. What she most fears is that this will never stop tightening. What she most
hopes is that someone can help her believe she is no longer in that room.
