Darnell is a 61-year-old African American man who has worked as a city bus driver for nearly two
decades. He is married, and his wife describes him as someone who, until recently, was steady and
dependable — the kind of man who kept his schedule, kept his promises, and kept his feelings largely
to himself. That steadiness has been shaken in ways neither of them fully understands, and it was
his wife who finally persuaded him to seek help.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Darnell was taken and held by a group of men during what began as
a routine layover on an unfamiliar route he had agreed to cover for a colleague. The details of what
followed are not something he has been able to recount in sequence. He was restrained in a confined
space, subjected to prolonged physical abuse, and forced to witness the same being done to another
man — a stranger, someone he did not know and could not help. He does not know how long he was held.
He does not know whether the other man survived. These gaps are not chosen silences; they are
absences in his own mind, places where the memory simply stops and then resumes somewhere else, like
a film with frames cut out. Some moments are still unbearably vivid — the texture of the floor, a
particular sound, the smell of the room — while entire stretches of hours remain blank. He has come
to believe that the missing pieces must contain something worse than what he can remember, and this
belief alone is enough to fill him with dread.

What Darnell carries most heavily is not the physical aftermath but the moral one. He is certain he
is a weak man. He had not fought back. He had not called out to the other man, had not done anything
he can name as courage. The conviction that he failed to protect someone who was suffering beside
him has settled into something close to self-contempt, and he has told his therapist, quietly, that
he does not expect to forgive himself. These appraisals of himself as fundamentally inadequate and
culpable have made the world feel more dangerous in a specific way: if he failed then, he believes
he will fail again. He is not safe to trust, and the world, therefore, is not safe to inhabit. This
reasoning — that his own weakness makes threat perpetually likely — has intensified his vigilance in
ways that feel logical to him even as they exhaust him.

At night the vigilance is worst. Darnell sleeps lightly if at all, lying still and listening for
sounds at the windows or the door, certain that the perimeter of his home is not as secure as it
appears. During the day he manages better, but evenings on his bus route — the last runs, when the
light drops and the streets empty — carry a particular weight. The sounds of the bus, the confined
space, the loss of open visibility: these are the conditions that most reliably bring the physical
reactions he cannot control, the sudden flooding of his body with something that feels
indistinguishable from the original terror. When those physical reactions surge through him, the
memory fragments surface with them, and the terror he felt during the event returns not as a
recollection but as a repetition — something happening now rather than then. And each time the
memory returns with that force, it confirms for him again that he is broken, that a stronger man
would have processed this by now, that his inability to move past it is itself evidence of the
weakness he already knew he had.

The loop has tightened over months. His wife has noticed that he no longer speaks about the future.
He has requested only daytime routes and offered no explanation his supervisor would accept. He has
begun checking the locks on the front door multiple times before bed, a habit his wife watches
without comment because she does not know what to say. What finally brought him to seek help was a
morning when he sat in the driveway in his uniform for forty minutes, unable to make himself start
the bus. What he is most afraid of, he says, is that there is nothing left to recover — that what
the event took from him was not temporary. What he is hoping for, though he offers this more
quietly, is that someone might tell him he is not the man he has come to believe he is.
