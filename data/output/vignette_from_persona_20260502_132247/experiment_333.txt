rehabilitation clinic in suburban Chicago. He has been married for two years and, until recently,
described his life as quietly purposeful — built around his patients, his faith, and his marriage.
He presented for psychological services approximately eight months after a sexual assault that he
has disclosed to no one except, haltingly, the intake coordinator who scheduled his appointment.

The assault occurred at a professional networking event. A senior colleague — a man Tariq had
trusted and admired — offered him a ride home after the gathering ran late. In the car, and then in
the parking structure where Tariq had left his own vehicle, the colleague assaulted him. What Tariq
remembers with particular vividness is the man's cologne — a heavy, cedar-and-musk fragrance he had
noticed earlier in the evening and associated, at the time, with authority and ease. He also
remembers the physical pain that accompanied the assault, a pain that he could not process or name
in the moment and that he has since tried, with limited success, to forget entirely.

In the months that followed, Tariq found himself unable to think about what had happened in any
sustained way. He adopted a strategy of treating the event as something that had occurred to someone
else — a dissociative reframing that allowed him to continue functioning at work and at home. He
also made a deliberate, effortful practice of not allowing his mind to settle on the memory,
redirecting his thoughts whenever the event surfaced. These strategies offered partial relief, but
they carried a cost: the more he pushed the experience away, the more the unprocessed material
seemed to press back. The dissociation in particular appeared to keep the trauma vivid and
unintegrated, so that ordinary moments continued to carry the capacity to rupture his sense of
safety without warning.

His work as a physical therapist became one of the primary arenas in which this rupture occurred.
The clinic required him to perform hands-on manual therapy, and on days when a patient arrived
wearing a strong cologne or perfume, Tariq found himself flooded — his body responding before his
mind could intervene. The scent alone was sufficient to return him, briefly and involuntarily, to
the parking structure. Similarly, when his own body registered pain during a long shift — a strained
shoulder, a sore lower back — the sensation carried an associative charge that was disproportionate
to its physical cause. These somatic triggers, arriving repeatedly in his professional environment,
began to consolidate a belief that something was permanently broken in him. He had come to believe
that he would never be able to feel normal emotions again, that the person he had been before the
assault was simply gone. He felt a deep, private shame about what had been done to his body — a
shame inflected by cultural and religious frameworks around masculinity and bodily integrity that he
had never before had reason to interrogate.

These appraisals, once established, fed back into his sensitivity to triggers. Because he was
certain that his body was now a site of permanent damage, ordinary physical sensations and
environmental cues carried a threat they would not otherwise have held, and his threshold for what
constituted danger dropped steadily. His nervous system had reorganised itself around vigilance. He
startled at unexpected sounds, recoiled when colleagues touched his shoulder to get his attention,
and once, during a session, nearly struck a patient who moved suddenly within his peripheral vision.
He began monitoring his body constantly throughout the day — scanning for signs of injury, for
echoes of the assault — in a hypervigilant practice that kept his arousal elevated and his attention
narrowed. This sustained threat state in turn made the dissociation and thought-suppression feel
more necessary, not less, reinforcing the cycle that was keeping him unwell.

Intimacy with his wife had become almost impossible. He had not explained why, and she had grown
quietly hurt and confused. It was, finally, the damage to his marriage — the recognition that his
avoidance was costing him the relationship he valued most — that brought Tariq to seek help. What he
fears most is that the clinician will confirm what he already suspects: that he is beyond repair.
What he hopes, with a tentativeness he can barely articulate, is that he is wrong.
