Adaeze is a 48-year-old Ethiopian-American rabbi, married, who presents with a PCL-5 score of 41
following her direct exposure to a man-made disaster approximately fourteen months ago. She had been
attending an interfaith community gathering held in a municipal building when a gas explosion tore
through an adjacent corridor. The blast was sudden and total — a concussive wall of sound and
pressure that she describes as feeling like the air itself had been weaponized. In the immediate
aftermath, she moved through smoke and debris, stepping over injured people, trying to direct others
toward exits. She had come to believe, in the weeks that followed, that she had not done enough —
that she had frozen for several seconds when the explosion first hit, and that those seconds had
cost people something irretrievable. Whether or not this was objectively true mattered less to her
than the certainty with which she held it.

The guilt settled into her quickly and deeply. Adaeze became convinced that she would never forgive
herself for her inaction in those first moments, and that conviction carried with it a broader
collapse of her sense of the future: nothing good, she felt, could happen to her anymore. This was
not depression in the ordinary sense so much as a moral foreclosure — a belief that she had
forfeited her right to safety or peace. Out of that belief grew a set of behaviors that felt, to
her, like the only rational response to a world that had revealed itself as irreducibly dangerous.
She had to be especially careful, she reasoned, because you never know what can happen next. She
began checking her surroundings constantly — at the synagogue where she leads services, in the
parking lot afterward, in grocery store aisles — scanning for anything that might signal another
catastrophe before it arrived.

The threat she perceived was not abstract. She found it harder to feel safe at night than during the
day, lying awake beside her husband while her eyes moved across the darkened bedroom. Even when he
told her she was safe, or when congregants offered reassurance after services, she found it
impossible to believe them. The scanning and the nighttime vigilance reinforced her appraisals
rather than relieving them: each sweep of the environment that found nothing threatening felt not
like safety confirmed but like danger merely deferred, and this kept her sense of the world as
permanently menacing alive and active. The threat she carried internally made her more sensitive to
what was outside, so that ordinary sounds — a car backfiring in the synagogue parking lot, a door
slamming during a service — landed in her body as potential explosions, pulling her back into the
corridor with a physical force she could not override.

Over months, she began avoiding anything that might return her to that place. She asked her husband
not to mention the incident, and she stopped reading the news, having learned that related coverage
could undo her for an entire day. Intimacy with her husband had quietly ceased — she could not fully
explain this to him, only that being physically close felt, in some way she could not articulate,
connected to vulnerability she was no longer willing to enter. These avoidances brought their own
costs. The withdrawal from her husband had introduced a distance between them that neither had named
but both felt. And paradoxically, the effort to seal off reminders kept the original event close:
the very vigilance required to avoid triggers meant she was constantly, implicitly, oriented toward
the explosion she was trying not to think about. The avoidance sustained the appraisals — if she had
to work this hard to stay safe, the world must indeed be as dangerous as she feared — and the
appraisals drove her back into the checking and the scanning, tightening a loop she could feel but
not escape.

What finally brought Adaeze to seek help was a Shabbat service three weeks ago during which a
congregant accidentally knocked over a metal stand, producing a loud clang that sent her stumbling
backward from the bimah in front of her entire community. She had stood there, trembling, unable to
speak for nearly a minute. What she is most afraid of is that she is no longer the person her
congregation needs her to be. What she is most hoping for, though she says it quietly, is to believe
again that something good is still possible.
