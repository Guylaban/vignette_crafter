Minh Tran is a 52-year-old Vietnamese American HVAC technician, married for twenty-three years, who
presented following a toxic chemical exposure incident at a commercial building site approximately
eight months ago. He carries a PCL-5 score of 73, reflecting a severe and entrenched symptom picture
that has progressively narrowed his world since the event.

The incident occurred on an otherwise routine service call to a large office complex where Minh had
been contracted to inspect and repair a rooftop ventilation system. Without adequate warning from
the building manager, he entered a mechanical room where a refrigerant line had ruptured overnight,
flooding the enclosed space with a high concentration of chemical vapour. Within seconds of
entering, Minh experienced a burning sensation in his throat and chest, his vision blurred, and he
lost his footing. He recalls the sound of the ventilation ducts humming above him — a sound
indistinguishable from any system he services every day — and the sensation of not being able to
draw a full breath, convinced in those moments that he was dying alone in a sealed room. A colleague
found him on the floor several minutes later. He was hospitalised for three days with chemical
bronchitis and discharged with a clean prognosis, but by that point the damage to his sense of
safety had already taken root.

In the weeks following discharge, Minh's body did not receive the message that the danger had
passed. He described his nervous system as permanently switched on — muscles held tight across his
shoulders and jaw from the moment he woke, a low-grade physical vigilance that never fully released.
He had come to believe, with a certainty that no reassurance from his pulmonologist or his wife
could dislodge, that his body remained in genuine peril, that the chemical had done something that
the tests had simply not yet detected. Even when his wife sat beside him and told him he was safe,
he found it impossible to believe her. The doctors' words slid off him. He began checking the locks
on the doors and windows of their home each evening — once, then again, then a third time — not out
of any rational concern about intruders, but because the act of checking gave him a few seconds of
something that resembled control over his environment, a brief interruption to the feeling that
danger could enter any sealed space without warning.

It was this unrelenting sense of threat that drove the behavioural patterns that have come to define
his daily life. Because his body felt perpetually braced for harm, relaxation itself had become
suspect — lying still, letting his guard down, felt not like rest but like exposure, like the moment
before the chemical hit. He stopped watching television in the evenings, stopped sitting quietly in
the garden. At work, the problem became acute: the sound of ductwork, the smell of refrigerant
compounds, the sight of a closed mechanical room door — all of it collapsed the present moment back
into that humming, burning room. He began declining solo service calls, telling his dispatcher he
preferred to work with a partner, and then declining any call that required entering enclosed
mechanical spaces alone. Being alone in a room or building had become genuinely intolerable; he
needed to know someone else was present and could find him. He avoided anything that might generate
additional stress — conflict, deadlines, unfamiliar environments — as though his system had
calculated, correctly from its own distorted logic, that it had no remaining capacity to absorb
further threat. His employer, initially accommodating, has grown frustrated. Minh has turned down
enough assignments that his hours have been quietly reduced, and he is aware the situation is
unsustainable.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment last month when he sat in his truck outside a
client's building for forty minutes, unable to make himself go in, and then drove home without
calling his dispatcher. He told his wife that evening, for the first time, that he was frightened
not of the building but of himself — of how small his life had become. What he hopes for,
tentatively, is to be able to work again without terror. What he fears most is that the tightness in
his chest means the danger was never over.
