Miriam is a 44-year-old single woman who works as a hospital chaplain, a vocation she had chosen
precisely because she believed in the presence of grace within suffering. That belief was violently
tested eighteen months ago when she was among the survivors of a coordinated bombing attack at a
civic gathering she had attended as part of an interfaith outreach initiative. The device detonated
without warning in a crowded hall. She remembers — or rather, she is seized by — the concussive
pressure against her chest, the smell of burning fabric and something she cannot name, the sight of
uniformed emergency responders arriving in what felt like both an instant and an eternity. What she
cannot do, even now, is reconstruct the sequence of those minutes with any coherence. The memories
do not arrange themselves into before and after; they arrive as fragments of sensation, each one
feeling more vivid and immediate than anything from her ordinary life, as though the event refuses
to recede into the past tense. When a memory surfaces — triggered by a sudden noise in a hospital
corridor, or by the sight of a security officer near the chaplaincy wing — it does not feel like
recollection. It feels like re-entry: the pressure returns to her chest, the smell is there, and for
several seconds she is not in the present at all.

The potency of those intrusions shapes nearly everything about how Miriam now moves through her
days. Because the memories arrive so completely and without warning, she has come to read her
environment as a continuous source of threat. She scans the faces of strangers in waiting rooms,
watching for signs of concealed intent, and whenever she enters a space — the hospital chapel, the
cafeteria, a family lounge — she maps it before sitting, choosing a position that places a wall at
her back and a clear line to the exit. Unexpected sounds, a dropped tray or a door slamming, send
her into a state of high alert that takes long minutes to subside. This hypervigilance is
exhausting, and it has begun to confirm for her a belief she cannot shake: that the world is
genuinely and irreversibly dangerous, and that the institutions she once trusted — the security
services, the event organizers, the structures of public life — failed catastrophically to protect
the people in that hall. She had believed in those structures. Now she finds she cannot be near
anyone in authority or uniform without her body tightening, and she routes her hospital rounds to
avoid the security desk and the administrative wing, which has not gone unnoticed by her supervisor.

The appraisals themselves become a kind of accelerant. When Miriam catches herself feeling a moment
of pleasure — a patient's gratitude, autumn light through a window — the feeling confuses and
frightens her, as though joy is a form of inattention that will cost her something. People behaving
carelessly or badly, a visitor arguing loudly in a corridor, confirms to her that the world she now
inhabits is one where harm is always proximate. And when she feels that she has no control over what
happens next, the sense of danger spikes, cycling back into the intrusive memories with renewed
force. The appraisals and the memories feed one another: her conviction that she is in a dangerous
world primes her to notice threat, and noticing threat pulls the sensory fragments of the bombing
forward again, each return deepening her certainty that she is permanently changed and poorly
equipped to manage it.

To hold this at bay, Miriam has developed a set of private rituals — sequences of thought she
performs each morning, certain phrases repeated in a particular order, which she understands,
without quite believing, are meant to prevent another catastrophe. She has also quietly reduced how
much she eats, though she would struggle to explain the logic; there is something about diminishing
herself that feels like a form of control. These strategies do not quieten the threat monitoring; if
anything, the vigilance they require keeps her nervous system primed, which she then interprets as
evidence that danger is real, and that she is right to be afraid.

she could not be fully present — that she was watching the door. She arrived at her first
appointment hoping, above everything, to be told that she is not, as she privately fears, broken
beyond repair.
