Caleb is a 25-year-old single man of North American background who works as a housekeeper at a mid-
sized hotel. He presented for psychological assessment reporting a PCL-5 score of 44, describing a
constellation of symptoms that have been intensifying over the past year and that he now recognises
as interfering significantly with his ability to function. The trauma at the centre of his
presentation is a history of severe physical abuse perpetrated by his primary caregiver during
childhood — a pattern that unfolded across years but that he returns to, again and again, in a
cluster of specific, visceral memories: the darkness of the room where it most often happened, the
rough texture of the fabric against his skin as he was restrained, and the sounds of younger
children crying in adjacent rooms, sounds that told him no one was coming and that he was entirely
alone with his abuser.

These three sensory details — darkness, texture, children's distress — have not remained in the
past. In Caleb's current life, they arrive without warning and with full force. Working a morning
shift, he strips a hotel bed and his hand catches the coarse weave of a particular institutional
pillowcase; within seconds he is no longer in a hotel corridor but somewhere else entirely, the
memory returning not as a recollection but as a full sensory reliving in which he can feel the
weight, smell the room, and hear the sounds as though they are happening now. These intrusions play
like a film that loops without his consent, and when they come he reports feeling so overwhelmed by
emotion that he has to stop whatever he is doing and brace himself against the nearest wall. It is
this quality of the memory — its insistence, its completeness, its refusal to stay historical — that
has driven most of what his life has become.

Because the memories arrive through these specific sensory gateways, Caleb has reorganised his daily
existence around avoiding them. He has requested only daytime shifts and leaves lights on throughout
his apartment at night, sleeping fitfully against a backdrop of lamps and screens; when he wakes in
the dark regardless, the disorientation is immediate and the physical reaction — racing heart, cold
sweat, a jolt of nausea — arrives before he is fully conscious. He has stopped taking on the heavier
cleaning responsibilities at the hotel that require him to enter storage rooms alone, citing vague
excuses to supervisors, and he has developed a strict rule that he must never be alone with another
person in an enclosed space. This last strategy has quietly eroded his social world: colleagues have
stopped inviting him for after-shift drinks, and a brief romantic interest ended when he could not
explain why he always needed to meet in crowded, well-lit public places and would not come inside.
He drifts, too — when the memories press close and avoidance is not possible, he finds himself
floating away from the present moment into an internal world of his own construction, a habit that
his supervisor has begun to notice and name as inattentiveness.

What Caleb has not been able to avoid is the way the memories have shaped what he believes about
himself and the world. He has come to feel, with a certainty that disturbs him, that others can see
what was done to him simply by looking at him — that it is written on his face or his posture in a
language everyone but him can read. More pervasively, he has arrived at the conviction that he is
not quite a person in the way others are; that he was made into an object by what happened, and that
this is what he remains. These appraisals do not stay contained: they make the world feel
perpetually legible as dangerous, and he is certain that you can never truly know who will harm you,
which means that every unfamiliar face, every enclosed space, every moment of proximity to another
person requires monitoring. The hypervigilance that results keeps him scanning constantly, and the
scanning keeps his nervous system primed, which means the triggers find him faster and the memories
return more readily. His efforts to manage this — the avoidance, the withdrawal, the fantasy escapes
— have paradoxically kept the memories fresh and his beliefs about himself unchallenged, because
nothing he does ever allows him to learn anything different.

Caleb sought help after his supervisor formally documented his performance as deteriorating. What
frightens him most, he said quietly at the end of the intake, is not the memories themselves but the
possibility that the person he has become is now permanent.
