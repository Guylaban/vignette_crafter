Kofi is a 33-year-old Ghanaian-born tailor, currently single, who presented for psychological
assessment following a period of severe and worsening distress that had begun to dismantle both his
working life and his sense of who he was. Approximately fourteen months before seeking help, Kofi
had been present at a crowded outdoor market in the city when a bomb detonated near the entrance of
a textile vendor's stall — a stall he visited weekly to source fabric for his shop. The blast threw
him against a concrete pillar. He remembered the smell of burning cloth, the sound of screaming, and
then a strange, muffled silence before he found himself on the ground surrounded by people who were
no longer moving. He does not remember how he got out. There is a gap of perhaps twenty minutes that
remains completely inaccessible to him no matter how carefully he tries to reconstruct it, and that
absence frightens him in ways he cannot fully articulate. What he does remember returns unbidden and
with ferocious clarity: the moment before the explosion, the particular orange of the fabric bolts
on the stall, the way the light changed. These images replay like a film he cannot switch off,
arriving at night and during the quiet hours of the afternoon when his sewing machine falls silent.

The memory's intrusive quality feeds directly into a state of bodily alarm that Kofi now carries
everywhere. He had come to believe, in the months following the attack, that danger was not an event
but a permanent condition — something ambient and waiting. In his small tailoring shop, he
rearranges his worktable so that his back is always against the rear wall and he can see the full
length of the room and the street beyond the window. When customers enter, he finds himself scanning
the door frame, noting where the exits are before he registers their faces. This hypervigilance is
exhausting, but abandoning it feels, to Kofi, like a form of suicide. The intrusive memories, when
they surge during working hours, sharpen this threat perception acutely — the film plays and his
nervous system responds as though the market is still burning, as though the orange fabric is still
catching light.

The threat-monitoring and the relentless replay together have gradually reshaped what Kofi believes
about himself, and this is where the clinical picture becomes most painful. He had once thought of
himself as someone capable and steady — a man who had built his tailoring business from a single
second-hand machine. Now he was certain he was weak. He could not trust his own judgment; during the
attack he had frozen, or at least that is the story the memory gaps allow him to construct, and the
uncertainty is worse than any clear recollection might be. He no longer feels he belongs in his
shop, in his neighbourhood, or among the other traders he once knew. The sense of dislocation is
total. And it is this constellation of beliefs — that he is weak, untrustworthy, belonging nowhere —
that drives him toward the behaviours that have come to structure his days. He spends long hours on
his phone, scrolling and playing games, because the screen fills the space where thought would
otherwise go. He has begun picking arguments with suppliers and neighbouring shopkeepers over small
things, manufacturing friction because the adrenaline of conflict briefly displaces the dread. He
has stopped making any plans for the future — no orders beyond the immediate week, no thought of
expanding the shop — because planning requires a self he no longer believes exists.

The elevated threat state reinforces these patterns: when the hypervigilance peaks, the phone
becomes a refuge, and the arguments become a way of externalising an internal alarm that has no
other outlet. Occasionally, Kofi can recall fragments of the event with complete emotional flatness,
as though narrating something that happened to someone else — a dissociated quality that sits
alongside the vivid replays without resolving them.

What finally brought him to seek help was a confrontation with a long-standing customer that ended
with the man leaving and not returning. Kofi had lost business before, but this time he sat alone in
his shop afterward and felt nothing — and the nothing frightened him more than the grief would have.
He came to the clinic not knowing what he hoped for, only that he could not continue disappearing
into himself.
