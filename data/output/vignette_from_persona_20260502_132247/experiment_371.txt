Mateo is a 22-year-old Latin American man who works as an HVAC technician and presented for intake
following a workplace accident that occurred approximately fourteen months ago. He had been
performing routine maintenance on a rooftop unit at a commercial building when a colleague, working
nearby on an adjacent platform, lost his footing on a surface Mateo had recently cleared of debris.
Mateo had moved a safety cable to access a panel and had not yet re-secured it when his colleague
fell, dropping nearly twelve feet onto a lower ledge. The man survived but sustained a severe spinal
injury and has not returned to work. Mateo was the last person to handle that cable. He knows this
with a certainty that has never loosened its grip on him.

In the weeks after the accident, Mateo found himself returning compulsively to every decision he had
made that morning — the angle at which he had moved the cable, whether he had called out a warning,
how many seconds had passed before he heard the impact. He had come to believe, with a conviction
that felt irrefutable, that he had failed to do what any competent person would have done, and that
this failure revealed something permanent and damning about who he was. The person he saw in the
mirror each morning felt unrecognisable — not the steady, capable young man who had taken pride in
the trade, but someone whose hands could not be trusted and whose body had proven itself unreliable
in the moment that mattered most. These appraisals drove him toward a set of strategies that seemed,
at first, like reasonable self-management. He stopped attending the quarterly safety briefings at
work, rerouted his morning commute to avoid the street where the building stood, and quietly marked
the date of the accident on his calendar only to spend the entire week surrounding it in a state of
preemptive withdrawal, calling in sick and barely leaving his apartment. Yet the more he contracted
his world in this way, the more his sense of himself as damaged and culpable deepened — the
avoidance itself became evidence of his guilt, proof that he could not face what he had done.

The memories did not stay contained. They forced their way into his mind without invitation — during
a service call, while eating, in the middle of a conversation — and when they arrived they carried a
sensory completeness that was almost unbearable. He could smell the rooftop: the hot tar, the
metallic bite of the HVAC housing, something faintly chemical from the sealant he had been using.
The memory had a quality he struggled to articulate, as though it had happened to someone else and
he was watching footage of a stranger, and yet the smell and the sound were entirely his own. His
conviction that he was unforgivable seemed to pull the memory forward more insistently, as though
the mind were returning again and again to a wound it could not close.

The memories, in turn, had recoloured the world around him. On job sites now — crawl spaces,
mechanical rooms, flat rooftops — Mateo's body responded before his thoughts could intervene: heart
rate spiking, hands going cold, breath shortening into something close to panic. He had come to feel
safe only in a small number of places: his apartment, his mother's kitchen, occasionally his truck.
Unusual silence on a job site — the moment between shutting down a unit and the ambient noise
returning — had become a specific source of dread, as though something catastrophic was always about
to fill that quiet. This hypervigilant orientation to his environment kept him in a state of
perpetual readiness that, in turn, made intrusive recall more likely; the body already braced, the
memory needing little invitation.

The strategies he had built around all of this had cost him concretely. His supervisor had begun
assigning him to solo residential calls rather than commercial sites, reading his reluctance to work
at height as a performance concern. Mateo had not corrected this impression. He had stopped
explaining himself to anyone.

What finally brought him in was a conversation with his mother, who told him quietly that she no
longer recognised her son. He had heard something in that — an echo of his own worst fear — and made
the call the following morning. What he most hoped for, he said at the end of the intake, was to be
able to work again without his body betraying him. What he most feared was that the person he had
been before the accident was simply gone.
