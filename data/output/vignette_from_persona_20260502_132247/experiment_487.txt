Malee Siriwan is a seventy-six-year-old Vietnamese-Thai American woman who has worked as a mechanic
for most of her adult life, a trade she learned from her father and carried with her through decades
of immigration, marriage, and reinvention. She and her husband live in a modest home on the
outskirts of a mid-sized Southern city, and until roughly eighteen months ago, she described her
life as quietly satisfying — her days spent under cars, her evenings in the garden she kept along
the back fence. That garden, and what happened in it, is where her current suffering begins.

The stalking began gradually, as it often does. A man she had known peripherally through a
neighborhood association began appearing with increasing frequency near her property — at the edge
of the lot when she worked in the yard, idling in a vehicle outside her shop, eventually leaving
objects at her fence line. For several months Malee minimized what she was experiencing, attributing
his presence to coincidence. The turning point came on an afternoon when she was kneeling in the
soil behind her house, her hands in the earth and the smell of turned garden soil thick in the air,
when she looked up and found him standing at the gate watching her. He said nothing. He did not need
to. She understood in that moment, with a clarity she has never been able to fully articulate, that
she was not safe. She called out; he walked away slowly, without urgency, as though he wanted her to
understand that leaving was his choice. The incident was reported to police and eventually resolved
legally, but Malee has not recovered.

What troubles her most now is the way memory operates — or fails to. When she attempts to recount
what happened, particularly that afternoon in the garden, she loses the thread mid-sentence, trails
off, and cannot finish. She has described this to her husband as feeling like the story dissolves
before she reaches the end of it. At other times, she finds herself pushing the memory away before
it can fully form, a reflex she does not entirely control. Yet when the memory does arrive, it
arrives with terrible vividness — not as a narrative but as a series of frozen images, photographic
and exact: his face at the gate, his hands at his sides, the particular angle of the afternoon
light. These fragments surface without warning, and when they do, they pull her immediately back
into a state of acute threat. The sense that danger is present and immediate floods her body before
any conscious reasoning can intervene, and she finds herself scanning the room, checking behind her,
listening for sounds she cannot name.

The triggers that activate this cycle are specific and, to Malee, bewildering in their reach. The
smell of soil — encountered every time she passes the raised beds she no longer tends, or when a
customer's vehicle carries mud from a rural job — returns her instantly to that afternoon. She
cannot smell the earth without the photographs appearing in her mind, and once they appear, her body
responds as though the threat is current. More distressing still is what happens when she is under
stress at the shop, or in the rare moments when she allows herself to feel relaxed or even happy.
Those states, which should be neutral or welcome, have become destabilizing — as though her nervous
system has learned to treat any emotional arousal as a signal that something is about to go wrong.
Feeling happy, she has come to believe, is a warning.

The threat monitoring has become constant. At work, she checks behind her when she hears footsteps
on the shop floor. She has told her husband she feels on guard even inside their home, particularly
near the windows that face the back yard. This vigilance, exhausting as it is, feeds back into the
memory: the act of scanning and checking keeps her attention fixed on danger, and the images return
more readily, which in turn makes the triggers more sensitive and the monitoring more urgent. The
loop has been tightening for months.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning when a customer brought in a truck caked in
field mud, and she froze completely at the lift — unable to work, unable to explain why. She is
hoping, she says carefully, to be able to go back into her garden. What she is most afraid of is
that she no longer can.
