Amara is a 72-year-old widowed woman of African origin who has worked as a forester in the Pacific
Northwest for the past decade, a role she took up after emigrating following years of civil conflict
in her home country. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 64, reflecting severe and persistent
posttraumatic stress, and was referred for psychological assessment after her supervisor noted a
marked deterioration in her functioning over recent months.

The events that brought Amara to this point unfolded across several years of living through active
conflict in a city that was repeatedly shelled. The nights were the most terrifying. Artillery fire
came without warning after dark, and Amara learned to lie still in the blackness of her home
listening for the sounds that would tell her whether to run. She lost her husband during one such
night — a mortar strike that hit their neighbourhood while the two of them were trying to reach a
shelter. She survived by lying flat in a drainage ditch for several hours, alone, in complete
darkness, surrounded by the sounds of explosions and the movement of armed men through the street
above her. The combination of darkness, sudden loud sounds, and the sight of men moving with
apparent purpose became fused, in those hours, with the absolute certainty of death.

In the years since resettlement, Amara has found that her work in the forest offers a degree of
solitude she values. She is most comfortable moving alone among the trees during daylight hours,
where the landscape is open and she can see clearly in all directions. But the forest also presents
its challenges. When other workers or hikers appear on a trail unexpectedly — particularly if they
are moving quickly or behaving in ways she cannot immediately interpret — she feels a cold alertness
spread through her chest. She has come to believe that unpredictable human behaviour is an early
warning sign of danger, a conviction rooted in the memory of armed men whose intentions she could
not read. On such occasions she positions herself behind a tree or finds high ground, scanning the
surrounding area methodically until she is satisfied that no threat is present. This constant
environmental checking, which she also carries out in car parks, grocery stores, and her own front
yard, occupies a significant portion of her mental energy throughout the day.

At home, Amara keeps the television running at high volume from early evening onward. She tells
herself it is company, but the honest reason is that the noise fills the silence she cannot tolerate
— the silence that, in her body's memory, precedes an explosion. She also plays music loudly when
driving. These strategies provide momentary relief, but they carry a cost she has not fully reckoned
with: by keeping her auditory environment saturated with sound, she inadvertently ensures that
sudden noises — a car backfiring, a door slamming, a programme cutting to an advertisement with a
sharp percussive sound — emerge from the noise with even greater shock and contrast. Each such
moment sends her back, briefly but completely, to the ditch. The loud television that was meant to
prevent intrusion instead sharpens the very triggers she is trying to muffle, and the cycle
reasserts itself night after night. Similarly, her vigilant scanning of people around her, rather
than confirming safety, keeps her attention locked on threat cues, so that any ambiguous movement or
expression reads as confirmation of danger. What began as a protective habit has become a lens that
makes the world look perpetually hostile.

Nights remain the most difficult. Waking in the dark of her bedroom, Amara experiences a
disorientation that is immediate and physical — she does not know, for a moment, whether she is in
the ditch or in Oregon, and the not-knowing is its own form of terror.

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with her adult daughter, who visited last
winter and found her mother sleeping with every light in the house on, the television at full
volume, and a chair wedged under the front door handle. Amara's greatest fear is that she will never
feel safe in the dark again. Her quieter hope, offered tentatively at the end of the intake session,
is that she might one day sleep through the night.
