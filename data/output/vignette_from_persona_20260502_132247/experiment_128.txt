Fatima is an eighty-year-old widowed woman of Lebanese descent who has spent the better part of five
decades working as a social worker, accompanying families through some of the most difficult
passages of their lives. She came to the clinic three months ago, referred by her own physician
after she disclosed, almost incidentally, that she had not left her apartment in weeks. What brought
her to that point began four years earlier, when her youngest son, Karim, was admitted to the
intensive care unit with a sudden and catastrophic cardiac event. For eleven days, Fatima sat beside
his bed as machines breathed for him, as nurses adjusted the drips that ran into his arms through
needles she could not look away from, as the unit filled with the particular sharp, chemical smell
of antiseptic that she had never before found anything but neutral. Karim survived, but something in
Fatima did not recover in the way she had expected.

In the months that followed his discharge, she began to notice that the world had become threaded
with reminders she could not control. Walking past a pharmacy, she would glimpse a display of
syringes behind the counter and feel the ICU snap back into her body before her mind had time to
register what was happening — a wave of terror and grief so complete it had no image attached to it,
only sensation and weight. The smell of antiseptic, encountered in a supermarket cleaning aisle or
on a neighbor's hands, produced the same effect: the memory forced itself forward without
invitation, arriving not as a coherent recollection but as an overwhelming emotional flood, the
grief of those eleven days arriving whole and unannounced. These intrusions, in turn, deepened her
conviction that she was permanently broken. She had come to believe that the woman who had sat with
hundreds of grieving families and held their suffering with professional steadiness no longer
existed — that what the ICU had left behind was someone diminished, someone who had failed her son
by feeling so helpless when it mattered most.

That belief — that she had acted in ways that violated who she understood herself to be, that she
had stood at her son's bedside paralyzed rather than competent — sharpened her sensitivity to
anything that might confirm it. She found that when her mind turned toward the appraisal that she
was permanently damaged, it also turned toward the memory itself, replaying not images but the
texture of helplessness, which in turn made the triggers feel more potent and more present.
Conversations about loss, which her professional life had once made routine, became unbearable. When
colleagues or clients spoke of someone losing a loved one, the words functioned as an immediate
conduit back to the ICU, and the grief on recall was so overwhelming that she began to find reasons
to leave the room. Over the course of a year, she stopped attending case conferences. She began
conducting fewer home visits. She told herself she was managing her caseload differently, but what
she was doing was contracting.

She tried, with great discipline, to push the memories away when they surfaced — a strategy that
seemed logical but that left her feeling more exhausted and more certain that she had no real
control over her own mind, which only reinforced the sense of permanent damage. Avoiding hospitals
and ambulances became non-negotiable; she arranged for a neighbor to accompany her to her own
medical appointments, then stopped attending those as well. The decision to stop going out
altogether followed gradually, the way a door closes in increments. By the time she presented to the
clinic, she had not seen a client in person in two months, and her supervisor had gently indicated
that her continued employment was uncertain. This occupational consequence landed heavily — Fatima
had defined herself through her work for fifty years, and its loss felt like confirmation of
everything she feared about herself.

What finally brought her to seek help was a phone call from Karim himself, who noticed during a
visit that her apartment smelled stale and that she flinched when he mentioned a friend's
hospitalization. She is most afraid, she told the intake clinician quietly, that she will never be
the person she was. What she is hoping for, she added after a pause, is simply to be able to sit
with someone else's pain again without drowning in her own.
