evaluation following a period of sustained stalking that began approximately eight months ago. He is
single, lives alone in a studio apartment, and works remotely for a small tech startup — a living

to messages, being open and collegial. When the classmate's attention shifted from professional

the person had begun appearing outside his building, sending repeated messages across multiple
platforms, and on one occasion following him for several blocks after he left a coffee shop where he
often worked. The incident at the coffee shop was the one that lodged most deeply. Kofi had been
sitting at his usual table near the window — laptop open, headphones in — when he noticed the figure
outside. He left quickly, and the figure followed. He did not feel safe until he had locked his
apartment door behind him and sat on the floor with his back against it, heart hammering.

In the months that followed, Kofi has come to understand the world in a way that feels both
inescapable and exhausting. He has come to believe, with a quiet but persistent certainty, that the
world is a fundamentally dangerous place — that the openness he once moved through it with was
naïve, perhaps even reckless. What troubles him most is a conviction he cannot fully shake: that he
brought this on himself. He replays the semester in his mind — the comments he made, the messages he
answered, the warmth he extended — and concludes that he acted in ways that invited what happened.

in him like a stone. If he had been less friendly, less visible, less himself, perhaps none of it
would have followed him home.

This belief has reorganised how he moves through his days. Working remotely as a UX designer means
Kofi spends long hours at his desk, and the same digital fluency that defines his profession now
carries a constant undercurrent of threat. He monitors his professional profiles with a vigilance
that has little to do with career management — scanning for unfamiliar names, checking who has
viewed his work, hesitating before posting anything that might signal his location or routine. He
has removed his neighbourhood from his bio, turned off read receipts, and stopped updating the
portfolio page that once made him proud. The coffee shop where he used to work most productively —
the one near the window — he has not returned to. He now works exclusively from his apartment, and
though the isolation is wearing on him, he tells himself this is simply what careful people do.

He is careful about everything now. He checks the street before leaving his building, varies his
routes on the rare occasions he goes out, and finds himself scanning faces in a way that never used
to be his habit. He is certain that you can never truly know what someone is capable of, that threat
can emerge from ordinary friendliness, and that the only reasonable response is sustained alertness.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a project review at work. His supervisor noted, gently

that feedback for two weeks before booking an appointment. He is not sure what he is hoping for,
exactly — he does not expect to feel safe again in the way he once did. What he wants, more than
anything, is to stop feeling responsible for what happened to him.
