Layla is a thirty-year-old divorced woman of Lebanese descent who works as a bartender at a busy
downtown cocktail bar. She presented for therapy with a PCL-5 score of 61, describing years of
escalating anxiety, hypervigilance, and a growing sense that her world was becoming smaller and less
navigable. Her presenting difficulties trace their roots to a childhood saturated with domestic
violence — not as a single incident, but as a recurring, unpredictable atmosphere in which her
father's rages could erupt without warning. What she remembers most viscerally is not any one night
but a quality of motion: the way his arm would rise before the blow, the way her mother's body would
flinch and contract, the way a door slamming could precede everything terrible. These were the
textures of danger in her childhood home, and they have never fully released her.

Layla cannot tell this story in a straightforward way. When asked to describe what she witnessed,
she finds herself arriving at the edge of something she cannot cross into language. She knows what
happened — the knowledge sits in her body, in her chest, in the way her hands grip things — but the
words dissolve before she can form them. The memories do not arrive as narrative; they arrive as
sensation, as a sudden and overwhelming flood of emotion that forces her to stop, to look away, to
leave the room. This nonverbal, emotionally saturating quality of the memories means that the past
has never been processed into something she can hold at a distance. Instead it remains close, raw,
and unresolved — and that unresolved quality has quietly shaped everything she believes about
herself and others. She has come to understand herself as someone who cannot be relied upon, who
will not do the right thing when it matters, and she has extended this distrust outward: people, in
her experience, are not safe. These convictions — that she is unreliable, that others are dangerous
— keep the threat of harm feeling perpetually present, as though the violence of her childhood is
still occurring somewhere just out of sight.

This sense of ongoing threat is sharpest in her body's response to movement. At the bar where she
works, a patron reaching suddenly across the counter, a colleague turning quickly in the narrow
space behind the taps — any abrupt movement by another person puts her immediately on guard, her
nervous system reading it as the prelude to something she has to survive. She has quietly rearranged
her working habits to keep her back to the wall when possible and to monitor the room constantly.
Outside of work, she has narrowed her world considerably: open or exposed spaces feel unbearable, as
though visibility means vulnerability, and she has stopped walking to her car through the parking
structure alone. She checks the locks on her apartment door multiple times each night before she can
attempt sleep, and even then she does not feel safe except in the one corner of her apartment she
has come to think of as hers.

To manage the threat she feels everywhere, Layla has developed a set of strategies that feel
protective but have become their own burden. She rehearses worst-case scenarios in careful, detailed
loops — imagining what could go wrong, how she would respond, what she would do if someone hurt her
— convinced that preparation is the only thing standing between her and catastrophe. She works hard
to suppress whatever emotion surfaces, believing that visible distress would expose her to further
harm or judgment. But these strategies, rather than quieting the threat, keep her nervous system in
a state of constant activation: the mental rehearsal of catastrophe refreshes the sense of danger
rather than resolving it, and the emotional suppression prevents the kind of processing that might
allow memories to settle. The rehearsal and suppression cycle back into her appraisals of herself —
she interprets the fact that she still feels this afraid as further evidence that she cannot trust
herself, that she is fundamentally inadequate to her own life.

What finally brought Layla to seek help was a panic response at work so severe that she had to leave
her shift early — something she had never done before, and which her manager noticed. She is afraid,
coming into therapy, that she will be unable to speak about what happened. What she hopes for,
tentatively and almost without believing it possible, is to one day feel safe somewhere other than
her single corner of a locked apartment.
