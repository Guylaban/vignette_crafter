Kareem is a 54-year-old Iranian-American software engineer who lives alone in a mid-sized city. He
presents with a PCL-5 score of 77, indicative of severe posttraumatic stress, and sought help only
after a prolonged period of self-managed isolation that had begun to threaten his employment. He
describes himself as a private man who had built a quiet, structured life before the assault — a
life he no longer recognises.

Approximately eighteen months ago, Kareem was sexually assaulted by a man in a position of
institutional authority. The assault occurred at a location he had visited routinely, in a context
that carried the implicit weight of hierarchy and power. During the assault, there were sirens
audible in the distance — an ambulance passing outside — and the sound became woven into the fabric
of the worst minutes of his life. When it was over, no formal action was taken. The perpetrator
faced no consequences. Kareem filed no report, in part because he could not imagine being believed,
and in part because the authority the perpetrator held made any formal process feel not like
protection but like further exposure.

In the months that followed, Kareem came to believe two things with a quiet, corrosive certainty.
The first was that what had happened to him was a profound injustice — something fundamentally wrong
had been done, and the world had simply continued turning. The second, which lived alongside the
first in painful contradiction, was that he himself bore some responsibility; that something in the
way he had presented himself, the way he had acted, had made him a target. This self-blame did not
absolve his sense of injustice but compounded it, producing a kind of internal deadlock. He had also
begun to feel, in a way he struggled to articulate, that his emotional life had been amputated —
that whatever had allowed him to feel warmth or ease or ordinary pleasure was simply no longer
available to him.

These appraisals drove his behaviour in ways that felt, to him, entirely rational. He stopped going
near the location where the assault had occurred, rerouting his commute even when it cost him forty
minutes. He avoided any man in uniform — police officers, security guards, military personnel —
crossing streets, leaving coffee shops, skipping the pharmacy if a uniformed officer was present. He
refused situations that placed him alone with other men. Because he had come to believe the world
was genuinely dangerous and that he was a poor judge of safe situations, these precautions felt not
like avoidance but like basic competence.

His sense of threat had become totalising. He scanned every room he entered — his open-plan office,
the break room, the car park — cataloguing exits, noting who was present, monitoring for anything
that felt wrong. He had narrowed the geography of his felt safety to his apartment and, partially,
his car. The office had become a place of sustained vigilance rather than work. His productivity
dropped sharply, and his manager had raised concerns about his disengagement and missed deadlines,
which Kareem registered as confirmation that he was losing his grip on the life he had built.

The avoidance, over time, made everything worse in ways he had not anticipated. By withdrawing from
the location, from authority figures, from situations involving other men, he never encountered
evidence that he could survive these contexts — and so the threat monitoring intensified rather than
abated. Each time he left a space because of a siren or the sight of a uniform, the alarm system
reset at a higher baseline. The hypervigilance, in turn, reinforced his belief that he was
permanently damaged, that a man who flinched at ambulances and could not sit in a meeting room was
not someone who would ever feel normal emotions again. The loop had been running, quietly and
relentlessly, for over a year.

What finally brought Kareem to the clinic was a formal performance review in which his employer used
the phrase "no longer the person we hired." He heard in it an echo of his own worst fear — that the
assault had taken something from him that could not be returned. He arrives hoping, with a
tentativeness he cannot quite suppress, that this is not true. What he is most afraid of is that it
is.
