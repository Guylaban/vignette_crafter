Adaeze is a 77-year-old Nigerian-American woman who has spent the better part of four decades in
public office, serving most recently on a state legislative committee. She presents with a PCL-5
score of 42, seeking help for the first time after what she describes as "a lifetime of keeping it
locked away." The trauma she is now attempting to address occurred during her first marriage, in her
late thirties, when her then-husband subjected her to a sustained pattern of physical abuse that
culminated one evening in a prolonged assault that left her bleeding on the kitchen floor of their
home. She remembers the particular quality of the light in that room — the overhead fluorescent, the
dark spread across the linoleum — and it is this image, incomplete and jagged, that has followed her
across the decades.

The assault itself was not a single moment but a convergence of sensations that her nervous system
encoded with terrible precision. He had come home angry, not shouting but coldly, quietly furious,
the kind of controlled rage she had learned to fear more than noise. He had closed the distance
between them — the physical proximity of his body filling the kitchen doorway — and then there was
blood, her own, and then there was nothing she can fully account for. This is the shape of what she
carries: a memory with pieces missing, a narrative that stops and restarts, and a deep, habitual
impulse to push it away before it can complete itself. She cannot say with certainty what happened
in the final minutes of that night, and the not-knowing has become its own torment.

In the decades since, Adaeze built a formidable professional life, and for long stretches she
managed to keep the past at a functional distance. But the triggers have never fully quieted. In her
current role in the legislature, she sits in committee rooms where colleagues raise their voices in
debate, where frustration sharpens into open anger — and even a measured, controlled expression of
displeasure from across the table is enough to send her body back to that kitchen doorway. The
startle is immediate and physical: her hands tighten, her chest constricts, and the incomplete
memory surges forward in fragments before she forces it back. On days when a colleague stands too
close during a hallway conversation, the same cascade begins. The sight of blood — encountered once
when a staff member cut their hand during a session recess — sent her from the room entirely,
shaking in a way she could not explain to the people around her.

These triggered states pull her into a hypervigilant alertness that extends far beyond the moments
themselves. She sleeps poorly, finding the nights particularly unsafe in a way she cannot entirely
rationalize, and she has developed a habit of checking behind her when she walks through the capitol
building's long corridors — a habit her staff have noticed and quietly remarked upon. The vigilance,
once activated by a trigger, does not release easily; instead it seems to reach back into the
fragmented memory and confirm her worst fears about what happened, making the missing pieces feel
more threatening for being absent. She has come to believe that the incompleteness of her recall is
itself evidence of something she cannot survive knowing.

What frightens Adaeze most, however, is not the memory but what she has concluded about herself
because of it. She is certain she has been permanently altered — that the woman who existed before
that night was a different person, one capable of warmth and trust, and that this version of herself
is a diminished substitute. She will never, she believes, feel normal emotions again; the numbness
she carries into her closest relationships, including her current marriage of twenty-two years,
feels like proof. This conviction that she cannot trust people — colleagues, her husband, even her
therapist — keeps her in a state of low-grade threat monitoring that itself resurrects the triggers,
so that ordinary proximity or a shift in someone's tone becomes, again, the doorway and the light
and the blood on the floor.

What finally brought her to seek help was a vote she had to cast on domestic violence legislation.
She found she could not speak during the session. She hopes, tentatively, to feel less alone with
what she carries. She is most afraid that she is right about herself.
