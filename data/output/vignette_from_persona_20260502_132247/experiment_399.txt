Kiet Phommasack is a thirty-five-year-old Lao-American accountant who presented for an initial
intake following a referral from his primary care physician, who had noted significant sleep
disturbance and social withdrawal during a routine appointment. Kiet's PCL-5 score of 57 places him
firmly within the severe range for post-traumatic stress, and the picture that emerged across the
first two sessions was one of a man whose inner life had been quietly dismantled over the course of
several years.

Kiet was detained by government authorities during a visit to his country of origin approximately
four years ago. Over a period of several days, he was subjected to systematic physical and
psychological torture — restrained, beaten, subjected to prolonged isolation, and interrogated by
men whose faces he never clearly saw. What he remembers most vividly is not a single moment but a
texture: the smell of the room, the sound of a door being unlocked, the feeling of not knowing
whether the next person to enter would bring food or further harm. He was eventually released
without explanation and returned to the United States, where he attempted to resume his life.

For a time, he managed. He returned to his position at a mid-sized accounting firm, filed his
reports, attended his meetings. But the memories did not recede. They began arriving unbidden — not
as ordinary recollections but as fragments that felt disordered and uncontrollable, a film that
played over and over without his consent, cutting between images that he could not arrange into a
coherent sequence. He found it impossible to say with confidence what had happened first, or second,
or last. More unsettling still was the quality of dissociation that accompanied these intrusions:
the memories felt as though they belonged to someone else, as though he were reviewing footage of a
stranger's suffering rather than his own. This strangeness did not comfort him — it frightened him,
because it confirmed what he had begun to suspect: that he had been permanently changed for the
worse, that the person who had boarded the plane for that visit no longer existed.

It was in the ordinary machinery of his workday that the triggers became impossible to ignore. His
role as an accountant required him to interact regularly with new clients — strangers, by definition
— and each such encounter activated a vigilance he could not suppress. Talking to someone he did not
know, someone whose intentions he could not verify, would bring the memory flooding back without
warning: the guards, the interrogators, men who had offered nothing in their faces to read. These
intrusions, arriving in the middle of a client meeting or a phone call, sharpened into the
conviction that you can never truly know who will harm you — that any stranger might be concealing
the same capacity for cruelty he had encountered in that room. The triggers did not merely recall
the past; they reorganised his understanding of the present, feeding directly into a settled belief
that the world was populated by unpredictable danger and that he himself was too damaged to navigate
it safely. He had come to believe that someone stronger, more capable, would have found a way to
stop what happened — a thought that returned whenever he caught himself flinching at a colleague's
raised voice or freezing at the sound of a door.

These appraisals — of permanent damage, of inexplicable personal failure, of a world in which harm
could come from any direction — did not remain abstract. They functioned as a lens through which the
memories themselves were re-entered. When Kiet found himself believing, again, that he was
irreparably broken, the looping film would begin to play: the restraints, the disorientation, the
faces he could not see clearly, the sequence he could not reconstruct. Talking about the future,
even in passing — a colleague mentioning a project timeline, a friend asking about plans — could set
the same cycle in motion, as though the future itself had become a threatening unknown.

Kiet divorced two years ago. His former wife has said he became unreachable. He has reduced his
client-facing responsibilities at work, citing administrative priorities, though he knows the real
reason. What finally brought him to seek help was a performance review in which his supervisor noted
his increasing withdrawal. He arrived at the intake session composed but exhausted, and what he said
he wanted most was to stop feeling like a stranger to himself.
