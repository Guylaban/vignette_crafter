Dr. Linh Nguyen is a 51-year-old Vietnamese-American physician, married, who works as an internist
at a busy urban medical centre. She presented for psychological assessment with a PCL-5 score of 73,
reporting severe and persistent symptoms following a sexual assault that occurred approximately
fourteen months ago. What follows is a reconstruction of her presentation across intake and the
first three sessions.

Linh was attacked in the parking structure adjacent to her hospital late on a weeknight after a
double shift. A man she did not recognise approached her from behind, restrained her against her
car, and assaulted her. The assault was prolonged, and what she remembers with terrible clarity is
the smell — antiseptic on her own coat, the man's sweat, the oil-stained concrete — and the silence
of the structure, broken only by the distant hum of ventilation shafts and then, suddenly, by her
own voice. When it ended, she lay on the ground for several minutes before a security guard found
her. Two police officers arrived shortly after, and she recalls the way they stood over her,
notebooks open, voices neutral, as she sat on the cold floor with her coat pulled around her. She
gave her statement and drove herself home. She told her husband only that she had been mugged.

In the weeks that followed, Linh found that the memory did not behave the way she expected a memory
to behave. Rather than fading, it returned with the same physiological force as the event itself —
the terror was not remembered but re-experienced, and the smells in particular arrived without
warning, triggered by the antiseptic corridors of the hospital where she spent ten hours a day. The
memory felt so extreme, so far outside the architecture of her life, that she sometimes could not
believe it had happened to her at all; and yet her body insisted it had. She had come to believe,
with a certainty that frightened her, that the world she had understood — ordered, predictable, safe
for a woman who worked hard and stayed careful — had been destroyed beyond reconstruction. She had
lost her faith in other people entirely, and something in her relationship to meaning itself had
collapsed. These beliefs did not remain abstract. Because she was now certain the world was
irreversibly dangerous, her nervous system treated every unfamiliar stimulus as a potential signal
of that danger, and the threat she felt was constant and bodily.

At work, that heightened vigilance became exhausting and disorganising. Unexpected sounds — a tray
dropped in a corridor, a door slamming — sent her heart into her throat. Unusual silences, like the
moment before a patient's family member spoke, carried what felt like the specific texture of the
parking structure. Strangers approaching her in the hallway, even colleagues she did not know well,
triggered an immediate and overwhelming sense of threat. Because her appraisals of the world had
shifted so completely — she was now someone to whom this could happen, in a world where it could
happen again — the threshold for perceived danger had dropped to nearly nothing, and these
perceptions of threat fed directly back into the memory, pulling her into re-experiencing that was
almost indistinguishable from the original event.

The triggers she encountered daily were specific and potent. Examining patients required her to
engage with bodies in clinical ways that she had performed thousands of times without distress; now,
certain anatomical exposures brought the assault back with physical immediacy. She began wearing
loose, concealing clothing to her shifts, hoping to make herself less visible, less a body in the
world. She restructured her routes through the hospital to avoid the car park, and she had not
returned to that level of the structure since the night of the assault. When, on two occasions, she
encountered police officers in the emergency department, she felt a surge of threat so acute she had
to leave the room. She had not seen her attacker again, but the possibility of it — a man of similar
build glimpsed in a waiting room, a voice with a similar register — was enough to send her into
avoidance that took hours to subside.

What had begun as deliberate avoidance gradually became something she could no longer fully control.
The strategies she used to manage her distress — avoiding anything that reminded her of the assault,
restructuring her clinical day, wearing clothing that felt protective — kept her from processing
what had happened and left the memory intact in its most raw form, so that each brush with a trigger
was as destabilising as the last. Several months in, she began gambling online in the evenings,
initially describing it as a way to occupy her mind with something that demanded attention without
asking her to feel anything. Her husband noticed the hours she spent on her phone. She had told him
nothing of the assault, and the concealment had become its own burden, confirming her belief that
she was now someone who could not be known or trusted with closeness — which only deepened her
conviction that she had lost something irrecoverable in her faith in other people, and in herself.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a clinical error — minor, caught in time, but enough. She
had been distracted during a patient review by a sudden loud noise in the corridor, had lost her
train of thought, and had nearly missed an abnormal result. She sat in her office afterward and
understood that the life she had built — her competence, her identity as a physician — was being
dismantled from the inside. What she most fears is that thinking directly about what happened will
undo her completely. What she most hopes, though she does not quite believe it yet, is that someone
can show her it will not.
