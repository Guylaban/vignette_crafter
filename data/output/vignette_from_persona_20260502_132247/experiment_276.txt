Valentina is a 27-year-old Salvadoran American woman who works as an HVAC technician in a mid-sized
city in Texas. She presents to the clinic with a PCL-5 score of 39, describing a presentation that
has quietly dismantled much of her daily functioning over the past eighteen months, though she has
told almost no one.

The event that precipitated her current difficulties occurred while Valentina was visiting extended
family in El Salvador. She was detained by a group of women affiliated with a local criminal
organization — an experience she describes haltingly, still struggling to accept that it happened to
her at all. She was held in a locked room for several hours, restrained, and subjected to deliberate
physical harm intended to intimidate her family. The women who hurt her were methodical and calm,
which she found more terrifying than rage would have been. She was left with visible wounds on her
arms and torso, and she remembers the sound of the door being locked from the outside as the moment
she understood, with absolute certainty, that she had no way out.

Since returning to the United States, Valentina has found that certain moments collapse the distance
between then and now with frightening speed. Being alone with a woman — a coworker in a crawlspace,
a client who meets her at the door — produces an immediate physical alarm that she cannot reason her
way out of. On job sites, when a female supervisor accompanies her to inspect ductwork in a tight
mechanical room, Valentina feels the walls contract around her. Seeing visible injuries on others,
even something as minor as a bandaged hand, pulls her back with a force she cannot control. These
triggers flood her with threat, her heart rate spiking, her body scanning exits before her mind has
fully registered what it is doing.

The memory itself is deeply disorganizing. Valentina describes it as belonging to someone else — a
dissociated quality that makes her doubt her own account of events — and yet when it surfaces, it
brings with it a shame so acute she has to stop whatever she is doing and press her hands flat
against a surface to stay present. The overwhelm is total. That the memory carries this quality of
shame and alienness does not protect her; if anything, the sense that she cannot fully claim or
examine it keeps it from settling, and it returns without warning, reactivating the same
hypervigilant state she experiences when triggered in person.

At home, Valentina has developed a set of nightly rituals that she understands, on some level, are
excessive, but that she cannot abandon. She checks every door and window lock before bed and wakes
at least twice each night to check again, convinced that the stillness of the apartment is not proof
of safety but merely the pause before something goes wrong. She startles at sounds — a neighbor's
door, a car alarm — in a way that leaves her embarrassed and exhausted. These behaviors feel to her
like the only rational response to a world she has come to understand as fundamentally unreadable.
She has come to believe that you can never really know who will harm you, and this conviction,
rather than being shaken by the checking and scanning, is reinforced by it: every night she checks
and survives feels less like evidence of safety than like a near miss she was lucky to catch.

What makes the loop most suffocating is that her appraisals of herself have become entangled with
everything else. She is certain she has been permanently changed for the worse — that the woman who
went to El Salvador no longer exists — and this belief colors how she reads her own memory, making
the shame on recall feel like confirmation rather than symptom. She doubts her own judgment so
thoroughly that she second-guesses her responses on job sites, worries she will misread a situation
and either overreact or fail to protect herself, and has begun turning down solo calls that would
take her to unfamiliar locations.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a moment last month when she declined a job assignment
because the site supervisor was a woman she did not know, and she realized, sitting in her truck,
that her world had become very small. She is not sure she can be helped. What she most fears is that
this is simply who she is now.
