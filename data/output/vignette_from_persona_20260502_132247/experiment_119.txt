Kwame is a 33-year-old Ghanaian-American man who works as a social media manager for a mid-sized
nonprofit in Atlanta. He is married, and until recently his colleagues and his wife alike described
him as steady, warm, and unflappable. He presented to the clinic following a referral from his
primary care physician, with a PCL-5 score of 68 and a quiet insistence that he was "not handling
things the way I should."

Three years ago, Kwame volunteered for an eight-month deployment with a humanitarian relief
organization operating in a conflict zone in the eastern Democratic Republic of Congo. He had done
similar work before and believed he was prepared. What he was not prepared for was the ambush on a
convoy he was travelling with during his sixth month — a coordinated attack that killed two local
drivers and left two aid workers critically wounded. Kwame was uninjured in any way that could be
photographed. He spent four hours pinned behind a vehicle, listening to the sounds of sustained
gunfire and the voices of men he could not help, before government forces arrived. He was evacuated
within days and told, in the bureaucratic language of incident reports, that the matter was under
investigation. It was not, to his knowledge, ever resolved. No one was held to account. The
organization issued a statement. Kwame flew home to Atlanta.

In the months that followed, Kwame came to carry a belief that felt less like a thought and more
like a physical fact: that the world had looked at what happened to him and to the men who died and
had simply turned away. He was certain that what had occurred was a profound injustice, and the
absence of any consequence for those responsible confirmed, in his mind, that such violence was not
aberrant but tolerated — which meant it could happen again, anywhere, to anyone. He had come to
believe, most painfully, that he could not rely on himself; the memory of those four hours, in which
he had done nothing, had calcified into evidence of some fundamental inadequacy. These appraisals
did not remain abstract. They fed directly into a state of chronic threat readiness that now
organized much of his waking life. He was superalert in a way that exhausted him — scanning parking
lots, tracking exits in restaurants, startling violently at the sound of a car backfiring or a door
slamming in the office corridor. He found it nearly impossible to concentrate on sustained work,
losing the thread of a sentence mid-draft, forgetting what he had opened a browser tab to find.

The hypervigilance and the startle responses were distressing enough that Kwame began, almost
without deciding to, to manage them through avoidance. He stopped letting himself think about the
convoy. When images surfaced — the angle of light on the road, the particular quality of the silence
before the shooting began — he learned to slide away from them, to detach himself so efficiently
that the memories felt like footage from someone else's life. He also began constantly scanning his
environment, a habit that had started as vigilance but had become its own compulsive ritual, as
though checking the room could forestall what checking the room could never actually prevent. The
irony was not lost on him, though he lacked the language to name it: the strategies he used to
manage the feeling of threat kept him perpetually oriented toward threat. Sitting at his desk
managing content calendars and engagement metrics, he would find himself pausing every few minutes
to look toward the office door — a behavior his colleagues had begun to notice and that had made him
feel, in his own words, "like a man pretending to be fine at a job that requires him to be fine."

His wife had gently and then less gently raised her concern. It was her observation — that he seemed
to be disappearing from their evenings together, present in body but somewhere far away — that
finally moved him to make the appointment. What Kwame said he feared most was that this was simply
who he was now. What he hoped, though he offered this quietly, was that someone might be able to
tell him otherwise.
