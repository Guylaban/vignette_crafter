Thanh is a 46-year-old Vietnamese-American firefighter, divorced, who presented for psychological
assessment following a referral from his department's employee assistance program. He served for two
years as a volunteer relief worker attached to an international humanitarian organization operating
in an active conflict zone in the Middle East, returning to the United States approximately three
years ago. His PCL-5 score of 44 places him in the moderate-to-severe range, and the picture that
emerged across the intake and first two sessions was one of a man whose interior life has been
reorganizing itself, quietly and catastrophically, around what he witnessed and failed to prevent.

During his deployment, Thanh was stationed at a field clinic when a mortar strike hit a civilian
shelter adjacent to the compound. He described running toward the structure and finding survivors,
including women and children who had been subjected to sexual violence before the attack. The
details he encountered in those hours — the particular silence that had preceded the blast, the
sounds of the explosion itself, the things people said about what had been done to the women —
lodged in him in ways he did not immediately recognize as injury. He worked for eleven more months
after that day before being rotated home.

The memories do not come to him as a coherent story. Thanh has said, more than once, that he knows
what happened but cannot find the words to explain it — that when he tries to speak, the account
breaks apart in his hands. The sequence is wrong; he cannot reliably place events before or after
one another. What does arrive with terrible fidelity is the physical dimension: a pressure across
his chest and a burning in his forearms that he recognizes, in retrospect, as the sensation of
pulling debris away from bodies. When a trigger breaks through — a car backfiring in the station
yard, a colleague making a careless comment about sexual violence during a news discussion, or a
moment when someone behaves in a way that strikes him as morally contemptible — the memory surges
forward not as recollection but as reinstatement, and with it comes the physical pain. These
triggers do not merely recall the past; they activate the full appraisal structure he has built
around it, and he finds himself flooded immediately with shame and the conviction that he acted in
ways that go against who he is. He had made decisions that day — which survivors to reach first,
what to prioritize — and he has come to believe those decisions were a form of moral failure that
defines him permanently.

That belief — that he is a man whose values collapsed under pressure — has become the organizing
principle of his current distress. He is certain that his life has been destroyed by what happened,
and he experiences a pervasive sense of belonging nowhere: not among his firefighter colleagues, who
he feels cannot understand, and not among his family, from whom he has grown distant since his
divorce. These appraisals do not remain static. They feed back into his perceptual world, so that
ordinary stimuli become charged with menace — an unusual quiet in the firehouse, the kind of silence
that preceded the blast, now reliably produces a sense that something catastrophic is imminent. The
shame and the sense of permanent damage amplify his irritability, and he has had two formal
complaints filed against him for aggressive outbursts at the station, which has only deepened his
conviction that he is fundamentally altered.

To manage the threat he feels almost constantly, Thanh has developed a set of private rituals —
sequences of counting and mental repetition that he performs when he senses danger, believing they
may prevent another catastrophe. He also works hard to suppress any conscious engagement with the
traumatic material, pushing the memories down whenever they surface. But both strategies have
compounded his difficulty: the suppression keeps the memories fragmented and wordless, preventing
any coherent processing, and the effort of constant vigilance and ritual sustains the physiological
arousal that makes the threat feel perpetually real. On nights when the rituals fail and the
memories break through fully, he has hurt himself physically — pressing his forearms against the
edge of his locker, he said, because the sharp sensation is the only thing that interrupts the loop.

What brought him to seek help was not the outbursts or the self-harm, which he had concealed
successfully, but a moment three weeks ago when he froze completely during a residential fire call —
stood outside the building in full gear and could not move. He is afraid, he said quietly at the end
of the second session, that he has become someone who cannot do the one thing he knows how to do.
What he hopes for, though he struggles to believe it is possible, is to find some way back to
himself.
