Tama Faleolo is a 63-year-old Samoan-American man who works as a publisher in a mid-sized firm in
Portland, Oregon. He has been married for thirty-one years and, until recently, was regarded by
colleagues and family alike as steady, dependable, and warm. He presented for psychological
assessment following what he described, with visible reluctance, as "months of not being able to
function properly." His PCL-5 score of 79 places him in the severe range, and the clinical picture
that emerged across intake and early sessions reflects a presentation firmly consistent with PTSD.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Tama was among a group of publishing industry professionals
attending a waterfront conference in Seattle when an industrial explosion occurred at a nearby
chemical storage facility. The blast sent a pressure wave through the conference hall, shattering
glass and collapsing part of a mezzanine structure. In the chaos that followed, Tama found himself
pinned briefly beneath debris, surrounded by smoke, screaming, and the sharp, acrid smell of
accelerant — a smell he would later identify as resembling industrial alcohol. He watched a
colleague lose consciousness metres away and was uncertain, in those minutes, whether the man was
dead. Tama eventually freed himself and assisted in evacuating others before emergency services
arrived. He was physically uninjured, which he has since used as a reason to minimise what happened
to him.

In the weeks that followed the explosion, Tama noticed that his memories of the event had a quality
unlike ordinary recollection. They were not summoned — they arrived. Vivid, pictorial, and
uninvited, images of the collapsed mezzanine and the smoke-filled hall would force their way into
his mind without warning, particularly when he was talking about the event with his wife or, on one
occasion, a concerned colleague who asked directly what had happened. These intrusive visual
memories began to feel dangerous to him in themselves, as though engaging with them would make
something worse. That conviction — that the memories were a source of ongoing threat — drove him
toward increasingly effortful avoidance: he stopped discussing the event entirely, declined social
invitations where the topic might arise, and began withdrawing from the friends he had seen
regularly for years. What had been a rich social life contracted sharply over the following months,
and several friendships have quietly lapsed.

The sense of threat that took hold after the explosion did not remain confined to the memories. Tama
came to feel, with a certainty that surprised and frightened him, that danger was present and
ambient — even inside his own home. He began startling violently at ordinary sounds: a door closing,
a truck passing outside his window, his wife setting down a pan in the kitchen. That persistent
state of alarm fed directly into his behaviour at work. As a publisher, Tama attends editorial
meetings, author events, and industry lunches regularly, and he has found himself unable to sit
anywhere in a room unless his back is against a wall and the full space is visible to him. He scans
every room he enters — the conference room at his office, the restaurant where his team holds
quarterly lunches — before he can settle enough to concentrate. His assistant has noticed the habit,
though nothing has been said directly.

The maintaining cycle that has come to define Tama's daily experience is one he cannot yet see
clearly from the inside. His constant environmental scanning and positional vigilance keep his
nervous system primed, which means that ordinary stimuli — a sudden sound, the smell of cleaning
solvent that faintly recalls the accelerant from that day — land with the force of alarms and pull
the visual memories forward again. The memories, once activated, confirm to him that the threat is
real and unresolved, which tightens his surveillance further and deepens his avoidance of anything
that might disturb the fragile equilibrium he has constructed. When he wakes from nightmares, the
residue of those images sits with him through the morning, making the workday feel like a sustained
exercise in containment.

What finally brought Tama to seek help was a conversation with his wife, who told him quietly one
evening that she felt she had lost him. He is most afraid that she is right — and most hoping,
though he struggles to say it plainly, that some version of the man he was before that afternoon in
Seattle might still be recoverable.
