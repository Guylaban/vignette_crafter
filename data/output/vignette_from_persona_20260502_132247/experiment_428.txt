Aroha is a nineteen-year-old Māori woman who works as a fisherman on the Pacific coast, rising
before dawn most mornings to crew a small commercial vessel with a handful of older colleagues. She
came to the clinic after her partner urged her to seek help, having watched her grow increasingly
distant and volatile over the preceding eight months. Her PCL-5 score of 49 places her firmly within
the clinical range for PTSD, and the picture that emerged across the intake and first two sessions
was one of a young woman caught inside a tightening loop she could neither name nor escape.

The event that precipitated her presentation occurred on an evening last winter when Aroha was
cycling home from the harbour along a coastal path. She heard footsteps behind her — rapid, closing
— and turned to see a man attacking a woman she recognised from the waterfront. The assault was
brief but savage. Aroha froze. She did not call out, did not intervene, did not even move until it
was over. By the time she found her voice, the man had gone. She had come to believe, with a
certainty that felt like a verdict, that she should have protected that woman and had failed her
entirely. That belief settled into her like ballast.

In the months that followed, the memory did not behave like an ordinary recollection. It forced
itself into Aroha's mind without invitation — on the boat, between hauls, while she was gutting fish
— arriving not always as an image but sometimes as a wave of guilt so complete that it had no
picture attached to it, only weight. When the memory surfaced, the guilt was immediate and crushing,
and it fed directly back into her conviction that she was someone who had failed when it mattered.
That appraisal of herself as a person who could not be trusted to act — or, worse, who might act
badly — began to extend in a new direction: she became frightened that she would lose control of her
anger, that the rage she sometimes felt would break out of her and cause harm. The memory, by
reinstating her sense of failure, kept that fear alive and sharpened it.

Her body had become its own source of alarm. Before she could consciously register a stimulus, her
heart would be pounding, her muscles braced — and this physical reactivity, which she experienced as
her body betraying her, reinforced her belief that something was fundamentally wrong with her. She
had difficulty concentrating during even routine tasks on the vessel, and when a piece of equipment
was slow to respond or a colleague took longer than expected to return from below deck, she assumed
something terrible was unfolding. This hypervigilant monitoring of the environment kept her scanning
for threat, and that scanning made it more likely that ordinary stimuli would register as dangerous
— particularly the sound of footsteps behind her and, above all, the sight of a bicycle, which she
now found almost unbearable. These triggers, when encountered, pulled the memory back with them, and
the memory returned the guilt, and the guilt returned the self-appraisal, and the appraisal returned
the fear — a circuit that completed itself rapidly and without her consent.

To manage it, Aroha had built a structure of avoidance that looked, from the outside, like
dedication. She volunteered for every extra shift, arriving at the harbour before anyone else and
staying late, because the physical exhaustion of work kept her thoughts from settling. She had
stopped being intimate with her partner. She spent hours each evening on her phone, cycling through
videos and games, not because she enjoyed them but because they held her attention just enough to
prevent the memory from surfacing. What she had not anticipated was that this constant motion — the
overwork, the screen time, the relentless occupation of her mind — was also preventing her from
processing anything, so that each morning she returned to the same raw state. The maladaptive
strategies that were meant to suppress the memory were, in practice, keeping her sensitised to every
trigger, because she never reached a moment of genuine rest in which her nervous system could
recalibrate. Her partner had noticed her flinch at footsteps in their hallway. She had not cycled
since the night of the assault.

What finally brought her in was a morning on the boat when she snapped at a colleague over something
trivial and saw fear cross his face. She had come to believe she was capable of something terrible,
and in that moment it felt confirmed. What she most hopes for, she said quietly, is to stop feeling
like a danger to the people around her. What she most fears is that the clinician will agree.
