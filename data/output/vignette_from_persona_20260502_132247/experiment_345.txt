Moana Tuilagi is a 43-year-old Tongan-American physical therapist currently living in the Pacific
Northwest with her long-term partner. She presented for assessment following a marked deterioration
in her functioning over the past fourteen months, reporting a PCL-5 score of 77 and describing
herself, quietly and with evident difficulty, as someone she no longer recognises.

Fourteen months ago, Moana was driving home after a colleague's farewell gathering. She had not
considered herself impaired — she had stopped drinking two hours before leaving, had eaten, had felt
steady on her feet. What she had not accounted for was the way the evening's drinks had accumulated
quietly in her system. At a residential intersection she knew well, she failed to brake in time and
struck a teenage boy who had stepped from between parked cars. He survived, but sustained a
traumatic spinal injury. Moana was the first person to reach him. She knelt on the asphalt, her
hands on his shoulders, and remembers with terrible clarity the smell of alcohol — her own breath,
she later realised — mingling with the cold air above his still body. She has never fully forgiven
herself for what she noticed in that moment.

In the weeks that followed, the smell of alcohol became the most reliable and devastating of her
triggers. Walking past a bar on her lunch break, catching the scent on a patient's breath, or even
opening a bottle of wine she no longer drinks — each instance returns her with shocking immediacy to
that intersection. When triggered this way, Moana does not simply remember; she relives. The
memories that surface carry a hyper-vivid, present-tense quality that ordinary recollections do not
— more real than real, she has said — and yet she cannot construct a coherent narrative of what
happened. The sequence breaks apart: she has fragments, sensory shards, but no story she can tell
even to herself. When these images arrive, the shame that accompanies them is immediate and
crushing, a physical weight she describes as landing in her chest before she has time to think. Any
conversation that approaches the event, or any news item or clinical scenario that touches on
trauma-related injury, can produce the same cascade: the vivid, unordered images, the shame, the
sense of the scene beginning to happen again.

This pervasive sense of threat has become a feature of Moana's daily life. In her work as a physical
therapist, she moves through clinical spaces filled with equipment — resistance bands, weight
machines, parallel bars — and she has found herself cataloguing each as a potential instrument of
harm, scanning rooms before sessions begin. The flashbacks themselves have come to feel like
evidence of ongoing danger, as though her nervous system is insisting the event is still unfolding.
When the threat state rises — whether triggered by a smell, a sound, or a patient describing an
accident — Moana positions herself near exits in treatment rooms and waiting areas, a habit her
colleagues have begun to notice without understanding.

What has tightened the cycle most severely are her appraisals of herself and the event. Moana has
come to believe, with a certainty she experiences as factual, that she will never forgive herself
for not doing more in those first moments — that she should have acted differently, more quickly,
more competently. She is equally certain that thinking directly about what happened will cause her
to lose control entirely, a conviction that has made any natural processing impossible. These
beliefs do not stay contained: they return her attention to the event, reactivating the triggers and
the unordered memories, which in turn intensify the shame and confirm her sense of herself as
irreparably altered. The person she sees in the mirror, she has said, is a stranger — a woman who
hurt a child and then could not hold herself together.

To manage the surges of threat and shame, Moana has turned to strategies that have themselves become
sources of distress. She has accumulated significant credit card debt through impulsive online
purchases made in the early hours when sleep fails her. She has begun using cannabis nightly,
initially to sleep, now to blunt the evenings generally. Both have introduced new self-recrimination
— a woman who prided herself on discipline and clinical precision now sees her own coping as further
proof of deterioration, which sharpens the shame and lowers the threshold at which the next trigger
finds her.

What finally brought Moana to seek help was a session three weeks ago in which a patient described a
road accident. She excused herself, sat in a supply closet for twenty minutes, and then returned and
finished the appointment. That night she told her partner, for the first time, that something was
seriously wrong. She is most afraid that the clinician will confirm what she already suspects — that
she is, at her core, unchanged and unforgivable. What she is hoping for, tentatively and almost
inaudibly, is that she might one day be able to do her job without fearing herself.
