Caleb is a 30-year-old carpenter from rural Ohio, married for four years, who presented for intake
following a significant deterioration in his daily functioning over the past eighteen months. He
scored 61 on the PCL-5, placing him firmly in the severe range, and described himself during the
first session as a man who no longer recognises himself.

The event that precipitated his current difficulties occurred on a job site approximately two years
ago. Caleb and a small crew were framing a residential addition when a coworker — a man he had known
for years — fell from scaffolding and landed badly on exposed rebar. Caleb was the first to reach
him. He remembers the specific warmth and wetness of his colleague's blood on his hands as he tried
to apply pressure to the wound, the particular metallic taste in the air that he later realised had
entered his mouth when he shouted for someone to call 911, and the sight of a deep laceration across
the man's abdomen that he could not look away from and cannot stop seeing. His colleague survived,
but Caleb has never been able to describe the event in any coherent sequence. When asked to recount
what happened, his speech fragments, he loses the thread, and he eventually shakes his head — the
experience sits in him not as a story but as a cluster of sensations that resist language.

The triggers that now govern Caleb's days map precisely onto those moments of contact. Physical
touch — a handshake, his wife resting her hand on his arm, even the grain of rough lumber against
his palm — can instantly collapse the distance between the present and the site, flooding him with
the full sensory weight of that afternoon: the heat, the smell, the sound of his own voice. A
particular taste, something metallic or iron-edged that occasionally appears in food or drink, does
the same, arriving without announcement and pulling him entirely out of the room he is standing in.
And visible injuries on others — a cut on a coworker's hand, a bruise on his wife's leg — are almost
unbearable; when these appear at work, Caleb has found himself freezing mid-task, unable to
continue, the memory returning not as recollection but as reliving, completely real across every
sense at once.

When these intrusions hit, Caleb has developed a set of responses that he experiences as necessary,
though he is dimly aware they are not helping. He eats — sometimes large amounts of whatever is
available — not from hunger but because the sensation of fullness seems to interrupt the intrusion,
grounding him in his body in a way that momentarily dulls the reactivation. He has also learned to
blank out deliberately, a kind of willed dissociation he describes as "going somewhere else," which
he reaches for especially when the reliving is most intense. Both strategies pull him briefly back
from the edge, but in the hours that follow he is left feeling disgusted with himself and more
convinced than ever that he is broken. The overeating and the deliberate detachment have become, in
his mind, evidence of a man who cannot manage himself — and that evidence feeds directly into the
belief, now deeply held, that he has been permanently changed for the worse, that the trauma has
destroyed the life he was building, and that he cannot rely on himself to be the husband or worker
he once was.

These appraisals have begun to function as triggers in their own right. When Caleb thinks of himself
as permanently damaged, the thought seems to summon the memory — as though the belief and the event
are stored together, each one unlocking the other. And the memory, when it returns, confirms the
belief so completely that he has stopped trying to argue with it. He has also, over months, narrowed
his physical world: he avoids touching anything he does not have to touch, eats alone when possible
to control what flavours he encounters, and turns away when anyone on site is injured. At work, this
last habit has become conspicuous — his foreman has noticed that Caleb leaves the area whenever
there is even a minor accident, and a conversation about his reliability has already happened once.

What finally brought Caleb in was his wife telling him, quietly and without anger, that she missed
being able to touch him. He is most afraid that she is right — that the man she married is simply
gone.
