Esperanza is a 44-year-old Latin American woman, divorced before she entered religious life, who has
served as a nun in a mid-sized American city for the past eleven years. She presented for
psychological consultation following a terrorist attack that occurred eight months ago at a public
memorial service she attended as part of her congregation's community outreach ministry. During the
attack, a device detonated near the outer edge of the crowd. Esperanza was close enough to be thrown
to the ground by the blast. When she raised her head, she saw blood — on the pavement, on the
clothing of the man beside her, on her own hands — and heard screaming that she has described as a
sound she cannot locate in any category she previously understood. She felt a sharp, burning pain
along her left side where debris had struck her. She lay still for what felt like a very long time,
uncertain whether a second device would detonate, watching the crowd surge and scatter around her.

In the weeks that followed, Esperanza found that the memory of the attack did not behave as memories
are supposed to. She could not summon it deliberately — when she tried to piece together the
sequence of events in order to pray over them or make some spiritual sense of them, the account
would dissolve, leaving her with fragments she could not arrange. Yet the memory arrived without
invitation, forcing itself into her awareness with a completeness that stunned her: all at once she
could smell the smoke, feel the impact of the ground, hear the screaming, and see the blood as
though she were still lying on that pavement. These intrusions came most reliably through specific
gateways. If she woke from a nightmare — which happened several times a week — the transition into
waking offered no relief; the residue of the dream deposited her directly back into the sensory
fullness of the attack. Physical pain, even minor discomfort, carried the same charge: a headache, a
bruised shin from a doorframe, the ache of her left side on cold mornings — any of these could pull
the full memory back into the present tense. And the sight of blood, encountered in even the most
mundane context, functioned as an almost instantaneous portal.

When the memory arrived in this way, it brought with it a quality of threat that Esperanza found
difficult to articulate but impossible to dismiss. She had come to believe, at a level beneath
conscious reasoning, that she was not safe — that the world contained violence that could reach her
anywhere, and that her own body was an unreliable early-warning system. Strong feelings of fear and
shame rose with the memory, and these emotions themselves began to feel dangerous, as though
experiencing them too fully might mean losing control entirely. She started to identify escape
routes before settling into any space — the chapel, the dining hall, the parish offices where she
worked — and found that she could only feel genuinely safe in her own small cell and, on good days,
the convent garden. Crowded places had become essentially impossible; she stopped accompanying her
congregation to the community events that had defined her ministry, telling herself this was
temporary.

The threat she carried did not stay quiet between intrusions. Because she remained in a state of
vigilance, ordinary stimuli — a sudden noise, the press of bodies in a corridor — could reactivate
the alarm, which in turn dragged the memory back toward the surface, which deepened her certainty
that danger was everywhere. The loop tightened steadily. Her strategies for managing this —
withdrawing from emotional closeness with her fellow sisters, avoiding any gathering larger than a
small prayer group, going over and over the details of the event in private in an attempt to impose
meaning on them — offered no relief and, she was beginning to suspect, made things worse. The
overanalysing kept the event alive without resolving it. The avoidance confirmed, each time, that
the world outside her safe zones was too dangerous to enter. The emotional distance she maintained
from her community left her feeling unwitnessed and more alone in her fear, which the fear itself
then fed upon.

What finally brought Esperanza to seek help was a morning in which she could not enter the chapel
for morning prayers. She stood at the door, identified the exits, felt her heart hammering, and
turned back. She is most afraid that she has been permanently changed — that the woman who walked
into that memorial service no longer exists. What she is hoping for, she said quietly, is to be able
to stand in a room full of people again and feel that God is there too.
