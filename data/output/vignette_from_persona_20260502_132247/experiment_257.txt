Adaeze is a 68-year-old Nigerian-American woman, currently unemployed and living with her husband of
many years in a modest home in the outer suburbs of Atlanta. She was referred for psychological
assessment following a visit to her primary care physician, who noted elevated distress and sleep
difficulties that had persisted for over two years. Adaeze presented as composed but guarded in her
first session, choosing her words carefully, as though she had spent a long time practising how much
to reveal.

The abuse began, by her account, not with a single catastrophic event but with a pattern that
culminated one evening in the back garden of the couple's previous home. Her then-partner — a man
she had been with for nearly a decade before her current marriage — had dragged her outside during
an argument, thrown her to the ground, and knelt over her in the dark, his hands at her throat, the
cold soil pressing into her back and the smell of wet earth filling her nostrils as she struggled to
breathe. She had believed, in those moments, that she was going to die. She did not. But something
in her understanding of safety — of where danger could find her — was permanently altered that
night.

In the months and years that followed, certain moments would return her, without warning, to that
garden. The smell of soil after rain, encountered now whenever she walks past the flower beds her
husband tends on weekends, can instantly collapse the distance between then and now, flooding her
with the full sensory weight of that night. Sleeping alone — when her husband travels to visit his
family — or waking in an unfamiliar bed during the rare occasions she visits relatives, carries a
particular dread; the vulnerability of lying unguarded in the dark is enough to bring the memory
pressing back into consciousness, uninvited and insistent. And when she feels emotionally exposed —
during an argument, or when someone raises their voice — the memory surges forward before she can
stop it, though she has become skilled at pushing it away before it resolves into something
complete. She cannot hold it long enough to look at it directly.

What the memory brings with it, beyond its raw sensory content, is a conviction that nowhere is
truly safe. Adaeze has come to feel that she can only let her guard down in a very small number of
places — chiefly one corner of her living room, in a chair that faces the door. Even within her own
home she remains alert, listening for sounds that do not belong, scanning the room when she enters
it. At night, she lies awake because some part of her believes that falling asleep means
surrendering the vigilance that keeps her alive. This persistent sense of threat, in turn, makes her
hyperaware of the same cues that first triggered the memory — the smell of earth, the onset of
darkness, the particular quality of being alone — so that ordinary domestic life becomes a landscape
of potential alarms.

To manage this, Adaeze has built a life organised around keeping still what might otherwise move.
She keeps herself busy from early morning — cleaning, cooking, running errands — because an occupied
mind is one that cannot be ambushed by memory. She keeps the television on. She fills silences. And
in her relationships, she has become someone who goes along, who does not push back, who agrees and
accommodates, because conflict — even mild disagreement — carries the texture of danger, and
appeasement feels like the only reliable way to stay safe. This people-pleasing, which her husband
mistakes for contentment, is itself a kind of trigger management: a strategy built from the memory's
lesson that resistance is what makes things worse. But the busyness and the compliance are imperfect
shields. When the soil smell reaches her on the breeze, or when she finds herself alone at night,
the strategies collapse and the memory returns, and the threat feels immediate again — which drives
her back into the same narrow routines, the same careful vigilance, the same practiced
agreeableness.

What finally brought Adaeze to seek help was a night last autumn when her husband was away and she
sat in her chair until dawn, unable to move, certain — though she could not say why — that something
terrible was about to happen. She wants, she said quietly, to be able to sleep.
