Linh Pham is a 37-year-old Vietnamese-American woman, married, who works as a refugee aid worker for
a resettlement organization in a mid-sized American city. She presented for therapy approximately
eighteen months after an incident in which she was caught in a police dispersal of a protest she had
attended in a professional capacity, documenting conditions for a report on civil liberties.
Officers on bicycles moved into the crowd without warning, and Linh was knocked to the ground,
struck with a baton, and held face-down on the pavement while people around her screamed and fled.
She was detained for several hours before being released without charge. No officer was disciplined.
No formal acknowledgment was ever made.

In the weeks that followed, Linh found that the memory did not behave like other memories. It did
not recede. When it returned — unbidden, or pulled back by something she saw or heard — it brought
with it the same terror she had felt on the pavement, her cheek against the asphalt, certain she was
about to be seriously hurt. The grief that accompanied these returns was equally disabling: a
flooding sorrow she described as mourning something she could not name. The emotional weight was so
total that she would have to stop whatever she was doing and sit, sometimes for twenty minutes,
until she could breathe normally again. These intrusions began shaping her appraisals of what had
happened, and what it meant. She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt irrefutable, that
what was done to her was profoundly unjust — and that the silence that followed compounded that
injustice into something she could not metabolize. Alongside this ran a more corrosive conviction:
that someone more competent, more careful, less naive, would never have been in that situation at
all. This self-comparison did not feel like self-criticism so much as a verdict. It fed directly
back into the memory, colouring each recall with shame as well as terror, and making the intrusions
feel not only frightening but confirming.

These appraisals also sensitized her to the world around her. Linh's work takes her regularly into
crowded community spaces — resettlement offices, transit hubs, intake centres — and it was in these
settings that the triggers became impossible to ignore. Crowded spaces now produced a visceral
alertness she could not suppress. People cycling past her on the street — a detail burned into the
event itself, the officers on bikes cutting through the crowd — would stop her mid-step. And the
presence of any uniformed authority, police in particular, activated something close to paralysis.
Each of these encounters pulled the memory forward with an immediacy that collapsed the distance
between then and now, so that standing in a resettlement waiting room felt, for a moment,
indistinguishable from lying on that pavement. The triggers did not merely remind her; they
reinstated.

To manage this, Linh developed strategies that made sense to her at the time. She began suppressing
the memory actively — pushing it down whenever it surfaced, telling herself she could not afford to
fall apart. She avoided anything that might produce hope or happiness, having learned that emotional
openness in any direction left her vulnerable to the grief breaking through. More troublingly, she
had begun gambling — online, late at night, after her husband was asleep — finding in the narrow,
rule-bound tension of card games a way to feel something controllable, something that was not grief
or rage. These strategies, however, were not neutral. The suppression kept the memory unprocessed
and close to the surface, ensuring it returned with undiminished force. Avoiding positive experience
confirmed her growing sense that she was no longer herself — that the person she had been before,
the one who had believed in the work and in her own competence, was gone. The gambling, which she
recognized as reckless, reinforced the appraisal that she was someone who had lost her judgment. The
strategies were maintaining the very threat state they were meant to quiet: she remained irritable,
prone to sudden anger with colleagues, and chronically unable to sleep, her body still monitoring
for a danger that the suppression had never allowed her to fully process.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a moment at work. A police officer arrived at her office
to assist with a documentation issue — routine, benign — and Linh's response was so extreme, her
hands shaking, her voice failing, that a colleague noticed and asked if she was all right. She had
not been able to answer. What she most feared, she said in her first session, was that she had
become unrecognizable to herself. What she most hoped was that she had not.
