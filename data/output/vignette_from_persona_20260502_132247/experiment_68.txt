Layla Mansouri is a fifty-one-year-old Iranian-American woman who has worked in hospitality
management for nearly two decades, currently serving as the general manager of a mid-sized hotel in
the Chicago metropolitan area. She presented for evaluation approximately eight months after a toxic
chemical exposure incident that occurred during a routine inspection of the hotel's basement
mechanical room. A maintenance contractor had inadvertently mixed incompatible cleaning solvents in
an enclosed space, and Layla, who had been conducting a walk-through with a junior staff member,
entered the room before the fumes had been detected. She recalls the smell hitting her before she
understood what it was — sharp, synthetic, and wrong in a way she could not immediately name — and
then the burning in her throat, the sudden inability to draw a full breath, and the panic of
realizing she could not get her employee out fast enough. Both were hospitalized overnight for
observation. Layla was discharged with no lasting pulmonary damage, but the event marked a before
and after in her experience of herself and her workplace.

In the weeks that followed, Layla noticed that her body had developed what felt like its own memory
of that morning. The hotel environment, which had once been entirely familiar, began to work against
her. The smell of cleaning products used by housekeeping staff — bleach, floor polish, the faint
antiseptic quality of the linen service — would stop her mid-stride in a corridor, her chest
tightening and her heart rate climbing before her mind had consciously registered the association.
The same response could be triggered by the antiseptic smell of the hospital where her mother had a
routine appointment, or by the cleaning cart outside a conference room. These sensory encounters
reliably activated a state of heightened physical alarm — her pulse would spike, her hands would
tremble, and she would feel the constriction in her throat that had first appeared in that basement
— and once her body was in that state, she found herself scanning for further danger, certain that
something else was about to go wrong.

That vigilance, once activated by a trigger, had a way of sustaining itself long past the initial
moment. At night, Layla found she could not allow herself to fully relax into sleep. She had come to
believe, in a way she could not entirely articulate but felt as absolute conviction, that remaining
alert was the only thing standing between her and another catastrophic failure to notice danger in
time. She would lie awake listening to the sounds of the hotel — a door closing on another floor,
the elevator mechanism, the distant clatter of the kitchen — and each unexpected sound would jolt
her into full wakefulness, her body flooding with the same physical reactivity that the smells
produced during the day. The state of threat she carried into each night made the environment feel
alive with potential harm, which in turn made the ordinary sensory landscape of the hotel — her
workplace, the place she spent ten or more hours a day — into a continuous source of re-triggering.

The occupational consequences had become impossible to ignore. Layla had begun delegating basement
and mechanical room inspections entirely, and had restructured her morning walk-throughs to avoid
the housekeeping staging areas where chemical smells were concentrated. Her staff had noticed her
uncharacteristic edginess, particularly her sharp reactions to unexpected sounds — a dropped tray, a
door slamming during a busy check-in period would produce a visible startle that she struggled to
conceal. She had not told anyone what had happened in any meaningful way. The stress of managing the
hotel, which had previously been something she absorbed with practiced calm, now functioned as its
own trigger: under pressure, her body moved quickly into the same alarm state that the smells and
sounds produced, and that alarm state in turn made the environment feel more threatening, more
saturated with the possibility of harm she had failed to anticipate. The loop had tightened over
months without her fully naming it.

What finally brought Layla to seek help was a conversation with her husband, who told her quietly
one evening that he was frightened by how unreachable she had become. She had not, until that
moment, understood how much ground she had lost. What she most feared was that this was now simply
who she was. What she most hoped was that it wasn't.
