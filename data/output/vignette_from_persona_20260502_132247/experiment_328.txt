Tariq Mansouri is a seventy-five-year-old widowed sculptor of Iranian descent who was referred for
psychological assessment following a life-threatening accident that occurred fourteen months ago. He
presents with a PCL-5 score of 75, indicating severe posttraumatic stress, and has been reluctant to
engage with mental health services until now, having spent the intervening period attempting to
manage his distress through his own means.

The accident occurred on an unremarkable Tuesday afternoon when Tariq was working alone in his
studio, a converted warehouse on the outskirts of the city where he has produced large-scale stone
and metal works for nearly four decades. A hydraulic lifting arm used to position heavy stone slabs
failed without warning, and a piece of marble weighing several hundred pounds fell, striking him
across the lower back and pinning him partially beneath it. He lay on the cold concrete floor for
nearly two hours before a delivery driver found him. During that time, he was conscious, unable to
move, and convinced — with a certainty he describes as absolute — that he was dying. He remembers
the particular quality of the light through the high studio windows, the smell of stone dust, and
the sensation of his own heartbeat as something frightening rather than reassuring, as though each
beat might simply stop.

He survived with significant spinal injuries requiring surgery and a prolonged rehabilitation.
Physically, his recovery has been partial. He walks with a cane, experiences chronic pain, and has
been told by his orthopedic surgeon that certain limitations are permanent. It is this word —
permanent — that has lodged itself at the center of Tariq's psychological world. He has come to
believe, with a thoroughness that shapes nearly every hour of his day, that his body has been
fundamentally and irreversibly damaged. He does not experience this as an adjustment to new
circumstances but as a verdict: that the man who entered that studio on that Tuesday no longer
exists, replaced by a diminished and untrustworthy version. He is certain he can no longer rely on
his body to do what he asks of it, and the memory of the hydraulic arm — of a mechanism he trusted
completely and that failed completely — has become a template through which he now reads every
physical sensation.

It is precisely these appraisals of permanent damage and bodily unreliability that have driven Tariq
toward a set of strategies that now govern his daily life. Because he believes his body cannot be
trusted, he monitors it constantly — cataloguing twinges, tracking his breathing after mild
exertion, pausing mid-movement to assess whether a sensation in his lower back represents the
ordinary ache of his injury or the onset of something catastrophic. He spends considerable time each
day in this internal surveillance, and the studio, once a place of physical absorption and creative
freedom, has become a site of careful self-management. He no longer lifts anything heavier than a
few kilograms, has stopped working with stone entirely, and declines commissions that would require
him to stand for extended periods. What had been a practice built on the physical encounter between
his body and resistant materials has contracted to small clay studies he works on seated. Several
long-standing professional relationships have quietly dissolved as he has turned down collaborative
projects, and a gallery that had represented him for twenty years has not been in contact since he
declined their last exhibition invitation.

Alongside this physical withdrawal, Tariq returns repeatedly to the question of why this happened to
him. He thinks about it in the evenings, in the specific silence of a house he has lived in alone
since his wife died six years ago. He is not a religious man in any formal sense, but he searches
for a meaning that would make the accident intelligible — some failure of attention, some
accumulated carelessness — because the alternative, that it was simply random, feels to him more
frightening than culpability would.

What finally brought Tariq to seek help was a conversation with his eldest daughter, who visited in
the spring and told him, carefully, that she no longer recognized how he spent his days. He is not
sure what he hopes for. What he fears most is being told that this is simply what seventy-five feels
like after an injury — that there is no distinction left between damage and age, between the
accident and the man.
