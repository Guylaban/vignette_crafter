Adaeze is a 33-year-old African American woman who has worked as a park ranger for seven years, a
role she once described as the truest expression of who she was — someone capable, grounded, and at
home in the natural world. She is married and, until recently, was regarded by her supervisors as
one of the most dependable rangers in her district. She presents now with a PCL-5 score of 46,
seeking help approximately eight months after the sudden death of her younger brother, Emeka, who
collapsed from an undetected cardiac arrhythmia during a family gathering. Adaeze was the first to
reach him. She performed CPR for nearly twelve minutes while waiting for the ambulance, watching his
face — so familiar, so still — and understanding, somewhere in the middle of those minutes, that she
was losing him. He was twenty-nine.

In the weeks that followed, Adaeze found that the memory of that afternoon did not behave like a
memory. It arrived in fragments without invitation — a sudden close-up of his lips, the texture of
the grass beneath her knees, the sound her own voice made when she called his name — and then
vanished before she could orient herself. At other moments the images were almost clinical in their
precision, arriving with the flat, terrible clarity of photographs: Emeka's face at a particular
angle, the exact position of his hands. When these fragments surfaced, they brought with them a
grief so overwhelming that she could not function for minutes at a time, sometimes longer. She began
to dread any moment of quiet, because stillness seemed to invite the images in. She stopped allowing
herself to rest, stopped sitting without her phone or the radio on, filling every pause with noise
or motion. What had begun as a response to the images gradually became a rule she enforced on
herself: she must never be unoccupied.

This compulsion to stay mentally distracted had a direct cost to her work. The long stretches of
solitary patrol that she had once treasured — walking fire roads through pine forest, monitoring
trail conditions — became intolerable. She began delegating tasks she would normally have handled
herself, calling in sick on days when she was assigned solo backcountry work, and quietly rerouting
her patrol routes to stay near the visitor centre. Her supervisors noticed. A formal review was
initiated two months ago, and Adaeze has not told her husband the full extent of what is happening
at work.

Underlying all of this is what Adaeze has come to believe about herself since Emeka died. She is
certain that she has been permanently altered — that whatever competence and steadiness once defined
her has been destroyed, not temporarily disrupted. She feels, as she put it in the intake session,
"like something that used to be a person." This conviction that she is no longer capable, that she
is damaged beyond recovery, has made her less willing to attempt the very tasks that might challenge
it. When she considers returning to solo patrol, or sitting quietly at home without distraction, she
interprets the anticipated distress not as manageable difficulty but as confirmation of her
permanent diminishment — and so she avoids. The avoidance, in turn, prevents her from processing
what happened, and the memory remains raw and unintegrated, erupting without warning when her
defences slip. Her body remains in a state of chronic readiness — tense, sleepless, scanning — and
this physical alertness further convinces her that danger is still present, reinforcing her need to
keep moving, keep busy, never stop.

The loop has tightened steadily. The more she avoids relaxation and drops her responsibilities, the
less she encounters the memory in any controlled or tolerable way, and the more potent and intrusive
it remains when it does arrive. Her sense of herself as permanently broken deepens with each
retreat. She has begun to feel emotionally numb in the evenings, present in her marriage only in the
most mechanical sense.

What finally brought her to seek help was her husband asking, quietly, whether she still wanted to
be married to him. She does. She is most afraid that the answer she would give honestly — that she
no longer knows who she is — means that nothing can be repaired.
