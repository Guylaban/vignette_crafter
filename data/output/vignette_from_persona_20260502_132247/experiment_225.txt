Mateo is a 25-year-old Colombian American data scientist, currently single, who presented for an
initial assessment reporting a PCL-5 score of 40. He was referred following a disclosure to his
primary care physician that he had been struggling, in his words, "for longer than I want to admit."
The trauma at the centre of his presentation occurred approximately two years ago, when his younger
sister — then four years old — collapsed suddenly at a family gathering and was rushed to hospital
in septic shock. Mateo rode in the ambulance. He remembers the siren screaming above them, the smell
of his own body drenched in fear-sweat as he held her small hand, and watching her face go slack
while the paramedics worked. She survived, but only just. In the days that followed, Mateo found he
could not assemble what had happened into anything coherent — the sequence of events felt scrambled
and wrong, as though the pieces belonged to several different nights. More than that, the experience
carried a quality so extreme that he sometimes struggled to believe it had been real at all. Yet
whenever the memory surfaced, the terror returned in full, not as recollection but as re-living, his
chest seizing and his hands shaking as if his sister were still dying in front of him.

The memories themselves have become a source of triggers in their own right. Because the recall is
so fragmented and so saturated with original terror, certain sensory details — particularly the wail
of sirens, the smell of sweat or body odour, and the sound of someone crying — have become tightly
bound to the traumatic material. These cues do not announce themselves gently. When Mateo hears an
ambulance pass on his commute, or catches the smell of sweat in a crowded gym, the memory does not
merely surface; it detonates. The physical reaction is immediate and intense — heart hammering,
muscles locking, a wave of nausea — and the sounds and smells that carry these associations have
come to feel genuinely dangerous to him, as if the emergency itself were recurring. He now startles
violently at ordinary sounds in the open-plan office where he works, and colleagues have noticed him
flinch when someone drops a mug or raises their voice across the room. He has not offered an
explanation.

That persistent sense of threat has in turn shaped the way Mateo manages himself day to day. Because
his body behaves as though danger is perpetually imminent, he cannot allow himself to fall asleep
without remaining partially alert — he lies in bed running through scenarios, listening. The
hypervigilance has also hardened into a set of deliberate strategies. He has stopped allowing
himself to feel or express anger, reasoning that any strong emotion might crack open the ones he
cannot afford to feel. He works hard to suppress any thought that edges toward the event. And he has
systematically avoided hospitals, ambulances, and physical examinations — a meaningful constraint
given that his mother has recently been pressing him to accompany her to a specialist appointment.
He declined, and the resulting tension between them has become one of the clearest relational costs
of his avoidance.

What Mateo has not yet understood is that these strategies, though they feel protective, are
sustaining the very system they were meant to quiet. The suppression of thoughts prevents the memory
from being processed or contextualised, leaving it fragmented and unordered, which in turn keeps the
sensory triggers potent and the terror on recall undiminished. The avoidance of medical settings
removes any opportunity to learn that sirens and the smell of sweat need not mean catastrophe, so
those cues remain loaded. And the effort of constant suppression keeps his nervous system primed,
which means the next ordinary sound is that much more likely to send him into a startle, which
surfaces the memory again. The loop has been running quietly for two years.

What finally brought Mateo to seek help was a moment at work: he was reviewing a dataset involving
hospital admission records when he heard a siren outside and began to cry at his desk — something he
had not done since the night itself. He was most afraid, he said, that this would never get better.
What he was hoping for, more quietly, was that someone could help him put the story back in the
right order.
