Priya Krishnamurthy is a 73-year-old widowed South Asian woman who has worked as an interior
designer for nearly four decades, building a quiet reputation for her precise eye and her ability to
transform a room through texture and material. She came to her first session carrying a leather
portfolio she kept pressed tightly against her body, and she spoke carefully, with long pauses, as
though language itself had become unreliable.

Approximately eight months ago, Priya was stopped and detained by police officers while visiting a
client's home in an affluent neighborhood. What began as a routine welfare check called in by a
neighbor escalated rapidly. Officers approached her with hands on their holsters, shouting commands
she described as contradictory. At one point she was pressed against a brick wall, her cheek and
forearms forced against its rough surface while her wrists were held behind her. She remembers the
coarse grain of the brick against her skin, the dark fabric of the officers' uniforms filling her
visual field, the smell of something chemical. Then there is a gap — a silence in her own account
that she cannot bridge. She knows she was eventually released without charge, but the interval
between the wall and standing alone on the pavement is simply missing. What she has instead are
disconnected fragments: a flash of a radio crackling, her own hands shaking against her thighs, a
face she cannot fully reconstruct. She has tried, in the months since, to assemble these pieces into
a coherent story and found she cannot. She knows what happened, she tells her clinician, but when
she reaches for the words to describe the worst of it, they dissolve. The event lives in her body
and in broken images rather than in any narrative she can hold.

In her work as an interior designer, Priya handles fabric swatches, tile samples, and upholstery
materials daily. Since the incident, running her fingers across a rough-textured surface — a raw
linen, a stone mosaic, a coarsely woven rug — can stop her mid-sentence, flooding her with a
physical sensation she cannot immediately name but that her body recognizes before her mind does.
These tactile triggers pull her back not to a memory with a beginning and end, but to a fragment, a
physical impression, the wall. And because her memories remain broken and unresolved, without the
organizing structure of a complete narrative, the fragments are easily reactivated — any sensory
echo finds a gap to rush into. The incompleteness of what she remembers seems to keep the past
perpetually close, as though the unfinished event is still searching for its ending.

What has come to frighten Priya most is not the triggers themselves but what she has concluded they
mean about her. She had come to believe, across these eight months, that her body was no longer her
own — that it reacted to the world without her consent, betraying her in front of clients and
colleagues. She was certain that she could not manage even minor professional frustrations that she
had once absorbed without thought; a delayed delivery, a contractor's error, now felt catastrophic.
She had arrived at a bleak conviction that nothing good could come to her anymore, that the woman
who had spent forty years building something beautiful had been permanently replaced by someone
fragile and diminished. These appraisals did not remain static — they fed back into her experience
of triggers, making her hypervigilant to any situation that might expose her inadequacy, so that she
began scanning her environment for uniforms, for authority, for anything that might confirm her
worst fears about herself. When she glimpsed a police officer through the window of a client's
building last spring, the sight of the dark uniform sent her from the room before she could explain
herself. The appraisal — I cannot cope, I am not safe, nothing will be well again — arrived
simultaneously with the visual cue, each amplifying the other. And in the wake of these moments, her
fragmented memories seemed to press closer, as though her certainty that she was permanently damaged
made the broken pieces of the event more insistent, more present.

Priya sought help after she declined a significant commission because the client's lobby had a stone
feature wall. She told her clinician she was most afraid that this was simply who she was now. What
she hoped, almost too quietly to say aloud, was that someone might help her find words for what
happened — and that finding them might finally let it end.
