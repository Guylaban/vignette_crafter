Miriam is a 35-year-old European American woman who works as a counselor at a community mental
health center. She is married and, until recently, described her life as purposeful and grounded.
She presented for treatment approximately eight months after the event that changed that, reporting
a PCL-5 score of 46 and describing herself, quietly, as someone who no longer recognizes who she is.

The incident occurred on a Saturday afternoon in late autumn. Miriam had been driving home from a
continuing education workshop, tired but not impaired, when she misjudged the speed of a cyclist
crossing an intersection and struck him. The impact was sudden — a sound she has never been able to
fully reconstruct — and what followed was a blur of screaming, her own hands shaking over her phone
as she called 911, and the image of the man lying at an angle that told her, even before the
paramedics arrived, that something was badly broken. He survived, but sustained a severe spinal
injury. Miriam stood at the edge of that intersection for what felt like hours. She has not driven
through it since.

In the months that followed, her memories of the event did not settle into a coherent narrative.
Instead they arrived as disconnected fragments — the sound of the impact, the color of the cyclist's
jacket, the way her own voice sounded foreign to her when she spoke to the 911 operator — pieces
that did not fit together into anything she could hold or examine. She found she could not tell the
story of what happened, even to herself, and this absence of a clear account left her in a state of
chronic unresolved alertness, as though some part of her nervous system remained at the
intersection, still waiting for what came next. The fragmentation itself fed her hypervigilance;
because she could never fully process what had occurred, she remained physiologically primed,
scanning for danger, sleeping poorly, waking in the early hours with her heart already racing.

She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt unassailable, that the accident happened because
of the way she had acted — that she had been careless, that a more attentive person would have seen
him in time. Layered beneath this was a deeper conviction: that her role, as someone who had spent
her career helping others navigate crisis, meant she should have been capable of preventing harm.
The failure felt categorical. These appraisals did not remain abstract; they amplified her sense of
ongoing threat, as though the world had become newly dangerous and she had proven herself unequal to
it. Fear, guilt, and shame moved through her in waves she could neither predict nor suppress.

At work, the maintaining cycles became concrete and costly. Her office occasionally adjoins the
intake suite, where clients sometimes arrive in visible distress following accidents or medical
emergencies. Miriam began avoiding eye contact with clients during sessions — a habit that formed
gradually and that she justified to herself as giving them space, though she recognized, with the
self-awareness that made everything worse, that she was protecting herself from seeing something in
their faces that might pull the memory forward. When the thought of the accident surfaced, she
pressed it down deliberately, redirecting her attention with a practiced firmness. But suppression
did not dissolve the fragments; it preserved them unprocessed, and the effort of sustained avoidance
kept her arousal elevated, which in turn made the intrusive pieces more likely to break through. The
memory remained raw precisely because she could not approach it.

She began declining any work-related referrals involving physical trauma, and she has not entered a
hospital since the accident, even when a family member was briefly admitted in the spring.
Ambulances on the street near her office — a common sound in that neighborhood — now reliably spike
her alertness and, in passing, sometimes briefly narrow her vision. Her husband has noticed that she
is unreachable in the evenings, present in body but somewhere else entirely. Nothing in her life,
she told her intake clinician, feels like it means anything anymore.

What finally brought Miriam to seek help was a supervision session in which her clinical director
gently observed that her recent work had seemed distant. She left that meeting and sat in her car
for forty minutes. She is most afraid that what she has become is permanent. She is hoping,
tentatively and with some shame at needing to hope, that it is not.
