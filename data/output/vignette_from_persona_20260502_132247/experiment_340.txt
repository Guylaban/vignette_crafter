Cara Lindström is a 33-year-old paramedic of Swedish descent, married and currently living in a mid-
sized city in the Pacific Northwest. She was referred for psychological assessment following a
critical incident review at her ambulance service, after her supervisor noted a marked deterioration
in her performance and a pattern of calling in sick on shifts that followed night runs. Her PCL-5
score of 64 places her firmly in the severe range, and the history she provided across intake and
two subsequent sessions reveals a presentation that has been quietly intensifying for the better
part of eight months.

The incident occurred on a wet Tuesday evening last autumn. Cara was the attending paramedic on a
call to a residential address — a reported fall in an elderly man's home. Arriving to find him
conscious and communicative, she made a clinical assessment and began moving him for transport.
During the transfer, she misjudged the positioning of his cervical spine, a decision made in a
moment of pressure and fatigue at the tail end of a double shift. The man suffered a serious spinal
injury that left him with partial paralysis in both legs. The image that has stayed with her — the
one she cannot shake — is not the chaos that followed, but a single frozen instant: the sound the
gurney made, the angle of his neck, and the precise expression on his face in the second before he
cried out. She remembers it not as a continuous event but as a series of still photographs, each one
sharp and terrible, suspended in isolation from the rest.

Those fragments arrive without warning. She might be filling in a run report at the station, or
standing at the kitchen counter making coffee, and suddenly one of those pictures is simply there —
the gurney, the neck, the face — vivid and complete, carrying with it exactly the terror she felt in
that moment, as though the eight months between then and now do not exist. The intrusions are not
accompanied by narrative or context; they arrive as pure sensory and emotional fact. When they come,
she finds herself scanning her environment almost involuntarily, checking for threats she cannot
name but is certain are present. By night this is most pronounced: she lies awake with a quality of
alertness that feels less like anxiety and more like readiness, as though the house itself might
require her to act. Concentration has become unreliable, and she has made two minor documentation
errors at work that she has not disclosed to her supervisor.

The relationship between the intrusions and her vigilance has become self-sustaining in a way she is
beginning to dimly recognise. When the fragments surface, her nervous system interprets them as
evidence of present danger, and that state of heightened alertness in turn seems to make the
memories more accessible, more easily triggered — a noise on the radio, the specific weight of a
cervical collar in her hands during a training drill, the smell of wet pavement. Each of these pulls
the photographs back. In the months since the incident, Cara has begun drinking more heavily in the
evenings, telling herself it is simply how she unwinds. She has also developed a habit of staying
very busy — picking up overtime, filling silences with podcasts, keeping her hands occupied — and
she has stopped talking about the incident entirely, deflecting any mention of it at work and
shutting down her husband's attempts to ask how she is doing. What she has noticed, though she has
not framed it this way, is that the drinking that was supposed to quiet the memories seems instead
to make the nights worse — she wakes in the early hours more agitated, more watchful, the fragments
closer to the surface rather than further away. The alcohol and the distraction have also eroded the
quality of her sleep and her daytime concentration, leaving her in a state of low-grade exhaustion
that makes the hypervigilance feel permanent rather than episodic.

What finally brought Cara to seek help was not the incident review, but a moment three weeks ago
when she hesitated — visibly, and in front of her partner — before moving a patient with a suspected
spinal injury. She is afraid, above everything, that she has become dangerous. What she is hoping
for, though she struggles to say it directly, is to be able to do her job again without being
ambushed by her own mind.
