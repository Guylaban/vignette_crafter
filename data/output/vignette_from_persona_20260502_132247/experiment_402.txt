Tane Haumia is a 65-year-old widowed Māori man working as a project manager for a mid-sized
construction firm in Auckland-born but now based in Honolulu, where he relocated following his
wife's death several years ago. He presented to an outpatient psychology clinic reporting
significant distress, sleep disruption, and what he described as an inability to "get out from
under" an experience that had occurred nearly three years prior. His PCL-5 score of 46 placed him
firmly in the clinical range, and across the first two sessions a coherent and painful picture
emerged.

The precipitating event was his youngest daughter Aroha's sudden collapse at home from a severe
asthma attack when she was nine years old. Tane had been the only adult present. He recalled
watching her lips turn blue as she struggled to draw breath, her small chest heaving with a
desperate, laboured rhythm he could not stop. He had called 911 and attempted to follow the
dispatcher's instructions, but by the time paramedics arrived she had lost consciousness. She
survived, but spent four days in the paediatric intensive care unit before stabilising. For Tane,
those four days — and especially the twenty minutes alone with her before help arrived — became the
fixed point around which his entire inner world reorganised.

In the weeks following Aroha's discharge, Tane found himself returning obsessively to what he had
done and, more precisely, what he believed he had failed to do. He had come to believe, with a
certainty that resisted all counter-evidence, that his slowness in recognising the severity of her
symptoms had caused the crisis to escalate. He told himself he should have known — should have acted
faster, kept better medications in the house, been more vigilant. The conviction that he had failed
to protect his daughter calcified into a broader self-doubt: he could no longer trust his own
judgment in any situation that carried stakes. This cluster of self-blame and perceived failure did
not remain contained to his private thoughts. It began to colour how he perceived the world around
him, making ordinary situations feel saturated with the potential for catastrophe. When a work
deliverable ran behind schedule or a subcontractor didn't return a call promptly, Tane found himself
certain that something had gone wrong — that delay meant disaster, in the same way that his own
delay had nearly cost Aroha her life. His body kept pace with his mind: his heart would race, his
chest would tighten, and on the worst occasions he would notice his own breathing becoming shallow
and laboured — a sensation that pulled him instantly back to the living room floor, to Aroha's face.

It was these physical echoes that proved most destabilising. Any encounter with breathlessness, his
own or another's, acted as a direct portal to the original scene. At work, when a colleague returned
from a run and stood panting in the corridor, Tane had to excuse himself. He began avoiding the
annual occupational health checks his employer required, finding reasons to reschedule, then simply
not booking them at all. He stopped visiting Aroha's paediatrician with her, delegating that role to
her older sister. Hospitals, ambulances glimpsed on the highway, even the antiseptic smell of a
pharmacy — all of it he routed around. To manage the intrusive imagery that broke through
regardless, he kept his mind in constant motion: long hours at the construction site, podcasts
running through the night, volunteer work on weekends. He told himself this was coping. What it was,
in practice, was a way of never sitting still long enough to examine the belief that he was a man
who could not be trusted to keep the people he loved safe. Because he never examined that belief, it
never softened. And because it never softened, every reminder of the event — every breathless
colleague, every delayed email, every routine medical form — arrived carrying its full freight of
threat.

What finally brought Tane to seek help was a mandatory pre-employment medical required for a new
project contract. He sat in the waiting room, heard a child coughing in the next bay, and walked out
before his name was called. Standing in the car park, he realised he had not seen a doctor himself
in nearly three years. What he feared most, he said quietly in session, was that he would never stop
waiting for the next thing to go wrong. What he hoped for, with considerably less certainty, was
that he might one day be able to trust himself again.
