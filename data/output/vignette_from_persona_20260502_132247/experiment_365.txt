Aleksander is a 56-year-old Polish-American professional dancer and choreographer who has spent the
better part of three decades teaching and performing in a mid-sized regional dance company in the
Pacific Northwest. He came to the intake appointment wearing a pressed shirt and moving with the
careful deliberateness of someone who had learned to manage his body through pain — though the pain
he was managing now was not physical. Approximately fourteen months ago, Aleksander was driving a
company van after a late rehearsal, transporting three younger dancers to their apartments. Fatigued
and momentarily distracted by a conversation in the back seat, he drifted at an intersection and
struck a cyclist who had entered on a green light. The impact was severe. The cyclist — a young
woman — sustained a fractured pelvis and a traumatic brain injury that left her with lasting
cognitive deficits. Aleksander was uninjured. He remembers the sound of the collision, the sudden
stillness, and the image of her bicycle folded beneath the van's front wheel. He also remembers the
faces of the three dancers staring at him from the back seat in the seconds afterward, and it is
that image — their eyes in the rearview mirror — that has become, in his mind, the moment he failed
everyone who depended on him.

In the months that followed, Aleksander found that certain songs — particularly anything that had
been playing on the van's radio that evening — could return him instantly to the intersection,
flooding him with the visual memory of cars and impact and the particular quality of light from the
streetlamps. Crowded spaces, especially the wings of a theatre before a performance, produced a
similar effect: the press of bodies and noise overwhelming his nervous system until the present
dissolved into that night. He had come to believe that these intrusions were not random — that they
were evidence of something he deserved, a kind of internal accounting for what he had done. The
triggers did not merely recall the event; they reactivated his certainty that he had failed
catastrophically, and conversely, the weight of that certainty kept him primed and scanning, so that
stimuli he might once have moved through easily now arrived already charged with threat.

What frightened him most was not the memory itself but the verdict it carried. He was certain he
would never forgive himself for not pulling over when he first felt tired, for not insisting someone
else drive, for allowing a young woman's life to be altered permanently because of a moment of
inattention. He had been the senior person in that van, the one responsible for those dancers, and
he had failed in precisely the role that defined him — not just as a company director but as someone
who had always understood his purpose to be the protection and cultivation of younger artists. That
sense of total role failure was not abstract; it sat in him like a verdict already handed down.

To manage the appraisals, Aleksander began constructing a life of avoidance. He stopped driving near
the intersection where the accident occurred, rerouting his commute by nearly twenty minutes each
day. He tried hard not to think about the trauma directly, pushing the images away whenever they
surfaced — an effort that paradoxically left him more exhausted and more alert, as though
suppression itself demanded continuous vigilance. He also began dwelling on who he had been before
the accident: the dancer, the teacher, the man whose body and judgment could be trusted. This
backward orientation, while offering momentary relief, kept him in a state of low-grade arousal,
unable to settle into the present. Over time, the suppression and avoidance maintained a persistent
sense of threat that made it harder to concentrate during rehearsals, impossible to relax even at
home in the evenings, and difficult to fall asleep without lying rigidly alert to sounds he could
not name. His wife noticed the distance. He had reduced his teaching hours, citing administrative
demands, but the truth was that the studio — with its mirrors, its music, its crowded warmth — had
become a place where triggers arrived without warning.

What finally brought him to seek help was a performance he could not attend. He stood outside the
theatre, heard the opening music through the lobby doors, and walked back to his car. He told
himself he was tired. He did not believe it. He is hoping, cautiously, to be able to teach again
without bracing himself. What he fears most is that the forgiveness he would need to do that is
simply not available to him.
