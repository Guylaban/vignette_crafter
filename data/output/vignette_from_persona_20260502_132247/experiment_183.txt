Diane is a 48-year-old single woman of Canadian descent who has worked for nearly two decades as a
social ministry worker in a mid-sized Midwestern city, coordinating food assistance and crisis
support for vulnerable community members. She presented for psychological assessment approximately
fourteen months after a serious road traffic accident in which the car she was travelling in as a
passenger was struck at speed by a vehicle running a red light. The impact was violent enough to
deploy the airbags, and Diane was briefly trapped by the crumpled passenger door before emergency
services arrived. During those minutes of entrapment — unable to move, unable to open the door,
unable to do anything but wait — she had the clear and terrifying conviction that she was going to
die and that no one could prevent it. The smell of the deployed airbag, the hissing of the engine,
and the sounds of other drivers shouting were all encoded in those moments of absolute helplessness.

In the weeks following the accident, Diane found that ordinary images of cars colliding — glimpsed
on a television in the break room at her ministry office, or passing a fender-bender on the street —
could pull her back into the passenger seat with an immediacy that felt less like memory than re-
occurrence. These intrusive crash images, together with any sensation of feeling physically unwell,
became powerful triggers for acute distress. Because her body had been injured in the crash, even
minor physical symptoms — a headache, a racing heart, a wave of nausea — now carried a freight of
danger they had never carried before. Being directed by a supervisor, asked to attend a mandatory
training, or required to follow a procedural protocol at work could also ignite the same alarm,
because each such moment echoed the helplessness of being trapped and unable to act for herself.
These triggers, once activated, fed directly into a state of heightened vigilance: upon entering any
room — the community hall where she ran weekly food distributions, the church basement where she met
clients — Diane would immediately and automatically scan for exits, her eyes moving to doorways
before she had consciously registered the impulse. Ordinary sounds, a door slamming in the corridor
or a car backfiring outside the ministry building, could make her flinch so sharply that colleagues
had begun to notice.

Diane had come to hold a set of beliefs about herself and the world that darkened everything she
experienced. She was certain that what had happened to her was a profound injustice — the driver who
struck them had faced only a minor traffic citation — and the absence of meaningful accountability
had calcified into a conviction that the world did not operate fairly and that she had no recourse
within it. More corrosively, she had come to believe that her own body was no longer a reliable
instrument: it had failed to protect her once, and she could not trust its signals now. This belief
that she was fundamentally helpless to prevent harm fed back into the crash imagery and the
sensitivity to physical symptoms, making each bodily sensation feel like evidence of an approaching
catastrophe she was powerless to stop. Her appraisals of helplessness and bodily unreliability also
kept her threat system perpetually armed — if she could not trust her body and could not trust
safety even when others assured her of it, then sustained vigilance felt not like a symptom but like
rational self-preservation.

Out of this cluster of beliefs and sensations, Diane had constructed a life of careful avoidance.
She no longer drove and refused to travel as a passenger, which in practical terms meant she had
stopped attending the satellite outreach sites her role required and had quietly withdrawn from a
significant portion of her professional responsibilities. She avoided any situation likely to place
her body under physical stress, and — perhaps most telling — she had come to avoid relaxing, because
stillness felt like lowering a guard she could not afford to lower. This avoidance, while
temporarily reducing distress, had the effect of keeping her world small and her triggers close: by
never habituating to the sensations of a passenger seat or the mild physical arousal of a walk to a
client's home, she ensured that these remained charged. The narrowing of her life also reinforced
her sense of helplessness and injustice, feeding her appraisals and sustaining the vigilance she
could not relinquish. What finally brought Diane to seek help was a conversation with a client in
crisis — a woman describing feeling trapped — that sent her out of the room shaking. She arrived at
the intake appointment hoping, above all, to be able to do her job again, and afraid that what the
accident had taken from her could not be given back.
