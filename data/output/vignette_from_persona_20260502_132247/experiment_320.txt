Marcus Hale is a 42-year-old married ship captain of mixed North American heritage who presented for
psychological assessment following a referral from his maritime employer after colleagues raised
concerns about his erratic decision-making on the water. He carries a PCL-5 score of 56, consistent
with significant post-traumatic stress, and his presentation is best understood against the backdrop
of a catastrophic industrial explosion he survived three years ago at a commercial port facility.

Marcus had been overseeing a cargo transfer operation when a pressurised tank on an adjacent vessel
detonated without warning. The blast wave threw him across the deck; he watched crew members he had
commanded for years disappear into smoke and fire. Port security personnel — uniformed officers he
did not recognise — arrived in the chaos and, in his account, gave contradictory orders that delayed
the evacuation and may have cost lives. No formal inquiry ever assigned individual responsibility to
those officers or to the facility operators whose maintenance failures precipitated the explosion.
Marcus left the scene physically intact but carrying the conviction that the people who should have
protected his crew had failed them, and that no one had been made to answer for it.

That conviction — that those responsible faced no consequences and that other people fundamentally
cannot be relied upon — became the lens through which Marcus now reads the world, and it has
amplified the threat he perceives in nearly every environment. He has come to believe that because
institutions failed once without penalty, they will fail again, and that the only variable he can
control is his own vigilance. In any room he enters, Marcus scans for dangerous individuals and
selects his seat based on sightlines and proximity to exits; at sea or ashore, he cannot fall asleep
without first satisfying himself that no threat is approaching, a process that can take hours. The
appraisal that others are unreliable feeds directly into a sustained sense that danger is always
present and unguarded against.

The triggers that return him most forcefully to the explosion are precisely those that were present
in those minutes on the deck: the sudden concussive sound of anything that bangs or detonates
nearby, the sight of military or police uniforms, and the physical act of objects being thrown,
which replicates the sensation of being hurled by the blast. Because Marcus now appraises the world
as perpetually unsafe and staffed by people who cannot be trusted, these sensory cues carry a weight
far beyond their objective content — the sight of a uniformed harbour officer is not merely
uncomfortable but registers as evidence that the conditions for catastrophe are reassembling. His
body responds accordingly, and the intrusive re-experiencing that follows confirms, in his mind,
that the danger was and remains real.

To manage this, Marcus has developed a set of strategies that initially provided relief but have
steadily eroded his functioning. He has quietly relinquished supervisory responsibilities on complex
docking manoeuvres, delegating decisions that are properly his so as to avoid the concentrated
exposure to noise, uniforms, and the unpredictability of other people that such operations involve.
He has also taken to gambling — first at online platforms, then at a casino two towns from his home
— finding that the focused, rule-bound tension of cards temporarily displaces the ambient
hypervigilance that otherwise dominates his waking hours. His wife has noticed both the absences and
the financial withdrawals but does not know their source. Over time, however, stepping back from his
professional duties has deepened Marcus's sense that he is no longer capable, and the gambling,
which began as distraction, has become a source of shame that reinforces his belief that he cannot
be relied upon — that the same unreliability he attributes to others has now colonised him. That
shame feeds back into the threat monitoring: if he cannot trust himself, he must be even more
vigilant externally.

He avoids unfamiliar ports and anchorages entirely, unwilling to enter environments whose danger he
cannot pre-calculate. He has not told his wife the full account of the explosion, partly because he
is certain that revisiting it in any sustained way will overwhelm him beyond recovery. What finally
brought Marcus to seek help was a near-miss incident in which his inattention during a routine
harbour approach — his attention consumed by scanning the dock for threats — almost resulted in a
collision. He is most afraid that he is becoming the kind of captain who gets people killed. What he
most hopes is that someone can explain why he cannot simply decide to stop.
