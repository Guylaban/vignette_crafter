Rosario is a 78-year-old Salvadoran woman who has been married for over fifty years and has worked
as a librarian at a small public branch in her city for the past two decades. She presents with a
PCL-5 score of 41, describing a constellation of symptoms that have intensified in recent months
despite decades of determined suppression. The traumatic events she carries originate from her
forced displacement as a young woman during a period of violent political upheaval in El Salvador.
She and her family fled on foot at night, moving through dense undergrowth, past the smoldering
remnants of a village, the acrid smell of smoke and charred wood thick in the air. At one point she
became separated from her younger brother in the darkness. She never saw him again. The family
eventually reached safety, but Rosario arrived carrying a silence she has never fully broken — she
has never been able to find language adequate to what she witnessed, and to this day she cannot put
the event into words in any coherent sequence. What she is left with instead are fragments: the
smell of smoke, the particular weight of darkness, the sound of her own breathing.

For most of her adult life, Rosario managed by building a life dense with structure and purpose. The
library became her sanctuary — the orderly rows of books, the hushed routine, the sense of being
useful. She worked hard at keeping busy, filling every hour so that stillness could not find her.
She also returned, privately and relentlessly, to the same unanswerable questions: why her family
had been targeted, what she could have done differently, whether she had failed her brother by
losing his hand in the dark. This self-blame and circular rumination had become so habitual that she
barely recognized them as suffering — they felt, to her, like a form of vigilance, a way of staying
prepared.

It was the memories themselves that first destabilized her sense of safety. When the olfactory
fragments surfaced — triggered, most recently, by a patron burning incense near the library entrance
— Rosario found herself instantly flooded, her body certain she was back in that field. These
involuntary sensory intrusions, arriving without narrative or context, left her feeling that the
world was fundamentally unpredictable and dangerous. Her hypervigilance sharpened: she began
monitoring the library's entrance obsessively, positioning herself so her back was always to the
wall, scanning every person who walked through the door. Concentration, never effortless, became
nearly impossible — she would lose her place mid-sentence while cataloguing, or forget a patron's
request moments after receiving it. She had come to believe that only certain corners of the
library, and her own home, were genuinely safe, and she declined social invitations and professional
meetings that would take her beyond those boundaries.

The heightened threat perception, in turn, drove her deeper into the strategies that had long
defined her coping. She worked longer hours, volunteered for additional cataloguing projects,
anything to stay in motion. But the busyness and the rumination fed back into the memories rather
than quieting them: the self-blame cycling through her mind at the circulation desk kept the
emotional core of the trauma perpetually activated, making the next olfactory intrusion or guilt-
laden recall more likely and more destabilizing. When the memories surfaced again — wordless,
somatic, accompanied by a wave of guilt so intense it felt like accusation — her body's alarm system
fired anew, reinforcing her certainty that she was not safe, that she could not trust her own
surroundings. The loop had been tightening for months without her naming it as such.

What finally brought Rosario to seek help was a morning when she smelled smoke from a distant brush
fire and found herself unable to move for several minutes, gripping a shelf in the stacks while a
colleague watched with concern. She was referred shortly afterward. She arrives quietly, choosing a
chair with a clear view of the door. What she fears most is that she will be asked to speak what she
has never been able to speak. What she most hopes for, though she has not yet said it aloud, is that
someone might help her understand why she is still, after all these decades, so afraid.
