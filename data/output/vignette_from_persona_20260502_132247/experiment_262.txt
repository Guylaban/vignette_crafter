Aleksander is a 21-year-old man of Eastern European descent who works as a public defender in a mid-
sized American city. He came to the United States as a refugee at the age of fifteen, having fled a
region of prolonged civil conflict where displacement, arbitrary detention, and sudden lethal
violence were features of daily life. The event that appears most centrally in his current
presentation occurred during the final weeks before his family's departure: while moving through a
checkpoint at night, the convoy he was travelling with was halted and searched. In the sustained,
heavy silence that followed the soldiers' commands, Aleksander heard a sound he could not identify —
a sharp metallic click — and then chaos erupted. He remembers being pressed flat against the ground,
the cold earth against his chest and cheek, the smell of diesel and damp soil, and the screaming of
people he could not see. He survived unharmed, but two people in the convoy did not. He was fifteen
years old and alone in the dark for what felt like hours before anyone came.

Aleksander arrived in the United States and, by most external measures, rebuilt his life with
remarkable speed. He learned English, excelled academically, and enrolled in an accelerated law
programme driven by a conviction that the law could be a shield for people who had none. He began
working as a public defender at twenty, and it is in that setting — in the corridors of county
courthouses, in holding cells, in the sudden silences that fall when a judge enters a room — that
his symptoms have become impossible to manage. When the courtroom goes quiet before a verdict is
read, Aleksander's body responds before his mind can catch up: his heart accelerates, his hands
press flat against the table, and he feels, with full physical certainty, that something terrible is
about to happen. Ordinary sounds — a door slamming in the hallway, a bailiff's keys — produce a
startle response that he has learned to disguise with practiced stillness, though the effort costs
him. He has come to believe, with a conviction that feels less like opinion and more like fact, that
the world is a dangerous place and that safety is always provisional, always about to be revoked.

These appraisals have shaped the way his memories operate. Because Aleksander moves through the
world in a state of near-constant anticipatory vigilance — certain he must stay alert because one
cannot know what will happen next — his nervous system remains primed, and the intrusive material
surfaces easily. His memories of the checkpoint are predominantly visual: the image of the ground
inches from his face, the dark shapes of boots, the particular quality of light from a handheld
torch. Sometimes, without any accompanying image at all, he is seized by the full emotional weight
of that night — a wave of dread and helplessness that arrives without warning and without
explanation. Most disturbingly, when these memories surface, he feels his body assume the position
he was in that night, a flattening and bracing that happens involuntarily. These bodily re-
enactments, in turn, confirm and deepen his sense that danger is present, sustaining the elevated
threat state and feeding back into the conviction that the world cannot be trusted.

In response to the relentless sense of threat, Aleksander has constructed a set of strategies that
feel protective but have steadily narrowed his life. He monitors his own thoughts with exhausting
vigilance, watching for signs that he is losing his grip on rationality — a fear that has grown,
paradoxically, from the very effort of watching. He avoids any conversation about what he
experienced as a refugee, including with his partner, who has noticed his withdrawal and has begun
to interpret it as emotional unavailability. He deflects when colleagues or clients — many of whom
are themselves immigrants — reference experiences that echo his own. This avoidance preserves the
appraisal that the memories are too dangerous to approach, reinforcing the belief that the world,
and his own mind, are fundamentally unsafe.

What finally brought Aleksander to seek help was a moment in a holding cell with a client. A door
slammed, the room went briefly silent, and he found himself pressed against the wall, unable to
speak. His client looked at him with confusion. Aleksander went home that evening and told his
partner, for the first time, that something was wrong. He is most afraid that the intrusions will
never stop — and most hoping that he is not, as he privately suspects, permanently broken.
