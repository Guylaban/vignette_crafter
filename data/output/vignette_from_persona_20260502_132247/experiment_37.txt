Valentina Cruz is a 53-year-old married Latina woman who has worked as a stockbroker for nearly two
decades, a profession that once suited her sharp instincts and tolerance for high-stakes
uncertainty. She presented for psychological assessment approximately eight months after being
mugged at knifepoint in the parking garage beneath her downtown office building. It was a Tuesday
evening in late autumn, the garage poorly lit at the lower levels, and she had stayed late to close
a difficult trade. As she walked to her car, a man stepped out from between two vehicles, pressed a
blade against her ribs, and demanded her bag and phone. What she cannot stop reliving is not simply
the knife or the demand, but the moment she heard his footsteps behind her — rapid, close — and the
way his sudden lunge from the shadows transformed an ordinary evening into something she now
describes as the moment her understanding of the world broke open. She complied immediately, handed
everything over, and stood frozen as he ran. When she finally reached her car, she sat shaking for
forty minutes before she could drive.

In the weeks that followed, Valentina came to believe something she had not previously allowed
herself to think: that the world is genuinely dangerous and that no environment, however familiar,
can be trusted. The parking garage was a place she had walked through hundreds of times. The
building had security. She had done nothing wrong. And yet none of that had protected her. She felt
let down not only by the garage's management, whose lighting she had complained about in an email
months prior, but by a broader sense that institutions — the building, the city, the systems she had
always assumed formed a kind of invisible scaffolding around ordinary life — had simply failed her.
This conviction that danger is ambient and protection is illusory saturated her appraisals of the
present, making it impossible for her to move through the world without a constant low-grade
certainty that harm was imminent again. She began scanning every environment she entered — the
trading floor, the elevator, the lobby — cataloguing exits, clocking strangers' positions, reading
posture and proximity for signs of threat. When a colleague reached across her desk without warning,
or a courier moved quickly through the bullpen, her body responded as though the knife were back at
her ribs.

The hypervigilance was exhausting, and over months it fed back into her appraisals in a way that
deepened rather than resolved her fear. The constant scanning confirmed, again and again, that the
world required watching — that relaxing attention was the same as inviting harm. Ordinary sounds
became unbearable; a dropped binder, a door slamming down the hall, the sudden buzz of her phone on
a hard surface would send a jolt through her chest that left her trembling and ashamed. She began
performing small mental rituals before leaving the office — repeating a sequence of reassurances to
herself, retracing her route mentally, touching her keycard in a specific order — as though the
right internal procedure might prevent the thing from happening again. She also became, in her own
words, "too agreeable," softening her voice and manner when anyone around her seemed agitated,
deferring in meetings she would once have dominated, as though keeping others calm was the only
reliable form of self-protection she had left.

These strategies, which had formed quietly and felt like reasonable adaptations, had begun to cost
her. Colleagues had noticed a change. Her supervisor raised concerns about her performance in a
review. Her husband described her as "somewhere else" even when she was home. Valentina herself
described feeling dead inside much of the time — not sad exactly, but hollowed out, as though the
emotional range that once made her effective at her work and present in her marriage had simply gone
quiet. It was her husband who finally persuaded her to seek help, after she spent an entire dinner
scanning the restaurant exits and could not recall a word of their conversation afterward. What she
most fears is that this is now who she is. What she most hopes is that it isn't.
