splayed and trembling on that floor — that has lodged most persistently in her mind, and it is why,
now, the sight of her own hands at certain angles, or the hands of a colleague reaching across a
laboratory bench, can send a jolt of alarm through her body that feels entirely indistinguishable
from that night.

In the weeks that followed the assault, Marlene attempted to return to her work in forensic analysis
— a field that had always given her a sense of competence and purpose. But the laboratory
environment, where she routinely examines physical evidence and handles detailed photographic
material, became unexpectedly hostile terrain. When a memory of the assault intruded — often
triggered by the sight of specific body parts in forensic photographs, or by a colleague asking her
casually about a case timeline — the memory did not arrive as a coherent narrative. It came in
fragments: a flash of rage so intense it frightened her, or, in other moments, a strange blankness,
as though she were reviewing footage of someone else's life. She found herself pushing the memory
away before it could fully form, telling herself she was simply being efficient, staying focused.
She had come to believe, with quiet certainty, that if she allowed herself to think about it fully,
something in her would give way entirely.

That belief — that she was inadequate to the task of her own memory — began to colour everything.
She stopped exercising, something she had done consistently for decades, because physical exertion
brought her heart rate up in a way that felt dangerous, too close to the panic of that night. She
began restricting her food, not consciously at first, but as a way of maintaining a sense of control
over a body that now felt unreliable. She rearranged her seating in every room she entered — at
work, at restaurants, at family dinners — so that her back was always against a wall and she could
see the full space in front of her. These habits felt rational, even necessary. But each one quietly
confirmed what she had already begun to suspect about herself: that she was not the person she used
to be, that the happiness she had once taken for granted was simply gone, replaced by a misery she
could not name to her husband without feeling she was burdening him with something he could not fix.

The strategies that were meant to contain her distress began, over months, to amplify it. By
avoiding physical activity and restricting her eating, Marlene kept her body in a state of low-grade
depletion that made emotional regulation harder, and the memories, when they broke through, felt
more overwhelming rather than less. The avoidance of full recall meant the memory never resolved —
it remained jagged, ready to be snagged by any passing stimulus. Sitting with her back to the wall,
scanning every room, meant she was always, in some part of her nervous system, still in that parking
structure. These vigilant postures kept the triggers close, so that conversations about the future —
what she and her husband might do when she retired, where they might travel — produced a sudden,
almost physical resistance, as though the future were a country she no longer had clearance to
enter.

What finally brought Marlene to seek help was a moment at work three weeks ago. A junior colleague
asked her, gently, to walk him through an old case she had handled. She felt rage rise in her chest
before a single word left her mouth — rage she could not explain and could not contain — and she
left the room without speaking. She sat in her car for forty minutes. She told her husband that
evening, for the first time, that she was not managing. What she is most afraid of, she said

considerably less certainty, is that she might not be right about that.
