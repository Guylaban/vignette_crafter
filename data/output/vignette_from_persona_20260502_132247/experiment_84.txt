Kwame is a 68-year-old widowed man of African descent who has worked as a professional dancer and
movement instructor for most of his adult life. He presented for psychological assessment
approximately seven months after surviving a life-threatening motor vehicle collision, reporting a
PCL-5 score of 67 and describing a daily life that has contracted dramatically since the crash.

The collision occurred on a busy urban intersection when a commercial truck ran a red light and
struck the driver's side of Kwame's sedan at high speed. The impact was sudden and total — the
shriek of tyres, the concussive roar of metal on metal, the shattering of glass, and then a period
Kwame cannot account for. He was extracted by emergency services and hospitalised for several weeks
with serious injuries. What haunts him now is not only what he remembers but what he cannot. He has
the impression of only fragments: the moment before impact, a sensation of weightlessness, then
fluorescent hospital lighting. The middle — the collision itself, the extraction, the first hours —
remains inaccessible, a gap he cannot bridge no matter how deliberately he tries to reach into it.
When he attempts to reconstruct the event, the memory resists him, yielding no coherent narrative he
can hold from beginning to end. This fragmentation has itself become a source of dread, because the
absence of a complete story means the worst possibilities — that he panicked, that he failed to act,
that his body simply gave out — remain unrefuted in his mind.

That interpretive void has fed a set of beliefs that now organise much of Kwame's inner life. He has
come to believe that he cannot trust himself to respond correctly in a crisis, a conviction that the
gap in his memory seems to confirm every time he tries and fails to retrieve it. His body, which
carried him through decades of demanding physical performance, now feels like an unreliable
instrument — it was injured in a moment he cannot even recall, and he is no longer certain it will
do what he needs it to do. He also carries a bitter sense that the traffic systems and municipal
infrastructure that should have prevented the crash failed him entirely, a belief that has
generalised into a broader suspicion of institutions and the built environment. These appraisals —
of personal unreliability, bodily betrayal, and institutional failure — have in turn amplified his
perception of the world as actively dangerous. He is certain that delays of any kind signal imminent
harm: a slow-moving elevator, a traffic light that holds a beat too long, a delivery that does not
arrive on time. Unexpected or loud sounds — a car door slamming in the street below, a sudden burst
of music from a passing vehicle — send him into an immediate state of high alert that takes many
minutes to subside. He insists on positioning himself with his back to a wall in any room, scanning
for exits, a habit that has made him conspicuous and exhausted in equal measure.

Because his body and his judgment feel untrustworthy, and because the fragmented memory offers no
reassurance that he coped well, Kwame has steadily withdrawn. He no longer goes out unless
absolutely necessary. The studio where he taught movement classes — the physical space most central
to his identity since his wife's death three years ago — has been entirely abandoned. He avoids the
colleagues and students he once socialised with, unable to face their concern or the questions he
could not answer. The avoidance has brought a kind of muted relief, but it has also sealed him
inside a world where the threats he monitors are never disconfirmed, and where the hypervigilance
that drove him indoors continues to intensify without correction. He has also found himself
withdrawing from the early sessions of therapy, deliberately pushing away anything discussed in
those meetings, as though examining the material might make the fragmented memory cohere into
something even more unbearable.

What finally brought Kwame to seek more intensive help was a phone call from a former student asking
whether he would consider returning to teach a single class. He realised he could not imagine
walking through the studio door. He arrived at this assessment hoping, above all, to understand why
his body and mind feel so foreign to him — and afraid, most deeply, that they always will.
