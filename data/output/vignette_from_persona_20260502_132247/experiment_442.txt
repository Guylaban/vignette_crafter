Kareem is a 64-year-old divorced man of Lebanese origin who has spent the better part of three
decades navigating American political life, first as a community advocate and later as an elected
official in a mid-sized northeastern city. His public role demands that he be visible, accessible,
and composed — qualities he had long cultivated with genuine pride. What brought him to the clinic
was not a single breaking point but an accumulation of years of quiet deterioration, finally made
undeniable when he nearly walked out of a town hall meeting after catching the faint smell of
something burning from the building's ventilation system.

The event that reshaped him occurred roughly eighteen years ago, during a period when Kareem took a
leave from his early political career to volunteer with a relief organization operating in a war-
affected region of the Middle East. He had wanted, he said, to do something real. What he
encountered was a landscape of collapsed infrastructure and overwhelmed field hospitals. One
afternoon, a strike hit a supply convoy he had been coordinating. The silence that followed the
initial blast was what he remembered most vividly — an unnatural, suspended quiet before the
screaming began — and the smell of burning metal and fabric that settled over everything. He helped
pull survivors from wreckage for hours. Several did not survive. He returned to the United States
weeks later and told almost no one what he had seen.

In the years that followed, his memories of that afternoon did not organize themselves into a
coherent narrative. Instead they arrived in disconnected fragments — a hand, a particular quality of
light, the silence — that refused to assemble into something he could file away. When the memory
returned, it brought with it the same terror he had felt in the field, as immediate and physical as
if no time had passed, and so overwhelming that he was often forced to stop whatever he was doing
and simply wait for it to pass. Over time, these intrusive fragments began to sensitize him to the
world around him. The silence itself became dangerous: any unusual quiet in a room — a lull in
conversation at a reception, an empty corridor in city hall — carried the texture of that post-blast
stillness, and his body responded accordingly, flooding with the same dread. This connection between
memory and his sense of present threat became one of the most disabling features of his
presentation. The memories did not stay in the past; they actively instructed him about what the
present meant.

His triggers were specific and potent. Conversations about military affairs — unavoidable in his
line of work, where defense appropriations and veterans' services came before his committee — could
surface the fragments without warning. Talking to strangers at public events, which his work
required constantly, carried the unpredictability he associated with that day. And the smell of
anything burning, however innocuous, was the most reliable and least controllable of all. When these
triggers fired, Kareem moved immediately to contain the experience: he worked to suppress any
visible emotional response, redirected his attention to procedural details, and when possible,
escaped into the next task on his agenda. His calendar, colleagues noted, had become almost inhuman
in its density. Overworking had become his primary architecture of survival, and it had functioned
well enough for years that he had mistaken it for recovery. But the suppression of emotion and the
relentless distraction did not metabolize the memories — they kept them pressurized, so that when a
fragment did break through, it arrived with undiminished force, and the cycle resumed.

His hypervigilance had become structural. He checked the locks on his office door multiple times
before leaving each evening. At public events he positioned himself near exits and tracked the room
continuously. The persistent scanning was exhausting in a way he found difficult to explain to
anyone, partly because he had no language for it that did not sound, to him, like weakness.

His divorce, finalized several years ago, had multiple causes, but his emotional unavailability and
his compulsive withdrawal into work were among them. He did not contest this. What finally brought
him to seek help was the town hall incident — the smell, the sudden narrowing of the room, the
certainty that something was about to happen. He is most afraid, he said quietly in his first
session, that this will never be something he can explain. What he hopes for, with some difficulty,
is to stop dreading silence.
