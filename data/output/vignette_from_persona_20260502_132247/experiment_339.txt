Martin is a 55-year-old single man of European descent who works as a counselor at a community
mental health center. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 74, seeking help for the first time
following a man-made disaster that occurred approximately fourteen months ago. Martin was present at
a warehouse fire caused by an industrial accident in his neighborhood — a fire that spread rapidly
and with terrifying speed, trapping several people inside. He was among the bystanders who attempted
to help, pulling a woman through a broken window while the structure groaned and smoke poured in
thick, acrid waves around them. The smell of burning material — synthetic, chemical, nothing like a
campfire — saturated his clothes and hair for days afterward. At one point, a panicked man nearby
hurled a fire extinguisher across the pavement in desperation, the clatter of metal on concrete
cutting through the roar of the flames. Martin made it out. Not everyone did.

In the weeks that followed, Martin noticed that the smell of smoke — from a neighbor's fireplace,
from a passing vehicle's exhaust, from anything that carried even a faint char — would stop him
cold. The burnt smell in particular, so specific to that afternoon, seemed to reach inside him and
pull something loose. These olfactory triggers did not merely remind him of the event; they
transported him into the emotional core of it, flooding him with fear and helplessness before he had
time to register what was happening. Similarly, the sound or sight of objects being thrown — even a
colleague setting down a heavy binder with a thud — could produce the same sudden activation. As
these triggers began to surface memory, Martin found himself caught in fragmentary, disorienting
recall: not clear images, but the smell of that smoke arising unbidden during the memory itself, and
waves of raw emotional intensity that arrived without any accompanying picture, as though the terror
had been encoded separately from everything else. Perhaps most unsettling to him was the quality of
the memory itself — it felt, he told his intake clinician, like something that had happened to a
stranger, like footage from someone else's life that he had somehow absorbed. He could not make it
feel like his own.

Martin had come to believe, with quiet but firm conviction, that his body had turned against him —
that its continued alarm responses were evidence of something broken and unreliable. He was certain
that he had to stay vigilant at all times, because the disaster had proven to him that catastrophe
could arrive without warning. These appraisals did not sit passively alongside his memory; they
actively colored it, lending every recollection a quality of ongoing threat, as though the danger
had never truly ended. And in turn, the memory reinforced these beliefs — each intrusive wave of
emotion without image seemed to confirm that he was not processing things normally, that something
was fundamentally wrong with him.

To manage this, Martin had developed a set of strategies that made intuitive sense to him but were
quietly narrowing his life. He had stopped seeing friends, partly because social gatherings
sometimes involved fireplaces or candles, and partly because he feared losing control of his
reactions in front of others. He had made a determined effort to avoid thinking about the event at
all, believing — with some force — that if he allowed himself to dwell on it, he would not be able
to cope, that he would unravel entirely. But suppressing thought about the event had an ironic
consequence: the memories became more intrusive, more fragmentary, and more emotionally raw, as
though the material that had never been processed kept pressing back against the door he had shut.
He had also begun monitoring his own thoughts with exhausting vigilance, scanning for any sign that
he was losing his grip on sanity. This constant internal surveillance left him little cognitive room
for anything else.

At work, the strain had become impossible to ignore. Martin counsels clients who have experienced
trauma, and the work now costs him enormously — a client describing a fire in a session, or the
faint smell of smoke drifting in from outside the building's ventilation system, could destabilize
him for the rest of the afternoon. He had begun scheduling fewer clients and leaving early on
difficult days, and his supervisor had noticed. It was this — the fear that his professional life,
the one domain that had always anchored him, was slipping — that finally brought Martin to seek
help. What he is most afraid of is that the damage is permanent. What he most hopes is that he can
feel, once again, like the events of that day belong to him — and that he might be able to bear
them.
