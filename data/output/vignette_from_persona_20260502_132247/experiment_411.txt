Rajan is a 58-year-old South Asian man, married and working as a Product Manager at a mid-sized
technology firm in the Pacific Northwest. He was referred for psychological assessment following a
disclosure to his primary care physician of persistent sleep disturbance and what he described,
quietly and with evident shame, as an inability to "function normally" since an experience of
detention and torture he endured approximately four years ago while visiting family in his country
of origin. He had been held for several days by state security forces, subjected to prolonged
physical abuse, hooding, and repeated interrogations conducted in a language he could not fully
follow — a disorienting blur of shouted commands, overlapping voices, and incomprehensible exchanges
that left him unable to distinguish threat from procedure. The sound of sirens had accompanied his
transfer between facilities. By the time he was released and eventually returned to the United
States, the damage had already taken root.

In the months that followed, Rajan began to carry a belief that has since become foundational to his
suffering: that something intrinsic to who he is had made him a target, that he had been identified
and selected because of some flaw or quality he could not name but could not dismiss. Alongside this
was a corrosive mistrust — of colleagues, of strangers, of systems he had once navigated without
anxiety. He had come to believe that people, broadly, could not be trusted, and that nothing
genuinely good lay ahead of him. These appraisals did not remain abstract. They drove him steadily
toward a life organized around containment and avoidance: he began refusing assignments that
required travel, declined team meetings he judged as unpredictable, and quietly restructured his
days at work to minimize any situation he anticipated as stressful. He also found himself unable to
pass a uniformed security guard in the office lobby without rerouting — a small but daily act of
avoidance that his colleagues noticed without understanding.

The same convictions that shaped his avoidance also kept his nervous system in a state of
unrelenting vigilance. Rajan could not relax even in environments he consciously recognized as safe.
At night, he woke repeatedly to check whether the bedroom door was locked, whether his wife was
still beside him, whether the sounds from the street were ordinary. He startled violently at
unexpected touch — a hand on his shoulder from a well-meaning colleague during a product review had
once caused him to knock over his chair. This chronic state of alert, never fully discharged, meant
that the threshold for perceived danger remained perpetually low, and the world was full of things
that crossed it. The sirens of emergency vehicles, audible from the open-plan office where he
worked, sent a jolt through him that he could neither suppress nor explain to those nearby. Patches
of incomprehensible chatter — a foreign-language phone call overheard in the corridor, a
multilingual meeting where voices overlapped — pulled him back with a force that felt physical. And
sometimes, without clear external cause, a wave of dizziness would rise through him, and his body
would seem to be telling him something his mind had not yet registered.

Rajan's attempts to manage these experiences had, over time, made them worse. His constant scanning
of his body for signs of injury or danger — a habit formed in detention, where physical damage had
to be privately assessed — now amplified every somatic sensation into potential evidence of threat,
which in turn made the dizziness and the startle responses feel more alarming and more confirming of
his belief that he was fundamentally unsafe. His avoidance of stressors narrowed his world steadily,
and the narrowing itself became proof, to him, that he was broken in some irreversible way — which
deepened his conviction that nothing good could come. The loop had been running for years before he
named it as a problem.

What finally brought Rajan to seek help was a conversation with his wife, who told him, carefully
and with evident grief, that she no longer recognized the man she had married. He had heard her.
What he most fears, sitting across from a clinician for the first time, is that she is right. What
he most hopes — though he holds the hope at arm's length — is that she is wrong.
