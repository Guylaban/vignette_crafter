Valentina Reyes is a 24-year-old Colombian-American woman who works the early shift at a large
distribution warehouse on the outskirts of the city. She came to the clinic eight months after
surviving a bombing at a crowded outdoor market — an attack that killed several bystanders and left
Valentina pinned briefly beneath a collapsed vendor stall, surrounded by smoke, screaming, and the
sudden, disorienting stillness that followed the blast. She had been shopping with a cousin when the
explosion occurred without warning. The percussion of it knocked her off her feet before she could
process what was happening; by the time she was pulled free, there were gaps in her experience she
could not account for — stretches of time that simply did not exist in her memory, no matter how
carefully she tried to reconstruct them. She knows she was there. She knows she survived. But the
middle of the event is missing, and that absence frightens her almost as much as what she can
recall.

In the months that followed, Valentina found herself arriving at a set of beliefs that felt, to her,
simply like accurate conclusions: the world was demonstrably dangerous, and her own body had proven
itself unreliable — it had frozen when she needed it most, had failed to warn her, had left her
without a clear account of what she had done or endured. She had come to believe that constant
vigilance was not anxiety but basic competence, the only rational response to a world that could
detonate without notice. These convictions shaped nearly everything about how she now moved through
her days. She avoided eye contact with strangers, not out of shyness but because meeting someone's
gaze felt like an act of exposure, a lowering of her guard. When the pressure of staying present
became unbearable, she would deliberately blank out — a practiced dissociation she had learned to
induce, a way of leaving her body before anything worse could reach her. At work, she spent her
shifts scanning the warehouse floor — the loading bays, the forklifts moving between aisles, the far
corners near the emergency exits — in a continuous, exhausting loop. These strategies gave her the
feeling of control, but they also quietly reinforced her certainty that the threat was real and
constant; the very act of scanning confirmed, in her own mind, that there was always something to
scan for.

The warehouse environment made this worse in ways that were hard to explain to anyone who had not
been there. A forklift reversing with a sharp beep, a pallet dropped from height, a coworker
appearing suddenly around a corner — any abrupt movement or sound would flood her system instantly,
her heart seizing before her thoughts could catch up. She was certain, in those moments, that
something terrible was beginning again. Nighttime brought its own particular weight; the warehouse
ran a second shift that sometimes required her to stay late, and she found that after dark the
building felt qualitatively more threatening, the shadows at the edges of her perception harder to
dismiss. These states of acute threat, in turn, pulled at her memory of the event — a sudden noise
would not just startle her but yank her back toward the market, toward the smoke and the noise and
the edges of what she could not remember, intensifying the rage and wordlessness that accompanied
those partial recollections. When the memories surfaced, she could not describe what she felt — not
to her cousin, not to the one friend she had told — because language simply was not available to
her. What she felt was a fury she could not name and could not place, and so she would dissociate
instead, or drop her eyes, or return to scanning, which temporarily damped the arousal but left her
more convinced than ever that her body was not to be trusted and the world was not safe.

Over the past several months, Valentina has called in sick with increasing frequency. A supervisor
noticed. A coworker asked if she was all right. She said yes both times. What finally brought her to
the clinic was a night shift two weeks ago when a fire alarm was tested without prior warning and
she found herself on the floor of the warehouse, unable to stand, certain — for approximately thirty
seconds — that she was back inside the blast. She is most afraid, she said quietly at intake, that
this is simply who she is now.
