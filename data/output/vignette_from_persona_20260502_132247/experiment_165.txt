Linh is a nineteen-year-old Vietnamese-American hairdresser who presents with a PCL-5 score of 54,
seeking help approximately eight months after the event that fractured her sense of herself and the
world. She lives with her boyfriend and works at a busy salon in a strip mall that sits directly
beside a four-lane road, a detail that has quietly shaped the texture of every working day since the
accident.

The incident occurred on a Saturday evening last autumn. Linh was driving a friend home after a
birthday gathering, navigating a wet intersection she had crossed dozens of times before. Distracted
for a moment — she has never forgiven herself for that moment — she failed to brake in time and her
car struck another vehicle that had entered on a yellow light. The impact was loud and sudden, a
sound she describes as the world cracking open. The other driver, an older man, was taken by
ambulance with a fractured pelvis and broken arm. He survived, but Linh stood on the wet asphalt
watching paramedics work and understood, with a certainty that has never quite left her, that she
had done that.

In the weeks that followed, images of the collision began arriving without warning — the flash of
the other car, the spray of glass, the man's face through the window — vivid, pictorial, as though
her memory had become a slideshow she could not switch off. At other times she could recount the
sequence of events in a flat, distant voice, as if describing something that had happened to someone
else entirely, the emotional content drained away. But then without warning the grief would return
with full force, a wave of it, and she would have to excuse herself from a client's chair and
collect herself in the back room. These shifts between numbness and overwhelming sorrow made her
feel as though her own mind was ungovernable.

The salon's proximity to the road means that loud noises — a truck backfiring, a tyre screeching on
wet tarmac — reach her constantly throughout the day. Each time, her body responds before her mind
can intervene: her chest tightens, her hands go cold, and the images come back. The sound pulls the
memory forward, and with it comes the familiar cascade of self-condemnation: she should have been
more careful, she should have protected someone who trusted the roads to be safe, and she failed.
These appraisals, once activated, feed back into the triggers themselves — she finds herself
scanning the road through the salon window between clients, watching for near-misses, which means
she is never far from the imagery even when nothing has happened.

She has not driven since the accident. Her boyfriend drives her to and from work, and she has
structured her life to avoid being a passenger whenever possible, which has narrowed her world
considerably. She avoids the anniversary of the accident and the birthday of the friend she was
driving that night. At work, the moment she enters a room — the salon, a waiting area, anywhere
unfamiliar — her eyes move automatically to the exits. She cannot settle. Even on quiet evenings at
home, when she knows she is safe, she is unable to relax, her body remaining at a low hum of
readiness. This hypervigilance keeps her nervous system primed, so that when the next trigger
arrives — a car horn, an unwanted thought surfacing mid-conversation — her physical reaction is
immediate and intense, which in turn confirms her growing belief that her body can no longer be
trusted to behave reasonably.

Her avoidance has had a relational cost. She has become compliant in ways that trouble her —
agreeing to things she does not want, deferring to clients and to her boyfriend, unable to tolerate
the possibility of conflict or disappointment, as though appeasement is the only thing keeping
further harm at bay. She has come to believe that she cannot truly rely on others to understand what
she carries, and that belief keeps her from asking for the support that might interrupt the cycle.
The avoidance that was meant to protect her has instead preserved the wound, keeping the memories
vivid and the appraisals sharp.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a morning three weeks ago when a car outside the salon
clipped a bollard with a bang, and she found herself on the floor of the back room, shaking, unable
to return to her client for twenty minutes. She is most afraid that this is simply who she is now.
She hopes, tentatively, that it does not have to be.
