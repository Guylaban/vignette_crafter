Aroha Tane is a 31-year-old Māori woman, married and working as a professor of Pacific studies at a
mid-sized university in the Pacific Northwest. She was referred for psychological assessment
following a life-threatening motor vehicle collision that occurred fourteen months ago, when a
vehicle ran a red light at speed and struck the driver's side of her car as she crossed an
intersection near campus. The impact was catastrophic: her door was sheared inward, the windshield
fractured, and she was conscious throughout, pinned and unable to move while she heard other drivers
shouting and the hiss of the engine dying. She sustained serious injuries and required surgery. She
remembers, with a clarity that still disturbs her, the precise angle of the other car's hood
crumpling toward her — and then almost nothing for long stretches after that. She was eventually
pulled free, but the transition between those vivid seconds of impact and the blurred, discontinuous
fragments that follow has never resolved into a coherent account.

Aroha's memory of the collision remains deeply disorganised. When she attempts to describe what
happened — to her husband, to a colleague who asked, to the insurance representative who needed a
statement — she loses the thread entirely, trails off mid-sentence, and is left with a feeling of
humiliation and dread. The event comes to her not as a story but as disconnected pieces: the sound
of the horn, a flash of her own hands on the wheel, a smell she cannot name. Some moments are
unbearably vivid; others are simply absent. This fragmentation has had direct consequences for how
she understands herself and her safety. Because she cannot construct a coherent narrative of what
happened or how she survived, she has come to believe, with quiet conviction, that the world is
fundamentally unpredictable and that her survival was arbitrary — that there is no reliable logic
that would protect her next time. This belief in ongoing, uncontrollable danger keeps her in a state
of persistent hypervigilance; she scans intersections, monitors the speed of cars in adjacent lanes,
and wakes repeatedly during the night to check that she is safe, lying still in the dark and
listening for sounds she cannot name. The fragmented memory also means that threat feels perpetually
unresolved — there is no ending she can return to, no moment of confirmed safety, and so the alarm
never fully quiets.

The hypervigilance and nocturnal checking have their most concrete expression in her working life.
Aroha drives to campus, and every commute has become an ordeal. She grips the wheel through
intersections, slowing well before the light changes, and has twice pulled over on the highway
because she felt the onset of a dissociative feeling — a creeping sense of detachment, of her hands
becoming unreal — that she associates directly with the moment before the impact. Photographs of
damaged cars in a student's presentation on road safety, or a colleague mentioning a recent accident
in conversation, can arrest her mid-thought, pulling her back into the sensory fragments of the
collision as though it were happening again. These intrusions are not random; the hyperalert state
she maintains seems to lower the threshold at which any crash-related stimulus becomes a full
flashback, and the flashbacks in turn confirm to her that she remains in danger — that her nervous
system is telling her something true about the world.

What has accumulated over fourteen months, alongside the fear, is a grief she finds harder to name.
She had considered herself, before the collision, to be a person of purpose — someone embedded in
community, in her work, in her marriage. Now she describes feeling that she belongs nowhere: not to
her Māori cultural community, from which she feels strangely cut off, not to her department, where
she performs competence while feeling hollow, not even to her own life. Nothing feels meaningful.
She used to be a happy person, she says, and she says it without anger, only a flat, bewildered
sadness. These appraisals of permanent personal loss intensify the sense of threat — if nothing is
worth protecting and nowhere is home, then there is no stable ground from which to feel safe — and
they lower her tolerance for any reminder of the crash, so that even ordinary conversations about
road conditions feel charged with an exposure she cannot bear.

Aroha sought help after she cancelled a keynote address at a conference because she could not face
the flight, and her husband, quietly, told her he was frightened for her. What she most fears is
that this is permanent. What she most hopes is that she might, one day, be able to explain what
happened — to finish the sentence.
