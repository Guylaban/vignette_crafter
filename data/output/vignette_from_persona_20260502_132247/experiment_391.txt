Boonsong Rattanakit is a sixty-year-old Thai-American fisherman who has lived on the Gulf Coast of
Louisiana for the better part of three decades. He came to the United States as a young man, built a
modest but stable life alongside his wife and a small community of Southeast Asian fishing families,
and found in the rhythms of the water something close to peace. That peace was shattered roughly
eight months ago when his younger brother, who had been working alongside him on the boat, was
pulled overboard during a sudden squall. Boonsong was at the helm when it happened. He saw his
brother go over the rail. By the time he had brought the vessel around and thrown the ring buoy, his
brother had already been taken under by the current. The Coast Guard recovered the body two days
later. Boonsong was on the dock when they brought him in.

In the weeks that followed, Boonsong returned to work because he did not know what else to do with
himself. The boat, the water, the early morning smell of diesel and brine — all of it had become a
landscape of unbearable association. When the hull pitched in choppy water, even mildly, his body
responded as though the squall had returned: his heart hammered, his hands went cold and rigid on
the wheel, his chest locked around a breath he could not release. These physical reactions were not
merely uncomfortable; they confirmed something he had already begun to believe. He had come to feel,
with a quiet but absolute conviction, that he was guilty of surviving — that he had been at the
helm, that he had not moved fast enough, that some essential failure of his had cost his brother his
life. This belief that he bore responsibility for the death, that he was in some fundamental sense
culpable, sat beneath every waking hour and fed directly into the state of vigilance his body seemed
unable to leave. The guilt and the shame kept the threat alive; his nervous system remained on
permanent alert, scanning the water, the weather, the faces of the other men on the dock, as though
catastrophe were always seconds away.

What made this worse — and what he found hardest to articulate to anyone, including his wife — was
that the physical alarm his body sounded each morning on the water seemed to confirm the appraisals
rather than simply accompany them. When his hands shook at the wheel, he interpreted this not as a
symptom of grief but as evidence: his body knew what he had done. The strong negative feelings — the
horror that surfaced without warning when he saw a life ring hanging on a dock post, the shame that
rose when a fellow fisherman mentioned his brother's name — these emotions felt to Boonsong like
verdicts rather than reactions. Each surge of fear or guilt reinforced his certainty that he was a
man who could not be trusted with the lives of others, and that the world had confirmed this.

Over the months, Boonsong had grown quieter and more withdrawn. He described feeling dead inside —
not sad exactly, but hollowed, as though the part of him that had once taken pleasure in the catch,
in his wife's cooking, in the company of the men he had known for decades, had simply stopped
functioning. He had come to believe that people, broadly, could not be trusted — not out of paranoia
exactly, but out of a kind of exhausted disillusionment, a sense that proximity to him carried risk.
He had pulled back from the small community of Thai and Vietnamese fishermen who had been his social
world, declining invitations, deflecting conversation. His wife had told him, carefully and more
than once, that she was frightened by how far away he seemed.

the room dark and unfamiliar for a disorienting moment. He sought help now because his wife had
finally said plainly that she could not watch him disappear. What he feared most, sitting in the
intake chair, was not the memories themselves but the possibility that the man he had been before
the squall was simply gone.
