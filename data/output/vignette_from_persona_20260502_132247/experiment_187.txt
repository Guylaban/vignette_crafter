Rangi Taumata is a 63-year-old Māori-American man, divorced, who works as a community organizer in a
mid-sized Pacific Northwest city where he coordinates neighborhood advocacy programs and liaises
regularly with municipal agencies. He was referred for psychological assessment following a life-
threatening accident approximately fourteen months ago in which he was struck by a vehicle at an
intersection while attempting to intervene in a heated altercation between a driver and a
pedestrian. The collision was sudden and severe; Rangi has fragmentary recollection of the moments
leading up to impact — the raised voices, the blur of a uniform on a nearby transit officer who had
not yet moved to intervene — and then almost nothing until he was on the ground, unable to speak,
with strangers standing over him. Some details remain grotesquely vivid: the particular angle of
afternoon light, the sensation of his cheek against the asphalt, a smell he has never been able to
name. Others are simply gone. He has tried many times to reconstruct the sequence of events and
found only silence where memory should be, and this incompleteness has become its own source of
suffering.

The fragmented quality of Rangi's memory has fed directly into a set of deeply troubling self-
appraisals. Because he cannot retrieve a continuous account of what happened, he has filled the gaps
with self-accusation — he had come to believe, with a conviction that resisted all counter-argument,
that the accident happened because of the way he acted, that his decision to step in was reckless,
that a wiser or more restrained man would have stayed back. The absence of clear memory does not
exonerate him in his own mind; if anything, the blanks feel like evidence of something he cannot
bear to see. This belief that he bears responsibility for what happened sits alongside a pervasive
sense of internal deadness. He describes feeling numb most of the time, as though the emotional
register that once made his advocacy work meaningful has simply gone offline. He is certain, in a
way he finds hard to articulate, that he is damaged in some permanent way — that his body keeps
reacting as if the danger is still present even when he is sitting quietly at his desk, and this
frightens him almost as much as the accident itself.

These appraisals, in turn, shape which moments in the present feel most threatening. Because Rangi
has concluded that his own conduct caused the accident, situations that resemble the conditions of
that day carry an amplified charge. Other people expressing anger — even in measured tones, even in
contexts entirely unrelated to violence — now register as precursors to catastrophe. In his work as
a community organizer, where raised voices in public meetings are not uncommon, this has become a
significant impairment. He has begun arriving late to avoid the opening exchanges of contentious
sessions and has delegated facilitation duties he once found energizing, telling colleagues he is
managing a heavy caseload. The sight of a uniform — a police officer at a community event, a
security guard in a municipal building — can stop him mid-sentence. These triggers do not merely
unsettle him in the moment; they pull him back into the fragmented sensory record of the accident,
activating the partial, wordless impressions that constitute his memory of it, so that the gaps and
the vividness arrive together, confirming his sense that something in him broke that afternoon and
has not repaired.

The loop has tightened steadily. When triggered, the rush of bodily alarm reinforces his belief that
he is permanently changed and culpable; that belief makes him more vigilant for the cues that
triggered it; and the memory's incompleteness — the parts he cannot find words for, the stretches he
simply cannot reach — sustains the self-blame by denying him any narrative that might contradict it.
He has grown more isolated over the past year, and his ex-wife, with whom he had maintained a civil
friendship, has noted that he seems unreachable.

What finally brought Rangi to seek help was a community meeting last month at which a resident
raised his voice at a city official. Rangi left the room, sat in his car for forty minutes, and
could not return. He is most afraid that this is simply who he is now. What he hopes, though he
holds the hope carefully, is that someone might help him find the words he has not been able to find
on his own.
