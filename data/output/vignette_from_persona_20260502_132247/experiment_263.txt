Cassandra is a thirty-year-old married woman of North American background who works as a bank teller
at a mid-sized branch in her city. She presented for psychological assessment with a PCL-5 score of
56, reporting a constellation of symptoms that have progressively narrowed her daily life over the
past two years. The traumatic event she disclosed was a period of prolonged captivity and torture,
during which she was held by an individual known to her who subjected her to systematic physical and
psychological abuse. Central to that experience was the perpetrator's habit of watching her in
silence before initiating harm — standing still, eyes fixed on her, saying nothing — so that silence
and the sensation of being observed became the reliable heralds of what was coming. He would express
displeasure in a low, controlled voice before escalating, and sexual violence was a recurring
feature of the abuse. By the time she escaped, her nervous system had been trained, with terrible
efficiency, to treat these particular signals as announcements of danger.

In the months following, Cassandra found that the memories did not stay in the past. They returned
without warning — a customer's steady gaze across the teller counter, a lull in the ambient noise of
the branch, a colleague raising his voice in mild frustration — and when they did, the return was
total. She has described it as every sense simultaneously dragged back: the smell of the room, the
quality of the light, the physical sensations of restraint. These intrusions arrive with such
overwhelming emotional force that she has had to excuse herself mid-transaction, retreating to the
staff bathroom until she could regulate her breathing. Because the memories carry this annihilating
quality, she has come to dread them as a second assault, and that dread has shaped almost every
adaptation she has made since.

The memories themselves sustain her sense of threat. When a flashback subsides, it leaves behind a
residue of alarm that does not fully clear — she wakes in the night to check the locks, to listen to
the silence of her house, which paradoxically intensifies rather than reassures her, because unusual
quiet has become, in her body's reckoning, the texture of imminent harm. She feels jumpy throughout
the day, startling at sounds her husband barely registers. This persistent state of physiological
threat, in turn, makes the memories more accessible: a body already primed for danger finds
confirmation everywhere, and the flashbacks come more readily.

In her attempts to manage this, Cassandra has developed a set of strategies that, over time, have
made things worse rather than better. She avoids eye contact with customers and colleagues — a habit
that began as self-protection but has left her feeling more isolated and conspicuous, more certain
she is being watched precisely because she cannot verify that she is not. She spends money
impulsively on her lunch breaks, buying things she does not need from the shops near the bank,
seeking the brief interruption of distraction; but the return to the branch afterwards, with its
rows of waiting faces and its patches of silence, reliably strips away whatever comfort she had
purchased. Most significantly, she goes over and over the details of the event in her mind,
convinced that if she can only find the logic in what was done to her she will stop feeling so
afraid. This rumination does not bring resolution — it keeps the memory vivid and close, ensuring
that the next trigger finds it ready, and that the physiological alarm it generates feeds directly
back into her threat monitoring. Her avoidance of eye contact and her compulsive reviewing of the
past have, together, kept her sensitised to exactly the stimuli she most fears: another person's
gaze, a controlled expression of anger, any approach toward intimacy with her husband, whom she
loves but from whom she has withdrawn in ways she cannot fully explain to him.

What finally brought Cassandra to seek help was a specific incident at work three weeks ago. A
customer raised his voice — not at her, but at a colleague nearby — and she found herself unable to
move, flooded and paralysed at her station, until a supervisor guided her out. She was humiliated.
She is afraid that she is losing her capacity to function, and afraid too that if she looks too
closely at what happened to her, she will not survive the looking. What she hopes, tentatively, is
that there is a way back to herself that does not require her to disappear entirely first.
