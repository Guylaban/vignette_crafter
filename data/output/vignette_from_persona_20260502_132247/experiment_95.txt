Linh Nguyen is a 42-year-old Vietnamese-American project manager, married, who was referred for
psychological assessment following a prolonged hospitalization eighteen months ago for septic shock
secondary to a perforated bowel. What began as a routine abdominal complaint escalated within hours
into a medical emergency; Linh spent eleven days in the intensive care unit, several of them sedated
and intubated, surrounded by the antiseptic smell of the ward, the cold of the IV fluids running
into her arm, and the constant presence of drips and needles puncturing her skin. She has described
lying in a bed she could not leave, uncertain whether she would survive, while her husband waited
outside a door she could not see. Her PCL-5 score of 67 places her presentation in the severe range,
and she arrived at the initial intake session visibly tense, speaking quickly, as though she had
rehearsed what she was willing to say.

The memory of those eleven days does not sit in Linh's mind as a coherent narrative. Certain moments
are rendered in almost unbearable clarity — the specific metallic smell of the ward, the sensation
of cold fluid entering her vein, the sight of a nurse adjusting a drip above her head — while entire
stretches of time are simply absent, blank spaces she cannot account for. She has described moments
when the full emotional weight of the experience crashes through her without any accompanying image,
a wave of terror and helplessness that arrives without warning and without context. This fragmented,
sensory quality of the memory means that ordinary life has become saturated with landmines: the
smell of antiseptic in a public restroom, a cold draft from an air conditioning vent at her office,
the sight of a needle in a colleague's desk drawer during a first-aid training session she could not
leave quickly enough. Each of these sensory fragments reaches back into the traumatic memory and
pulls it forward, flooding her with the fragmented, emotionally raw material of the ICU before she
has any chance to orient herself.

When triggers intrude — the cold, a smell, the sight of medical equipment — Linh's immediate
response is to attempt to suppress and erase the memory entirely, to push it back behind a wall she
has been constructing since she was discharged. She monitors her own thoughts with exhausting
vigilance, scanning for any sign that the intrusions are evidence she is losing her mind. She avoids
hospitals, ambulances, and any physical examination with a consistency that has become structurally
embedded in her life: she has not attended a single follow-up appointment since her discharge, has
declined her GP's requests for routine blood work, and recently told her husband she would not
accompany him to his own specialist visit. At work, where she manages large cross-functional teams,
she has delegated all tasks that might require her to visit the company's on-site medical suite.
These strategies bring momentary relief, but the suppression itself keeps the memory raw and
unprocessed — the blanks stay blank, the fragments stay jagged — and the avoidance of medical cues
ensures that the triggers retain their full potency, never losing their charge through exposure. The
thought monitoring, meanwhile, has convinced her that the very act of watching her own mind so
closely confirms that something is wrong with her.

What frightens Linh most, beneath the symptom management, is what she has come to believe the trauma
means. She is certain that her body has been permanently damaged — not merely by the illness itself,
but by what the illness revealed about her fragility — and this conviction colours every intrusion
and every blank in her memory as further proof of irreversible harm. She has come to believe she has
failed in what she considers her most important role: she is a mother of two children, and she is
certain she has been unable to be present for them in the way they need since her illness. The
memory fragments, arriving without narrative or resolution, feel to her like evidence that her life
has been destroyed rather than interrupted. Each maladaptive effort to contain or erase the
experience reinforces these appraisals — the avoidance confirms that the world is too dangerous to
re-enter; the failed suppression confirms she is not recovering as she should.

Linh sought help now because her youngest child, aged eight, asked her why she always flinched when
they passed an ambulance. She could not answer him. What she hopes for, tentatively and with
considerable doubt, is that she might one day be able to sit with her son in a doctor's waiting room
without feeling that she is dying again.
