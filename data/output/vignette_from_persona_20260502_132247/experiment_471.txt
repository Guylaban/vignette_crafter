Marcus Heller, a 52-year-old European-American film director, presented for psychological assessment
approximately eight months after sustaining serious injuries in a near-fatal automobile accident. He
had been driving home alone at night along a rain-slicked highway when a tyre blowout caused him to
lose control of the vehicle, which rolled twice before coming to rest in a drainage ditch. He was
conscious throughout, trapped in the dark interior for nearly forty minutes before emergency
services arrived, the only sounds the hiss of the engine, distant traffic, and the irregular creak
of the buckled chassis. He was ultimately discharged from hospital with physical injuries that
healed within weeks. What did not heal was everything else.

The memories Marcus carries from those forty minutes are not narrative. They are almost entirely
visual — a fractured windscreen lit white by a passing car's headlights, the dark ceiling of the
cabin inches from his face, his own hands pressed against the door that would not open. He has told
his partner, and later his intake clinician, that he cannot seem to put any of it into words in a
way that captures what it was. The images arrive without context or sequence, vivid and complete in
their detail, and when they do, his body responds as though the ditch is still around him. What the
memory reactivates most reliably is a state of acute physical alertness — a scanning, bracing
quality that does not easily switch off. He finds himself on guard against sounds in particular: the
sudden bark of a car horn, a door slamming on a film set, a colleague dropping equipment. These
sounds make him startle violently, in ways he knows are disproportionate, and the startle itself
prolongs the hypervigilant state rather than resolving it.

Marcus has come to believe, with quiet but firm conviction, that he is inadequate — that a more
capable man would have regained his footing by now. He describes himself as a lousy coper, and the
person he encounters in the mirror each morning feels unrecognisable to him: cautious, reactive,
diminished. These appraisals have a direct relationship to how he moves through the world. Because
he has concluded that he cannot trust his own responses, he has progressively organised his life
around avoiding the circumstances most likely to test them. He no longer works night shoots, which
once formed a significant portion of his professional practice. He avoids enclosed spaces with poor
lighting — editing suites, screening rooms, the kind of dark, quiet environments that are
structurally inseparable from his occupation. His producer has noticed. Projects have been
reassigned. The appraisal of inadequacy has, in this way, begun to generate its own evidence.

The avoidance, while providing short-term relief, has compounded the problem in ways Marcus is only
beginning to articulate. By withdrawing from the environments that trigger his symptoms, he has also
withdrawn from the conditions under which new learning might occur, and the memories remain as raw
and non-integrated as they were in the first weeks. He distracts himself with administrative work,
with reading, with anything that keeps him out of the sensory conditions that would bring the images
back — but the images return anyway, and each time they do, the threat feels confirmed. The
avoidance itself has become a signal: if he must organise his life so carefully around these
situations, then the situations must genuinely be dangerous, and he must genuinely be someone who
cannot handle them. This reasoning tightens the loop. The heightened vigilance he carries into every
working day — the scanning for unexpected sounds, the braced readiness — is both produced and
sustained by the strategies meant to contain it.

He also spends time, particularly in the evenings, turning the question of why this happened to him
over and over without resolution. It does not yield answers, but it occupies the space where sleep
might otherwise be.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a conversation with his partner, who told him gently
that she no longer recognised him either. He came to the first session hoping, above everything
else, to find his way back to the person he was before — the one who could walk onto a dark set and
feel at home there.
