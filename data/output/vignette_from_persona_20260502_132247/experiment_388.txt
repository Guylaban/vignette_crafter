Aroha Tane is a 33-year-old Māori woman working as a network engineer, recently divorced, who
presented for psychological assessment following what she described as months of feeling "wired and
raw" in ways she could no longer manage alone. The event that reorganised her inner life occurred
approximately two years ago, when her then seven-year-old son was admitted to the paediatric
intensive care unit following a rapid deterioration from what had initially appeared to be a routine
infection. Over the course of several days, Aroha sat beside monitors, watched nurses move with
sudden urgency, heard alarms fire without warning, and endured a succession of procedures performed
on her child's small body — cannulas inserted, blood drawn, a lumbar puncture conducted while she
waited outside a closed door. Her son survived. She has not, in any meaningful sense, recovered.

In the weeks following his discharge, Aroha noticed that her body had not received the news that the
crisis was over. She remained in a state of vigilance that felt less like anxiety and more like
readiness — a soldier who had been told to stand down but whose muscles refused the order. Inside
her own home, where she had once felt settled, she now moved through rooms with a low-level
alertness she could not switch off, checking on her son repeatedly during the night, listening for
changes in his breathing from the hallway. She had come to believe, in a way that felt entirely
rational to her, that danger had no reliable address — that it could arrive in a familiar place
without announcement. Unexpected movement nearby — a colleague stepping quickly around a corner at
the office, a contractor crossing her field of vision while she was mid-task at a server rack —
would trigger a sharp physical jolt: her heart rate spiking, her chest tightening, her hands
momentarily unreliable on the keyboard. These reactions embarrassed her and confirmed her fear that
something in her was permanently altered.

The connections between her triggers and this state of bodily alarm became self-sustaining in ways
that gradually narrowed her world. Certain people — her ex-husband, her son's paediatrician, even
her own mother, who had been present during the hospitalisation — carried an associative charge she
could not neutralise. Being around them, or anticipating contact with them, was enough to activate
the same physical reactivity she experienced in the hospital itself: the racing pulse, the
constriction in the chest, the sensation that something terrible was about to happen. Her ex-
husband's presence, in particular, had become almost unbearable; the arguments they had during those
hospital days — about decisions, about who had missed the early signs, about fault — meant that
conflict of any kind now landed in her body as physical threat. A raised voice in a meeting at work,
even a tense exchange over a ticketing system, could leave her shaking at her desk for twenty
minutes afterward.

This hyperarousal in turn made her more sensitised to the very triggers that produced it. Because
her body was already primed — already scanning — she encountered reminders everywhere, and each
encounter deepened the conviction that she was not safe. When her own GP referred her for a routine
blood draw last spring, she sat in the phlebotomy chair and felt the room tilt; the smell of the
antiseptic swab, the tourniquet on her arm, the small sharp entry of the needle — each sensation
arrived not as a minor discomfort but as a full-body re-entry into the PICU. She cancelled two
follow-up appointments and has not rescheduled them. At work, she has quietly restructured her
schedule to avoid a colleague who underwent a visible health crisis earlier this year, rerouting her
commute through the building to bypass the area where he sits. Her manager has noticed her
withdrawal from team meetings and raised it formally.

What finally brought Aroha to seek help was her son asking her, with the directness of a nine-year-
old, why she always looked scared. She is most afraid that she has become someone her child cannot
rely on to be calm. What she is most hoping for is to stop living as though the worst has not yet
happened — because she knows, intellectually, that it already did, and that he is here, and that she
is still waiting.
