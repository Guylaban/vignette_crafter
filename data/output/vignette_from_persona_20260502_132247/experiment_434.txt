Nadia Brennan is a thirty-one-year-old paramedic, recently divorced, who presented for an initial
intake following a referral from her department's employee assistance programme. Her PCL-5 score of
44 places her firmly within the clinical range for PTSD, and the picture that emerged across the
first two sessions is one of a woman whose professional competence has remained largely intact while
her inner life has been quietly dismantling itself for the better part of eighteen months.

The incident that precipitated her difficulties occurred during an off-duty evening when Nadia was
stopped by two officers responding to a reported disturbance near her apartment building. What began
as a routine interaction escalated when one officer, apparently mistaking her for a suspect, forced
her against a parked car and held her there by the back of the neck while the other restrained her
wrists. She was not charged. No explanation was offered. The physical contact — sudden, forceful,
and from behind — lasted less than two minutes, but it was those two minutes that seem to have
lodged most deeply. She remembers the cold metal of the car hood against her cheek, the weight of a
hand she had not seen coming, and the absolute certainty, in that moment, that she had no control
over what would happen to her body.

The memories of that night have never organised themselves into a coherent sequence. When Nadia
attempts to describe what happened — to her ex-husband in the weeks after, to the EAP counsellor who
made the referral — she loses the thread somewhere in the middle, the events fragmenting and
reordering themselves until she simply stops speaking. More distressing still is the quality of the
memories themselves: they do not feel like recollections of something past but like something still
occurring. This sense that the event is perpetually present, unresolved and immediate, appears to be
the engine driving much of her current threat perception. Because the memory never settles into the
past, her nervous system has never received the signal that the danger is over, and she moves
through her days in a state of constant low-grade vigilance — scanning the interior of every room
she enters, choosing seats in waiting areas and break rooms that place her back against a wall,
reacting with sharp alarm whenever a colleague or patient touches her without warning.

That hypervigilance has, in turn, shaped the strategies Nadia has built around herself. She avoids
any setting where she might encounter uniformed authority figures, which has created a specific and
concrete problem: her station houses a liaison officer who attends weekly safety briefings, and
Nadia has called in sick on four of the last seven occasions that briefing was scheduled. Her
supervisor has noticed. She also works to suppress any anger she feels — she described this with
some precision, saying she does not permit herself to feel it, let alone express it — and she avoids
anything that carries sensory or contextual resemblance to the officer involved, including a
particular brand of jacket she has seen on colleagues. These strategies have not brought relief. The
suppression of anger and the systematic avoidance of cues keep the original memory sealed off from
any processing that might contextualise or integrate it, so it remains raw and present-tense,
perpetuating the very sense of ongoing threat that made avoidance feel necessary in the first place.
The threat monitoring, meanwhile, generates its own confirmatory logic: every startle response to an
unexpected touch, every careful calculation about where to sit, reads to Nadia as evidence that the
world remains dangerous, which floods the memory back with renewed urgency.

The somatic dimension is worth noting. Nadia reports that stress and the physical sensation of
nausea can each pull her back into the emotional register of that night, the body apparently
registering threat before the mind has caught up.

What finally brought her in was not a single crisis but an accumulation: the missed briefings, a
growing distance from her crew, and a moment last month when a patient reached for her arm and she
flinched so visibly that a colleague asked if she was all right. She was not, she realised, and had
not been for some time. What she most fears is that something essential in her has been permanently
altered. What she most hopes is that she can be a paramedic again without bracing herself every time
someone needs her hands.
