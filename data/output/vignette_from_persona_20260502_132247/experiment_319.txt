Aroha Tane is a thirty-year-old Māori woman who works as a parole officer in a mid-sized Pacific
Northwest city. She came to the clinic presenting with a PCL-5 score of 48, describing a
constellation of intrusive and avoidant experiences that she had been quietly managing — or failing
to manage — for the better part of two years. Her trauma occurred during a humanitarian deployment
to a conflict zone in the Middle East, where she had volunteered as a relief worker in her late
twenties, a decision she had made with idealism and a deep sense of cultural obligation to service.
What she encountered there reshaped her in ways she is only beginning to articulate.

During her third month in the field, Aroha was stationed at a distribution centre that came under
sustained attack. The hours that followed involved the deaths of civilians she had been working
alongside, including children she had been feeding that morning from communal pots of a lentil-based
stew — a dish she had eaten dozens of times since arriving, something that had come to feel almost
like comfort. She remembers the smell of it, the particular warmth of the bowl in her hands, and
then a rupture so violent and disorienting that the memory of what followed is, in her own words,
simply not there. She cannot say how she got out. She cannot say how long she was in the rubble. She
knows she survived, and she knows others did not, and between those two facts is a space she cannot
enter.

The gaps in her memory are not experienced as relief. Aroha describes them as a source of profound
unease — she is aware that something happened in the missing time, and the not-knowing feels worse,
she says, than knowing could. When she has tried, in quieter moments, to deliberately reconstruct
the sequence of events, she finds the memory inaccessible in a way that feels almost physical, as
though she is reaching for something in a darkened room and her hand keeps passing through air. More
often, she does not try at all. She has noticed, with some clinical self-awareness given her
professional background, that she tends to push the memory away before it can fully surface — a
reflex that operates faster than intention, leaving her with fragments and a vague sense of having
narrowly avoided something.

The triggers that have disrupted her daily life are specific and, to her, bewildering in their
ordinariness. She can no longer eat lentils, or anything with a similar earthy, slightly smoky taste
— a dietary restriction she has not fully explained to anyone, including her ex-husband, from whom
she separated in the year following her return. At work, where her role as a parole officer places
her in regular contact with individuals who are frustrated, volatile, or openly hostile, she finds
herself acutely sensitised to expressions of anger. Even when a parolee raises his voice only
slightly, or when a colleague speaks sharply in a meeting, something in Aroha's nervous system
responds as though the anger is a precursor to catastrophe. She has become skilled at appearing
composed in these moments, but the internal experience is one of bracing, of waiting. It costs her
considerably, and she has begun taking longer routes between appointments and scheduling fewer in-
person meetings than her caseload technically requires.

The third trigger is perhaps the most quietly painful. Reminders of the victims — a news photograph
of a refugee camp, a child's drawing on a parolee's refrigerator, a particular quality of afternoon
light — arrive without warning and carry a weight that is difficult to name, partly because the
memories themselves remain so fragmented. She grieves people she cannot fully remember, which she
finds disorienting and, at times, shameful.

What finally brought Aroha to seek help was a moment at work last month when a parolee's mild
frustration caused her to leave the room abruptly and sit in her car for forty minutes, unable to
re-enter the building. She is not afraid, she says, of falling apart. She is afraid of what it means
that she already has, and that no one has noticed.
