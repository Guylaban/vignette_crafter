Martin is a 68-year-old European-American psychologist who presented for intake following a period
of sustained psychological deterioration he could no longer manage alone. He is married,
professionally accomplished, and by his own account had spent decades helping others process trauma
— a fact that now carries a particular, painful irony. The abuse he suffered at the hands of a
former female partner, years before his current marriage, had never been addressed clinically. What
brought him finally to seek help was a near-collapse at work: he had frozen entirely during a
session with a female client in a small consulting room, convinced, for several seconds, that he was
in danger.

The abuse occurred over the course of a relationship Martin has described in fragments rather than
as a coherent narrative. What he can report comes not as a story but as a series of arrested images
— a hand raised, a particular jacket, a bicycle leaning against a wall outside the apartment where
the worst incidents occurred. He cannot tell a clear story of what happened even to himself; the
chronology dissolves when he attempts it, and what remains are these photographic flashes, vivid and
static, like stills from a film with no connecting footage. At other times there is no image at all,
only the full emotional weight of what happened arriving without warning — a wave of shame or terror
with no picture attached. This fragmented memory structure means the past has never been processed
into something bounded and finished. It continues to feel present, and that felt-present quality
keeps Martin's nervous system oriented toward threat even in objectively safe environments.

The triggers that activate this threat response are specific and, once understood in context, grimly
logical. The former partner rode a bicycle; she wore clothes of a particular style; she would corner
him in crowded social situations where his distress would be invisible. Today, the sight of a
bicycle on his commute or parked outside his office building is enough to spike his alertness to a
level that takes hours to subside. Being alone with a woman — a colleague, a client, even a
neighbour — carries a charge he cannot fully suppress. Crowded spaces, with their press of bodies
and noise, reproduce the overstimulation of those social occasions where he felt trapped and
helpless. These triggers do not merely startle him; they actively summon his negative appraisals of
the world and of himself, so that encountering a bicycle is also, in the same moment, an encounter
with the belief that people are not what they seem and that he, a man who should have known better,
is fundamentally inadequate. The triggers and the appraisals feed each other: his conviction that
the world is dangerous sharpens his sensitivity to the triggers, so that he notices bicycles and
crowded rooms before he notices almost anything else.

Martin's sense of threat is not confined to waking hours. He wakes during the night to check whether
he is safe, scanning the bedroom in the dark before he can allow himself to return to sleep. He
finds the night qualitatively more dangerous than the day, and watches people closely in any social
setting for signs that they might harm him. This state of hypervigilance is itself a maintaining
force: the sustained alertness keeps traumatic material close to the surface, making intrusive
images and bodyless emotional surges more likely, which in turn confirms to Martin that the danger
is real and ongoing. The threat he monitors for is not merely external; it is the threat of his own
memory breaking through.

To manage all of this, Martin has built a set of strategies that feel like protection but function
as a cage. He avoids wearing clothes similar to those he wore during the abuse. He tries hard to
control his emotions at all times, and he never allows himself to feel or express anger — a
prohibition so total that he experiences any flicker of it as evidence of his own inadequacy. These
strategies prevent the emotional processing that might allow the memory to become a coherent story;
by suppressing and controlling, he keeps the fragments frozen, which means the triggers remain
potent, the threat remains unresolved, and the appraisals remain intact. His professional
relationships have narrowed as a result: he has quietly restructured his caseload to avoid one-on-
one sessions with women where possible, a change his colleagues have noticed without understanding.

What Martin most fears is that the damage to his beliefs — that the world is legible, that people
can be trusted, that he is competent — is permanent. What he most hopes, though he says it with
evident difficulty, is that someone else might be able to hold the story together long enough for
him to finally hear it as something that happened, rather than something still happening.
