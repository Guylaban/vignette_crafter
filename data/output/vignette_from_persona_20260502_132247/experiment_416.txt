Marguerite is a 73-year-old married woman of European descent who works as a QA engineer — a role
she has held for decades and one that, in its demand for precision and the detection of error,
reflects something of the vigilance she has carried since childhood. She was referred for
psychological assessment following a significant deterioration in her functioning over the past
year, presenting with a PCL-5 score of 74, indicative of severe posttraumatic stress. The trauma at
the centre of her presentation is not recent. It reaches back to her earliest years, when the man
responsible for her care — her father — was also the source of sustained physical abuse. He was
unpredictable in the way that only caregivers can be: capable of warmth one hour and violence the
next, his moods arriving without warning. Marguerite learned early to read the room, to watch for
the shift in his posture, the angle of his approach, the quality of silence before something broke.
She did not always read it correctly. She was a child, and she could not stop what happened.

Now, at 73, she finds that the past is not as distant as the calendar suggests. Certain moments in
her present life carry the texture of those early years with an immediacy that frightens her. Being
alone with a man — a colleague staying late in the office, a repairman in the house — activates
something in her body that precedes any conscious thought: her muscles tighten, her breathing
shallows, her eyes track movement before her mind has registered why. Sudden movements by others,
even benign ones, put her immediately on guard in a way she cannot override by reasoning. At work,
where she spends long hours reviewing systems for flaw and failure, she has become increasingly
difficult to be around. Colleagues have noticed her flinching, her hyperalert scanning of the room,
the way she positions herself near exits. A recent team restructuring placed her under a new male
supervisor, and she has since requested remote assignments with a persistence that has strained her
professional relationships. The occupational environment that once gave her a sense of control has
become a daily negotiation with threat.

When these triggers fire, they do not simply raise her alertness — they pull the past into the
present with a specificity that unnerves her. Marguerite describes her memories of the abuse as
photographic: not a blur or a narrative, but a series of still images, each one precise and lit with
terrible clarity. The angle of a raised arm. The particular shadow of a doorway. She can look at
these images in her mind as though they are pinned to a board, and yet at other times the same
memories arrive with a strange emotional flatness, as though they happened to someone else entirely
— a dissociation she finds almost more disturbing than the vividness, because it makes her feel she
cannot fully claim her own history. She knows what happened. She cannot find words for it. When she
has tried to explain it to her husband, she trails into silence, not from unwillingness but from a
genuine failure of language, as though the experience exists in a register that speech cannot reach.

The memories, when they surface, do not arrive neutrally. They carry with them a set of conclusions
she has come to hold about herself and her life. She believes she was betrayed by the person most
obligated to protect her, and this conviction has calcified into a broader certainty that she cannot
stop bad things from happening to her — that harm is a condition of her existence rather than an
event that occurred. She has never forgiven herself for not doing more to stop what happened as a
child, a guilt that reason cannot dissolve. These appraisals, in turn, lower her threshold for
perceiving threat in the present: the world feels dangerous because she has always been unable to
defend herself within it, and so she watches, and waits, and braces. The heightened threat
perception then confirms what she already believes about her helplessness, feeding back into the
guilt and the sense of betrayal in a cycle she has not yet named but clearly feels tightening.

Funerals and grief-related events have become intolerable for her — she has declined two family
memorial services this year — and uncertainty of any kind, even minor ambiguity at work, provokes a
disproportionate dread she struggles to explain. What finally brought her to seek help was her
husband's quiet observation, offered without reproach, that she had not laughed in a very long time.
What she fears most is that this is simply who she has become. What she hopes, tentatively, is that
it isn't.
