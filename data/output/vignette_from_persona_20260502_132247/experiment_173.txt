Priya is a sixty-six-year-old South Asian woman, widowed for several years, who works part-time at a
home goods retail store in a mid-sized American city. She presented for psychological assessment
following what she described, haltingly, as "something that happened to me that I cannot put away."
Her PCL-5 score of 63 places her in the severe range, and the picture that emerged across intake and
early sessions is one of a woman whose inner world has been quietly reorganising itself around a
single catastrophic event for months.

Approximately eight months before seeking help, Priya was raped by an acquaintance in the back room
of a building she had entered alone with him, trusting that she was safe. The assault took place in
a confined space that smelled strongly of cigarette smoke, and it unfolded with a quality of
wrongness that she still cannot fully articulate — not only the violation itself but the way he
spoke to her afterward, as though nothing had occurred, as though she owed him her composure. She
drove herself home. She told no one for weeks.

The memories of that evening do not come to Priya as a narrative. They arrive as a series of still
images — what she describes as photographs she cannot stop looking at, each one fixed and
overexposed: his face at a particular angle, the texture of the wall she stared at, the arrangement
of objects on a shelf. Some moments are searingly clear in this photographic way, while others are
simply absent, blank frames where something must have happened but nothing remains. She has come to
experience these snapshot memories as a form of ambush, because they arrive not when she chooses to
reflect but when her environment reaches into the past and pulls them forward.

Smell is the most reliable trigger. Priya works on the floor of a retail store, and on days when a
colleague steps outside to smoke and returns carrying the scent on their clothes, she finds herself
suddenly not in the store at all — she is back in that room, looking at one of her photographs. The
triggered memory then floods her with a sense of deep injustice, a hot and wordless certainty that
something monstrous was done to her and that no one has answered for it. She reported that her
employer does not know why, on certain afternoons, she goes very still at the register and has to be
spoken to twice.

Being alone with men produces the same cascade. When a male customer lingers near her section or a
male manager asks her to stay behind after a shift, the proximity itself functions as a warning, and
the warning summons the images. The triggered memories, in turn, confirm what she has come to
believe: that the world is not safe, that men cannot be trusted, and that she was foolish to have
believed otherwise. This belief has begun to precede the triggers rather than simply follow them —
she now scans every male customer who enters the store before he has done anything at all, and that
vigilance keeps her nervous system in a state that makes the next trigger more likely to land.

Priya's appraisals of herself and her situation have hardened into something that feels, to her,
simply like realism. She is certain she cannot manage even small frustrations — a mislabelled price,
a rude customer — without her composure crumbling, and this perceived fragility makes her dread the
ordinary provocations of her job. Witnessing even minor social transgressions, someone cutting in
line or speaking rudely to a cashier, strikes her as unbearable now, not only because it is
unpleasant but because moral violation of any kind pulls her back toward the central one, the one
that went unanswered. She had come to believe that nothing in her life held meaning anymore — not
her work, not her routines, not the small pleasures that once sustained her as a widow living alone
— and this hollowness makes her less equipped to tolerate the daily encounters that trigger her,
which in turn deepen her conviction that she is broken beyond repair.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a conversation with her daughter, who noticed that her
mother had stopped attending the weekly temple gatherings she had kept for thirty years. Priya came
to the first session saying very little about what she wanted, but at the end she said, quietly,
that she wanted to know whether what happened to her would be wrong forever, or only for now.
