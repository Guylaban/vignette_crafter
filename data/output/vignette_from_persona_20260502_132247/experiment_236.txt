and traumatic death of a loved one. He was referred for psychological assessment after his wife
expressed concern about changes in his personality and functioning over the preceding months. Gerald
arrived at the intake appointment quietly dressed, speaking in measured tones, his hands —
accustomed to shaping clay — resting still in his lap in a way that seemed unfamiliar even to him.

The loss came without warning. Gerald had not had the chance to prepare, to say what needed saying,
or to move through any of the ordinary rituals of anticipatory grief. The death arrived as a
rupture, and in its aftermath Gerald found that the world continued turning in a way that felt
obscene. Friends sent food. His wife tried to talk. The studio, where he had spent thirty years with
his hands in wet earth, remained as it had always been — and that, somehow, was the worst of it. The
smell of the clay, the particular grey light through the workshop windows on a winter morning, the
sound of the wheel — all of it had once been saturated with a shared life, with conversations and
visits and the easy presence of someone who was now gone. To return to work was to walk directly
into that absence.

What Gerald developed in the months that followed was a set of strategies that felt, to him, not
like avoidance but like survival. He had come to believe, in a way he could not fully articulate but
felt with absolute conviction, that positive emotion was dangerous — that to feel pleasure or warmth
or even ordinary contentment was to betray the dead, or worse, to lower his guard against a world
that had already shown it could take everything without notice. When his wife laughed at something
on television and he felt the reflex of his own smile begin, he pressed it down. He described this
in session not as a choice but as a reflex of protection, a sealing off. Joy, in his current
understanding of the world, was simply the leading edge of future devastation.

His avoidance of grief reminders had reshaped his daily life in ways that were increasingly
difficult to conceal. Gerald had stopped entering the room in his home where certain photographs
were kept. He had rerouted his morning walk to avoid passing a particular café. Most significantly,
he had found himself unable to work with a specific line of ceramic pieces — vessels he had been
developing for years — because the design had been shaped in conversation with the person he lost.
The studio, once the center of his identity and livelihood, had become a place he entered only when
necessary, moving through it with a kind of deliberate blankness, avoiding the corner where those
unfinished pieces still sat under their cloth. A commission he had accepted months before the death
remained incomplete, and a gallery relationship he had cultivated for years was quietly fraying.

Perhaps most telling was Gerald's complete withdrawal from any thought of the future. He had stopped
making plans — not in the dramatic sense, but in the small, accumulative sense that constitutes a
life. He no longer booked ahead for anything. He had declined an invitation to a residency he would
previously have welcomed. When his wife mentioned a trip they had discussed for years, he changed
the subject. He had come to feel that planning was a form of presumption — that to imagine a future
was to invite the same kind of sudden erasure that had already found him once. The future, in his
mind, was simply where loss lived.

What finally brought Gerald to seek help was his wife's quiet statement, one evening, that she felt
she had lost him too. He had not cried when she said it. But he had made the appointment the
following morning. What he most fears is that the person he was before the loss is also gone. What
he most hopes, though he would not use this word, is that it might be possible to return to the
wheel.
