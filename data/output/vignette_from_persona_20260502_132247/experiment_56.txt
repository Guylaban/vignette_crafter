Ingrid is a 73-year-old divorced woman of European descent who has spent the better part of four
decades working as a forester in the Pacific Northwest. She lives alone in a small house at the edge
of a managed woodland, a life she built deliberately around solitude and trees — the kind of
company, she has said, that does not raise its hand against you. She came to therapy recently,
reluctantly, after a younger female colleague found her sitting motionless on a fallen log in the
middle of a work shift, unable to explain why she could not move or speak. That incident, Ingrid
said quietly at intake, was not the first time. It was simply the first time someone had witnessed
it.

The trauma that organises Ingrid's inner life reaches back to her earliest years. She was raised in
a household where her primary caregiver — her mother — was the source of sustained physical abuse.
The violence was not chaotic; it was methodical and quiet. Her mother would enter a room without
warning and the silence that preceded her arrival became indistinguishable, in Ingrid's nervous
system, from the blow itself. The particular quality of absolute stillness — the sudden drop in
ambient sound before something happened — became encoded as the event, not merely its precursor.
Decades later, in the forest, when the birdsong stops and the wind drops and the trees go completely
still, Ingrid feels it in her chest before she feels it anywhere else: a cold certainty that
something is coming for her. The sounds associated with those early years — a particular cadence of
footsteps, the creak of a door, a woman's low voice in an adjacent room — carry the same charge. And
because her work sometimes requires her to coordinate with female colleagues, proximity to women,
particularly when alone with them in a remote section of the forest, collapses the distance between
then and now entirely. She has come to believe, with a conviction she cannot argue herself out of,
that you can never truly know who will harm you — that the capacity for cruelty is distributed
invisibly among people, and that women in particular represent a threat she cannot assess in
advance. This appraisal does not feel like a belief to Ingrid; it feels like knowledge, the kind
earned through the body. And it is precisely this sense of permanent, unlocatable danger that keeps
her triggers so raw: when the world feels fundamentally unsafe, every silence and every female
presence carries the potential weight of the original harm.

Over the years, Ingrid developed ways of managing what she could not escape. She stopped exercising
— the physical exertion, the vulnerability of a body pushed to its limits, felt intolerable, too
close to the helplessness she had known as a child. She arranged her schedule to avoid being alone
in enclosed spaces, lingering in the forest equipment shed long past the end of her shift simply
because other workers were still present, then driving to a shopping centre rather than returning to
an empty house. The compulsive spending began gradually — small purchases at first, things she did
not need, but the transaction itself provided a brief, reliable interruption in the dread. Over
months it became a pattern she could not easily stop, and the debt it generated became its own
source of shame. These strategies, taken together, have not quieted the fear; they have confirmed
it. Each act of avoidance tells her body that the threat is real and serious enough to flee, and
each flight makes the next trigger more potent. The spending, the rerouted routines, the refusal to
let the body move freely — all of it has narrowed her life to a set of managed distances, and in
managing those distances she has come to feel that the trauma has taken everything from her that
mattered: her ease in her own body, her capacity for ordinary companionship, the decades she might
have lived differently. She describes herself, with a flatness that is more disturbing than
distress, as feeling like an object that has been used and set aside — not a person who has
suffered, but a thing that was acted upon.

What finally brought her in was not the incident in the forest, exactly. It was the realisation,
sitting on that log, that she was 73 years old and still waiting for her mother to come through the
door. What she fears most is that it is too late. What she hopes, almost against her will, is that
it is not.
