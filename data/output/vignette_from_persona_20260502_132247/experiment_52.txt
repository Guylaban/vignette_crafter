the same stretch of farmland in California's Central Valley for most of her adult life. She was
referred for psychological assessment following a disclosure she made to her primary care physician,
whom she had visited complaining of chronic sleep difficulties and what she described as a "broken
nervous system." Her PCL-5 score of 72 places her presentation in the severe range, and the picture
that emerged across the intake and early sessions is one of a woman whose inner world has been
fundamentally reorganised around a single catastrophic event.

Approximately eight months ago, Priya was attacked and raped by a man who had followed her to a
remote section of the fields where she was working alone in the early morning. She had heard a dog
barking somewhere behind the tree line in the moments before the assault began — a sound she had not
understood as warning until it was too late. The attack was prolonged, and she was left on the
ground unable to call for help for some time afterward. When she was eventually found by a co-
worker, she did not tell him what had happened. She told no one for nearly three months.

The memory of the event did not recede. Instead it took on a quality she described as unbearable:
not something she recalled, but something that replayed. She said it was like a film she could not
switch off, running in full from beginning to end, and when it came, it did not feel like
remembering — it felt like being there again, her body responding as though the assault were
actively occurring. The replaying was total and involuntary, and it left her disoriented and
exhausted in ways she struggled to explain to her husband.

What made daily life increasingly untenable was how little warning she received before the memory
returned. Any element of her surroundings that echoed the morning of the attack could instantly
restore the full sensory weight of it. The farm, which had been her livelihood and in many ways her
identity for decades, became a landscape of ambush. The sound of a dog — common on the rural
properties surrounding the fields — became the most reliable and devastating of these triggers. When
a dog barked anywhere near her worksite, the memory arrived without transition, and with it came an
immediate physiological cascade: her heart rate surged, her muscles locked, her breathing shortened
before she had formed a conscious thought about danger. Her body, she told her clinician, knew
before she did. This pattern — in which the trigger activated a full threat response faster than any
deliberate reasoning could intervene — meant that her sense of safety at work had collapsed
entirely. She began arriving later in the morning, avoiding the far sections of the fields, and
eventually asking to be assigned only to tasks near the main building where other workers were
visible. Her supervisor noticed the change. She offered no explanation.

At home, the threat vigilance followed her. She lay awake each night telling herself she needed to
remain alert, that sleep was a form of surrender she could not afford. When her husband reached for
her in the dark — even a hand on her shoulder — she startled violently, pulling away before she
registered who had touched her. She had come to believe that her body was permanently rewired for
danger, that the capacity for ordinary rest or ordinary closeness had been taken from her. Sexual
intimacy, once unremarkable in her long marriage, had become something she could not approach
without the memory reasserting itself. She had not explained this to her husband, and the distance
between them had grown in ways neither had named aloud.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a morning when a neighbour's dog ran loose near the farm
entrance and she found herself unable to move or speak for several minutes afterward, standing in
