Kareem Haddad is a 78-year-old environmental scientist of Lebanese descent who has been married to
his wife Nadia for over fifty years. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 70 and was referred for
psychological assessment following a disclosure to his primary care physician that he had not slept
through the night in several months. What Kareem had not yet told anyone in full was the reason why.

Approximately eight months ago, Kareem was babysitting his youngest grandchild, a four-year-old boy
named Sami, at the family home. It was an ordinary afternoon. Sami was laughing and running through
the backyard, and Kareem felt the particular warmth of those hours — unhurried, domestic, full of
small joy. He was carrying Sami down the back porch steps when he misjudged the final step. He fell
forward, and Sami, held against his chest, took the full weight of the impact. The child suffered a
fractured collarbone and a concussion. Sami recovered fully within weeks, but Kareem has not
recovered at all.

What makes Kareem's memory of that afternoon so difficult to approach is its quality as much as its
content. He describes the event as feeling, even now, not entirely real — as though it happened to
someone else, or in a story he was told rather than a life he lived. He says he knows what occurred,
knows the sequence of it, but when he tries to speak about it, the words dissolve. He will sit in
session and open his mouth and find nothing there — no language adequate to what he saw in Sami's
face, to the sound the child made, to the weight of him going still. This speechlessness is not
confusion; it is something more like the mind's refusal to fully metabolise what happened. And yet,
paradoxically, when a trigger reaches him, the distance collapses entirely. He does not remember the
fall so much as re-enter it — the present moment stripped away, replaced by the porch steps, the
afternoon light, the weight of his grandson.

The triggers that carry this power are precise and, on the surface, unlikely. Heavy breathing — the
sound of exertion, of sleep, of a child running — is the most potent of them. In his current work as
a retired environmental scientist who still consults part-time for a regional conservation agency,
Kareem attends occasional field meetings and site visits. During one such visit last spring, a
younger colleague walked briskly up a hillside beside him, breathing hard from the climb. The sound
reached Kareem before he could prepare for it, and within seconds he was no longer on the hillside.
He excused himself and sat alone by the vehicle for forty minutes. He has declined two field visits
since then.

Perhaps more painful is what has happened to moments of happiness and physical affection. Kareem had
come to understand, in the months since the accident, that feeling relaxed or content was itself a
kind of danger — because it was precisely that state of ease, that unguarded afternoon warmth, that
preceded the fall. Joy, for him, now carries the texture of a warning. When Nadia reaches for his
hand or leans into him, or when one of his grandchildren — including Sami, now fully healed and
oblivious — runs to embrace him, the physical contact does not bring comfort. It brings the porch
steps. His arms tighten around the child and simultaneously he is back there, and the memory floods
in with the full conviction that it is happening again, now, to this child, in his arms.

The loop this creates is quiet but relentless. The triggers reach him in the moments he is most open
— in affection, in contentment, in the ordinary sounds of people near him — and each time they do,
the memory reasserts itself with a vividness that makes the past feel more real than the present.

What finally brought Kareem to seek help was Nadia noticing that he had begun to flinch when she
embraced him. He is most afraid, he says, that he will never be able to hold his grandson without
feeling that he is, in some essential way, still falling.
