Marisol is a 55-year-old divorced Latina woman who works as a bank teller in a mid-sized branch in
suburban Texas. She presents for psychological evaluation reporting significant distress that she
has been managing largely in silence for several years, distress rooted in a period of her life that
most of her colleagues and customers would never suspect: her service as a military medic during a
combat deployment in her late twenties. It was during that deployment that Marisol witnessed and
physically participated in the aftermath of an improvised explosive device strike that killed two
soldiers she had been treating in the field. She remembers — or tries to remember — kneeling in the
dirt, her hands pressing through the specific texture of shredded fabric and blood-soaked material
against skin and bone, the concussive ring of the blast still vibrating in her ears, the faces of
the men around her contorted in expressions she could not then, and cannot now, fully read. The loss
was immediate and total. She came home, divorced within three years, and told almost no one what she
had seen.

What makes Marisol's presentation particularly complex is the fractured quality of the memory
itself. She cannot reconstruct the event in any reliable sequence — she knows things happened, but
the order shifts and collapses when she reaches for it, and when she tries to explain what occurred
to her therapist, she loses the thread mid-sentence and falls silent, sometimes for minutes at a
time. The memory feels, in her own words, "like something that happened to someone else in a movie,"
so extreme and so severed from her ordinary life that she struggles to accept it as her own history.
Yet the very incoherence of the memory seems to keep it volatile: because she cannot place it firmly
in the past, fragments surface without warning, and those surfacing fragments become triggers in
their own right — a half-formed image of fabric, a sound, a phrase — pulling her back into the
physiological state of the event itself.

The triggers she encounters are specific and, given her daily environment, nearly unavoidable. At
the bank, the pneumatic transaction tubes occasionally produce a sharp percussive sound when they
seal, and on two occasions a car backfiring in the parking lot has sent Marisol's heart rate
surging, her hands gripping the counter. She has described a near-paralysing physical reaction —
chest tightening, limbs going cold — that she cannot suppress and that she is certain her customers
notice. More quietly devastating is the texture trigger: handling certain worn or rough-fibred
currency, the kind that has passed through many hands and feels almost organic, produces a visceral
recognition she cannot name aloud. When these triggers fire, they do not merely startle her — they
pull the fragmented memory forward, flooding her with the sensory residue of the field, and in those
moments the world of the bank branch collapses into the world of the blast. Equally, when a customer
at the window begins talking about losing someone — a parent, a child, a spouse — Marisol feels the
conversation reach into her chest, and the grief of those two soldiers she could not save rises
immediately, transforming a routine transaction into something she can barely survive.

These moments of activation have steadily shaped how Marisol moves through the world. She has come
to believe, with quiet certainty, that the world is a dangerous place and that her only rational
response is permanent vigilance. She studies the faces of every customer who approaches her window,
reading microexpressions for signs of hostility or concealed threat, and she scans the bank floor
and the parking lot visible through the glass with the systematic attention of someone who genuinely
expects harm. This hypervigilance, which she experiences not as a symptom but as a necessary
survival strategy, feeds back into her threat sensitivity — every ambiguous face or unfamiliar sound
becomes evidence confirming her belief, and the confirmation sharpens her scanning, which in turn
makes her more likely to register the next trigger as dangerous. The cycle has narrowed her life
considerably: she no longer socialises after work, has declined two promotions that would have moved
her to a busier branch, and describes a pervasive sense that nothing she does or plans carries any
meaning anymore. The loss of meaning is not dramatic — it is flat and grey, the absence of
anticipation.

What finally brought Marisol to seek help was a morning three weeks ago when a customer's young son
dropped a book on the tile floor with a sharp crack, and she found herself crouched behind the
counter before she was aware of having moved. A supervisor asked if she was all right. She said yes.
She made an appointment the following day. What she most fears is that the memory will never cohere
into something she can live with. What she most hopes, though she offers this quietly and without
much conviction, is that she might one day stand at her window without scanning every face for the
danger she is certain is coming.
