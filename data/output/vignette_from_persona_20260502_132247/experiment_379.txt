Marcus is a 49-year-old architect, married, who presented for his initial assessment carrying the
particular stillness of a man who has spent months trying not to draw attention to himself. He was
referred following a significant decline in his occupational functioning and a recent disclosure to
his wife that he had not slept more than a few consecutive hours in over a year. His PCL-5 score of
52 places him firmly in the severe range, and the picture that emerged across the first two sessions
was one of a man whose inner world had been fundamentally reorganised by something he saw — not
something that happened to him, a distinction he returns to with something close to shame.

Fourteen months ago, Marcus was on a site visit to a mixed-use development project in a
neighbourhood undergoing rapid redevelopment. He was walking through a partially completed ground-
floor corridor when he witnessed a violent assault — a sustained, close-range attack on a man he did
not know, carried out by two others. The smell of the space — concrete dust, something metallic, a
sharp chemical note from nearby adhesive work — saturated the experience. He was close enough to
feel nauseous, close enough that the details became involuntary and total. He did not intervene. He
pressed himself against the wall and watched until the assailants left, and by the time he reached
for his phone his hands were shaking badly enough that he misdialled twice.

In the weeks that followed, the memory refused to settle into the past. Marcus found he could not
reconstruct the event in any reliable sequence — he could not say with confidence what happened
first, what happened after — and when he tried to describe it to the police or later to his wife,
the words simply were not there. He knew what he had witnessed; he could not render it in language.
This fragmentation meant that when memories surfaced they did not arrive as recollections but as re-
immersions: the smell of adhesive on a job site, or the particular quality of nausea he had felt,
could collapse the distance between then and now entirely, so that he was not remembering the
corridor but back inside it. These sensory triggers — a related smell, a wave of nausea, anything
that carried even a distant echo of violence — became the fault lines of his daily life, and each
time one activated, the fragmented memory surged forward with it, uncontrolled and present-tense.

The nature of that memory — its unprocessed, here-and-now quality — fed directly into a state of
near-constant threat perception. Marcus had come to understand the world as a place in which
violence could arrive without warning in any enclosed space, and his nervous system appeared to have
accepted this as operational fact. He began positioning himself with his back to the wall in every
room he entered — a habit that had become automatic — and scanning for exits the moment he crossed
any threshold. His work as an architect now placed him daily in the very environments that most
activated this vigilance: building sites, open-plan offices, client presentation rooms, corridors
under construction. What had once been his professional domain had become a landscape of threat
assessments. He was jumpy in ways his junior colleagues had begun to notice, flinching at unexpected
sounds, and this hypervigilance in turn kept him in a state of perpetual readiness that made the
next trigger easier to find and harder to dismiss. The threat he perceived in any given room
sharpened his attention to exactly the sensory details — smells, sounds, physical sensations — most
likely to return him to the assault, and the assault, once activated, confirmed that the threat had
been real all along.

Beneath all of this ran a current of belief that Marcus found most difficult to articulate. He had
come to feel, in a way he recognised as irrational but could not dislodge, that something intrinsic
to him had made the event happen — that a person without his particular flaw would not have been
there, would not have frozen, would not have watched. He felt isolated from colleagues and from his
wife in a way that had become structural rather than situational, certain that what he carried could
not be shared or understood. These appraisals of fundamental fault and helplessness intensified his
sense of ongoing danger, as though his own nature marked him as a target, and they lowered the
threshold at which ordinary sensory experience became threatening. It was his wife who finally
insisted he seek help, after finding him one evening standing in their kitchen with his back against
the far wall, scanning the room. What Marcus most fears is that the man he was before that corridor
no longer exists. What he most hopes is that someone will tell him he is wrong.
