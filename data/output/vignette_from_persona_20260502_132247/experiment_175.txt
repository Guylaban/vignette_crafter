Rajan is a 61-year-old South Asian man who has worked as a paramedic for over three decades, a
career he once described as his calling. He is married and, by most outward measures, presents as
composed and professionally seasoned. He was referred for psychological assessment following a
period of escalating distress that his wife had noticed over the preceding eight months, and which
Rajan himself had struggled to name until recently.

The incident that precipitated his current difficulties occurred during a routine emergency call to
an industrial facility. Rajan and his partner arrived to find a worker collapsed in a poorly
ventilated storage area where a chemical coolant had been leaking for hours. The smell was sharp and
medicinal — something between antiseptic and industrial solvent — and the space was dim, lit only by
a single emergency strip light that cast long shadows across the floor. Rajan entered without full
respiratory protection, a decision made in seconds under pressure, and within minutes he felt his
eyes burning, his chest tightening, and a wave of disorientation that he initially mistook for
exertion. It was only when he stumbled and his partner dragged him out that he understood something
was wrong. He spent two days in hospital under observation. The medical team reassured him that
there had been no lasting physiological damage, but Rajan left with something they could not measure
on a chart.

In the weeks that followed, the memory did not settle. It remained close to the surface, and certain
things could bring it back without any warning at all — not gradually, but instantaneously and in
full. The smell of rubbing alcohol on a colleague's hands, the particular quality of darkness in an
unlit stairwell, the ambient sounds and smells of the ambulance bay itself: any of these could
collapse the distance between then and now, returning him not to a recollection but to the
experience itself, complete with the same terror he had felt on the floor of that storage room. When
the memory returned this way, the emotional force of it was so overwhelming that he would have to
stop whatever he was doing — mid-sentence, mid-task — unable to continue until the wave passed.
Because the memory could be triggered without warning, Rajan began to experience his own workplace
as a source of constant latent threat, and the ambulance, the bay, the equipment — things he had
once handled with ease — became charged with the possibility of re-exposure.

It was the triggers themselves that began to organise his behaviour. To manage the risk of being
ambushed by the memory at work, he started avoiding anything that might concentrate the exposure: he
declined calls that involved hospitals when he could, found reasons to step back from physical
examinations, and felt a creeping dread whenever a shift began. At the same time, the memory's
capacity to overwhelm him when it surfaced led him to a strategy that felt, at first, like simple
practicality: he made sure he was never alone. He filled every available space in his day with
conversation, company, noise — surrounding himself with people so that the silence in which the
memory tended to find him could not take hold. His wife noticed that he no longer sat quietly in the
evenings, that he sought her company with an urgency that was different from affection. But the
constant socialising, while it suppressed the memory's immediate return, also prevented him from
processing what had happened, and so the memory remained unintegrated — ready to be triggered again
by the next encounter with darkness, or the sharp chemical note of alcohol, or the interior of the
vehicle he drove to work each day. The avoidance of medical settings, similarly, foreclosed any
opportunity to build a different relationship with the context of the event, keeping the workplace
and its associated cues perpetually threatening. Over months, the loop tightened: the triggers fed
the memory, the memory sharpened his vigilance toward triggers, and the strategies that were meant
to contain both instead preserved them.

He also found himself returning, in quiet moments that broke through despite his efforts, to the
question of what he should have done differently — rehearsing the decision not to wait for full
equipment, turning it over without resolution. This rumination, though not the centre of his
distress, sat beneath everything else.

What finally brought Rajan to seek help was a near-miss at work: he froze at the entrance to a
darkened corridor during a call, and his partner had to take over. He is most afraid, he says, that
he has become unreliable — that the person he has been for thirty years is no longer there. What he
hopes for, more quietly, is to be able to walk into a dark room without bracing for the floor to
disappear beneath him.
