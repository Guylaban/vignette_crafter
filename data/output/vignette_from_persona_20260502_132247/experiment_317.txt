Marcus Holt is a 46-year-old graphic designer of European descent who lives with his wife in a mid-
sized American city. He was referred for psychological assessment following a significant
deterioration in his functioning over the past eighteen months, presenting with a PCL-5 score of 65.
The events that brought him to this point began on an otherwise unremarkable evening when Marcus was
stopped by police officers while walking home from a late client meeting. What followed was not a
brief procedural encounter. Officers restrained him against a wall, and during the altercation one
officer struck him repeatedly. Marcus sustained visible lacerations to his face and a deep bruise
across his ribs. He lay on the pavement for several minutes before anyone helped him. No charges
were filed against the officers. No formal investigation was opened. He was simply left there,
bleeding, and told to go home.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus found that the memory of the incident did not behave like an
ordinary memory. It did not sit in the past. Instead, something in his surroundings — the flash of a
uniform, a police siren three blocks away, or even the sight of a stranger's bruised arm — could
instantly collapse the distance between then and now, returning him to the pavement with a
completeness that left him breathless. These triggers did not simply remind him of what happened;
they detonated it. His body would flood with alarm, his chest tighten, and the sense of imminent
danger would arrive fully formed, as though the attack were still ongoing. When a trigger struck,
the full memory surged back with it, and the two fed each other — the alarm in his body confirming
that the threat was real, the memory supplying the evidence.

What made this worse, in ways Marcus struggled to articulate even to himself, was the belief that
had quietly taken root in him: that what was done to him was profoundly unjust, that nothing had
been done about it, and that his life had been irreparably destroyed. He had come to feel that the
world he once moved through with reasonable confidence was no longer his world. These convictions
did not stay contained. Because he had come to believe that authority figures represented
unaddressed danger, the sight of any police presence was not merely unpleasant — it was a
confirmation that he remained unprotected, and his body responded accordingly, snapping back to high
alert. The same beliefs coloured the memory itself: when the injustice felt most vivid, the memory
felt most raw, and he would find himself revisiting the pavement again without choosing to.

When he tried to explain what had happened — to his wife, briefly, or to his GP — he would lose the
thread entirely, the emotion rising so fast that he had to stop. He could not finish the account.
The overwhelm was total. And so he stopped trying to explain, and started trying not to think. He
began spending long hours on his phone, cycling through games and social media, keeping his mind
occupied every waking moment so that nothing could surface. He withdrew from friends and,
increasingly, from his wife. At his design studio, where he had once worked with easy concentration,
he now sat behind his screen for different reasons — not creating, but escaping. Deadlines slipped.
A long-standing client relationship ended. His wife had begun to ask questions he could not answer.

The avoidance, however, did not protect him. By keeping his mind perpetually busy, Marcus never
fully processed what had happened, and the memory remained as volatile and fragmented as ever. When
distraction occasionally failed — a notification sound that echoed a radio dispatch, a news image of
a man with a bloodied face — the memory broke through with renewed force, and his body, already
primed by weeks of unresolved tension, was instantly back on alert. He could not relax anywhere.
Before entering any room, he noted the exits. His muscles had not fully unclenched in over a year.

Marcus sought help now because his wife told him quietly one evening that she was frightened — not
of him, but for him. What he fears most is that nothing can be made right, that the injustice is
permanent and the damage is done. What he is hoping for, though he can barely say it, is that he
might one day feel safe in his own body again.
