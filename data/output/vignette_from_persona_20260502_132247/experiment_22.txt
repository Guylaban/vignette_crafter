Fatima is a fifty-year-old divorced social worker of Syrian origin who presented for psychological
assessment following a referral from her primary care physician, who had noted escalating anxiety
symptoms and significant sleep disruption over the preceding year. Her PCL-5 score of 66 places her
firmly within the severe range, and the clinical picture that emerged across the initial sessions is
one of a woman caught in a tightening loop of perceived danger and the very behaviours she has
deployed to manage it.

Fatima's trauma history is rooted in the years she spent fleeing Syria in her early forties. The
journey she described was not a single catastrophic moment but a sustained period of exposure to
unpredictable violence, border crossings conducted in darkness, and the constant knowledge that
visibility meant danger. She recalled one night in particular — pressed against a wall with a group
of other women, instructed not to move, not to speak, not to draw attention — in which she
understood, with the kind of clarity that does not leave a person, that being seen could mean being
killed. She arrived in the United States several years later, eventually rebuilding a professional
life as a social worker in a resettlement agency, a role that drew directly on her own experience.
She had come to believe, however, that the safety others seemed to take for granted was simply not
available to her.

The threat she perceives is not abstract. Fatima wakes most nights, sometimes two or three times,
sitting up in the dark to listen, to look, to confirm that the locks are still holding and that no
one is outside her apartment door. She cannot say what she expects to find; she knows, in some part
of herself, that she is in a Midwestern city and not at a border crossing. But the knowing does not
reach the part of her that is already upright and scanning. During the day, this vigilance continues
without interruption. She is unable to relax even in settings she can rationally identify as safe —
her own office, a colleague's car, a quiet waiting room. Her body remains oriented toward exit
routes and unfamiliar sounds. She often reacts as if she is in danger when nothing is actually
wrong: a door slamming in the corridor of the agency where she works sends her heart into her
throat; a client raising their voice, even in frustration rather than threat, produces a
physiological response she can neither suppress nor easily explain to those around her.

It is precisely this state of chronic alarm that has shaped the strategies Fatima now uses to move
through her days. She dresses carefully each morning in loose, layered clothing that minimises her
outline and draws no particular attention — a habit she formed during the journey and never
abandoned, because being unnoticed still feels, in some cellular way, like being safe. She avoids
situations that might produce strong emotions: she has declined social invitations, stepped back
from a supervision role at work that she once found meaningful, and ended a friendship that had
become, in her words, "too much." She scans every environment she enters — the agency lobby, the
grocery store, the parking structure — cataloguing faces and positions and possible threats before
she allows herself to cross a threshold. These strategies were, at their origin, survival tools.
What Fatima has not yet fully recognised is that they now sustain the very alarm they were designed
to quiet. Because she is always dressed to disappear, always scanning, always withdrawing from
emotional intensity, her nervous system receives the continuous message that danger is present and
that vigilance is warranted. The threat never resolves, because her behaviour never permits the
evidence that it has passed.

This cycle has had concrete consequences. Her supervisor noted, gently but formally, that her
withdrawal from the supervision role had left a gap in the team. A colleague she trusts described
noticing that Fatima seemed "far away." These observations landed on her with shame rather than
curiosity.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment at work when a newly arrived refugee family — a
mother with two daughters — reminded her so acutely of her own crossing that she had to leave the
room. She sat in the bathroom for twenty minutes, unable to return. She is most afraid, she said
quietly at the end of the first session, that she will never stop waiting for something terrible to
happen. What she hopes for, almost shyly, is to sleep through the night.
