Priya is a sixty-six-year-old South Asian woman who has worked as a midwife for nearly four decades,
a vocation she has always described as a calling. She is married, the mother of adult children, and
by most outward measures a figure of quiet authority in the labor and delivery ward where she has
spent the better part of her professional life. She presented for assessment with a PCL-5 score of
74, referred by her primary care physician after she disclosed, haltingly and for the first time,
that something had happened to her during a military medical deployment many years ago — something
she had spent the intervening decades trying to outrun.

During that deployment, Priya had been assigned to a forward medical unit. One night, a senior
officer entered the supply room where she was working alone. What followed was a sustained episode
of sexual coercion and threatened violence by a man in uniform whose rank made her silence feel like
the only survivable option. She remembered the fluorescent light above flickering. She remembered
being watched — his eyes tracking her every movement as though cataloguing her. She remembered the
particular way a man's presence in a confined space could fill it entirely, leaving no air. These
are not memories she has words for easily; they are, instead, the architecture of her present fear.

In the years after the deployment, Priya noticed that certain moments would pull her back without
warning. A male colleague stepping into the medication room while she was alone, a security guard in
uniform pausing near the nurses' station, the sensation of being observed by a supervisor during a
procedure — each of these would snap something taut inside her. What she felt was not merely
discomfort. Her body moved immediately into a state of high alert: scanning the room, tracking
exits, watching hands and posture for the first sign of threat. She became superalert in ways she
could not fully explain to herself, unable to settle even in the staff break room when she knew,
rationally, that she was safe. Sudden movements by male colleagues — a hand reaching past her, a
door swinging open — would send a bolt of alarm through her chest before she had time to think. The
triggers she encountered most reliably were not abstract; they were woven into the daily fabric of a
busy hospital ward, and each one reliably ignited the same state of vigilant, unrelenting threat.

Because that state was so unbearable, Priya had, over the years, built her life around managing it.
She had become expert at never being alone with a man — scheduling her breaks carefully, positioning
herself near other nurses, finding reasons to step out when a male physician lingered. She began
working longer and longer hours, filling every quiet moment with tasks, because stillness allowed
the vigilance to turn inward and become unbearable. Hypervigilance toward people in general had
become a constant low-level occupation: she read body language compulsively, assessed every new face
on the ward, and kept herself oriented toward the room's exits as a matter of habit. These
strategies had, in their way, worked — they had kept her functioning, kept her present at bedsides,
kept her marriage intact in a surface sense. But they had also narrowed her world considerably. She
had declined a promotion to ward manager because the role would have required one-on-one meetings
with male administrators in closed offices. She had stopped attending the monthly clinical reviews.
Her husband had noticed her restlessness at home, her inability to sit quietly in the evenings,
though she had never told him why.

What Priya had not anticipated was the way her strategies fed the very sensitivity she was trying to
manage. By never being alone with men, she had ensured that male presence remained charged with
danger; by scanning every room for threat, she found it everywhere. The constant vigilance that was
meant to protect her kept her nervous system primed, so that each new trigger — a uniform, a gaze, a
man at the edge of her peripheral vision — landed with full force. The loop had been tightening for
years without her naming it.

What finally brought her in was a moment on the ward when a young male student midwife, well-meaning
and oblivious, touched her shoulder briefly to get her attention. She had flinched so visibly that a
colleague asked if she was all right. Standing in the corridor afterward, she had thought: I cannot
keep living like this. What she most fears is that the damage is permanent. What she most hopes is
that someone will tell her it is not.
