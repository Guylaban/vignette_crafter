Moshe is a 63-year-old rabbi who came to the United States as a young man after fleeing violent
civil conflict in his country of origin. He presents now with a PCL-5 score of 57, seeking help for
the first time after decades of managing — or believing he was managing — on his own.

The events that shaped his current presentation occurred when Moshe was in his late twenties, during
a period of forced displacement. He and a group of others were detained at a checkpoint by armed
soldiers. There was shouting — sustained, chaotic, aimed directly at him and those around him — and
at one point a soldier grabbed him by the collar without warning, pressing a rifle barrel against
his chest. He was eventually released, but two people he had been travelling with were not. He has
never spoken about this publicly, not even in the pastoral context where he has spent thirty years
helping others speak about their pain.

In the years that followed, Moshe built a life of considerable structure and meaning. He leads a
mid-sized congregation, teaches regularly, and is in a long-term relationship with a partner who
describes him as warm but unreachable. The symptoms did not arrive all at once. They accumulated
quietly, like sediment. He noticed, perhaps five or six years after arriving in the United States,
that he could no longer sit with his back to a door. He began arriving early to synagogue board
meetings so he could take the chair nearest the exit. Over time this extended to restaurants,
waiting rooms, and family gatherings — he now feels genuinely safe only at home, in a specific
chair, in a room he can see entirely. He has arranged his office at the synagogue in the same way.

The triggers that most reliably fracture his composure are the ones that echo the checkpoint: a hand
placed unexpectedly on his shoulder during a congregational embrace, the sight of a security guard's
holstered weapon at a community event, or raised voices in any context — even celebratory ones. When
these occur, his body floods with a response that he finds deeply distressing and deeply shaming. He
has come to believe that his body is working against him, that it is reacting as though the
checkpoint is still happening, and this belief — that he cannot trust his own physiology — feeds a
broader conviction that he cannot rely on himself at all. These appraisals of personal unreliability
then sharpen his vigilance: if he cannot trust himself to remain calm or rational, the environment
must be controlled instead, and so his scanning for threat intensifies, and the range of places and
situations that feel tolerable continues to narrow. The narrowing, in turn, means he encounters
triggers more reliably in the few contexts he cannot avoid — the synagogue most of all, where
unexpected touch is a feature of pastoral life and where, on two recent occasions, raised voices
during a congregational dispute sent him into a state of acute distress that he concealed with
visible effort.

When a trigger does break through, Moshe's immediate response is to pull his attention elsewhere —
to a text he is preparing, a logistical task, anything that provides cognitive cover. This
distraction offers short-term relief but leaves the underlying activation unprocessed, so that the
next trigger finds him already primed. He also engages in what he himself named in the second
session as "punishment thinking" — a quiet, persistent conviction that he deserves to suffer for
what happened, that a stronger or better man would have intervened, that someone else would have
stopped it. This self-punishing stance has made it nearly impossible to allow emotional closeness
with his partner, whom he loves but keeps at a careful distance, offering presence without
vulnerability. The avoidance of intimacy, and the ongoing self-punishment, have paradoxically kept
the traumatic material close and unresolved, sustaining the appraisals of personal failure that make
the threat monitoring feel not only necessary but deserved.

What finally brought Moshe in was a moment three months ago when a young congregant placed both
hands on his shoulders in gratitude, and Moshe — a man known for his steadiness — flinched so
visibly that the congregant apologised. He drove home in silence. What he most fears is that he will
never be the person his community believes him to be. What he most hopes, though he has not yet said
this directly, is that he might be allowed to put something down.
