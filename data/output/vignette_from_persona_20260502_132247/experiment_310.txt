Aleksander Nowak is a 36-year-old Polish-American man who works the floor of a mid-sized home goods
retail store in suburban Ohio. He is married, and until roughly fourteen months ago, his wife
described him as steady, even-tempered, and reliable. He presents now with a PCL-5 score of 59, and
the story he has pieced together across the first two sessions is one of a man caught in a loop he
cannot find the exit to.

The event that fractured his sense of normalcy occurred during a large-scale industrial fire at a
distribution warehouse adjacent to his store's supply depot, where he had been sent to collect an
emergency stock transfer. The fire had been caused by negligence — a contractor had bypassed safety
protocols, and the resulting explosion trapped several workers. Aleksander was not himself injured,
but he was close enough to pull a colleague partway from debris before emergency responders arrived.
What he remembers is not a continuous scene but a series of frozen frames: the colleague's face, a
specific angle of orange light against grey smoke, and the sensation of gripping a section of
scorched, textured industrial mesh — a coarse, almost serrated weave — that he had grabbed to brace
himself. Those snapshots are what remain, each one more vivid and immediate than anything from his
ordinary life, as though the fire exists in higher resolution than the rest of his history.

In the months that followed, Aleksander began to notice what he could not yet name. At the store,
handling certain merchandise — particularly the rough-weave storage baskets that arrived in a
seasonal shipment — would cause his hands to freeze mid-reach. The texture of the mesh was close
enough to what he had gripped that afternoon to pull him instantly back into one of those snapshot
moments, and with it came a wave of shame so acute he would have to step away from the aisle. He had
come to believe, without fully articulating it, that he had not done enough — that he had let go too
soon, that a better man would have stayed longer, pulled harder. The shame was not peripheral; it
was the emotional core of every recalled image. When those memories surfaced, triggered by the
texture or by the physical closeness of a crowd pressing around him in a busy aisle, the recalled
scenes felt not like remembering but like re-entering — more present and more real than the
fluorescent-lit store around him.

To manage this, Aleksander had developed a set of strategies that made sense to him even as they
quietly dismantled his life. His first line of defence was a kind of internal revision: he would
tell himself the event had not been as serious as he remembered, that he was exaggerating, that
people had been fine. This magical reframing offered momentary relief but left the memories
unprocessed and intact, so that the next time a trigger arrived — a customer standing too close,
someone in the store behaving rudely or carelessly in a way that echoed the contractor's negligence
— the images returned with the same undiminished force. He also tried to suppress the memories by
sheer will, pushing them down whenever they surfaced. But suppression, he had found, had a rebound
quality: the harder he pushed, the more insistently the snapshots returned, and with them the shame,
and with the shame the need to suppress again. On three occasions over the past four months, when
the loop became unbearable, he had resorted to pressing his forearm against the edge of a shelf
bracket hard enough to leave a bruise — not out of any wish to harm himself seriously, he insisted,
but because the physical sensation broke the circuit, at least briefly. His wife had noticed the
bruises.

It was his wife who finally told him she was frightened — not of him, but for him. She had watched
him reorganise his entire section of the store to avoid the basket aisle, seen him flinch when
customers crowded him, and found him sitting awake at 2 a.m. more nights than not. He had agreed to
seek help partly to reassure her and partly because the loop had grown too tight to endure alone.
What he most fears is that the shame he feels when he remembers is accurate — that it reflects
something true about who he is. What he most hopes is that someone can tell him it does not.
