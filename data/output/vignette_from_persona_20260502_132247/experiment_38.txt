Mere Tuilagi is a 66-year-old Fijian-American woman, divorced, who works as a curriculum designer
for a mid-sized educational nonprofit. She was referred for psychological assessment following a
disclosure to her primary care physician that she had not been sleeping properly for years and had
recently stopped attending her organisation's in-person staff meetings altogether. Her PCL-5 score
of 49 places her well within the clinical range for PTSD, and her presentation reflects a long and
largely unaddressed history rooted in a combat exposure she sustained decades ago while serving as a
civilian medical liaison in a forward operating zone.

The event itself occurred during a coordinated attack on a field compound. Mere had been in a
briefing room with a commanding officer when the compound came under fire. She had no authority, no
weapon, and no clear role in what followed — she was told to stay, to move, to wait, by people whose
orders contradicted one another, and she complied because she had no choice. The sounds of rotor
blades and aircraft filled the air throughout, first as the sound of potential rescue and then as
something more ambiguous and terrifying when a helicopter passed low and the firing intensified. At
one point she was physically moved by a soldier who grabbed her arm and directed her behind a wall
without explanation. She survived. Several others did not. What she remembers of the aftermath is
not a coherent sequence but a series of disconnected impressions — the colour of dust on a boot, the
particular silence after the last shot, a face she cannot place — fragments that do not assemble
into a story she can tell. She has said to her therapist that the memory is so extreme it is hard to
believe it actually happened to her, and when she tries to describe it, the words simply do not
come. She knows what occurred, but the language for it seems to belong to someone else.

In the years since, these fragmented, wordless memories have not remained contained. They surface
suddenly and without invitation — while Mere is reviewing curriculum materials, while she is eating
lunch at her desk — and when they do, they arrive not as narrative recollection but as sensation and
image, pulling her back into the compound with a vividness that makes the present feel thin. The
sound of a helicopter is particularly destabilising. Working near an urban flight corridor, she
hears rotors several times a week, and each time the sound reaches her, the fragments reassemble
into something urgent and uncontrolled, flooding her with the same helplessness she felt that day.
These triggered intrusions have, over time, deepened her conviction that she cannot trust herself to
respond adequately — that in a moment of real danger, she would again be someone without agency,
without the right words, without the capacity to act correctly. Being in any situation where another
person holds authority over her reactivates this same fear, and she has come to dread team meetings,
performance reviews, and any professional interaction in which her role is subordinate.

What Mere had come to believe, increasingly, was that she had to be on guard at all times, that she
was fundamentally set apart from people around her who had not seen what she had seen, and that this
separateness was permanent. These appraisals drove the strategies that now organise her daily life.
She avoids going out beyond what her work requires, and when she must attend in-person events, she
positions herself near exits — a habit so automatic she does not always notice she is doing it. She
has also learned to suppress any strong emotion as it arises, treating feeling itself as a threat to
her composure and safety. The consequence of these strategies has been substantial: by staying home,
by cutting off her emotional responses, by withdrawing from professional gatherings, she has
prevented the natural processing of what happened. The memories remain fragmented and unintegrated,
and because they cannot be worked through, they continue to surface intrusively, confirming her
sense of danger and perpetuating the vigilance and isolation she has tried to use as protection. Her
avoidance has also cost her professionally — she was recently passed over for a project lead role,
her manager noting her absence from collaborative spaces — and this loss has reinforced her belief
that she is fundamentally different from others, someone who cannot be relied upon.

What finally brought Mere to seek help was a morning when a helicopter passed over her apartment
building and she found herself on the floor of her kitchen, unable to stand for several minutes. She
is most afraid that nothing can change at this point in her life. What she hopes, tentatively, is
that someone might help her find the words.
