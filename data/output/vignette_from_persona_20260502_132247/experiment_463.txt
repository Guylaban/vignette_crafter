Fatima had spent four decades on the bench, and in that time she had developed what colleagues
called an uncommon steadiness — the ability to hear the worst of human circumstances and deliver
judgment without flinching. She had presided over homicide cases, custody battles, the slow
unraveling of families in crisis, and she had always believed, quietly but firmly, that she
possessed the temperament to absorb difficulty without being undone by it. That belief did not
survive the autumn her youngest son, Tariq, died.

He had called her the evening before, and she had let the call go to voicemail. She was preparing
for a complex sentencing hearing the following morning, and she told herself she would phone him
back after dinner. She did not. The voicemail was ordinary — his voice easy and warm, asking if she
wanted to come for dinner that weekend, saying he had something he wanted to show her. By the time
she listened to it the next morning, Tariq had already been found. A sudden cardiac event, the
paramedics said. He was forty-three years old and had seemed, to everyone who loved him, entirely
well.

In the weeks that followed, Fatima returned to the courtroom because she did not know what else to
do with herself. Her husband, Samir, urged her to take leave, but she resisted, convinced that
structure would hold her together the way it always had. It did not. She found herself sitting on
the bench, listening to testimony, and becoming suddenly and completely unreachable — not to the
room, but to herself. The quality of her attention had changed in ways she could not fully account
for, and she was frightened by it.

What consumed her most was not grief in any form she recognized, but a relentless, grinding self-
indictment. She had let the call go to voicemail. She had chosen a sentencing memo over her son's
voice. In the architecture of her mind, this had become not a lapse but a verdict, and she was both
its author and its subject. She had come to believe, with a certainty that resisted every counter-
argument Samir offered, that Tariq's death was connected to her failure to answer — that had she
called back that night, something might have been different, some intervention made, some sign
noticed. She knew, in the part of her mind that had spent forty years weighing evidence, that this
was not logical. She knew it. And yet the knowing changed nothing.

Alongside this was a second conviction, equally immovable: that she should have seen it coming. She
was his mother. She had read people professionally for decades. She had failed to read the one
person who mattered most, failed to register whatever signal had been there, failed to protect him
the way a mother — the way she — should have been able to. The word she returned to, privately and
repeatedly, was failure. Not sadness. Not loss. Failure.

What troubled her most in the present moment was the growing suspicion that her response to Tariq's
death confirmed something she had long feared about herself beneath the professional composure: that
she was not, in fact, capable. That the steadiness her colleagues admired was a performance, and
that when something truly terrible arrived, she had collapsed inward in ways that a stronger person
would not have. She described this to her physician — the first person she had told anything — as
feeling like she had been exposed. Forty years of being the person who held things together, and now
she could see clearly that she had never actually been that person at all.

It was her physician who referred her for psychological consultation, and Fatima came to the first
session with the careful, measured manner of someone accustomed to being the authority in the room.
What she was most afraid of, she said, was that therapy would confirm what she already suspected —
that there was nothing wrong with her thinking, that she really had failed, that she really was as
inadequate as she felt. What she was hoping for, though she offered this more quietly, was that
someone might show her she was wrong.
