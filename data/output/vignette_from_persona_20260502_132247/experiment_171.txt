decades, a trade he learned shortly after immigrating and one that gave him a quiet, durable pride

Approximately fourteen months ago, Boon-Nam was contracted to re-seal a flat commercial roof. The
job required working with a solvent-based coating compound in conditions with inadequate ventilation
— a detail that was not communicated to him beforehand. Within the first hour, he began to feel a
burning in his throat and chest. By the time he understood what was happening, he had inhaled a
substantial quantity of chemical fumes and collapsed near the roofline. He was found by a coworker
and taken by ambulance to the hospital, where he spent three days in a respiratory unit. He
remembers the antiseptic smell of the ward with terrible clarity — the way it mixed with the
residual chemical taste still sitting in his lungs — and he remembers waking repeatedly in the
darkened room, disoriented, unable to locate himself in time or place, certain at several moments
that he was dying.

In the months that followed, those sensory details did not recede. The smell of antiseptic —
encountered now whenever he drives past the hospital on the way to job sites, or catches a cleaning
product in a hardware store — hits him before conscious recognition arrives, and in those moments
the memories return with a vividness that feels less like recollection and less like imagination and
more like the event simply continuing. His memories of the roof, the collapse, and the ward are
clear and detailed in a way that ordinary memories are not: he can describe the exact quality of the
light, the sound of the monitors, the weight of the oxygen mask. Sometimes, without any image
arriving at all, he is flooded with the fear of those three days — a full-bodied dread with no
picture attached, which he finds even more disorienting than the visual replays. These intrusive
memories have quietly hardened his belief that he cannot rely on himself — that a man who did not
notice the danger until it felled him is not a man who can be trusted to assess risk — and from that
conviction has grown a broader, darker certainty: that the world is dangerous in ways that cannot be
predicted, and that he, specifically, has no reliable future. He has come to believe that his body
was permanently changed by what happened, that his lungs are compromised in ways the doctors have
not fully disclosed, and that the life he imagined for his remaining years is simply no longer
available to him.

These appraisals have made the triggers more potent, not less. Because he is certain the world is
dangerous and that he cannot trust his own judgment, he is hypervigilant to anything that might
signal exposure — smells especially, but also darkness, which carries the memory of waking in the

repeatedly, and the dark room is enough to pull the whole sensory experience back. The threat he
feels is constant and does not respond to reassurance. Even at home, in a space he has lived in for
twenty years, he cannot fully relax. He has difficulty concentrating on conversations, on paperwork,
on the small tasks of daily life. He avoids unfamiliar places because he cannot quickly scan them
for chemical hazards or exits, and he has quietly stopped accepting jobs at sites he has not worked
before — a significant professional contraction for a man who once took pride in his adaptability.

Rather than speaking about any of this, Boon-Nam has tried to manage it alone. He suppresses
thoughts of the event deliberately, pushing them down when they surface, which leaves him exhausted
and, paradoxically, more vulnerable to intrusion. He dwells on the man he was before — the roofer
who climbed without hesitation, the husband who did not flinch — and the distance between that self
and his current one confirms, again and again, that he cannot rely on himself. He never disagrees
with his wife or his coworkers, never asserts a preference or raises a concern, because conflict

strategies fail, which tightens the sense of threat and confirms the appraisals all over again.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a conversation with his wife, who told him she was

hopes, though he said it quietly and only once, is that he might be able to sleep through the dark
again.
