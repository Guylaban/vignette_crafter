He presented for treatment approximately four months after being sexually assaulted by an
acquaintance at a gathering he attended after a late shift. The assault occurred in a back room
while music played loudly elsewhere in the apartment; at one point a car alarm outside cut through
the noise, and Kofi remembers the sound clearly because it was the moment he understood he could not
get away. He could not move freely, could not call out without consequence, and that absolute loss
of physical agency became the defining texture of the experience.

In the weeks that followed, Kofi found that ordinary sounds in the kitchen — the sharp clatter of a
pan, a colleague shouting an order, and most acutely the rumble and beep of delivery trucks in the
alley behind the restaurant — could pull the full memory back without warning, as though no time had
passed at all. When the memory arrived it came with sound: he would hear, internally and vividly,
the car alarm from that night, and the voices, and the specific quality of his own silence. He could
not explain this to his supervisor when asked why he had frozen mid-service; he would begin to
describe what happened and lose the thread entirely, the words dissolving before he could finish.
Those intrusive returns of the memory, in turn, reliably reactivated his sense that danger was
present right now, and he would scan the kitchen for threats — tracking the movements of colleagues,
tensing whenever someone crossed behind him quickly — as if the assault were still in progress.

That persistent sense of threat had become structural in his life. Sudden movements by anyone nearby
put him on guard immediately, and he felt no relief even at home, remaining watchful in his own
apartment in ways that exhausted him. The threat state fed back into the memory directly: when his
body was already primed and scanning, the threshold for a full intrusion dropped, and sounds that
might otherwise pass unnoticed — a car outside, a raised voice through the wall — were enough to
trigger the complete recollection again. To manage this, Kofi had developed several strategies that
felt necessary even when he recognised they were not helping. He monitored his body constantly,
checking for signs of injury or physiological alarm as though verifying he was still intact. He
detached himself from the memories when they surfaced, pushing them away before they could fully
form. And in quieter moments, particularly at night, he found himself constructing elaborate revenge
fantasies about the person responsible — replaying scenarios in which he had power and the outcome
was different.

These strategies, however, had begun to reinforce the very difficulties they were meant to contain.
The detachment prevented any coherent processing of what had happened, so the memories remained
fragmented, easily triggered, and loaded with the same raw intensity as the night itself. The body
monitoring kept his attention fixed on internal threat signals, which meant the threshold for false
alarms stayed low, and he continued to react as though danger were imminent in situations that were
objectively safe. The revenge fantasies, rather than relieving distress, circled back into his
appraisals of the event — because imagining what he should have done implied, to Kofi, that he had
failed to do it. He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt unshakeable, that someone else
in his position would have stopped it. He felt that he had acted in ways that violated who he
understood himself to be, though he struggled to articulate exactly what he meant by this, only that
something essential had been compromised. These beliefs, in turn, made the memories harder to bear
when they surfaced, amplifying their emotional charge, and they lowered his tolerance for any
reminder — so that even the minor associations of daily life, a car sound, a moment of feeling
constrained during a busy service, could become intolerable. He had no sense of a future he was
moving toward. That hopelessness sat separately from everything else, a flat and quiet certainty
that had settled over him.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a service incident in which he walked off the line mid-

He came to the first session saying he did not expect therapy to change anything, but that he
needed, at minimum, to be able to finish a shift.
