Rosario Mendez is a sixty-six-year-old Latina paramedic with thirty-one years of emergency service
behind her, a woman who has pulled people from wreckage and talked strangers back from ledges. She
came to the clinic carrying a PCL-5 score of 53 and a quiet, watchful stillness that her husband had
described to her as frightening — not because she seemed dangerous, but because she no longer seemed
entirely present. The event that brought her here had occurred fourteen months earlier, when Rosario
was taken captive during what began as a routine welfare check in a rural county on the outskirts of
her district. The man inside the house had barricaded the door behind her, cut the power, and held
her in near-total darkness and silence for eleven hours before a tactical team made entry. There
were no sounds from outside for most of that time — no sirens, no voices, no radio traffic — only
the man's breathing and the periodic creak of floorboards as he moved through the dark. She had been
certain, at several points, that she was about to die.

In the months that followed, Rosario found that the memory did not behave the way other difficult
memories had across her career. It did not recede. Instead it arrived without invitation — during a
meal, mid-shift, once while she was completing a patient report at her station desk — forcing itself
into her mind with a vividness she had no way to interrupt. When it returned, the emotional force of
it was so total that she would have to stop whatever she was doing: set down her pen, step away from
the rig, press her back against a wall and wait for it to pass. The memory had a quality she
struggled to articulate, describing it to her intake clinician as feeling like something that
couldn't have happened to her — so extreme, so outside anything she had imagined for her own life,
that part of her still refused to accept it as real. Yet that very quality of unreality seemed to
amplify her distress. When she tried to make sense of the memory — to examine it, to understand it —
she found herself arriving at conclusions that frightened her further. She had come to believe that
her inability to control the memory's intrusions, the way it overwhelmed her completely each time it
surfaced, was evidence that something had broken in her mind permanently. The fear that she was
going crazy was not a passing worry; it was a conviction she returned to daily, and it sat alongside
an equally firm belief that the world was dangerous in ways she had previously underestimated, and
that she was constitutionally unable to stop bad things from happening to her.

These appraisals did not remain separate from the memory — they fed back into it. When Rosario told
herself she was losing her mind, or that she was helpless against the next catastrophe, the memory
became more accessible, not less, rising more readily and with greater emotional force, as though
her own interpretations were holding the door open. And the memory, in turn, shaped how she
experienced the present. The particular silence of those eleven hours had become a perceptual
trigger she could not neutralize. At her station, during the quiet stretches between calls that she
had once used for rest, the absence of sound now registered in her body as warning. She would scan
the room, check the doorway, look behind her in the corridor. At home, in the evenings when her
husband was in another room and the house went still, she felt the same alertness rise in her chest
— a certainty that something dangerous was gathering just outside her awareness. She had begun
sleeping with a light on. She checked behind herself when walking from the rig to the hospital
entrance, a habit her partner had noticed and gently questioned.

Occupationally, the consequences had accumulated quietly. She had requested fewer overnight shifts,
citing fatigue, but the real reason was that the silence of those hours was unbearable. Her
supervisor had noted a change in her documentation — slower, less detailed — without knowing why.

What finally brought Rosario to seek help was her husband telling her, without anger, that he missed
her. What she feared most was that he was right to grieve.
