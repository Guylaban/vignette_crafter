Caleb is a 32-year-old single man of North American descent who works as a park ranger in a remote
national forest in the Pacific Northwest. He was referred for psychological assessment following a
period of escalating difficulty at work and a near-total withdrawal from social contact. His PCL-5
score of 41 places him in the clinically significant range for PTSD, and the history he has
disclosed across the intake and first two sessions points to a traumatic event of sustained and
deliberate violence.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Caleb was abducted during a solo patrol in a backcountry sector
of the park. He was held for an indeterminate period — he believes between two and four days, though
he cannot be certain — during which he was subjected to repeated physical abuse by two men whose
identities remain unknown to law enforcement. He was restrained in a confined space, and much of
what was done to him occurred in near-darkness and near-silence, broken only by the sound of locks
being thrown and the creak of a heavy door. He was eventually found by a search team at the edge of
a fire road, disoriented and unable to give a coherent account of where he had been or what had
happened. He has not been able to give a fully coherent account since.

The memory of those days does not come to Caleb as a story he can tell. Certain moments are seared
in with terrible precision — the specific weight of a hand on his throat, the smell of diesel —
while entire stretches are simply absent, replaced by a blankness that frightens him as much as the
images that do surface. When he has tried to explain what happened, even to the investigator
assigned to his case, he loses the thread mid-sentence, sometimes stopping entirely, unable to
locate the next word. He has come to believe this fragmentation is evidence that something is
fundamentally wrong with his mind, that his reactions since the event mean he is going crazy. This
conviction — that he is damaged in ways that may be permanent, both physically and mentally — sits
at the centre of his current presentation and radiates outward into nearly everything else. Because
he cannot trust his own mind to hold the account together, he has become intensely guarded about any
attempt to reconstruct or revisit it, which in turn keeps the memory disorganised and inaccessible,
deepening his certainty that something is broken in him.

The belief that he cannot rely on other people, and that his body has been permanently damaged, has
made Caleb vigilant in ways that exhaust him. He checks the locks on the doors and windows of his
ranger station repeatedly before he can rest, sometimes cycling through the same three locks four or
five times in a single evening. When a radio check-in runs late or a supply delivery does not arrive
on schedule, he is immediately certain something bad is happening — not a logistical delay, but a
threat. His body, he says, seems to know before he does: his heart is already racing, his hands
already moving toward the door latch, before any conscious thought has formed. This hair-trigger
physiological readiness keeps him in a state of chronic arousal that makes it harder to access and
process the fragmented memory, reinforcing the sense that the event remains unfinished and present.

In response to these appraisals and the relentless alarm they generate, Caleb has organised his life
around a set of strategies that feel like protection but function as constraints. He has stopped
driving entirely — a significant loss for a ranger whose patrol area requires vehicle access —
relying on a colleague to transport him, an arrangement he has not explained. Before any interaction
with supervisors or colleagues, he rehearses what he will say, running through possible exchanges
until he feels he has controlled for every variable. He numbs himself emotionally as a matter of
habit, describing his inner life as "flat." These strategies, however, prevent him from encountering
the disconfirming experiences that might soften his threat appraisals, and they keep the traumatic
material sealed rather than processed, sustaining the very fragmentation and sense of damage they
were meant to manage.

What finally brought Caleb to seek help was a formal performance review in which his supervisor
noted his diminished patrol capacity and asked directly whether he was fit for duty. He said he was.
He is not sure he believes it. What he hopes for, tentatively, is to feel less like a man waiting
for something terrible to happen. What he fears most is that the waiting is simply what he is now.
