Linh Tran is a 31-year-old Vietnamese-American journalist, recently divorced, who presented for an
initial intake following the sudden death of her younger brother Minh eight months ago. Minh, who
was 27, collapsed without warning at a family gathering — a cerebral aneurysm, the coroner later
confirmed, that killed him within minutes before an ambulance could arrive. Linh had been the one
standing closest to him when he fell. She held his head in her lap on the kitchen floor of their
parents' home in San Jose, speaking to him, watching his face change, not understanding at first and
then understanding completely. There was no violence, no threat she could have anticipated or
defended against — only the sudden, absolute subtraction of someone who had been entirely alive
seconds before. That unanswerability became the wound she could not close.

In the weeks following Minh's death, Linh found that her nervous system had quietly reorganised
itself around a single premise: that catastrophe could arrive without signal, at any moment, in any
ordinary place. A kitchen. A Sunday afternoon. A person laughing. She had come to believe, not as an
intellectual position but as something closer to bodily knowledge, that safety was a story people
told themselves — and that she had simply stopped being able to tell it. The result was a state of
near-constant vigilance that she could neither justify to others nor switch off in herself. She
scanned rooms when she entered them. She watched the faces of people she loved for signs of
something going wrong. At family dinners, which she now attended with a kind of dread, she found
herself monitoring her parents' colour, their breathing, the way they moved, alert to any deviation
from the ordinary. She could not name what she was watching for, exactly — only that she was always
watching.

Her work as a journalist, which had once felt like a natural expression of her curiosity and drive,
had become one of the primary arenas in which this vigilance played out. Linh covers local politics
and community affairs for a mid-sized Bay Area outlet, and her days involve interviews, press
briefings, and long hours at her desk or in the field. She had noticed, over the months since Minh's
death, that she could no longer sit through a briefing without her attention fragmenting — one part
of her tracking the speaker, another part sweeping the room, cataloguing exits, assessing who looked
unwell, who was pale, who was holding their head in a way that seemed wrong. Colleagues had begun to
comment, gently at first and then with more concern, that she seemed distracted, that her copy had
grown thinner. Her editor, who knew about Minh, had offered her leave. She had declined, believing
that stopping would be worse — that work was the one structure holding the rest of her together.
That belief had cost her a feature assignment she had been developing for months, reassigned quietly
while she was on a deadline she had missed.

The emotional texture of her days was one of relentless, exhausting intensity. She described strong
surges of fear that arrived without clear provocation — sitting at her laptop, waiting at a
crosswalk, lying in bed in the apartment she had moved into after her divorce finalised last year.
Alongside the fear was something she struggled to name: a compound of horror and guilt and a rage
she found shameful, directed at no one in particular and at herself most of all, for having been
unable to do anything, for having simply held him and waited. She had not relaxed, she said — not
once, not fully — since the afternoon he died. Even in moments she recognised as objectively safe,
her body refused the information. Sleep was shallow and easily broken. Stillness felt dangerous, as
though vigilance itself were the only thing standing between her and the next unbearable thing.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a conversation with her mother, who told her, with quiet
devastation, that being around Linh now felt frightening — that her watchfulness had begun to make
everyone around her feel as though something terrible was always about to happen. Linh had cried in
her car afterwards for a long time. What she most hoped for, she said at intake, was to be able to
sit in a room with the people she loved without waiting for one of them to disappear.
