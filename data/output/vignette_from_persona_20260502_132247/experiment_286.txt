Linh Tran is a 52-year-old Vietnamese-American woman who works as a librarian at a mid-sized public
library in the Pacific Northwest. She is married and has lived in the United States for over two
decades. She presented for psychological assessment with a PCL-5 score of 61, describing a
presentation that has progressively worsened over the fourteen months since she survived a
catastrophic flash flood that tore through a rural valley town where she had been visiting extended
family. The flood struck without warning in the early hours of the morning. Linh woke in complete
darkness to a silence that felt wrong — the kind of absolute stillness that precedes disaster — and
within seconds that silence was obliterated by the sound of water crashing through the walls. She
spent hours clinging to debris in the dark, surrounded by the voices and then the absence of voices
of people she knew. Several of her relatives perished. By the time she was pulled to safety, dawn
had barely broken, and the world she had known the night before was gone.

In the months that followed, Linh found that the memory did not stay in the past. It existed instead
as something that could be summoned without her consent by almost anything — a sudden hush falling
over the library stacks, the faces of her surviving relatives when they visited, the moment each
evening when the light faded from the windows. These triggers did not merely remind her of the
flood; they returned her to it. Something in her surroundings could instantly reconstitute the full
sensory weight of that night, and when the memory forced its way back in, what accompanied it was
not fear alone but an overwhelming grief — for the family members lost, for the person she had been
before, for the ordinary life that had been swept away. That grief, in turn, made the memory itself
feel even more dangerous to approach, and so the memory began to function as a trigger in its own
right, each recollection seeding the conditions for the next intrusion.

At work, the library's particular quiet — the professional silence she had once found restorative —
became a source of dread. Linh began choosing her seat at the reference desk based on which position
gave her the clearest sightlines to the exits. She scanned the reading room compulsively, tracking
the movements of patrons, noting who entered and who left. Any colleague connected to the period of
the disaster — a coworker who had sent condolences, a regular patron who had asked about the flood —
could activate the same hyperalert state that the silence itself produced. This constant threat
monitoring kept her nervous system primed, and in that primed state, ordinary sensory details became
potential alarms, so that the very act of scanning for danger increased the number of things that
felt dangerous, and the triggers multiplied.

To manage this, Linh developed a set of strategies that offered momentary relief but gradually
narrowed her world. She began deliberately dissociating — consciously trying to blank out, to feel
herself leave her body — when the threat-monitoring became unbearable. She stopped exercising,
because the elevated heart rate and physical exertion felt too close to the bodily terror of the
flood. She avoided any circumstance that carried the texture of that night: darkness, enclosed
spaces, situations where she could not see clearly around her. These strategies did provide short-
term relief, but they also preserved the conditions for the next crisis. When she dissociated or
avoided, she never completed the processing of the memory, so it remained raw and unintegrated,
ready to erupt again. The avoidance of darkness meant that nighttime became its own ritual of dread
— she slept with lights on, woke frequently, and each time she surfaced into the dark room, the
memory was waiting. The dissociation, rather than containing the trauma, seemed to keep it just
beneath the surface, so that the triggers she had not faced remained fully potent.

Linh's husband has noticed that she is no longer the person he married — quieter in a different way
now, absent even when present. It was he who finally persuaded her to seek help. What Linh fears
most is that this is permanent, that the flood did not just take her relatives but took something
essential in her. What she hopes, tentatively, is that someone might show her how to be in a quiet
room again without drowning.
