Richard is a 63-year-old physician, married and practicing internal medicine at a mid-sized urban
hospital, who was referred for psychological assessment following what his wife described as a
marked and sustained change in his personality over the past two years. The precipitating event
occurred when Richard was riding a city bus home from a medical conference — an unusual choice for
him, made only because his car was in the shop. The bus was crowded and he had taken a window seat
near the middle when, at a downtown stop, a man boarded carrying a backpack and began shouting
commands at passengers, ordering them to move to the back, not to use their phones, not to look at
him. Richard froze. The man's coat was bulging in a way that Richard, in those suspended seconds,
read as a vest. A transit officer eventually subdued the man — it turned out to be a disturbed
individual with no weapon — but not before Richard had spent nearly four minutes certain he was
about to die in a terrorist attack. He had pressed himself against the window, listening to the
shouting, watching the officer's hand move toward his holster, and had experienced what he later
described as "the clearest thought I've ever had: that none of us were getting off that bus."

In the weeks that followed, Richard found that the world had reorganized itself around that four-
minute certainty. He had come to believe, with a conviction that felt more like knowledge than
opinion, that the world is a genuinely dangerous place and that people are not what they seem — that
the man on the bus had looked ordinary, had looked like anyone, and that this was precisely the
point. These appraisals did not stay abstract. They reached back into the triggers themselves,
amplifying them: the sound of raised voices in a hospital corridor, a security guard issuing
instructions to a visitor, the distant rumble of a city bus passing the clinic window — each of
these now carried the charge of that afternoon, not merely as a reminder but as evidence confirming
what Richard had concluded about the world. The loop was self-sealing. Each encounter with a trigger
renewed his conviction that danger was everywhere and that people concealed their intentions, and
that renewed conviction made the triggers more potent still.

What this meant in practice was a state of sustained physiological and psychological vigilance that
had become the texture of his daily life. Inside his own home — a place that had once felt
unambiguously safe — Richard now listened for sounds at the perimeter, tracking the creak of the
house settling, the approach of a car in the street, any noise that might mean someone was entering.
His wife had noticed him checking the locks multiple times before bed. At the hospital, where he
spent long hours on a ward that required him to give and receive clear instructions all day, he
found himself reacting to the ordinary authority-laden language of medicine — a charge nurse
directing him, a colleague asking him to step into a procedure — with a flash of something close to
rage, followed by shame. His irritability had begun to affect his relationships with colleagues and,
more painfully, with his wife, who told him he had become someone she did not recognize.

The appraisal that his life had been destroyed by what happened on that bus fed back into his
hypervigilance, deepening it: if the damage was already total, then the threat that caused it must
be real and ongoing, and so he listened harder, trusted less, and found fresh confirmation in each
uneasy moment. Richard had stopped riding public transit entirely, and now drove to the hospital
even when parking was difficult, rerouting to avoid bus stops. A fleeting awareness that this was
narrowing his world sat somewhere at the edge of his thinking, unexamined.

said he most feared was that the person he had been before the bus was simply gone. What he said he
most hoped was that someone could explain why a man who had spent thirty years calmly managing
medical emergencies could be undone by four minutes on a city bus.
