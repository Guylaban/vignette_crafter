Arjun is a 20-year-old South Asian male writer, currently single, who was referred for psychological
assessment following a PCL-5 score of 72. He presents with a severe and entrenched post-traumatic
stress picture that developed in the months following the sudden death of his older brother, Rohan,
who collapsed without warning from an undetected cardiac arrhythmia at the age of 24. Rohan had been
Arjun's closest confidant and, in many ways, the person around whom Arjun had organised his sense of
the future — they had spoken the week before about sharing an apartment once Arjun finished his
undergraduate degree. The death came with no warning, no illness, no logic. Rohan had been laughing
at dinner and was gone before the ambulance arrived. It was Arjun who had been in the room.

In the weeks that followed, Arjun found that the world he had understood himself to be living in had
simply ceased to exist. He had come to believe, with a quiet and terrible certainty, that safety was
an illusion — that the world was dangerous in a way he had never previously registered, that the
ordinary fabric of daily life concealed catastrophic ruptures that could open without notice. These
beliefs were not abstract. They sat in his body. He was unable to relax even in environments he knew
to be physically safe; what frightened him most was not any specific threat but the felt
impossibility of letting his guard down when he now understood, viscerally, that nothing guaranteed
the next moment. He startled violently at ordinary sounds — a door closing in the hallway of his
apartment, a phone ringing across the room — and his nights were fractured, sleep arriving only
briefly before anxiety pulled him back to wakefulness. His nervous system had reorganised itself
around a new premise: that danger was ambient and constant.

These shattered beliefs about how the world worked drove Arjun toward a set of strategies that felt,
at first, like reasonable self-protection. He stopped making plans. He had been the kind of person
who mapped out months in advance — deadlines, trips, ambitions — but any attempt to project himself
into the future now produced a nauseating sense of futility. Why plan, when plans assumed a
continuity that Rohan's death had proved was not guaranteed? He also began systematically avoiding
anything that reminded him of his loss: he stopped listening to the music they had shared, stopped
visiting the neighbourhood where they had grown up, and found himself unable to open the folder on
his laptop where he had saved Rohan's messages. As a writer, this avoidance carried a particular
cost. His work had always drawn on emotional depth and autobiographical resonance, but now whole
territories of feeling were cordoned off. He had missed two submission deadlines and withdrawn from
a mentorship programme he had worked hard to join. His editor, who had once described him as
unusually mature for his age, had begun asking carefully worded questions about whether he was
managing. He was not.

Alongside the avoidance, Arjun had developed a pattern of seeking reassurance from friends and
family — texting his mother after any period of silence, asking friends repeatedly whether they
thought he was coping normally, whether they thought he seemed okay. Yet each reassurance faded
within hours, because the underlying conviction that something was irreparably wrong with him and
with the world was not touched by it. He had lost the person he used to be — he described this
plainly: he used to be happy, and now he was always miserable, and he was no longer sure the earlier
version of himself was recoverable. This belief that he was permanently changed fed directly back
into the avoidance and the hypervigilance: if he was broken and the world was dangerous, then
planning was futile and relaxation was irresponsible, and so the strategies that were meant to
protect him instead kept confirming the appraisals that made them feel necessary. The reassurance-
seeking left him feeling more dependent and less capable; the avoidance of grief reminders meant the
loss was never processed, only circled. His sleep remained poor, his startle response remained hair-
trigger, and the sense of safety he had once taken entirely for granted felt further away with each
passing week.

What finally brought Arjun to seek help was a conversation with his mother in which she wept and
told him she was frightened she was losing him too. He had not realised, until that moment, how
visible his withdrawal had become. He arrived at the intake session quiet and precise, choosing his
words carefully in the way writers do. What he most feared was that nothing would change. What he
most hoped, though he said it almost as an aside, was that it might be possible to remember Rohan
without it destroying him.
