Amara is a 21-year-old woman of African descent who works as a counselor at a community youth center
in a mid-sized American city. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 80, seeking help approximately
eight months after returning from a humanitarian mission in a conflict zone where she was embedded
with a relief organization operating near active combat. What she witnessed there — and what was
done in the darkness around her — has not released her.

The incident that fractured her sense of self occurred during a night operation gone
catastrophically wrong. Amara and several colleagues had been sheltering in a compound when fighting
broke out nearby. The power failed. In the complete darkness of the building, she heard the
unmistakable percussion of helicopter rotors — gunships circling overhead — and in the chaos that
followed, a colleague she had been speaking with moments earlier was killed. She survived. The
darkness, the sound of those rotors, and the helplessness she felt in those minutes became the
architecture of what would haunt her.

In the weeks that followed her return, ordinary life became a minefield of sensation. Helicopter
sounds — a news broadcast, a police chopper passing over the youth center where she now worked with
teenagers — would pull her back into that compound instantly, her body flooding with the same terror
she had felt pinned in the dark. She began avoiding the TV room at the center and stopped watching
the news entirely, unwilling to risk the footage of conflict zones or the distant sound of rotor
blades that sometimes accompanied such reports. She stopped seeing the friends she had known before
the mission, people who had shared her world before everything changed, because their presence felt
unbearable — a reminder of who she used to be. When a colleague's offhand comment referenced her
family's history of service and sacrifice, something in her chest caved in; family-related comments,
however benign, had become threaded with grief and accusation she could not fully articulate.

These avoidances, initially experienced as relief, began to calcify into something more corrosive.
By withdrawing from friends, from media, from any new connection, Amara removed the very contexts in
which she might have encountered evidence that she was still herself. Instead, the silence she had
constructed became a chamber in which her worst beliefs about herself grew louder. She had come to
believe, with a certainty that felt almost physical, that there was something intrinsic to her —
some flaw in her character, her judgment, her very nature — that had made the event happen to her.
She was certain that someone stronger, more capable, more deserving of survival would never have
found herself in that situation. These convictions did not feel like distortions to her; they felt
like the only honest accounting of the evidence.

And the beliefs fed back into the avoidance. If she was fundamentally broken, what was the point of
starting new friendships or relationships? New people would eventually see what she suspected was
true about her. At the youth center, where her job required her to be present, emotionally
available, and relationally warm with young people navigating their own crises, she found herself
going through the motions with a growing sense of fraudulence. She had once been defined by her
capacity to connect, to hold space for others' pain — and she was certain she no longer possessed
that capacity. This conviction made her pull back further, cancel plans, deflect personal questions,
keep every interaction at a managed distance.

The withdrawal itself, however, kept the triggers potent. Because she never stayed long enough in
any social situation to let her nervous system recalibrate, the sound of a helicopter on someone's
phone, a darkened hallway at the center after hours, a passing comment about family — each retained
its full charge, undiminished. The loop had been running for months before she recognized it as a
loop at all.

What finally brought Amara in was a moment at work when a fourteen-year-old client asked her,
gently, if she was okay. She had been unable to answer. She is most afraid that the counselor she
was before the mission is simply gone. What she is hoping, tentatively and with considerable doubt,
is that someone might tell her otherwise.
