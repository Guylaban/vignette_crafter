Martin is a 54-year-old civil servant of British descent, currently in a relationship, who presented
for psychological assessment following what he described as a prolonged period of feeling "not quite
right in my own skin." His PCL-5 score of 59 places him firmly in the severe range for post-
traumatic stress, and the clinical picture that emerged across intake and early sessions points to a
presentation rooted in physical abuse perpetrated by a former male partner — a relationship Martin
had entered in his late forties, at a point in his life when he considered himself settled and self-
aware enough to recognise danger. He had not recognised it quickly enough.

The abuse unfolded over approximately two years and included incidents of significant physical
violence. One episode in particular appears to have become the organising centre of his traumatic
memory: he had been alone with his partner in their shared flat when an argument escalated without
warning. Martin recalled being struck repeatedly, losing his footing, and at some point becoming
aware of a metallic taste in his mouth — blood from a split lip — while the room seemed to freeze
around him. What struck him most, in the retelling, was not the pain but the absolute loss of any
agency: he could not move, could not speak, could not make it stop. That sense of helplessness
became the emotional signature of the event.

Martin's memory of what happened is unusual in its texture. He cannot access it deliberately — when
he sits down and tries to think through what occurred, the sequence eludes him, as though the
narrative has been filed somewhere without an index. Yet when the memory returns uninvited, it does
so with startling sensory completeness: he finds himself tasting blood again, that same flat copper
weight on his tongue, and the images arrive not as a film but as a series of frozen frames —
photographic, still, hyper-detailed — a bruised forearm, the pattern of the carpet, his partner's
expression. The involuntary nature of these returns is disorienting precisely because the voluntary
access is so blocked.

It is these intrusive moments that have come to govern much of Martin's daily architecture. Certain
situations act as reliable ignitions: being alone in a room, sleeping without another person nearby,
and most acutely, finding himself alone in the presence of a man. In his role as a civil servant,
this last trigger is an occupational reality he cannot fully avoid — corridor conversations, meeting
rooms, a colleague closing a door. Each such moment carries the possibility of the memory
reasserting itself, and when it does, the taste returns first, then the photographs, then the
feeling of the floor giving way beneath him.

To manage this, Martin has built a set of strategies that feel, to him, entirely rational. He
detaches himself from the memories when they begin to surface — a kind of internal stepping-back
that he describes as "going somewhere else for a moment" — and he has arranged his social life so
that he is almost never alone with his thoughts. He keeps himself surrounded by people: colleagues,
his partner, friends he calls in the evenings. He will not remain in a building alone. These
strategies, however, have begun to generate their own problems. The constant socialising means the
memories are never processed, never examined; they remain raw and unintegrated, which has made the
involuntary returns more rather than less frequent. The detachment, similarly, prevents the kind of
reflective engagement that might reduce the memories' charge, and instead the triggers retain their
full potency. There is a loop Martin has not yet named but lives inside: the strategies that protect
him from the memories also ensure the memories keep returning, and the memories keep feeding the
triggers that make the strategies feel necessary.

Over the past year, Martin's partner has noticed the changes — the refusal to be home alone, the
insistence on company at what seem like odd hours, a certain flinching quality that appears without
apparent cause. Martin has not explained it fully. What finally brought him to seek help was a
moment at work when a male colleague closed an office door and Martin felt, with absolute certainty,
that something terrible was about to happen. Nothing did. But the certainty had been total, and that
frightened him more than anything else. He is hoping, cautiously, that he might be able to eat a
meal without tasting blood.
