Harold is a 77-year-old married man who has worked as a delivery driver for most of his adult life,
a job he has always taken quiet pride in — the independence of the road, the predictability of
routes, the small dignities of showing up on time and doing things right. That sense of reliable
order was shattered several months ago when he was assaulted during a late-afternoon delivery in a
neighbourhood he had driven through hundreds of times before. A man approached him at the back of
his truck, demanded his phone and the cash in his shirt pocket, and when Harold hesitated — not out
of defiance but out of sheer disbelief that this was actually happening — he was struck twice across
the face and shoved hard against the vehicle's rear door. The whole episode lasted under two
minutes. Harold drove the rest of his route in a daze, hands trembling on the wheel, and did not
tell his wife what had happened until three days later.

In the weeks that followed, Harold found that the world he thought he understood had quietly ceased
to make sense. He had lived nearly eight decades on the assumption that if you kept your head down,
worked honestly, and treated people decently, you would be more or less safe — that the social
contract, however imperfect, held. The assault had not merely frightened him; it had demolished
something foundational. He had come to believe, in a way he could not fully articulate but felt
constantly, that his judgement about people and situations could no longer be trusted. Standing at a
customer's door, reading someone's expression, deciding whether to linger or move on — these had
once been automatic. Now they felt like gambles he was certain to lose. The self-doubt was not
abstract. It was in his hands when he reached for a package, in his eyes scanning a driveway, in the
half-second pause before he knocked. He was no longer sure he would do the right thing when it
mattered, and that uncertainty sat in him like a stone.

What frightened Harold most about the memory itself was the conviction that engaging with it would
undo him entirely. He was certain that if he allowed himself to think about the assault — to replay
the sound of the impact, the cold metal of the truck door against his back — he would not be able to
hold himself together, that something would break that could not be repaired. So he did not let
himself go there. He worked longer hours than his body probably warranted for a man his age, filling
every available hour with the motion of driving, loading, unloading, checking manifests. Busyness
had become a kind of architecture he built around himself each day, and when the routes ended and
the quiet came, he ate — more than he needed, more than felt good — because the fullness was
something physical to attend to, something that briefly displaced the formless dread. His wife had
noticed the weight he was putting on and had gently said something twice; Harold had changed the
subject both times.

These strategies — the relentless movement, the overeating, the deliberate suppression of any
thought that veered too close to that afternoon — were not arbitrary. They were the direct
consequence of what Harold had come to believe about himself and the world. Because he was convinced
that thinking about the event would destroy him, he suppressed it. Because he no longer trusted his
own responses to be adequate, he kept himself too busy to be tested. The appraisals drove the
coping, and the coping, in its way, preserved the appraisals intact — never examined, never
challenged, growing heavier with each passing week.

The concrete cost had become impossible to ignore. Harold had begun refusing certain delivery zones
— areas that resembled, in some quality of light or street layout, the neighbourhood where the
assault occurred. His dispatcher had noticed the pattern and raised it with him directly last week.
It was that conversation, more than anything, that brought Harold to seek help. He was not ready to
lose the work. Driving was who he was. What he most hoped for, sitting in the intake chair, was some
way to feel like himself again on the road — and what he most feared was that the person he had been
before that afternoon was simply gone.
