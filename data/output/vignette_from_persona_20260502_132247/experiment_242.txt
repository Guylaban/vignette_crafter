Rohan came to therapy at twenty-six, referred by his primary care physician after months of
declining function and a PCL-5 score of 56. He is a magazine editor, married for two years, and
until recently had described his life as quietly purposeful. That sense of purpose collapsed
following an encounter with police officers approximately eight months ago, during a late-night stop
on his walk home from the office. What began as a request for identification escalated without
warning: Rohan was pushed against a wall, his face pressed into the cold concrete, arms wrenched
behind his back while officers shouted at him. He recalls the particular bite of the winter air on
his exposed wrists and neck, the smell of exhaust and damp pavement, and the sensation of his body
locked rigid and helpless, chest crushed flat, cheek against the ground. A female officer stood
nearby, watching. He was released without charge. No one apologised. He walked the rest of the way
home in silence and told his wife only that he had been stopped. He did not yet have language for
what had happened to him.

In the weeks that followed, the memories did not stay in the past. When they returned, they came
with the body. Rohan would find himself suddenly aware that his shoulders were drawn back and his
cheek was pressed toward the floor, his muscles reproducing the exact posture of that night without
his permission. Sometimes the smell of exhaust or damp concrete would arrive with the image — not as
metaphor but as sensation, immediate and physical. And the memories were not neutral; they carried
with them a grief so total and disorienting that he would sit at his desk unable to work, certain
that something irreplaceable had been destroyed. These intrusive returns of the event fed directly
into a darkening set of conclusions about himself and his future. He had come to believe, with a
conviction that felt like clear-eyed realism rather than distortion, that his inability to move past
what had happened proved he was a lousy coper — that other men would have recovered by now, filed it
away, continued. The grief on recall only confirmed this: a stronger person, he reasoned, would not
still be crying. From this belief that he was fundamentally inadequate, a broader despair followed —
nothing in his life felt meaningful anymore, and he was certain that nothing good could happen to
him. The work he had once loved felt hollow. His marriage, though his wife remained patient, felt
like something he was failing to inhabit fully.

These appraisals did not simply colour his mood; they shaped what his nervous system attended to.
Convinced that he was permanently damaged and that the world held nothing restorative, Rohan found
himself hypervigilant to the very cues that had been present during the assault. A drop in
temperature — the particular cold of winter air against skin — could pull him back to the wall, to
the concrete, to the smell. At work, the editorial office is open-plan and he frequently needs to
review copy with female colleagues in small meeting rooms. Being alone with a woman had become
unexpectedly charged: the female officer's silent presence during the assault had lodged in him as
something threatening, and now proximity to women triggered a creeping sense of detachment, as
though the room were becoming unreal and he were watching himself from a slight distance. That
dissociative feeling, once it began, itself became a trigger — a signal to Rohan that he was losing
his grip again, which confirmed his belief that he could not cope, which in turn sharpened his
vigilance and made the next temperature shift or the next meeting with a female colleague more
loaded than the last. The loop was not named but it was felt: each triggered memory deepened the
appraisal of permanent damage, and each appraisal of permanent damage narrowed the world until
almost everything in it could become a trigger.

What finally brought Rohan to seek help was a morning when he sat at his desk for three hours and
edited nothing. His wife had noticed he was not sleeping. He came to therapy saying he did not
expect to feel better — he was not sure he believed that was still possible — but that he could not
continue to disappear from his own life.
