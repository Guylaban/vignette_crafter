Rajan is a 50-year-old South Asian American animator, married for twenty-three years, who was
referred for psychological assessment following a prolonged period of distress that he traces back
to a single, devastating stretch of time roughly two years ago, when his youngest son was admitted
to the intensive care unit with a diagnosis of acute bacterial meningitis. The boy was seven years
old. For eleven days, Rajan slept in a reclining chair beside the hospital bed, watching nurses
adjust IV lines, listening to monitors, breathing in the sharp, antiseptic smell that permeated
every corridor. On the fourth night, the attending physician pulled him aside and told him, with
careful professional restraint, that the next forty-eight hours would be critical. Rajan stood in
the hallway under fluorescent light and understood, for the first time in his life, that his child
might die. His son survived, and recovered fully. Rajan has not.

In the months that followed the discharge, Rajan began noticing that certain things undid him in
ways he could not explain to his wife or to himself. The smell of antiseptic — encountered when he
accompanied his son to a routine pediatric follow-up, or when a colleague at the animation studio
mentioned using hand sanitizer — would arrive before he had time to brace, pulling him back to that
corridor, that fluorescent light, that carefully restrained voice. Medical appointments of any kind,
including his own annual physical, became almost impossible to attend. He sat in waiting rooms
certain that the examination itself would confirm something catastrophic, that the ordinary rituals
of clinical care were preludes to another crisis. When business travel required him to stay in a
hotel room alone, the unfamiliarity of the space — the wrong sounds, the wrong light, the absence of
his wife beside him — would stretch into sleeplessness, his mind running over every worst-case
scenario he could construct, as though thorough mental preparation might protect him from outcomes
he could no longer trust the world to withhold.

It is this last habit that has come to govern much of Rajan's inner life. When a trigger strikes —
the smell, a stethoscope, the silence of an unfamiliar room — he finds himself almost immediately
rehearsing catastrophe: his son relapsing, a new diagnosis, something missed. He had come to
believe, with a logic that felt entirely reasonable to him, that if he thought through the worst in
sufficient detail, he would not be ambushed by it again. The rehearsal felt like vigilance, like
responsible fatherhood. But the rehearsal itself, playing out in vivid, granular detail, kept the
sense of threat alive and immediate, so that the next time he encountered the hospital smell on a
colleague's hands, or found himself alone in a hotel room during a conference, the triggers landed
with undiminished force. The loop had tightened over months without his noticing: the triggers
feeding the mental rehearsal, the rehearsal ensuring that no trigger ever lost its potency.

Alongside this, Rajan frequently seeks reassurance from his wife, calling her from work to confirm
that the children are well, asking her repeatedly whether she thinks his own health is stable. She
answers patiently, though the calls have increased in frequency, and she has begun gently suggesting
that he speak to someone. At the studio, his concentration has suffered. He works alone for long
stretches, animating sequences that require sustained imaginative focus, and colleagues have noticed
that he is slower to deliver, quicker to apologise, and rarely stays for the informal conversations
that once anchored his working day. There is also, present but not central to the picture, a strand
of bitter anger he has not spoken about to anyone: a recurring, almost obsessive preoccupation with
the hospital administrator who he felt dismissed his questions during those eleven days, a
rumination about accountability, about what it would mean to make that indifference felt.

What finally brought Rajan to seek help was a panic response during his own medical examination last
month — a routine blood draw that he could not complete. He left the clinic without explanation, sat
in his car for forty minutes, and drove home. He is most afraid, he said at intake, that he will
never stop waiting for something terrible to happen. What he hopes for, more quietly, is to sleep
beside his wife without checking, in the dark, whether his son is still breathing.
