Hinerangi is a 75-year-old Māori woman, married and working as an auditor, who was referred for
psychological assessment following a significant deterioration in her functioning over the past
eighteen months. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 74, indicating severe posttraumatic stress,
rooted in an experience she lived through as a civilian caught in an active war zone during a period
of sustained urban conflict several decades ago. Though she had carried the weight of that time
quietly for years, the symptoms have sharpened into something she can no longer manage alone.

small group of civilians while fighting moved through the surrounding streets. The air was thick and
unmoving — the smell of unwashed bodies pressed together in fear, the sweat of people who had not
slept, who did not know if they would survive. She remembers the quality of those hours not as a
continuous narrative but as a series of still, vivid images — photographic, exact, and merciless —
the face of a man crouching beside her, the particular angle of light through a broken shutter. What
she cannot do, even now, is speak about it coherently. She knows what happened. She simply cannot
find the words, and this silence has sat in her marriage and her friendships like an uninvited
presence.

The triggers she encounters in daily life are not abstract. At work — where she spends long hours
reviewing financial records in open-plan offices — the ambient smell of a colleague's sweat on a
warm afternoon is enough to pull her back without warning. The olfactory recall is immediate and
involuntary: she can smell that building again, and the photographs begin. These sensory intrusions
do not merely distress her; they activate a set of beliefs she has come to hold about herself with
quiet certainty. She is convinced that others can see what happened to her simply by looking at her
— that her face, her stillness, her eyes betray something broken — and this conviction floods in
alongside the memories, each one reinforcing the other. When the memories surface, they carry the
appraisals with them, and when she catches someone glancing at her across the office, the appraisals
summon the memories back.

What frightens her most is the belief that she will never feel normal emotions again — that
happiness itself has become suspect. Feeling relaxed or content, when it briefly occurs, does not
bring relief; it brings dread, as though joy is a signal that her guard has dropped and something
terrible is imminent. This appraisal has shaped her threat monitoring into something exhausting and
constant. She will not sit with her back to a room. She checks behind her when she walks the
corridor to the bathroom at work, certain someone is there. She takes risks that she herself
recognises as reckless — a fleeting observation she cannot fully explain — as though some part of
her has quietly decided the question of her survival is already settled. The memories, in turn, are
kept raw by this state of hypervigilance; the body primed for danger keeps the past present.

Over the months, Hinerangi developed habits she understood as protective. She rehearses every
workplace conversation before it happens, scripting her words so carefully that she can avoid any
moment of unguarded disclosure. She ruminates at length about whether the event could have been
prevented, going over and over the choices made and not made, why it happened to her specifically.
These strategies, meant to contain the past, have instead kept it circulating. The rumination does
not resolve into meaning; it refreshes the memory, and with the memory come the appraisals — that
her life may not be worth living, that she is permanently altered — and these beliefs feed directly
back into her vigilance and her sense of imminent threat. The loop has tightened over eighteen
months until even her marriage has felt the strain; her husband describes her as somewhere else,
even when she is beside him.

It was her husband who finally persuaded her to seek help. Hinerangi came to the first session
composed and precise, the auditor's habit of careful presentation intact. What she most hopes for,
she said quietly, is to sit in a room without watching the door. What she most fears is that it is
already too late for that.
