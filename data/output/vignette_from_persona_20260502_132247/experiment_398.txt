Minh Tran is a 74-year-old Vietnamese American man who has worked as a welder for most of his adult
life, arriving in the United States as a refugee in the late 1970s after surviving the fall of
Saigon and its brutal aftermath. He is married, lives in a modest home in a mid-sized city, and
until recently continued part-time welding work at a small fabrication shop — a trade he had carried
with him across oceans and decades. He presented for psychological assessment after his wife,
increasingly alarmed by his deteriorating sleep and his habit of sitting alone in the dark after
nightmares, insisted he speak with someone.

The events Minh describes — haltingly, and only in fragments — took place during a period of intense
urban fighting in 1975, when he was a young man in his mid-twenties. He was caught in a district
under bombardment, sheltering with several neighbours in a building that was struck. The sound of
artillery and collapsing masonry was deafening. In the chaos that followed, he ran through open
streets under fire, struggling to breathe through smoke and dust, watching people fall around him.
He survived; others he had been sheltering with did not. The specific texture of that flight — the
roar of vehicles, the open sky above exposed streets, the sensation of his lungs seizing — is
precisely what makes his present environment so treacherous. When a car backfires or accelerates
sharply near the fabrication shop where he still works, Minh's body responds before his mind can
intervene: his chest tightens, his breathing becomes laboured, and he is pulled back into the
streets of 1975 with a force that feels less like remembering and more like returning. The memory,
when it comes, is fragmentary and strange to him — he describes it as though it happened to someone
else, a story so extreme it is hard to believe it belongs to his own life, with whole sections
simply missing, as if the tape had been cut.

Because the memory arrives in pieces and feels alien to him, Minh has never been able to construct a
coherent account of what happened, and this incompleteness keeps the past perpetually unresolved and
close. The fragmented quality of his recall means that certain sensory details — a rushing engine,
the feeling of breathlessness, an image glimpsed on a television news broadcast showing bombed
streets — carry disproportionate emotional charge, functioning as portals rather than reminders.
When these triggers activate, Minh immediately redirects his attention, forcing himself to think
about his welding work, the grain of metal under his hands, anything concrete and present. He avoids
open spaces where he feels exposed — he no longer walks to the corner store and plans his routes to
avoid wide intersections — and he refuses to talk with anyone, including his wife, about what he
witnessed. These strategies have provided short-term relief but have ensured that the memory remains
unprocessed and unintegrated, preserving its alien, fragmented character and allowing it to continue
generating triggers without context or resolution.

Minh carries a corrosive sense of injustice that has deepened over five decades. He is certain that
what was done to the civilians of his city was profoundly unfair, that those responsible have never
been held to account, and — most painfully — that he bears some portion of blame himself, convinced
that a different decision, a faster turn, might have saved the neighbours who died beside him. These
appraisals are not abstract; they surface as a persistent, low-grade fury and a self-directed shame
that together keep him in a state of chronic threat. He has come to believe that the world remains
fundamentally dangerous and that he cannot afford to lower his guard, which manifests as difficulty
concentrating at the welding bench, hypervigilance in any public setting, and an inability to fall
asleep without first scanning the house. This sustained sense of threat, in turn, makes ordinary
sounds — a car accelerating outside the shop, a door slamming — register as potential danger,
feeding back into the cycle of intrusion and avoidance. His refusal to speak about the events has
also allowed his self-blame to go unchallenged, hardening over years into something that feels like
established fact rather than distorted appraisal.

What finally brought Minh to seek help was a moment at work: a delivery truck backfired in the yard
outside the shop, and he dropped to the floor behind his workbench, unable to speak for several
minutes while a younger colleague looked on. He said nothing to explain himself, drove home, and sat
in the dark until morning. What he fears most is that he will never be free of it. What he hopes,
though he can barely articulate it, is that someone might help him make the memory feel like his
own.
