Linh is a 25-year-old Vietnamese-American woman who works as a database administrator at a mid-sized
logistics firm. She is married and was referred for psychological assessment following a life-
threatening motor vehicle collision that occurred approximately eight months ago. Her PCL-5 score of
74 places her firmly in the severe range, and across several intake sessions she has described a
presentation that has progressively tightened around her, narrowing the boundaries of her daily life
in ways she is only beginning to name.

The collision occurred on a wet highway evening when Linh was a passenger in a car driven by a close
friend. A dispute had broken out between them moments before the other vehicle crossed the centre
line — an argument about plans, about trust, about something that had felt urgent and then became
irrelevant in an instant. The car rolled. Linh's friend was critically injured. Linh was not. In the
weeks that followed, she became convinced that the argument had been the proximate cause — that her
agitation, her raised voice, her refusal to let the matter drop had distracted her friend at the
worst possible moment. She had come to believe, with a certainty that resisted all counter-evidence,
that the event happened because of the way she acted. Alongside this sat a guilt she described as
bottomless: she had not done more to pull her friend from the wreckage before help arrived, and she
was certain she would never forgive herself for that inaction. Over time, a broader mistrust had
calcified — she had come to feel that people, including those closest to her, could not be relied
upon, that care was conditional and could be withdrawn without warning.

These appraisals did not stay abstract. When they surfaced, Linh found herself reaching for whatever
would interrupt them fastest. She began avoiding situations that placed any physical demand on her
body — she stopped the gym, declined walks, sat very still during her commute — as though physical
stress might crack something open. More significantly, she started withdrawing from friends,
cancelling plans, declining calls, until her social world contracted to almost nothing. And on the
worst evenings, when the guilt and self-blame pressed hardest, she hurt herself physically to stop
the thinking — a behaviour she disclosed quietly, with evident shame, as though naming it confirmed
something she feared about herself.

The avoidance itself became a new source of exposure. At work, where Linh spends long hours managing
server infrastructure in an open-plan office, she cannot always control what she hears. A colleague
whose name sounds similar to her injured friend's triggers an immediate, full-bodied return to the
highway — the sound of breaking glass, the smell of rain on metal. Arguments in the office, even
low-level disagreements between colleagues across the room, land on her like physical blows, pulling
her back into the moment before impact when she was still shouting and the road was still wet. Any
uncertainty — an ambiguous email, an unresolved ticket, a manager whose tone she cannot read —
carries the same charge, because uncertainty now feels structurally identical to the seconds before
the car began to roll. These triggers do not merely distress her; they confirm what she already
believes. Each intrusion feels like evidence that she is right to blame herself, right not to trust,
right to expect that things will go wrong because of something she has done.

The strategies that were meant to protect her have begun to generate the very contacts she is trying
to avoid. Withdrawing from friends means she is alone with her thoughts more often, and loneliness
surfaces the guilt with nothing to dilute it. Avoiding physical stress has made her hypervigilant to
bodily sensation, so that any minor tension reads as threat. The self-harm, which briefly interrupts
the cognitive spiral, leaves her afterwards with a deepened sense that she is someone who cannot be
trusted — least of all by herself — which feeds directly back into the appraisals she was trying to
escape.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was her husband's quiet, frightened observation that she had
not laughed in months. She came to the first session saying she did not expect to be believed. What
she hopes for, she said after a long pause, is to stop being the person who caused it. What she
fears most is that she already is.
