in the Central Valley of California for nearly two decades. He came to the clinic at his wife's

man whose inner life had been profoundly reorganised by a single act of violence.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Tariq was attacked by a man he knew only loosely — a dispute over
a boundary fence that had simmered for years before it turned physical. The assault happened at
dusk, at the far edge of his property where no one could easily see or hear. The man came up behind
him, and what Tariq remembers most vividly from that moment — before memory fractures into
disconnected shards — is the sharp, sour smell of the other man's sweat and the sensation of his own
heart slamming against his ribs as he struggled to break free. The attack left him with bruised ribs
and a laceration, but the physical injuries healed in weeks. What did not heal was the way those two
sensory details — the smell of sweat, the pounding in his chest — had been burned into him as
signals of mortal danger.

In the months that followed, Tariq found that his own body had become unreliable. When he worked
through the heat of the day and felt his heart rate climb, or caught the smell of his own
perspiration, something in him would snap back to that dusk at the fence line, not as a coherent
recollection but as a wave of alarm without clear shape. He also became acutely watchful of other
people; when a farmhand lingered too long near a piece of equipment, or when a stranger moved in a
way that seemed to lack a clear purpose, Tariq felt certain that something was about to happen.
These moments of triggered arousal did not simply pass — they fed directly into a pattern of
behaviour that, over time, became entrenched. When his heart raced and the fear surged, he would
throw himself back into physical labour with an urgency that had nothing to do with the work itself.
He drove the tractor longer than necessary, repaired fences that did not need repairing, and stayed
in the fields past dark — not because the farm demanded it, but because motion and exhaustion were
the only things that quieted the alarm. His wife noticed that he was no longer coming to dinner,
that he had stopped sitting with her in the evenings, and that the distance between them had grown
into something she could not name.

The memory itself presents its own difficulty and adds its own fuel to these patterns. Tariq cannot
tell the story of the assault in a way that holds together. He begins, loses the thread, and stops —
not because he is unwilling but because the event does not exist for him as a continuous narrative.
It comes in pieces: the smell, the weight of a hand on his shoulder, a sound he cannot identify,
then nothing, then the ground. When he tries to make sense of it, the whole thing feels strangely
foreign, as though it happened to a man he once knew rather than to himself. This alienation from
the memory does not bring relief; instead, it leaves him with an unresolved anger that has nowhere
to go. He finds himself rehearsing, in elaborate detail, what he would do if he encountered the man
again — mapping out confrontations, calculating responses. The rumination does not resolve anything,
but it feels, at least, like a form of agency in the face of something that still does not fully
feel real to him. He avoids the anniversary of the attack with careful deliberateness, rerouting his
schedule for that entire week.

What finally brought Tariq to the clinic was a morning when he felt his heart race during routine
irrigation work and spent the next four hours unable to leave the tractor cab. He told the intake
clinician, quietly, that he did not recognise himself anymore. What he is most afraid of, he said,
is that this is simply who he is now.
