Leilani Fono is a thirty-one-year-old Samoan-American woman who works as a professional dancer with
a contemporary company in Los Angeles. She is married and presents for her first psychological
consultation with a PCL-5 score of 44, describing a constellation of symptoms she has struggled to
name for most of her adult life but which have recently intensified to the point of interfering with
her work and her marriage.

Leilani grew up in a household governed by her father's violence. From approximately age five
through her early adolescence, she witnessed repeated episodes of her father assaulting her mother —
shouting that built into physical confrontations, her mother's body pressed against the kitchen
wall, the particular sound of impact, and the silence that followed. What stayed with Leilani most
viscerally was not only the violence itself but the way her father would use her mother's name — a
soft, three-syllable name — immediately before the worst episodes, almost as a summons. She learned,
without ever consciously articulating it, that a name could precede annihilation. She also
remembered the feeling of being watched from the hallway, her own small body frozen in the
doorframe, certain that if she moved she would either be hurt or cause something worse to happen.
Her heart would pound so hard she was sure it was audible.

These sensory details did not remain safely in the past. In the years since childhood, Leilani has
found that hearing names phonetically similar to her mother's can instantly collapse the distance
between then and now, the full memory returning without warning — not as a narrative she retrieves
but as something that happens to her body before her mind has caught up. During rehearsals, when a
choreographer calls out instructions to another dancer whose name carries a similar sound, Leilani
has found herself suddenly back in that hallway, her chest tightening, her heart racing in precisely
the way it did at seven years old. The somatic alarm of her own heart pounding has itself become a
trigger, a signal her nervous system reads as confirmation that danger is present, and in those
moments the memory floods in with the full emotional weight of the original scene.

What the memories carry, when they arrive, is not only fear but a corrosive shame. When Leilani
recalls what she witnessed, she does not experience herself as a child who survived something
terrible; she experiences herself as someone implicated in it. She has come to believe, in a way she
finds difficult to articulate but impossible to dismiss, that there is something about her — some
quality she was born with or failed to develop — that made the violence happen, or at least that
meant she could not stop it. This sense of intrinsic fault shapes how she reads the world around
her. She is certain that people are not what they seem, that warmth conceals agenda, and that the
trust she extended as a child was met with betrayal by the very adults who were supposed to protect
her. These appraisals do not stay abstract. They colour every interaction in the dance studio, where
being watched — by directors, by mirrors, by audiences during performance — carries a charge that
other dancers do not seem to feel. The sensation of being observed reactivates the old vigilance of
the hallway, and with it the shame and the distrust, as though being seen is itself a form of
exposure to judgment or harm.

The loop has tightened over the past year. The more Leilani's appraisals tell her that observation
means danger and that something about her invites harm, the more acutely she monitors her
environment for the triggers that confirm this — a name, a pair of eyes on her during a rehearsal
run-through, the first flutter of her own pulse. And the more those triggers fire, the more her
negative appraisals feel like facts rather than interpretations. Occasionally the memories arrive in
a different register entirely — flat, affectless, as though she is watching a stranger's childhood
on a screen — and in those moments she feels neither relief nor distance, only a dissociated
blankness that frightens her in its own way.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was a performance last month in which she froze mid-
sequence when a stagehand called out a name from the wings. She left the stage, could not explain
why to her director, and drove home in silence. She told her husband only that she was tired. She is
most afraid that what happened to her family has marked her in some permanent and invisible way.
What she is hoping for, tentatively, is to be able to stand in front of an audience and feel nothing
more threatening than the ordinary vulnerability of being seen.
