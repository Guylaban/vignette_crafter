Kwame is a 62-year-old married African American man who has spent the better part of four decades as
a bomb disposal technician — a career built on precision, composure, and the willingness to walk
toward danger that others run from. He presented for psychological assessment following increasing
concern from his wife and his unit supervisor, both of whom had noticed a marked deterioration in
his functioning over the preceding eighteen months. His PCL-5 score of 57 places him firmly in the
severe range, and the clinical picture that emerged across the intake and early sessions was one of
a man whose professional identity had become both a refuge and a trap.

The precipitating event occurred approximately two years ago during a routine inspection of a
suspected improvised device at an industrial site. What appeared to be a standard render-safe
procedure became something else entirely when Kwame detected an unexpected chemical odour — sharp,
synthetic, nothing he could immediately classify — seconds before a partial detonation released a
cloud of toxic gas. He was evacuated and hospitalised for eleven days. The ward smelled of
antiseptic and machinery. Nurses moved quickly around him. Alarms sounded at intervals he could not
predict. He was told, at one point, that had he been standing two feet closer, the exposure would
have been fatal. He was discharged with a clean bill of physical health, and he returned to work
within six weeks. He told no one that anything was wrong.

The symptoms announced themselves gradually. Back at the disposal unit, the ambient smells of the
workplace — industrial solvents, metalwork, the particular chemical tang of protective equipment —
began to carry something they had not carried before. On two occasions in his first month back,
Kwame found himself frozen at his workstation, heart hammering, certain that he was again breathing
something toxic. The hospital smell — antiseptic, sterile, faintly sweet — became equally
unbearable; a routine physical examination eight months after the incident triggered such acute
distress that he left the clinic before it was completed, and he has not returned since. Loud,
sudden noises — a dropped canister, a vehicle backfiring in the yard — produced an immediate, full-
body alarm response that he found humiliating and impossible to explain to younger colleagues. These
sensory triggers reliably activated a state of heightened threat perception that, once switched on,
was very difficult to switch off. He would spend the remainder of those shifts scanning the room
continuously, cataloguing exit points, tracking the movements of everyone around him, his body
certain that whatever had nearly killed him once was still present, still circling.

What Kwame had come to believe — quietly, without ever articulating it clearly — was that the world
had become genuinely less safe, and that his survival depended on his own vigilance. He began
mapping exits as a matter of course, in the canteen, in briefing rooms, at church. An unexpected
hand on his shoulder from a colleague once caused him to spin around with enough force to alarm the
man who had touched him. This persistent state of readiness fed back into the triggers themselves:
because he was always scanning, he was always finding things — a smell, a sound, a visual detail —
that confirmed his suspicion that danger was near, and the cycle tightened.

The strategies Kwame developed to manage this were logical to him, if costly. He began volunteering
for every available shift, arriving early and staying late, filling every hour with the structured
demands of work so that he would never be left alone in a room with his own thoughts. He avoided
being at home alone. He refused all medical appointments. But the overwork, paradoxical as it was,
kept him embedded in the very environment — the workplace smells, the sudden noises, the physical
proximity of colleagues who might brush against him — that sustained his threat monitoring. His
hypervigilance, fed by constant re-exposure, never fully subsided.

What finally brought Kwame to the clinic was his wife's ultimatum, delivered quietly one evening
after he had slept, for the fourth consecutive night, in his car rather than come inside to an empty
house. He arrived presenting as controlled and somewhat reluctant. What he feared most, he said near
the end of the first session, was that if he stopped working, stopped moving, stopped watching —
something would get through.
