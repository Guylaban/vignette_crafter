Marcus Holt, a 33-year-old prosecutor, had built his career on the belief that careful preparation
and sharp judgment could protect people from harm. That belief was shattered on a Saturday afternoon
eighteen months ago, when a bombing tore through a civic event he had attended with colleagues. He
had been standing near the perimeter of the gathering, having stepped away from the main crowd to
take a phone call, when the device detonated. The concussive blast threw him off his feet. When he
came to, the air was thick with smoke and the sharp, acrid smell of alcohol from overturned vendor
carts mixed with burning debris. Around him, people he recognized were injured. A junior colleague —
someone he had mentored — lay badly hurt thirty feet away. Marcus had been close enough to reach her
before the secondary chaos set in, and he had not moved fast enough. That moment of hesitation, that
paralyzed second before he acted, became the fixed point around which his entire inner life began to
orbit.

In the months that followed, Marcus found that the event had not settled into the past the way other
memories did. Instead, it remained strangely alive — more vivid and textured than anything else in
his life, as though the afternoon of the bombing had been filmed in high resolution while everything
before and after it was blurred. What was worse was that the memory did not feel finished. When it
surfaced, it did not arrive as a recollection of something over; it arrived as something happening
now, with the same urgency and terror as the original moment. He could not construct a coherent
account of what had happened even when he tried privately, alone in his office late at night. The
sequence broke apart — there were gaps, fragments, sensory shards that refused to assemble into a
story he could tell himself or anyone else.

These memory qualities fed directly into the self-condemnation that had taken root in Marcus's
thinking. Because the event could not be narrated cleanly, it could not be resolved or
contextualized, and the absence of resolution left his mind circling the same terrible conclusion:
that he had failed. He had come to believe, with the certainty he usually reserved for courtroom
arguments, that he should have protected the people around him and had not. He would never forgive
himself for not doing more. And because the memory felt present rather than past, each return to it
was not a review of history but a fresh experience of his own failure, reactivating the guilt and
shame as though no time had passed at all.

Marcus's daily life as a prosecutor placed him in environments where the triggers he had developed
were impossible to avoid. Courtroom proceedings sometimes ran long and grueling, and by late
afternoon he was often exhausted — and it was precisely in that state of fatigue that the bombing
felt closest. Feeling tired had become a cue his nervous system had learned to treat as danger, and
with the danger came the guilt: the immediate, almost automatic thought that he had been tired that
day too, that his slowness had been a moral failure rather than a physiological fact. At a
colleague's retirement gathering several months after the attack, someone nearby opened a bottle of
whiskey, and the smell hit Marcus before he could register it consciously. The room contracted. He
was back on the pavement, and with the sensory return came the familiar cascade — the image of his
colleague on the ground, the certainty that he had not been enough. Loud sounds in the courthouse —
a door slamming, a fire drill — could produce the same sudden collapse of distance between then and
now.

In the wake of these experiences, Marcus had grown quietly convinced that people were not what they
seemed — that the ordinary social world concealed threats that competent, vigilant people should be
able to detect. He had become harder to reach at home. His wife had noticed the withdrawal, the way
he scanned rooms, the cancelled dinners. He had not told her the full shape of what he was carrying.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment in court when a loud bang from a malfunctioning
projector sent him rigid at the prosecution table, visibly shaken in front of a jury. He came to the
first session hoping, above all, to be able to do his job again — and terrified that the version of
himself capable of that no longer existed.
