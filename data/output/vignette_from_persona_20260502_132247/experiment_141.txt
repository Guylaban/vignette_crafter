Marcus is a 53-year-old married social worker who presented for psychological assessment
approximately eight months after being sexually assaulted by an acquaintance in a parking garage
adjacent to a community services building where he had worked for nearly two decades. The assault
occurred late on a weeknight when Marcus had stayed after hours to complete case documentation. The
garage was poorly lit, the elevator had been out of service, and he had taken the stairwell — a
narrow, concrete enclosure that smelled of damp and exhaust — when the man, someone he recognised
from a client support group, cornered him between two floors. The confinement of that stairwell, the
footsteps that had closed in behind him before he understood what was happening, and the complete
absence of any route of escape left an imprint that now organises much of Marcus's daily life.

In the months that followed, Marcus found that remembering the assault did not feel like
recollection — it felt like return. When the memory surfaced, the terror he had experienced in that
stairwell returned with it at the same pitch and urgency, as though his nervous system had never
been informed that the event had ended. Alongside the terror came something he described as harder
to carry: an intense, wordless shame. He knows what happened. He can place it in time and sequence.
But when he tries to speak about it — to his wife, briefly to his primary care physician — the
language simply is not there. He stops mid-sentence, gestures vaguely, and changes the subject. The
memory, in this way, remains sealed and unprocessed, and it is precisely this quality — its rawness,
its resistance to narrative — that has made the memories themselves feel dangerous. Because
remembering produces terror and shame he cannot metabolise, Marcus has worked hard to push the
memories out of his mind the moment they appear, treating suppression as the most reasonable
available option. He has also begun spending long evening hours turning over, in exhaustive and
circular detail, what he might do to confront or expose the man who assaulted him — fantasies of
retribution that feel, in the moment, like the only form of agency available to him. These
strategies — suppression, rumination about revenge — emerged directly from the unbearable quality of
the memories themselves, an attempt to manage what he could not yet articulate.

The memories have also reshaped how Marcus reads the world around him. Having been attacked from
behind in a confined space he believed was safe, he now experiences the environment as fundamentally
unreadable and threatening. He scans constantly. At the office, in supermarkets, in corridors, he
checks behind him with a regularity that has begun to draw quiet comment from colleagues. Unfamiliar
settings — a new client's neighbourhood, a building he hasn't been to before — feel genuinely
dangerous because he cannot pre-assess them, and he has begun declining field visits that would have
been routine before. When a meeting runs long or a colleague is late to respond to a message, he
does not register it as ordinary delay; he assumes something bad is unfolding. This pervasive sense
of threat, in turn, drives him back toward the memories — scanning for details, trying to identify
what he missed, what warning he should have read — which only re-exposes him to the terror and shame
he is simultaneously trying to suppress. The loop is quiet but relentless. His threat monitoring,
meanwhile, has reinforced every avoidance behaviour: he no longer uses the parking garage at work,
refuses stairwells, and will not enter any enclosed or confined space if he can avoid it. These
restrictions have accumulated into something his wife has noticed — a man who used to be physically
easy in the world now mapping every room for exits before he can settle.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a supervision session in which he was unable to enter
the small conference room where the meeting was scheduled, standing in the doorway until a colleague
quietly suggested they move to a larger space. He said nothing that day, but he made the referral
call the following morning. What he fears most is that something essential has been permanently
altered in him. What he is hoping for, more cautiously, is to find words for what happened — to be
able to say it plainly, once, without the terror coming with it.
