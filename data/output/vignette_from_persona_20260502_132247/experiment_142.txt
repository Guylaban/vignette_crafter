Aleksander Nowak is a 34-year-old firefighter of Polish descent who has been married for six years.
He presents with a PCL-5 score of 40, seeking help for the first time after what he describes as
months of feeling like he is "living behind glass" — present in his life but unable to fully inhabit
it.

Several years ago, Aleksander volunteered through an international relief organization to spend
three months supporting emergency medical and fire-response operations in an active conflict zone in
Eastern Europe. He had gone in believing that his professional training would be sufficient
preparation. It was not. The environment bore almost no resemblance to the structured incident
command he knew from home. Buildings collapsed without warning. Convoys were redirected mid-route
because roads had been shelled. Strangers — civilians, fighters, aid workers — moved through
checkpoints in states of desperation or concealed intent that Aleksander could rarely read in time.
On one afternoon that remains vivid to him, he was inside a partially destroyed administrative
building coordinating a casualty evacuation when secondary shelling began. The stairwell he had
entered through was blocked. He spent nearly forty minutes in a ground-floor corridor, methodically
identifying every possible exit while the structure groaned above him, before he and two colleagues
found a way out through a blown-out wall. No one was killed. But something shifted in him that
afternoon that has not shifted back.

In the months following his return, Aleksander began organizing his environment in ways that felt,
to him, entirely rational. He stopped exercising — the elevated heart rate and shortness of breath
felt too close to what his body had done in that corridor, and he had come to associate those
sensations with immediate danger. He made sure to position himself near exits in any room he
entered: at the fire station, at restaurants, at his in-laws' home during Sunday dinners. He began
scanning doorways and windows as a matter of reflex, cataloguing routes before he had even taken off
his coat. What he understood less clearly was that these behaviors, rather than quieting the alarm,
were feeding it. By treating every environment as a potential trap that required a pre-planned
escape, Aleksander was continuously signaling to himself that the danger was real and present. The
vigilance his strategies were meant to manage had become inseparable from them. His sense of threat
had not diminished with time; it had been maintained, and in some respects amplified, by the very
habits he had built to contain it.

He is alert inside his own home in a way that exhausts his wife, Marta. He sits facing the door. He
notices when a neighbor's car parks differently than usual. When strangers approach him in ordinary
settings — a man asking for directions outside the grocery store, a colleague's friend extending a
hand at a barbecue — he experiences a spike of threat that he recognizes, intellectually, as
disproportionate, but cannot override. At the fire station, where his occupation requires him to
enter unfamiliar and structurally compromised buildings regularly, the scanning has become almost
continuous. His crew has noticed that he insists on walking the perimeter of a structure before
entering, even on calls where time does not comfortably permit it. He has not told them why.

Aleksander also pushes away positive emotions when they arise — moments of warmth with Marta,
satisfaction after a successful call — because they feel, in some way he struggles to articulate,
unsafe. As though allowing himself to feel well would mean lowering a guard he cannot afford to
lower. This has cost him something in his marriage that both he and Marta are aware of but have not
yet named directly.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment last month when his seven-year-old nephew ran
toward him unexpectedly in a hallway and Aleksander's body responded as though the child were a
threat. He caught himself before reacting physically, but the look on his nephew's face stayed with
him. He is most afraid, he says, that he has become someone his family needs to be careful around.
What he is hoping for is to stop having to plan his escape from every room he walks into.
