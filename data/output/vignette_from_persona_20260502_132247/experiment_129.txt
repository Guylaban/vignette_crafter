Tariq is a 38-year-old Lebanese-American hairdresser who works at a mid-range salon in a mid-sized
city, a man who built his professional identity around physical touch, sensory precision, and the
quiet intimacy of caring for others. Eighteen months ago, that identity was violently interrupted
when he was hospitalised for what initially presented as a severe respiratory infection and was
rapidly diagnosed as a life-threatening case of bacterial sepsis. Over eleven days in the ICU, Tariq
underwent repeated invasive procedures — central line insertions, blood draws every few hours, a
brief period on supplemental oxygen delivered through a tight-fitting mask. He remembers the smell
of the ICU with a particular clarity that still disturbs him: a combination of antiseptic, plastic
tubing, and something underneath it that he can only describe as "the smell of being very close to
not existing." He recalls a moment, around the fourth day, when the pain in his chest and arms was
so severe that he was certain he was dying, and that certainty — that his own body had turned
against him without warning — became the lens through which he has interpreted nearly everything
since.

In the months following his discharge, Tariq found that certain experiences would pull him back
toward the hospital with a force that felt physical. Waking from a nightmare left him in a state
where his body could not distinguish the present bedroom from the ICU — heart pounding, muscles
locked, chest tight — and those mornings became among the most disorienting of his week. Related
photographs were another source of unexpected intrusion: a friend once sent him a photo from a
hospital fundraiser, and Tariq found himself unable to look away and unable to look at it, his hands
trembling, his body flooding with the same physical reactions he had experienced during the worst of
his illness. These triggers did not merely recall the event — they seemed to reconstitute it. When
something reminded him of those eleven days, he could sometimes smell the antiseptic again, and on
worse occasions he felt a dull, spreading ache in his chest and arms in the same places the pain had
lived during his hospitalisation, as though the memory were stored in tissue rather than thought. At
other times the emotional weight arrived without any accompanying image — a sudden wave of fear or
shame with no clear object, which he found even more difficult to explain or manage.

What Tariq found most frightening, however, was not the intrusions themselves but what he had come
to believe they meant. He was increasingly certain that his body was no longer trustworthy — that it
had failed him once without warning and could do so again at any moment — and this conviction kept
him in a state of constant physiological vigilance, muscles perpetually braced, attention
perpetually scanning. He had come to believe that reacting this way, months after leaving the
hospital, meant something was fundamentally wrong with his mind, that he was, as he put it during
intake, "losing the thread of what is real." These appraisals were not inert; believing his body was
unreliable and his mind potentially fractured made the intrusive memories feel more threatening when
they arrived, amplifying their emotional intensity and making it harder to let them pass. Worse, the
appraisals themselves seemed to function as triggers — the moment Tariq noticed his body tensing or
his thoughts becoming fragmented, he would begin scanning for evidence of illness or breakdown, and
that scanning would pull forward the sensory and emotional residue of the hospitalisation, feeding
the very intrusions he was trying to prevent.

The most concrete consequence of this cycle has emerged in his work. Tariq now finds it difficult to
perform medical-adjacent tasks — even the routine act of a client mentioning a recent surgery or
illness can activate the full cascade. He has begun declining certain clients and has called in sick
on days when he knows a client with a chronic condition is scheduled. His income has dropped
noticeably, and his colleagues have begun to notice his absences. He remains single, and the social
withdrawal that has accompanied his avoidance has deepened his isolation.

What finally brought Tariq to seek help was a routine dental appointment last month — a medical
examination that triggered a panic response so severe he had to leave before it was completed. He
arrived at the intake session exhausted and ashamed, hoping above all to be told that he was not
going crazy, and afraid, more than anything, that he already was.
