Marguerite is an eighty-year-old European American woman who has worked as a social worker for most
of her adult life, spending decades in community service roles that required her to enter unfamiliar
homes, sit across from strangers, and hold space for other people's suffering. She presents now with
a PCL-5 score of 66, seeking help for the first time following a period of physical abuse
perpetrated by a long-term romantic partner that ended approximately two years ago. The abuse was
severe and sustained. What Marguerite describes most vividly — in fragments, in pictures — is a
particular evening when her partner came through the front door in a rage. She remembers the sound
of the door slamming, the shape of his silhouette filling the frame, the moment his hand connected
with her face. She does not remember the sequence clearly; the event exists in her mind as a series
of frozen images, each one sharp and terrible, with the connective tissue between them missing
entirely. When she recalls these snapshots, what she feels is not fear but grief — a heavy, almost
unbearable sorrow for the woman she was before it happened.

Those frozen images do not stay contained. In Marguerite's daily work, she still visits clients in
their homes, and it is in those settings that the memories surface most forcefully. When a door
opens or slams in an adjacent room, her body responds before her mind can intervene — the sound
pulls a snapshot forward, and she is briefly back in that hallway. Being alone with a male client,
or finding herself unexpectedly in the company of a male relative of someone she is visiting,
produces the same rupture. Even contact with people she knows — family members, longtime colleagues
— can activate the memories, because her partner was someone she had trusted completely, and
closeness itself has become a cue. These triggers arrive not as abstract reminders but as sensory
ambushes that drag the fragmented memory back into the present, and each time they do, they
reinforce her belief that the world is not what she thought it was.

What the memories have done to Marguerite's understanding of herself and others is perhaps the most
disabling part of her presentation. She had come to believe, across eighty years of life and decades
of professional care, that people were fundamentally reachable — that relationships, however
complicated, were safe enough to enter. That belief has been completely destroyed. She feels
contaminated in a way she struggles to articulate, as though what happened to her body left a
residue she cannot wash away. She trusts no one. These convictions, in turn, have made the triggers
more potent: because she believes people cannot be trusted, any male presence or familiar face now
carries threat, and she approaches each encounter already scanning for exits, already certain that
danger is possible.

The appraisals and the memories together have driven Marguerite toward a set of strategies that made
immediate sense but have steadily narrowed her life. She avoids the late afternoon and early evening
hours when she knows nightmares are more likely to follow sleep — she stays up late, keeps lights
on, resists the transition to bed — and the resulting sleep disruption leaves her exhausted and
hypervigilant throughout the day. She works constantly to push the memories away, to erase them
rather than process them, and she spends long hours turning over the question of why this happened
to her, searching for an explanation that might restore some order to her shattered understanding of
the world. But the erasure does not hold, and the meaning-seeking yields no answer, and both
strategies quietly confirm her sense that she is contaminated and alone in a way others would not
understand. The avoidance of sleep-triggering situations keeps her chronically under-rested, which
sharpens her hypervigilance and lowers the threshold at which a slamming door or a man's voice sends
her scanning for the nearest exit. The loop has been running for two years.

What finally brought Marguerite in was a visit to a client's home last month in which an unknown
male relative entered the room without warning. She froze, said nothing, left early, and drove home
shaking. She sat in her car for an hour. She has spent her career helping other people survive
exactly this kind of harm, and what she is most afraid of — and most hoping for — is that it is not
too late to survive it herself.
