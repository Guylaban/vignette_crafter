Callum is a 21-year-old single man of European descent who works as a bus driver in a mid-sized
American city. He presented for psychological assessment several months after returning from a
military deployment overseas, reporting a PCL-5 score of 53 and a constellation of symptoms that had
quietly dismantled much of the life he had expected to return to.

During his deployment, Callum's unit was involved in a convoy ambush in which an improvised
explosive device detonated beneath a vehicle travelling directly ahead of him. The blast was
immediate and total — a concussive wall of sound and heat, the air suddenly thick with smoke and the
acrid, unmistakable smell of burning metal and flesh. In the chaotic minutes that followed, medevac
helicopters circled overhead as Callum worked alongside others to pull survivors from the wreckage.
He was not physically injured, but what he witnessed in those minutes — the sounds, the smells, the
fragmented images of what remained — lodged in him in ways that did not resolve when he came home.

Back in the United States, Callum found that his memory of the event refused to behave like an
ordinary recollection. It did not sit in the past where he expected it. Instead, it arrived in
disconnected pieces that refused to cohere — a flash of orange, a texture underfoot, a sound without
context — and at other times it played in full, looping sequence, like footage he could not switch
off. What frightened him most was that he could not find words for what he had experienced; he knew
what had happened, but when he tried to speak of it, language failed him entirely, leaving him with
the sense that something fundamental had been broken in him. These intrusive, fragmented memories
fed directly into how he came to see his world: he had come to believe, with quiet certainty, that
the world was a genuinely dangerous place, that safety was an illusion, and that he needed to remain
on guard at all times simply to survive. There were moments, increasingly frequent, when he was not
sure his life was worth living at all.

These appraisals — that he was permanently altered, that danger was everywhere, that vigilance was
the only rational response — drove him toward strategies that offered short-term relief. He began
spending long hours on his phone, losing himself in games and internet content late into the night,
not because he enjoyed it but because it quieted the internal noise enough to get through another
evening. He stopped attending the church his family had worshipped at for generations, and he began
avoiding crowded places — shopping centres, public events — anywhere that felt unpredictable or
uncontrollable. These withdrawals felt logical to him; the world had shown him what it was capable
of, and so reducing exposure to it seemed like the only sensible thing to do.

The difficulty was that the avoidance and the screen use, while dulling the edges of his distress,
prevented him from ever processing what had happened. The hours spent scrolling or gaming kept him
from sitting with the memories long enough to integrate them, so they remained raw and disorganised
— still arriving in pieces, still replaying without warning — which in turn reinforced his belief
that he was not healing, that something was permanently wrong with him, that the world remained as
dangerous as the day of the ambush. The loop tightened.

His occupation placed him directly inside this cycle. Driving a bus through the city, Callum was
exposed daily to the sounds of urban life — a truck backfiring, a construction crew, the distant
thrum of a news helicopter — each of which could collapse the distance between the present and the
convoy road in an instant. The smell of exhaust on a bad day, or anything carrying even a trace of
burning, was enough to pull him back completely. He had begun gripping the wheel harder on certain
routes, scanning intersections with the kind of attention that had kept him alive overseas but made
ordinary city driving feel like a mission.

What finally brought Callum to seek help was a near-miss incident at work — a moment of inattention
during a flashback that he recognised, afterward, could have hurt someone. He came to the intake
appointment quiet and guarded, uncertain whether therapy could offer anything real. What he said he
wanted, in a voice that carried more exhaustion than hope, was simply to feel like the world was not
about to end every time he heard a loud noise.
