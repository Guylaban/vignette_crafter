Gerald is a 68-year-old married politician of European descent who presents with a PCL-5 score of 73
following a life-threatening road accident that occurred approximately fourteen months ago. He was
travelling as a passenger in a government vehicle when a truck ran a red light and struck the car
broadside at high speed. The driver of Gerald's vehicle had been drinking — Gerald recalls the
faint, sour smell of alcohol on the man's breath moments before impact — and the collision left
Gerald with broken ribs, a fractured wrist, and severe bruising along his left side. What he
remembers of the event is not a continuous sequence but a series of arrested images: the blur of the
truck's grille filling the window, the white of the airbag, the strange stillness of the overturned
cabin, and then the sound of his own breathing in the silence that followed. He cannot narrate the
event coherently; when he has tried to explain what happened — to his wife, to a colleague, to an
insurance assessor — he loses the thread partway through and trails into silence, unable to locate
the connective tissue between one image and the next. When the memories do surface, they are
accompanied by a physical sensation: a sharp ache across his ribs and a deep throb in his left
wrist, as though the body has stored the event in parallel with the mind.

In the months that followed, Gerald found that certain sensory encounters were capable of pulling
these frozen images back to the surface with startling force. The smell of alcohol — at a reception,
at a colleague's dinner, even the faint trace of hand sanitiser on certain occasions — would trigger
an immediate cascade of intrusive imagery and, with it, a flood of self-recrimination. He would find
himself dwelling obsessively on what he should have done: he should have noticed the driver's state,
should have insisted on a different arrangement, should have trusted his instinct that something was
wrong. This rumination, which he pursued with the thoroughness of a man accustomed to post-mortems,
only sharpened the images rather than resolving them, so that the act of reviewing the event seemed
to carve the snapshots more deeply rather than wear them smooth. Similarly, the sight of cars that
had been in accidents — photographs in news coverage, vehicles at the roadside — would arrest him
mid-thought and return him instantly to the cabin. The smell of alcohol and the sight of damaged
vehicles had become, in his experience, almost interchangeable in their power to reinstate the
physical and emotional texture of the crash.

Gerald had come to believe, with a conviction that resisted rational examination, that the accident
had happened to him because of the kind of man he was — that some flaw of character or accumulated
moral debt had made him deserving of it. This appraisal had a corrosive effect on his sense of
safety. He was certain, in a way he could not fully articulate, that he had to remain on guard at
all times, and this belief in pervasive, unspecified threat had reorganised his daily life. He woke
several times each night to check that the house was secure, scanning for sounds he could not name.
Unusual silence — the kind that settles over a room when a meeting pauses, or that fills a corridor
in the parliamentary building where he works — produced in him a visceral certainty that something
dangerous was imminent. His concentration, already compromised by the intrusive memories,
deteriorated further under the weight of this constant monitoring, and colleagues had begun to
notice that he lost his place during briefings. The belief that others could see what had happened
to him simply by looking — that the accident was somehow legible on his face or his bearing — made
public appearances feel acutely exposing, and for a man whose occupation required daily visibility,
this had become increasingly untenable.

Gerald had stopped driving entirely and refused to travel as a passenger whenever he could avoid it,
an avoidance that had required elaborate logistical management and had drawn comment from his staff.
His self-blame rumination, rather than offering relief, kept cycling back to reinforce his appraisal
of himself as fundamentally culpable, which in turn heightened his sense that danger was always near
and that the triggers surrounding him — the smell, the images, the silence — were confirmations of a
threat that had never truly passed. He sought help, finally, after missing a critical committee vote
because he could not bring himself to enter the car. What he most fears is that the man his
colleagues see is already diminished beyond recovery. What he most hopes, though he states it
cautiously, is that he might one day sit in a moving vehicle without feeling that he is about to
die.
