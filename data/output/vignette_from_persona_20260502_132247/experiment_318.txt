Renée Calloway is a sixty-six-year-old widowed woman who has worked as a model for most of her adult
life, a career that has demanded a precise and unforgiving awareness of her own body and
presentation. She was referred for psychological assessment following a traumatic incident that
occurred approximately eight months ago, when, while driving to an early-morning fitting in poor
visibility conditions, she struck a cyclist who had entered the road without warning. The cyclist, a
young man, sustained a fractured pelvis and a serious head injury. Renée was not cited for reckless
driving, and witnesses confirmed she had been travelling within the speed limit, yet none of this
has offered her any relief. She remembers the sound before she saw him — a dull, terrible impact —
and the way she sat frozen in the driver's seat for what felt like minutes before she was able to
move. It is that frozen moment she cannot forgive herself for. She believes, with a certainty that
no amount of external reassurance has softened, that she should have reacted faster, that she should
have somehow seen him sooner, that she should have done more in the immediate aftermath than stand
at the roadside while others called for help.

In the months since, Renée has described a persistent state of physiological vigilance that has
reshaped the texture of her daily life. Her body feels constantly braced, as though poised at the
edge of the next catastrophe, and she has not been able to fully relax even in her apartment, even
in moments that she recognises intellectually as safe. She wakes early and lies rigid in the dark,
her muscles tight, her attention scanning. Concentration has become effortful in a way it never was
before; she has missed call times, forgotten fittings, and had to ask photographers to repeat
directions she would once have absorbed instantly. On set — an environment that once felt like a
second home — she now finds herself hyperaware of movement in her peripheral vision, of cars passing
on the street outside the studio, of any sudden shift in the room. The professional composure she
has spent decades cultivating feels increasingly like a performance she can barely sustain.

What has proved most corrosive, however, is the way this constant state of alertness feeds directly
into the conclusions she draws about herself. When her body will not settle, when she startles at a
car horn or loses the thread of a conversation mid-sentence, Renée does not read these as symptoms
of a traumatised nervous system — she reads them as evidence. The hypervigilance becomes, in her
mind, proof of her fundamental inadequacy: a composed, competent person, she reasons, would not be
falling apart like this. The relentless tension in her body, the failure to concentrate, the
inability to simply be calm — each one confirms what she has already decided about herself. She has
come to believe, with quiet but immovable conviction, that someone else — someone more capable, more
alert, more deserving of the life she has been given — would never have been in that situation at
all. She feels permanently humiliated, not in a social sense, but in something deeper and more
private: a sense that the incident has exposed something that was always true about her and can
never now be unseen.

She will never forgive herself for not doing more, she has said this in session more than once, in
the same flat tone, as though reading from a verdict already handed down. She has largely stopped
driving, which has complicated her work significantly, requiring her to arrange car services for
early calls in ways that draw attention to a limitation she finds shameful. She has told no one in
her professional circle what happened.

What finally brought Renée to seek help was a conversation with her daughter, who told her plainly
that she seemed to have disappeared. What she is most afraid of, she said at the end of the first
session, is that she is right about herself. What she is hoping for, though she offered this more
quietly, is that she might be wrong.
