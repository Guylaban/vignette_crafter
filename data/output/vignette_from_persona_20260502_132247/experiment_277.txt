Kwame is a 47-year-old Ghanaian-American man who works as a park ranger in a large national forest
in the Pacific Northwest. He presents for his first clinical intake with a PCL-5 score of 74,
describing a presentation that has progressively worsened over the past several years following his
experiences as a civilian caught in a prolonged armed conflict during a visit to relatives in West
Africa in his late thirties. He has never previously sought mental health support, and describes
himself as someone who "handled things alone" — a self-conception that has, over time, become both a
coping strategy and a source of profound shame.

The events that brought Kwame to this point unfolded over several weeks in a region where fighting
between armed factions had escalated suddenly. He had been staying in a rural village when militia
forces moved through, burning homes and fields. He survived by hiding in dense bush while the fires
spread around him, watching structures collapse and hearing sounds he has never been able to name
aloud. Several people he had sheltered with did not survive. He made it out; they did not. He has
carried that fact like a wound that never closed.

Kwame returned to the United States and resumed his work as a park ranger, a role that placed him —
with devastating regularity — in the very landscape that most reliably pulls him back. The smell of
controlled burns, the sight of smoke threading through tree canopy, or simply the quality of light
falling across open grassland at dusk is enough to fracture his sense of where and when he is. In
those moments, his body floods with alarm: his heart hammers, his breathing shortens, and the world
around him narrows to something that feels imminently dangerous. He cannot relax even when he knows,
intellectually, that he is safe — the knowledge sits in one part of his mind while his nervous
system operates entirely independently of it. Sleep, when it comes at all, comes late and leaves
early. He has learned to dread the hours between two and five in the morning, when there is nothing
to occupy him and the memories move in freely.

What Kwame finds hardest to articulate — and what seems most central to his suffering — is the
weight of his own self-judgment. He feels guilty for surviving. He is certain, in a way that resists
logical challenge, that he acted in ways that violated who he understood himself to be during those
weeks, though he is reluctant to specify what he means. He feels severed from other people, as
though the experience placed him behind glass, able to observe ordinary life but unable to
participate in it. These appraisals do not sit quietly — they actively return him to the trauma.
When he feels the guilt rise, the images follow. When he feels the isolation sharpen, the sensory
details of the burning village become more vivid, not less. The appraisals themselves function as
internal triggers, keeping the wound open.

To manage this, Kwame has built a life of radical contraction. He avoids any contact with police or
military uniforms — crossing the street, leaving shops, declining to attend his nephew's police
academy graduation — because the sight of a uniform floods him with a dread he cannot contain. He
has cut off nearly all contact with friends and family over the past three years, telling himself it
is easier for everyone. He drinks most evenings, enough to dull the edge of hypervigilance and push
sleep a little closer. But the isolation that was meant to protect him has deepened his sense of
being set apart from humanity, feeding the very appraisals it was meant to quiet. The drinking
blunts his alertness just enough to make the mornings worse, the threat-monitoring sharper, the
assumption — when a radio call takes too long to come back or a trail goes unexpectedly quiet — that
something terrible is already underway. And when his supervisor corrected his incident report last
spring, a routine professional exchange, Kwame experienced it as an assault: his body responded as
though danger had arrived, and he did not return to the ranger station for two days.

What finally brought him in was a controlled burn he was required to supervise last autumn. Standing
at the perimeter of the flames, in uniform, in the forest, he lost several minutes entirely. He was
found sitting on the ground by a colleague. He is most afraid, he says, that he will never be able
to work outdoors again — and most afraid, beneath that, that he deserves exactly that.
