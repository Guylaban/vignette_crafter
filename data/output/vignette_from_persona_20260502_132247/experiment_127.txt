Malia Thongvane is a thirty-eight-year-old Lao-American woman who works as a welder at a mid-sized
fabrication yard in the Pacific Northwest. She presented for psychological assessment approximately
eight months after an industrial accident in which a piece of structural steel she was positioning
shifted unexpectedly, striking a younger co-worker and crushing his right hand. The injury required
multiple surgeries and left the man with permanent functional impairment. Malia was not found
negligent by the subsequent safety review — the equipment involved had a pre-existing fault — but
the determination offered her no relief. She had been the one holding the line. She had been the one
who told him to stand there.

In the weeks immediately following the accident, Malia found that memory of the event returned to
her not as a coherent narrative but in shards. She could recall the sound of the impact and the way
her co-worker's face changed before he even looked down, but the precise sequence of what she had
done in the seconds before remained opaque to her, as though the film had been cut. What did return,
unbidden and with terrible clarity, was taste — the acrid metallic tang of the air near the torch,
indistinguishable now from the taste she associates with those moments, so that any afternoon shift
in the welding bay can suddenly deposit her back inside that afternoon. When the memory surfaces
this way, what floods in alongside the sensory detail is not fear but guilt — a crushing, immediate
conviction that she has done something unforgivable. The fragmented and guilt-saturated quality of
these recollections began driving her behaviour in ways that compounded her distress. Because
remembering felt intolerable, she started avoiding anything that might prompt it: she stopped
attending the weekly crew briefings where his absence was visible, declined invitations from
colleagues, and began eating lunch alone in her truck. She also tried, with increasing effort,
simply not to think about it — pushing the images away whenever they surfaced. Over months, this
meant she was spending enormous cognitive energy in suppression rather than in the work itself, and
her concentration at the welding bench deteriorated noticeably. Her supervisor flagged two minor
errors in the same quarter, something that had never happened before in her twelve years at the
yard.

The suppression, however, did not quiet the memories — it seemed to feed them. Each time she forced
the images down, they returned with greater urgency, and the guilt on recall grew sharper rather
than duller. She had come to believe that the gaps in her memory were not the result of shock but of
something worse — that what she could not remember was the moment she had made a choice, the moment
she had been careless, and that recovering it fully would confirm what she already suspected about
herself. This belief kept her in a state of chronic vigilance that never resolved into rest. She lay
awake most nights, her mind cycling through partial reconstructions of the event, her body taut and
unreleased. Concentration during waking hours was unreliable; she would find herself mid-weld, the
torch live, with no clear sense of how long she had been standing still. And when a particular
combination of smells or sounds at the yard landed close enough to the original context, her body
responded before her mind could intervene — heart rate spiking, hands going cold, a wave of nausea
that took minutes to subside. These physical reactions confirmed for her that she was not
recovering, and the confirmation sent her back into the memories, searching again for the thing she
had done wrong.

The avoidance that had begun as self-protection had, by the time she sought help, narrowed her life
considerably. She had not visited her co-worker in hospital, had not contacted him since. She told
herself this was to spare him, though she was certain it was also because facing him would require
facing what she could not yet hold in full. What finally brought her to the clinic was a
conversation with her younger sister, who told her plainly that she had become unreachable. Malia
did not want to lose what remained of her relationships. What she feared most was that the missing
pieces of her memory, if they ever returned, would prove her right about herself.
