Minh is a 77-year-old Vietnamese American surgeon, married for over fifty years, who presents with a
PCL-5 score of 56 following his witnessing of a catastrophic fire that broke out in the hospital
wing where he was working. Several months ago, during a late surgical shift, a flash fire ignited in
an adjacent ward. Minh did not escape the building immediately; instead, he moved toward the flames
to help evacuate patients who could not move themselves. He watched a colleague become engulfed. The
smell of burning — of cloth, of flesh — saturated the corridor. The heat pressed against his skin
with an almost physical weight, and the sound of the fire alarm, shrill and irregular, cut through
everything. He got out. His colleague did not. In the weeks that followed, what had seemed like the
controlled competence of a lifetime in surgery began to fracture quietly and then loudly.

The memories that formed in the aftermath of that night are not memories in any ordinary sense. Minh
describes them as disordered and strange — he cannot place the sequence of events in any reliable
order, cannot say with certainty what came before what, and sometimes the emotional force of the
event arrives without any accompanying image at all, a sudden crush of grief and terror that has no
face. Most disorienting to him is the sense that the memory belongs to someone else, that the man in
that corridor was not quite him — a dissociative quality that, rather than offering relief, deepens
his conviction that something is fundamentally wrong with him as a person. He has come to believe
that a surgeon of his experience and temperament should not be undone like this, and the memory's
alien quality reads to him not as a natural response to horror but as evidence of a defective self.
This appraisal — that he is broken in some essential way — pushes him toward the memory's edges
rather than through it, and so the memory remains fragmented, its sequence unresolved, because every
approach to it is also a retreat.

The fragmented and emotionally unpredictable nature of the memory keeps him in a state of chronic
readiness. He is easily startled — a door closing in the hospital corridor will bring him upright,
heart hammering — and he monitors ambient sounds with an attention that exhausts him. He is on high
alert for anything sudden or loud, scanning his environment in the operating theatre, in the
hallways, at home. This vigilance, which he experiences as necessary and rational given what he now
believes about the world's unpredictability, is itself a form of re-exposure: the scanning keeps the
threat architecture active, and in that heightened state, the sensory residue of the fire becomes
easier to reach. A specific warmth against his skin — the ambient heat of a busy surgical suite, or
simply a warm afternoon — can return him instantly to the corridor, and the smell of anything burnt,
even toast in the hospital cafeteria, collapses the distance between then and now entirely. Stress,
too — the ordinary stress of a complex case — functions as a trigger, flooding him with the same
physiological urgency he felt that night.

When the triggers fire, Minh's response is to clamp down. He pushes away whatever is arising —
thought, image, feeling — with a deliberate internal force, and he has stopped watching television
or reading news that might carry stories of fires or disasters. He has also, more quietly, begun to
push away positive emotions, moments of satisfaction after a successful procedure or warmth with his
wife, because those feelings seem to him unsafe, as though allowing them would lower his guard. Yet
the suppression does not clear the ground; it keeps the material pressurised and close, so that the
triggers he is trying to avoid remain potent, and the fragmented memory stays unprocessed and
available to intrude. His conviction that he must stay vigilant because anything can happen at any
moment — a belief the fire confirmed for him — sustains the same hyperalert state that makes
suppression feel necessary, and so the loop holds.

Concretely, Minh has begun declining complex elective cases, citing scheduling. His colleagues have
noticed. His wife has noticed the silence at dinner. What finally brought him to seek help was a
moment in the scrub room when the warm water running over his hands stopped feeling like water and
felt, briefly, like heat — and he understood that the operating theatre, the place that has defined
his life, was no longer safe ground. He hopes to be able to work again without fear. What he is most
afraid of is that the defect he senses in himself is permanent.
