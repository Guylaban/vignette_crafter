Adaeze is an eighty-year-old widowed policy analyst of Nigerian descent who has spent four decades
shaping public health legislation from a modest but well-appointed office in Washington, D.C. She is
precise, methodical, and accustomed to managing complexity — qualities that made it all the more
disorienting when, fourteen months ago, a life-threatening motor vehicle collision dismantled her
sense of safety in ways she is only now beginning to name.

It happened on a rain-slicked evening in late autumn. Adaeze was returning from a legislative
briefing when a driver who had been drinking ran a red light and struck her sedan at speed. The
impact was catastrophic. She remembers the cold — a biting, specific cold that pressed through the
shattered window and settled against her left arm and cheek — and she remembers the smell: first the
sharp, unmistakable odour of alcohol drifting from the other vehicle, then, seconds later, the acrid
bite of something burning beneath the hood of her car. After that, there are gaps. She recalls
voices, a fragment of a face, the sensation of being moved. Then nothing until the hospital. The
collision left her with a fractured pelvis and a traumatic brain injury, and she spent eleven weeks
in inpatient rehabilitation before returning home.

The months that followed were marked by a growing and bewildering instability. Her memory of the
event is fractured in a way she finds deeply unsettling: certain instants — the cold against her
skin, the smell of alcohol, the orange flicker she believes she saw — are engraved with terrible
precision, while the minutes surrounding them are entirely blank. She has come to dread those clear
fragments precisely because they arrive without permission. Something in her surroundings — the
burnt smell from a colleague's reheated lunch in the office break room, or the particular
temperature of an air-conditioned corridor in winter — can instantly restore the full sensory weight
of the collision, pulling her out of the present without warning. When that happens, she does not
merely remember; she is briefly, terrifyingly back inside it, certain the car is still moving. These
triggers do not announce themselves, and because they do not, Adaeze has come to treat her
environment as a minefield. The smell of alcohol at a departmental reception, a cold draft from an
open window during a policy meeting — each one places her immediately on guard, her body braced for
impact before her mind has caught up.

The threat she perceives in her surroundings has in turn made the memory itself more volatile. When
Adaeze is already on edge — already scanning, already braced — any sudden movement by a colleague
reaching across a conference table or a door swinging open unexpectedly floods her with the horror
and shame she associates with that night, and the fragments of the crash press forward with renewed
force. She has come to believe that strong negative feelings are themselves dangerous signals, that
fear or anger means something terrible is imminent, and this belief keeps her nervous system in a
state of near-constant readiness. That readiness, paradoxically, makes her more sensitive to the
very triggers she fears: a cold glass of water pressed into her hand by a well-meaning intern, the
faint smell of something scorched from the building's ventilation system — each one detonates the
alarm before she can reason her way around it.

She has also developed a habit of cutting the memory short. When a fragment begins to surface — when
she can feel the cold or smell the alcohol approaching in her mind — she pushes it away before it
can complete itself, a strategy that has given her brief relief but left the event unprocessed and
perpetually close to the surface.

Over the past year, Adaeze has quietly reorganised her professional life around these sensitivities.
She no longer attends evening receptions. She sits near exits. She has declined two field
assignments that would have required travel by car. Colleagues have noticed her withdrawal, and a
junior analyst she mentored for years recently asked if she was all right.

That question, more than anything, brought her to seek help. What she fears most is that she will
never again feel safe in the world she has spent her life trying to improve. What she hopes for,
with cautious and exhausted honesty, is simply to get through a working day without bracing for a
crash that has already happened.
