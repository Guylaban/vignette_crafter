Dorothea Calloway is a 77-year-old widowed school counselor of North American background who was
referred for psychological assessment following a significant decline in her occupational
functioning and a reported increase in alcohol use. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 58,
consistent with a severe posttraumatic stress presentation, and her history traces a clear line from
a specific traumatic event to the tightening constellation of symptoms that now governs her daily
life.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Dorothea was working late in her office at the middle school
where she has counseled students for nearly two decades. A man entered the building through an
unlocked side door, forced his way into her office, and assaulted her. He fled before staff arrived.
No arrest has been made. The attack was brief but brutal, and what has remained with Dorothea — with
a persistence that has only deepened over time — is not simply the memory of what happened but the
overwhelming sense that the world has revealed itself to be a place where such a thing can happen to
her, in her own office, and where no one is ever held to account.

In the weeks that followed, Dorothea found herself unable to sit comfortably in any room without
first locating the nearest exit. She began doing this at school as well — before meeting with a
student, before sitting down in the staff lounge — a habit that formed so quietly she barely noticed
it becoming compulsory. She started drinking in the evenings, a glass of wine that became two, then
a sedative her physician had prescribed for sleep, and eventually a nightly ritual she told herself
was simply managing stress. She also found herself returning, again and again in her mind, to
fantasies of what she would do if she ever encountered her assailant — rehearsing confrontations,
imagining consequences, cycling through scenarios of retribution that never resolved. These
strategies, taken together, have not brought her relief. The drinking has left her feeling foggy and
ashamed the following mornings, and the rumination has only deepened her conviction that the
injustice done to her is total and permanent, that nothing will ever be made right, and that she is
therefore trapped in a version of her life from which there is no exit. The sense of unresolved
grievance has fed directly into her appraisal of ongoing danger — because she has come to believe
that a man who was never caught is still out there, that the system has failed her entirely, and
that she therefore cannot trust any environment to be safe.

This belief about the world — that threat is unresolved and institutional protection is an illusion
— has in turn made her hypervigilant in ways she cannot switch off. At home in the evenings, she
listens for sounds at the doors and windows, interpreting the ordinary creaks of her house as
potential intrusions. At school, she startles visibly when a student knocks unexpectedly, and
colleagues have begun to notice. She cannot relax even in moments when she knows, rationally, that
she is not in danger — the knowledge does nothing to quiet the monitoring. And that inability to
relax has fed back into her appraisals of herself and her situation: she reads her own persistent
fear as confirmation that she is permanently changed, permanently at risk, that the woman she was
before — warm, steady, the person students came to with their hardest problems — is simply gone. She
used to describe herself as someone who was fundamentally happy. She no longer recognizes that
person.

The identity loss has been among the most painful aspects of her presentation. Dorothea has spent
her career helping young people make sense of difficulty, and she now sits across from students
feeling, as she put it, like an impostor — going through motions while privately convinced she is
always miserable and always will be. A colleague recently asked whether she was all right, and
Dorothea broke down in her office for the first time. That moment — the recognition that her
suffering had become visible, that her professional self was eroding — is what finally brought her
to seek help. What she fears most is that she has already become someone she cannot recover. What
she hopes, quietly and with some difficulty, is that she is wrong.
