Gerald Whitmore is a 67-year-old hotel manager, married for over thirty years, who presents with a
PCL-5 score of 62 following a mugging that occurred approximately eight months ago in the parking
structure adjacent to the downtown hotel he manages. Gerald had worked late that evening, something
he had done without incident for decades, and was walking to his car when a man approached him from
behind a concrete pillar. The assailant smelled strongly of alcohol — Gerald would later describe it
as the first thing that registered before the violence — and demanded his wallet and keys. When
Gerald hesitated, the man grabbed him by the collar, shoved him hard against a parked car, and
struck him across the face before fleeing with his belongings. The entire episode lasted perhaps
ninety seconds. Gerald lay on the cold concrete for several minutes before a security camera
operator noticed him on a monitor and sent help. He was 67 years old, alone in a structure he had
considered safe, and he had been unable to do anything.

In the weeks that followed, Gerald found himself reorganizing his understanding of the world around
what had happened to him. He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt entirely rational to
him, that people simply cannot be trusted — that the veneer of civility is thin and that anyone
could be concealing dangerous intentions. More than this, he felt permanently humiliated, not only
by the assault itself but by what he experienced as his own helplessness, his inability to protect
himself, the image of a grown man crumpled against a car door in his own workplace. These appraisals
— that the world is fundamentally dangerous and that he had been exposed as vulnerable — took root
quickly and began to organize his behavior. Because he was now certain that threat was everywhere
and that his own judgment about safety had once failed him catastrophically, he moved through daily
life in a state of sustained alertness, scanning for danger with an exhausting thoroughness. He felt
on guard even inside his own home, checking the locks multiple times each evening, sitting with his
back to walls, unable to fully settle. His wife, Diane, noticed that he flinched badly if she
touched his shoulder from behind — a habit she had never thought twice about in thirty years of
marriage — and she eventually stopped initiating physical contact, uncertain of what he needed.

Because Gerald remained convinced that the world was dangerous and that his vigilance was the only
thing standing between him and another assault, his heightened state of threat monitoring made him
acutely sensitive to anything that resembled the conditions of that night. At work, which had once
been his domain of competence and authority, the lobby and corridors of the hotel had become a
landscape of potential threat. A guest moving in a way that seemed purposeful or unpredictable,
someone lingering near an entrance, a patron whose behavior he could not immediately read — all of
it now registered as suspicious and required his full attention. The smell of alcohol, which in a
hotel bar is entirely ordinary, had become one of the most potent cues he encountered; when it
reached him, something in him went cold and alert, as though the parking structure were reassembling
itself around him. Loud noises — a luggage cart dropped, a door slamming, a burst of laughter from
the bar — produced a startle response he could not suppress, and he had begun to find reasons to
avoid the ground floor during peak evening hours, delegating tasks he had previously handled
himself. His deputy manager had noticed and asked, with genuine concern, whether Gerald was all
right. Gerald told him he was fine.

What finally brought Gerald to seek help was a conversation with Diane, who told him quietly one
evening that she felt she was living with a stranger. He had not realized, until she said it, how
completely he had withdrawn. He came to the first session hoping, in his words, to "get back to
normal" — though he was privately unsure whether normal still existed for him, or whether the man
who had walked through that parking structure without fear was simply gone.
