Miriam Taufa is a 58-year-old divorced woman of Tongan descent who serves as a rabbi at a mid-sized
congregation in the Pacific Northwest. She came to the clinic carrying a PCL-5 score of 78,
describing herself in the intake paperwork as someone she no longer recognises. The event that
fractured her sense of self occurred fourteen months ago, when the vehicle she was driving was
struck by a fuel tanker that ran a red light. Her car spun into a guardrail and caught fire. Miriam
was conscious throughout — she remembers the smell of burning plastic and fuel filling the cabin,
the heat against her face, and the sound of her own screaming before a bystander pulled her free.
She escaped with burns to her forearms and a shattered left wrist, but she has never fully left that
intersection.

In the weeks following the accident, Miriam found that certain sensory details had become portals
back into the burning car. The smell of anything scorched — a forgotten pan on the stove, the
incense sometimes used at interfaith services she attended — would drop her immediately back into
that moment of helpless terror. Trauma-related events on the congregational calendar, such as
memorial services for accident victims or pastoral visits to hospital burn units, carried the same
gravitational pull. Even waking from a nightmare, still damp with sweat and heart hammering, was
enough to keep her body convinced the danger had not passed. These triggers reliably activated a
state of physiological alarm: she became irritable and snapped at her cantor and administrative
staff in ways that left her ashamed, and she had begun taking risks that puzzled even her — driving
too fast on rain-slicked roads, as though daring fate to finish what it had started. When someone
touched her shoulder unexpectedly during a service, she flinched so violently that a congregant
stepped back in alarm.

The threat state, once activated, fed directly back into the landscape of danger. Her heightened
startle and the angry outbursts kept her nervous system primed, so that ordinary moments — a car
backfiring, a congregant approaching from behind — read as emergencies requiring re-scanning. This
constant vigilance made her more sensitive to the very cues she was trying to outrun, and the cycle
tightened. The burnt smell, the residue of nightmares, the trauma-laden pastoral work she could not
entirely avoid: all of it arrived with greater force because her body was already braced.

What Miriam had developed to manage this were strategies that made sense in the short term and cost
her dearly over months. When a trigger struck, she would cover her face with her hands — a gesture
that had begun as something almost involuntary but had become a visible habit her staff had quietly
started to work around. She forced herself to think only positive thoughts, rehearsing blessings and
gratitude in a kind of internal chant that kept her from processing what had actually happened to
her. She had also begun eating excessively in the evenings, working through the contents of her
refrigerator after Shabbat services in a fog of distress she could not name. These strategies, over
time, prevented any real integration of the traumatic material; the suppressed images pressed back
harder, and the forced positivity collapsed each time a nightmare broke through, leaving her more
disoriented than before. The very rituals meant to contain distress became new triggers — the act of
covering her face, the frantic reframing — signalling to her body that something threatening was
still present.

What frightened Miriam most was what she had come to believe about herself. She felt profoundly
isolated from her congregation, from her adult children in Auckland, from the woman who had stood at
a bimah for thirty years and held other people's grief with steadiness. She was certain that her
body had become her enemy, reacting with alarms she could neither justify nor silence. The sense
that she was unrecognisable to herself — that the person who flinched, overate, and snapped at
colleagues was not the rabbi she had built her life around being — deepened the threat she perceived
in every interaction, and that deepened threat made the triggers more potent, and so the loop
continued. She sought help when she cancelled a family bereavement service because she could not
face the smell of memorial candles, and a senior congregant gently asked if she was all right. She
was not. What she is most hoping for, she said quietly at the end of the intake, is to feel like
herself again.
