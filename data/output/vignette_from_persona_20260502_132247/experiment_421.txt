pattern, though understanding this has done little to diminish the damage. The worst incident

of alcohol on his breath before the first blow, and the acrid, burning smell that drifted in from
something left too long on the stove — a detail that lodged itself in his nervous system with a
precision his conscious mind could not have managed. He was struck repeatedly, mostly to the face
and torso. He remembers lying on the floor, certain he was going to be seriously hurt, the terror
absolute and unambiguous. His partner said nothing was going to happen to him and laughed. Kofi did
not call the police. He left the apartment the next morning and never returned. Nothing formal was
ever done.

In the months that followed, his body did not allow him to simply move on. When the memories return
— and they return without warning — Kofi does not experience them as recollections. He feels them. A
dull, pressured ache spreads across his ribs and jaw, the same places that absorbed the blows, and
the terror he felt on that floor reconstitutes itself with an immediacy that leaves him gripping the
edge of whatever surface is nearest. These memories carry their own sensory world: he can sometimes
smell both the alcohol and the faint burn, even in a clean, quiet room. And it is precisely this
quality of the memories — their physicality, their insistence on the present tense — that makes
certain moments in his daily life so treacherous. The smell of alcohol at a work social event, or
the burnt odour from a toaster in the office break room, does not remind Kofi of what happened. It
returns him there, pulling the full memory forward with a force that bypasses any intention he has
to stay grounded in the present. Even a colleague's offhand comment about family — the kind of
remark that would pass unnoticed in any other context — can be enough, because his partner had
frequently invoked Kofi's family during episodes, weaponising them, and those associations are now
embedded in the architecture of his fear.

What makes this harder still is what these intrusions confirm to him about himself. Each time a
trigger pulls the memory forward, and each time the memory surfaces on its own, Kofi is left with a
set of convictions that have grown more entrenched over time. He had come to believe, with a quiet
but immovable certainty, that the person he was before — curious, socially at ease, someone who
trusted his own judgment — no longer exists. What replaced him is someone he does not recognise and
does not particularly like. He is also haunted by a sense of injustice that has no outlet: what was
done to him was real, it was serious, and it was never acknowledged by anyone with the authority to
name it as wrong. Beneath both of these runs something more visceral — a feeling of contamination,
as though the abuse left something on him that ordinary living cannot wash off. These appraisals are
not beliefs he holds abstractly; they are conclusions the memories keep re-teaching him, and the
triggers keep re-confirming.
