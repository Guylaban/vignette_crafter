Mere Tuilagi is a 46-year-old Fijian-American woman who works as a forensic analyst for a county
crime laboratory. She is married and lives with her husband in a mid-sized city in the Pacific
Northwest. She was referred for psychological assessment following a PCL-5 score of 78, obtained
after her primary care physician became concerned about her deteriorating physical health and what
she described, in her own words, as "not being able to function anymore."

Eleven months ago, Mere received a phone call at her workstation — surrounded by evidence bags and
case files — informing her that her younger sister, Lani, had been killed in a road accident. The
call came on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon. Mere later described the sound of the dispatcher's voice
as something she could not unhear: a flat, procedural tone delivering words that seemed to arrive
from outside of time. She drove to the hospital alone. What she found there — the clinical whiteness
of the corridor, the sound of a monitor still alarmed in a room she was not allowed to enter, the
particular silence that followed — became the material from which her memory of the event is now
constructed. She does not recall it as a continuous narrative. Instead, it exists in her mind as a
series of fixed images: the fluorescent light above the reception desk, the grain of the linoleum
floor where she stood when a nurse told her Lani was gone, the shape of her own hands in her lap in
the car afterward. Each image is vivid and complete, like a photograph, and each carries with it the
sound that accompanied it — the alarm, the nurse's voice, the silence. When the memory returns, it
does so without warning, and Mere has described being so overwhelmed by the emotion it carries that
she has to stop whatever she is doing entirely, sometimes sitting motionless at her desk for minutes
at a time before she can recover herself.

In the months that followed, Mere came to believe, with a conviction she found difficult to
articulate but impossible to dismiss, that her life had been fundamentally and irreversibly
destroyed. She had lost her faith — in the predictability of the world, in the fairness she had once
assumed operated beneath ordinary events, in the religious practice that had anchored her Fijian
upbringing. Nothing she did felt like it carried weight or purpose. These appraisals — that meaning
was gone, that people and life itself could not be trusted — began to shape her behaviour in ways
that felt, to Mere, entirely logical. If nothing mattered, there was no reason to face the day. She
began calling in sick to the forensic laboratory with increasing frequency, and on the days she did
go in, she found herself unable to process case materials without the images returning — the
evidence photographs, the chain-of-custody documents, the clinical language of harm — all of it
resonating with the visual and auditory texture of the hospital corridor. Her supervisor has noted a
significant decline in her output, and two cases have been reassigned. Mere is aware of this. She
experiences it as confirmation rather than consequence.

At home, she began staying up until two or three in the morning, not from insomnia precisely but
from a deliberate reluctance to enter the vulnerability of sleep, where she had less control over
what returned to her. She also began eating very little — skipping meals, reducing portions — not
from appetite loss alone but as a form of management, a way of imposing some structure on a body
that otherwise felt ungoverned. These strategies, however, did not contain the memories. The sleep
deprivation and the physical depletion that followed the restricted eating left her cognitively and
emotionally raw, so that when the auditory and visual fragments of the hospital did surface, she had
fewer resources to absorb them, and the overwhelm was more complete. The exhaustion, in turn,
reinforced her certainty that she was broken — that someone who could not sleep, could not eat,
could not go to work was someone whose life had, in fact, been destroyed — and this belief made it
harder still to imagine that returning to the laboratory or sitting at the dinner table could mean
anything at all.

What finally brought Mere to seek help was her husband, who told her quietly one evening that he was
frightened he was losing her. She did not disagree with him. What she is most afraid of is that he
is right. What she is most hoping for, though she struggles to say it plainly, is that something in
her can still be reached.
