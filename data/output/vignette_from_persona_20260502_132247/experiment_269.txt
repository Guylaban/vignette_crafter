Thanh is an eighty-year-old widowed Vietnamese-American man who spent much of his career as a data
scientist but who, in his mid-sixties, volunteered for two extended deployments as a relief worker
in an active conflict zone in Southeast Asia. What he witnessed there — malnourished children being
turned away from supply convoys by armed factions, colleagues shot at checkpoints, the bureaucratic
paralysis that left entire villages without aid while he stood helpless with clipboards and
satellite phones — left marks that did not surface fully until years later. The specific texture of
those moments matters: the feeling of having no options, of watching moral order collapse in front
of him while institutional constraints made intervention impossible, became the emotional signature
of the trauma. He returned home, cared for his ailing wife until her death three years ago, and
managed, for a time, to hold the memories at a distance. It was only after her death removed his
last stabilising relationship that the symptoms began to consolidate into something he could no
longer manage alone.

Thanh now lives by himself in a quiet suburb of Seattle, working part-time as a remote data
scientist — a role that, ironically, keeps him tethered to screens and news feeds for much of the
day. It is in that occupational context that one of his most reliable triggers appears: news reports
about humanitarian crises, armed conflict, or supply-chain failures will surface in aggregated feeds
while he works, and when they do, the memories do not arrive as recollections but as immediate
experience. He has come to dread the moment a headline loads before he can look away, because he is
certain that what follows will not feel like reading about something distant — it will feel like
being back at the checkpoint, paralysed, watching something terrible unfold. The memory arrives with
the quality of film on a loop: the same sequence of images, the same sounds, the same visceral sense
that it is happening now rather than fifteen years ago. Waking from nightmares — which come several
times a week — produces the same collapse of temporal distance, so that mornings carry the residue
of the night into the first hours of his working day.

Because the memories feel so present and so uncontrollable, Thanh has come to believe that his mind
is failing him in a fundamental way — that the intensity and repetition of what he experiences means
he is losing his grip on reality. He is also frightened of his own anger. In the conflict zone he
had witnessed what unchecked rage could do, and he is convinced that the irritability he now feels
constantly — the short fuse that ignites during video calls, the aggressive edge that enters his
voice when colleagues make careless remarks — means he is capable of something terrible. These
appraisals, the fear of going crazy and the fear of his own rage, feed directly back into
hypervigilance: if he believes the world is dangerous and that he himself is a potential source of
harm, then every interaction must be monitored and prepared for. He rehearses conversations before
any meeting, scripting his responses to avoid the moment where anger might break through. He scans
his environment constantly, reading the body language of delivery drivers and neighbours the way he
once read the demeanour of armed guards. These strategies, intended to contain the threat, instead
keep his nervous system perpetually primed, and a body that never fully relaxes is a body that finds
danger everywhere — which means ordinary moments of mild social friction or moral transgression,
someone cutting in line, a colleague dismissing a concern, register as genuine violations and pull
the checkpoint back into the present.

The loop tightens in a particular way: the rehearsal and the scanning prevent him from ever having
an experience that disconfirms the threat, so the appraisals calcify, and the memories remain
unprocessed and easily activated. He avoids news deliberately, but the avoidance means he never
learns that he can tolerate exposure without disintegrating, and so the triggers retain their full
potency.

What finally brought Thanh to seek help was a video call last month in which he lost his temper with
a junior colleague over a minor data error. He did not shout, but the cold fury in his voice
frightened him. He is eighty years old, he is alone, and what he is most afraid of is that he has
become, in some irreversible way, a dangerous man. What he is most hoping for is that someone can
tell him otherwise.
