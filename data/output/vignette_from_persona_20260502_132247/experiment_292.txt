Priya is a 25-year-old South Asian woman who works as a counselor at a community mental health
center in a mid-sized American city. She came to therapy eight months after an incident in which she
was forcibly stopped, searched, and thrown to the ground by police officers while waiting at a bus
stop after an evening shift. The officers, responding to a reported disturbance nearby, apparently
misidentified her. She was pressed face-down into the wet soil of a grass verge beside the pavement,
her cheek against the earth, the smell of damp ground filling her nostrils while she was held
immobile and shouted at. She was released without charge, but the encounter left marks that no
formal apology could address.

In the weeks that followed, Priya noticed that the smell of soil or damp outdoor air — something she
had never previously registered — would stop her mid-step, flooding her with a sensation she could
barely name. The memory did not arrive as a thought so much as a total reinstatement: she would feel
the weight on her back, the cold ground beneath her cheek, her arms pinned at an angle she could
still feel in her shoulders. Her body would drop into that same position before her mind had formed
a single word, and she would find herself hunched or bracing, as though the officers were still
there. These full-sensory intrusions began occurring on her morning commute. Riding the bus to work
— the same route she had taken for two years — became almost unbearable. The enclosed space, the
proximity of strangers, the occasional sight of a police cruiser through the window: any of these
could trigger the reliving, and the reliving would leave her shaking for the rest of the journey.

What the memory kept confirming, in Priya's mind, was something she had come to believe about the
world with a certainty that frightened even her: that people are not what they seem. The officers
had looked ordinary. The bystanders who watched had done nothing. She had begun scanning faces on
the bus and in the waiting room at work, reading microexpressions and posture for signs of concealed
hostility, unable to stop even when she recognized what she was doing. This hypervigilant scrutiny
kept her nervous system primed, and a primed nervous system made the next encounter with a trigger —
a uniform, a smell, a crowded bus — hit harder and earlier than the last. At night, she could not
let herself fall asleep properly; staying alert felt like the only rational response to a world she
now experienced as fundamentally unsafe, and the exhaustion this produced made her appraisals darker
and her body's reactions faster.

Priya had also come to feel, in a way she found deeply shameful, that something of the event had
gotten into her — that she was contaminated by it, marked in a way others could sense. This belief
quietly shaped her behavior. She stopped attending the cultural gatherings and community events she
had previously organized with friends from her South Asian neighborhood, telling herself she would
go back once she felt more like herself, though she had come to doubt she ever would. The withdrawal
only deepened her sense that she had lost her place in her community and culture, and that loss fed
back into her conviction that people could not be trusted — a conviction that made the prospect of
social contact feel not just uncomfortable but genuinely dangerous. When she did leave the house,
she sometimes covered her face with her hands in moments of acute distress, and she had developed a
habit of checking mirrors obsessively — car mirrors, bathroom mirrors, the reflection in shop
windows — scanning for threats approaching from behind. These behaviors brought momentary relief but
left her more sensitized, more certain of danger, and more likely to read the next neutral face or
outdoor smell as a signal she needed to act on.

At work, the irony was not lost on her. She sat across from clients daily, offering a steadiness she
no longer felt, while quietly checking the hallway mirror for anyone in uniform. A colleague
recently asked if she was all right. Priya said she was tired. Sometimes, recalling the event, she
felt nothing at all — a flat, distant numbness, as though it had happened to someone else — and
those moments frightened her almost as much as the reliving did, because they suggested she might be
disappearing from herself entirely. It was this fear, finally, that brought her to seek help: not
the terror, which she had learned to manage in small, costly ways, but the growing suspicion that
the person she had been before the bus stop — connected, trusted, present — might not come back.
