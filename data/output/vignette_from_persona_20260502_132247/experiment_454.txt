Priya is a 42-year-old South Asian woman who works as a residential maid, employed by a family in a
quiet suburban neighbourhood. She is married, and until approximately fourteen months ago, she
described her life as unremarkable in the best possible way — predictable, safe, and centred on her
family. That changed when a man began stalking her over the course of several months. He would
appear outside the homes where she worked, follow her on foot between bus stops, and on one occasion
cornered her in a narrow service corridor of a client's house. He had been drinking; she remembers
the sharp, sour smell of alcohol on his breath as he stood too close, his eyes moving over her body
in a way that made her feel stripped of personhood. She felt a sudden, searing pain as he grabbed
her wrist and twisted it before she managed to pull free and run. He was eventually reported to
police, but was never prosecuted, and Priya has never been certain he is gone.

In the months that followed, Priya found that certain moments at work would pull her back without
warning. The smell of alcohol — a client leaving a wine glass on the counter, a bottle opened at a
dinner party she was cleaning up after — would immediately flood her with a sense of imminent
danger, her body bracing before her mind could catch up. Being looked at in a way she perceived as
sexual, even fleetingly, produced the same lurching alarm. Physical pain anywhere in her body, even
minor aches from lifting or kneeling, seemed to reactivate something deeper — as though her wrist
still remembered. Each of these triggers did not merely unsettle her; they pulled fragments of the
memory forward, arriving not as a coherent narrative but as sensory shards: the taste of the coffee
she had been carrying, the particular quality of the silence in that corridor just before he
appeared. The memory itself remained fractured. Priya cannot tell a clear story of what happened,
even to herself. When she tries, something in her pushes it away before it is fully formed, leaving
her with an unresolved sense of incompleteness that feels more disturbing than the fragments
themselves.

This fractured quality of the memory has made everything harder to manage. Because she cannot fully
process what happened, she has begun rehearsing every interaction before it occurs — scripting
conversations with clients, with her husband, even with shopkeepers — as though careful preparation
could protect her from being caught off guard the way she was in that corridor. She has gradually
stopped seeing friends, finding that social situations feel uncontrollable and that she cannot
predict what might be said or seen that would unsettle her. She worries obsessively about harm
coming to her husband and children, imagining scenarios of danger with a vividness she cannot switch
off. These strategies, rather than providing relief, keep the threat feeling close: the rehearsing
and the withdrawal narrow her world until the stalker seems to occupy all of it, and the
catastrophic worry pulls her attention back again and again to images of harm — images that blur
into memory fragments and make the original event feel current rather than past.

At home, Priya checks the locks on doors and windows repeatedly before she can attempt to sleep.
Even inside her own house she remains on guard, scanning for anything out of place. Unusual silence
— the kind that falls when her husband steps outside or the children are at school — carries a
specific dread, as though quiet itself is a warning sign. This hypervigilant state means that
ordinary sensory details in her home environment can suddenly feel like precursors to threat, which
in turn makes the sensory triggers — an alcohol smell from a neighbour's gathering, a twinge of pain
in her wrist — feel like confirmation that danger has returned. The loop between vigilance and re-
experiencing has tightened steadily over the months.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a morning when she froze completely in a client's
kitchen, unable to move or speak for nearly a minute after smelling wine in a glass left from the
night before. Her employer asked if she was all right, and Priya could not answer. She is most
afraid that she will never feel safe again — and most hoping, tentatively, that someone might help
her find a way to tell the story she has not yet been able to finish.
