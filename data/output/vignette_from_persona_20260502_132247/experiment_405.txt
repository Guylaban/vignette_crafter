Kiet Phanomvan is a thirty-one-year-old Cambodian-American judge who has been married for three
years and, until recently, was regarded by colleagues as one of the most composed and methodical
members of the bench. He presented for psychological assessment approximately eight months after
surviving a catastrophic flash flood that struck his coastal hometown during what had begun as an
unremarkable autumn afternoon. The sky that day had been a particular shade of bruised grey, the air
carrying the thick, mineral weight that precedes serious rain, and within two hours the streets had
become channels of brown water moving with the force and indifference of something alive. Kiet was
trapped for nearly six hours on the second floor of a community building, watching through a window
as vehicles were carried past and listening to voices that eventually went silent. When emergency
services finally reached him, he was transported by ambulance through streets still partially
submerged, the interior of the vehicle saturated with the sharp antiseptic smell of medical supplies
pressed against his face. He was physically unharmed. He told the intake clinician he had not
expected that to matter as much as it apparently does.

In the weeks that followed, Kiet found that certain sensory details had lodged themselves in his
mind not as a continuous narrative but as a series of discrete, frozen images — the waterline
creeping up the exterior wall, a child's shoe turning slowly in the current below, the particular
angle of light through a cracked window. Each image was vivid and complete in itself, and when one
surfaced it brought with it no before or after, only the moment, rendered in full sensory detail. He
described the experience as being pulled without warning back into the room — not remembering it,
but inhabiting it again, the cold and the smell and the sound of the water entirely present. These
intrusions were not frequent in the early weeks, but they were total when they came.

The triggers that reliably activated these episodes became apparent to Kiet before he had the
language to name what was happening. The sound of an ambulance siren — a sound he encountered with
some regularity given that the courthouse sat two blocks from a major hospital — would stop him mid-
sentence during proceedings and send a current of alarm through his chest that he struggled to
conceal from counsel and defendants. The antiseptic smell that clung to the hospital corridor he
sometimes passed during lunch became something he began routing his walk to avoid. Most reliably of
all, overcast skies carrying that particular grey-green quality would produce, within minutes, a
vivid intrusion of the flood's earliest moments. On those days the memories arrived with a force
that felt less like recollection and more like ambush, and Kiet found himself scanning the courtroom
windows with an attention that had nothing to do with the case before him.

It was the intrusions themselves — their completeness, their insistence — that drove the strategies
Kiet constructed to manage his days. He had come to believe that if he could keep his mind occupied
and vigilant at all times, he could prevent the memories from finding purchase. He worked to
suppress any thought that drifted toward the flood, redirecting his attention with a concentration
that left him exhausted by evening. He had stopped going out beyond what his professional
obligations required, reasoning that exposure to unpredictable sensory environments — a passing
ambulance, a change in weather, the wrong smell in a corridor — was simply an unnecessary risk. He
had also stopped allowing himself to rest. Relaxation, he had concluded, felt genuinely dangerous,
as though lowering his guard was the condition the memories were waiting for. He had not sat in the
garden with his wife since the summer. She had stopped suggesting it. The avoidance had its own
logic, and for a time it seemed to hold. But the strategies themselves had begun to reshape his
environment: the routes he took, the windows he avoided, the silences he kept at home all
constituted a landscape that kept the flood perpetually near, its absence a form of presence, so
that the very effort of not-remembering became the thing that kept the triggers close.

Kiet sought help after missing a day on the bench for the first time in his career — a morning when
the sky turned the wrong colour and he could not make himself leave the house. What he most feared,
he said quietly, was that this was now simply who he was. What he most hoped was that it wasn't.
