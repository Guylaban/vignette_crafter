Adaeze is a 43-year-old Nigerian-American woman who works in IT support and has been married for
eleven years. She came to the clinic after her PCL-5 score of 55 prompted a referral from her
primary care physician, though she had been carrying what she described as "this weight" for most of
her adult life. The events that gave rise to that weight began in childhood, in a rural setting
outside Lagos where she spent summers with an aunt and uncle before her family emigrated. The abuse
occurred across several seasons, administered by the uncle in an outbuilding surrounded by dense
bush and tall grass, the air thick with the smell of red earth and vegetation. He would lock the
door behind them. Adaeze remembers the particular quality of silence in that room — the way the
outside sounds of insects and wind in the trees were muffled, the way the light came in only through
a single high window. She was told, repeatedly, that she brought it on herself by being difficult,
by being the kind of child who invited trouble. She was eight years old the first time.

Adaeze has come to believe, with a conviction that feels to her less like an opinion and more like a
fact she has simply always known, that there is something fundamentally wrong with her — not
something that was done to her, but something intrinsic, a flaw woven into her character that made
her the kind of person the abuse could find. She does not frame this as a belief she holds; she
experiences it as a truth she lives inside. This deep appraisal of herself as inherently defective
keeps her nervous system in a state of chronic readiness. Her body, she says, never quite settles.
At her desk in the IT support office, she sits with her shoulders drawn up toward her ears without
noticing, and when a ticket comes in marked urgent she feels a spike of alarm that seems
disproportionate even to her. She has difficulty concentrating on the step-by-step troubleshooting
her job requires, losing her thread mid-task, and she is aware that her colleagues have noticed. She
was passed over for a team lead position last year, and though nothing was said directly, she
suspects it was because she seems distracted and unreliable.

The chronic physiological tension and the false alarms that fire through her body throughout the day
have, in turn, deepened her conviction that something is wrong with her. She interprets her own
reactivity as further evidence of her defectiveness — normal people, she reasons, are not like this;
the fact that she is confirms what the uncle told her. This loop has been running quietly for
decades, each spike of alarm feeding back into the story she carries about who she is.

The appraisals also shape what she can and cannot approach in the world. Because she understands the
abuse as something her character attracted, the triggers that are most potent for her are those that
place her back inside the conditions of that original scene. A walk in a park or a green space —
something she encounters on her lunch break when colleagues suggest eating outside — can stop her
mid-stride. The smell of damp earth or the sound of wind moving through leaves pulls the past
forward in a way that feels less like remembering and more like return. Confined spaces are worse:
if a server room door closes behind her during a maintenance call and she hears the latch engage,
her body responds before her mind can intervene, heart rate climbing, chest tightening, the old
silence of that outbuilding pressing in. She has begun routing her support calls to avoid rooms
without propped-open doors. Talking about the events directly — which she has never done in any
therapeutic context — remains almost entirely foreclosed. The body's alarm, once triggered, also
makes these situations harder to leave behind; the residual tension from a false alarm at work can
linger for hours and make the natural environment on her evening commute feel newly threatening.

What finally brought Adaeze in was a moment three months ago when she was locked briefly inside a
server closet after a door malfunction. She sat on the floor for twenty minutes before someone heard
her. She has not been able to enter that room since. What she is most afraid of, she says, is that
the therapy will confirm what she already suspects — that the problem is not what happened to her,
but what she is. What she is most hoping for, though she can barely say it aloud, is that someone
will tell her she is wrong.
