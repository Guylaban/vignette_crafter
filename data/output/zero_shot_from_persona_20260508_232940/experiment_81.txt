clear next step, and the promise that he will become tougher than he feels. He is one of the
youngest in his unit and spends a lot of time watching the older men, copying how they move and
talk. At first, he enjoys the routine and the camaraderie. He learns to keep his bunk neat, to move
quickly when called, to laugh off discomfort. He feels proud wearing the uniform. His mother
worries, but he tells her he will be fine.

The event he returns to again and again happens during a convoy. It is hot, bright, and strangely

moving before his mind catches up. He is out of the vehicle, heart hammering, trying to locate where
the fire is coming from. He sees one of the men he has eaten beside, joked with, and tried to
impress lying injured in a way that does not make sense at first glance. He remembers the smell most
vividly: fuel, metal, dirt, something burnt. He remembers trying to do what he has been trained to
do while feeling, with humiliating clarity, that he is no longer inside himself. Everything becomes
both painfully sharp and far away.

Afterward, he tells himself he should be able to handle it. Other men seem to keep going. He keeps
going too. He does what is required, but sleep changes almost immediately. When he closes his eyes,
the road appears again. Sometimes it is the explosion exactly as it happened; sometimes the scene

sweat, already standing before he is fully awake. Loud sounds begin to get to him. A dropped metal
