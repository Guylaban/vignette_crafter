wife. At first he thinks a heavy truck is passing too close to the building, but within seconds the
floor begins to roll under him in a way that makes no sense. Cupboards burst open. Glass breaks. He
hears the deep, unnatural groan of the structure around them. Training takes over before thought
does. He shouts to his wife, reaches for her, and tries to move them both toward the doorway, but
the room pitches violently. Part of the ceiling gives way. Dust fills the air so fast he cannot
breathe without coughing. He remembers the sound more than anything: concrete cracking, pipes
screaming, people somewhere below already yelling for help.

A beam and broken masonry trap his leg. His wife is only a few feet away, pinned under debris he
cannot shift. He keeps talking to her in the same voice he has used with casualties for years—calm,
direct, certain help is coming—even as he knows the building is still moving and they are alone. He
can see blood at her temple and dust on her eyelashes. She answers at first. Then less. Then not at
all. He spends hours trying to free himself and then trying to reach her. He tears skin from his
hands. He calls her name until his throat is raw. When rescuers finally reach them, he is already a
medic again, telling them where to cut, what to lift first, what equipment they need. It is only
when someone touches his shoulder and says her body has to be left until the structure is stabilized
that he understands she is dead.
