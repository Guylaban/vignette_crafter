He is sixty-eight now, a seasoned European politician who has spent most of his adult life in public
rooms: council chambers, campaign halls, television studios, parliamentary corridors where people
clap him on the shoulder and expect him to know what to say. Before the accident, he thinks of
himself as someone who moved through pressure almost effortlessly. He liked the theater of politics,
the quick thinking, the negotiation, the physical stamina of long days and late-night votes. His
wife used to joke that he was most relaxed when three crises were unfolding at once.

The accident happens on a winter evening five years ago, after a regional party meeting in a
provincial city. He is being driven back along a dark motorway in heavy rain. He remembers looking
down at briefing papers under the dim car light, rehearsing lines for a morning interview, when he
feels the sudden sickening loss of control. There is the shriek of tires, a violent sideways pull,
headlights rushing toward him from the opposite lane, and then the impact. Metal folds around him.
Glass bursts across his face and lap. For a few seconds, maybe longer, he is trapped in a silence
that does not feel real, followed by the smell of fuel and hot rubber. He cannot move his left leg.
He is sure the car will catch fire. He hears the driver making a sound he cannot identify at first,
then realizes it is screaming.

What remains most vivid is not the pain but the certainty that he is about to die while fully
conscious. He remembers trying to unbuckle himself with shaking hands that no longer seem to belong
to him. He remembers thinking of his wife not sentimentally but with startling practical clarity—who
would call her, how she would hear, what unfinished things he had left in motion. When emergency
workers finally pull him out, he feels humiliated by his own terror. Even on the roadside, soaked
and shivering under foil blankets, he apologizes for “making a fuss.”

His injuries heal better than anyone first expects. He returns to public life within months, helped
along by aides, party colleagues, and his own insistence that he is fortunate. He tells the story as
others want to hear it: terrible crash, lucky escape, onward. He gives interviews from a cane-backed
chair and smiles grimly about resilience. What he does not say is that from the first week home,
sleep becomes a place of ambush. He wakes drenched, his heart hammering, hearing again the crunch of
metal and seeing those headlights aimed at him. Sometimes he wakes with his hands clawing at the
bedsheets as if trying to free himself from twisted seatbelts. His wife touches his shoulder and he
jerks away before he knows where he is.

his chest tightens and his breath goes shallow. More than once he has barked at drivers to slow down
or pull over, then sat rigid with embarrassment, pretending irritation rather than fear.

His work changes with him. In debate, he is still articulate, but staff notice he startles at

calculations required to keep panic from overtaking him. He says, with a mixture of shame and
disbelief, that surviving the crash was straightforward compared with living afterward, when nothing
