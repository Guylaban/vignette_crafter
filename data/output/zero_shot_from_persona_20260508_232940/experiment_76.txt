He is 22, a product manager at a small tech company, and he still looks younger than that when he
talks about home. He describes the city he grew up in as noisy, crowded, alive at all hours—street
vendors calling out, motorcycles weaving through traffic, music leaking from apartment windows.
Before the war reached his neighborhood, he is mostly worried about ordinary things: exams, flirting
badly over text, whether he is falling behind classmates who already seem to know what they want
from life. He likes organizing people, turning chaos into plans, which is part of why he eventually
moves into product work. Even as a teenager, he is the one making spreadsheets for school projects
and messaging everyone reminders.

When the fighting begins nearby, at first it does not feel real. It is something happening in other
districts, then something visible as smoke in the distance, then something that interrupts sleep. He
remembers the first night the windows shake hard enough to rattle the dishes in the kitchen. His
mother tells him to stay away from the balcony, but he keeps going back to look. He says this with
embarrassment now, as if curiosity were a kind of failure. Soon curiosity is replaced by routine
fear. He learns which sounds mean gunfire and which mean shelling. He learns how quickly a normal
afternoon can collapse.

The moment he returns to most often happens on an ordinary errand. He is out buying bread and
batteries because electricity has become unreliable and everyone is planning around outages. The
street is busy in that strained way people have when they are pretending life is intact. Then there
is a blast close enough to throw him sideways. He does not remember falling, only the pressure in
his ears and the sense that the world has been emptied out of sound. When hearing returns, it comes
all at once—screaming, car alarms, someone shouting for a child. He sees broken glass glittering
across the pavement and a man lying twisted near the curb. He sees blood on his own sleeve and
thinks for several seconds that he has been torn open, then realizes it belongs to someone who
collided with him. He runs without deciding to run. Later, this is the part that bothers him almost
as much as the explosion itself: not what he saw, but that his body chose escape before anything
else.

After that day, he stops going out unless he has to. He tells himself he is being practical.
Everyone is adapting. But his world becomes narrow. He sits away from windows. He keeps his phone
charged and checks routes obsessively. If his mother or younger cousin is late coming home, he
imagines the worst with a speed that feels mechanical. At night he sleeps in fragments. Sometimes he
wakes already sitting up, heart pounding, before he fully knows where he is. Sometimes he dreams of
the street after the blast, but often the dreams are less direct: corridors he cannot exit, phones
that will not connect, a city with no faces in it.
