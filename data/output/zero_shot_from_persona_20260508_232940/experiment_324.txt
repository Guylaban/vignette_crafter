He is seventy-one now, a married North American man who still works evenings as a security guard in
a mid-sized office complex, mostly because he likes routine and does not know what to do with
himself without it. When he talks about what happened, he does not begin with the attack itself. He
begins with the ordinary day that came before it, as if his mind still wants to prove there had once
been a world in which people drank coffee, complained about traffic, and expected to come home
unchanged.

At the time, he is working security in a downtown transportation terminal. He has worked some
version of this job most of his adult life and takes pride in being steady, observant, the one who
notices when a door is left unsecured or a stranger is moving against the flow of the crowd. That
morning feels busy but unremarkable. He remembers teasing a younger coworker about being late,
remembers a woman asking for directions, remembers thinking about whether he and his wife should
visit their daughter that weekend.

Then the explosion happens.

He still describes it first as a force in his chest rather than a sound. The terminal seems to lift
and split at once. Glass breaks inward. Alarms begin screaming. For a second he cannot understand
what he is seeing: smoke, people on the ground, others running, someone shouting that there is
another device. Training takes over before thought does. He moves toward the injured, tries to clear
a path, tries to make his voice louder than panic. He sees a young man with severe burns, a woman
clutching her leg, a child wandering and calling for his father. He smells metal, fuel, and
something burned that he later realizes is flesh. Even now, years later, that smell can return to
him so vividly that he has to grip the arms of his chair.

In the days after, he tells himself he is lucky. He is alive. He has cuts and bruises, some hearing
loss that fades, and a stiffness in his shoulder that eventually improves. The news calls it
terrorism. There are photographs of the dead, speculation about motives, officials praising first
responders and security personnel. Neighbors shake his hand. He does not sleep much, but he assumes
that is normal. His wife notices he jerks awake, soaked in sweat, and once he nearly swings at her
when she touches his shoulder in the dark. He apologizes and says it is just stress.

What lingers surprises him. He cannot stop seeing fragments of the scene when he least expects it: a
dropped shoe near a bench, blood spreading beneath a newspaper, the child’s open mouth. At the
grocery store, if a cart crashes into a display, his whole body bolts before his mind catches up. On
buses or trains, he scans everyone’s hands, bags, and faces. He positions himself near exits. If
someone leaves a backpack unattended, he feels a surge of heat and anger so intense it seems to
erase whatever conversation he was having.

His wife wants to go to crowded family events, street fairs, holiday concerts. He starts finding
reasons not to go. He says parking is bad, the weather might turn, his knees are acting up. In
truth, crowds make him feel trapped and exposed at the same time. He hates how quickly his mind
turns strangers into threats. He hates even more that part of him believes this vigilance is the
only thing keeping people alive. At home, he becomes more irritable, snapping over small things—the
television volume, a late phone call, a dish left near the sink. Afterwards he often sits in
silence, ashamed, while his wife moves carefully around him.

Work remains complicated. Security had once felt simple to him: keep watch, follow procedure,
protect people. After the bombing, the uniform carries a different weight. He retires briefly,
thinking he is done, but within months he feels useless and restless. Eventually he takes a quieter
security job in an office building. Even there, he checks stairwells repeatedly, studies delivery
trucks too long, notices who hesitates at the entrance. Coworkers describe him as thorough. They do
not see how much effort it takes for him to stay in his chair when a fire alarm test begins, or how
he avoids the lobby on anniversaries of the attack because the echo of footsteps on polished floors
sounds too much like that terminal.

He sleeps in short stretches. Some nights he dreams he is back in the smoke and cannot reach the
people calling to him. Other nights there is no dream, only the sensation of danger arriving before
he is fully awake. He keeps the television on low for background noise because silence feels like
waiting. He does not talk much about guilt, but it is there. He wonders whether he missed something,
whether better attention might have prevented it, whether the people who died would still be alive
if he had reached them faster.

What troubles him most now is not only fear but the narrowing of his life. He still loves his wife,
still shows up for work, still mows the lawn and pays the bills and asks after his grandchildren.
From the outside he appears reliable, disciplined, intact. But he feels as if part of him never left
that station. He lives in the present while constantly bracing for the past to happen again.
