when other people lose theirs. Before the storm, that is how he understands his role at work and at
home. He likes routine, clear chains of command, and the comfort of believing that preparation
matters. He and his wife live near the coast because of his assignment, and in the weeks before the
disaster he follows the forecasts with the practiced attention of someone trained to respect weather
without dramatizing it. He helps secure the house, fills containers with water, checks batteries,
and reassures his wife that they are ready.

When the hurricane makes landfall, it becomes clear very quickly that “ready” is a relative word.
The wind sounds, in his memory, less like weather than like something alive pushing against the
walls. At first he moves through the house with purpose, checking windows, bringing in towels,
making calls while the power still flickers on and off. Then the water begins coming in faster than
he expects. The street disappears. A neighbor shouts from outside, and somewhere nearby glass
breaks. He remembers the smell most vividly now: wet drywall, sewage, gasoline, and the briny odor
of floodwater moving where it should not be.

He gets his wife into the attic space when the lower floor starts filling. He tells himself what he
tells junior officers in emergencies: focus on the next task, not the whole picture. But in the
attic, with the dark pressing in and the house creaking beneath them, he feels something he still
has trouble naming. It is not only fear. It is the sickening realization that his rank, his
training, and his confidence do not matter against water rising inch by inch. He uses a tool to
break through part of the roof in case they need to climb out. His hands shake so badly that he cuts
himself without noticing until his wife points out the blood. They wait for hours listening to
distant voices, sirens, and the thud of helicopter blades that seem both near and unreachable. He
keeps scanning, calculating, listening for structural collapse. Even after they are rescued by boat,
he cannot shake the image of their house half-submerged, as if the ground itself has betrayed them.

In the months afterward, he returns to duty sooner than his wife thinks he should. He tells her he
needs to get back to normal, though “normal” turns out to be something he can no longer find. At
work he performs well enough to avoid attention. He is early, uniform pressed, paperwork complete.
