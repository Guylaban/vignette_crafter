He is 73 now, a North American man who has spent most of his adult life working as a journalist, the
kind who once believed that bearing witness was a form of service. For decades he learned to stand

The event happens late in his career, when he is sent to cover an industrial explosion at a chemical

is streaked with black smoke. Sirens overlap. People run in directions that make no sense. He
remembers the smell most vividly, a sharp, metallic burn that catches in his throat and makes his
eyes water. He moves closer than he should. He tells himself he needs to see for himself.

Then a second blast throws debris across the lot and knocks him to the ground. For a few seconds he
cannot hear anything but a high ringing. When he looks up, a man near the chain-link fence is on
fire. Another worker is screaming for his brother. There are pieces of concrete, twisted metal, and
something he knows is part of a body before his mind can fully name it. He crawls behind a news van,
stunned and ashamed at how quickly his training disappears. Later he cannot remember how he gets
out, only that he does, and that he keeps his notebook clenched in his fist so hard the spiral
binding cuts into his palm.

colleagues’ praise for staying composed. He does not feel composed. He feels split in two: the

the burning man the moment he closes his eyes. Sometimes the dream is exact; sometimes it changes
