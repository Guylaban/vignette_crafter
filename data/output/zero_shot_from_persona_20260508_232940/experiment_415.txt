way he has learned to talk in briefing rooms: flatly, in sequence, with the unnecessary emotion
stripped out. He says the weather was manageable, the aircraft was functioning, the mission routine.
Even now, years later, he can list the indicators that first looked wrong. What he cannot do, at
least at first, is stay in the room while he tells it.

The accident happens during what should have been an ordinary training flight. He is in the cockpit,
moving through well-practiced checks, when a sudden mechanical failure turns familiar procedures
into a rapidly narrowing set of bad options. He remembers the warning tone, the violent shudder
through the frame, the smell of something hot and electrical. He is trained to act, so he acts. He
runs the emergency sequence with disciplined speed, but the aircraft is losing altitude too fast.
For a few seconds he is certain he is going to die. That certainty is what stays with him most
clearly—not a thought, exactly, but a complete bodily conviction. Time stretches. He can see the
ocean below and the instrument panel in front of him with impossible sharpness. He ejects late,
later than he thinks he should have, and survives with injuries that heal more quickly than his
sense of what happened.

In the hospital, people call him lucky. His wife sits beside the bed, relieved, grateful, wanting
him home. He says the right things and tries to feel them. Outwardly he recovers well. He attends
debriefings, answers questions, and allows colleagues to slap his shoulder and joke about buying
lottery tickets. He notices that everyone prefers the version of the story with a competent ending:
system failure, skilled response, survival. He tries to prefer that version too.

What interrupts it are the nights. Sleep becomes thin and unreliable. He wakes with his heart
racing, already sitting upright before he fully understands where he is. Sometimes he has the same
dream: the aircraft trembling beneath him, the altimeter unwinding, his gloved hand reaching for a
control that no longer matters. Sometimes there is no dream, only the surge of panic and the smell
of burning that seems, for a second, present in the room. His wife reaches for him and he startles
hard enough to frighten both of them. He begins sleeping closer to the edge of the bed. Later, on
worse nights, he says he does not want to disturb her and moves to the spare room.

During the day, he returns to work, because that is what men like him do and because flying has

intensely attentive to sounds and gauges, so vigilant that it is exhausting. He still performs well,
perhaps too well. Colleagues notice he is more rigid in the cockpit, less tolerant of deviation,
quicker to snap during preflight checks. He begins reviewing emergency procedures compulsively,
staying late, running through scenarios over and over as if repetition might erase the memory of
helplessness.

prevented the whole thing. He has survived, returned to duty, kept his marriage intact on paper, and
