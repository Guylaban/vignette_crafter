He is 74 now, a European man who has spent most of his life around books, blackboards, and kitchen
tables turned into makeshift classrooms. Even in retirement age, he still works as a tutor, seeing a
handful of students each week, proud that he can still explain a difficult poem or walk a nervous
teenager through algebra without making them feel small. He lives with his partner in a modest house
and keeps a careful routine. On the surface, he appears composed, courteous, even slightly formal.
It takes time before the shape of his days becomes clear.

The turning point comes several years ago, during a flood that sweeps through the town where he is
living at the time. He remembers the day in fragments that feel sharper than ordinary memory: the
strange yellow-gray light outside, the radio warnings that seem overcautious until they are not, the
speed with which water begins filling the street. At first he tells himself it will pass. He moves
books from the lower shelves, lifts a few boxes, tries to reassure his partner. Then the water keeps
rising. There is a moment he returns to often, against his will, when he opens the back door and
sees the garden already gone, replaced by a moving brown current carrying branches, rubbish, and a
child’s plastic chair. The force of it makes him understand, all at once, that the house is no
longer a shelter.

They leave in a hurry, carrying almost nothing. He can still feel the cold water soaking through his
trousers as they make their way out. Sirens sound somewhere nearby. A neighbor shouts something he
cannot make out. What stays with him most is not only fear, but helplessness: standing in late life,
after decades of believing that calm thought can solve most things, and finding himself reduced to
watching water take whatever it wants. In the temporary shelter that night, he cannot sleep. Every
creak and raised voice startles him. He keeps looking for his partner, needing to confirm she is
still there.

Afterward, there are practical matters to handle—insurance forms, damaged furniture, ruined papers,
the exhausting business of sorting what can be saved and what must be thrown away. He throws himself
into these tasks with the discipline that has always served him well. People comment on how well he
is coping. He says little. He does not mention that when he closes his eyes he sees the waterline on
the walls. He does not tell anyone that he wakes with his heart pounding, sure for a few seconds
that he is back in that house, trapped by the rising flood.

In the months that follow, his world grows smaller. Rain becomes intolerable. If clouds gather, he
begins checking weather forecasts repeatedly, then rechecking them as if the outcome might change
through vigilance alone. He moves important documents upstairs, then feels foolish, then moves them
again. During storms he stands at the window scanning the street drains, unable to sit, unable to
read. His partner notices he is irritable on those days and distant afterward. He snaps at small
things and then apologizes with visible shame.

Tutoring, once a pleasure, becomes complicated. He still enjoys the students themselves and speaks
warmly to them, but concentration is harder than it used to be. A sudden downpour on the roof can
pull him out of a lesson mid-sentence. One student drops a heavy backpack by the front door and the
thud makes him jolt so noticeably that the boy pauses and stares. He laughs it off, but afterward he
feels embarrassed and old in a way that has nothing to do with age. When a student writes an essay

What troubles him nearly as much as the fear is the change in how he thinks about himself. He has
always been measured, intellectually curious, someone students rely on for steadiness. Now he finds

outside. He sometimes sits with a book open on his lap and realizes he has not turned a page.

forecast, and he says, “Just being sensible.” He does not easily admit how frightened he still
feels. Part of him believes he should be over it by now, that a man of 74 ought to have perspective.

continues tutoring, continues making tea before students arrive, continues laying out pencils and

in that doorway, watching the water rise.
