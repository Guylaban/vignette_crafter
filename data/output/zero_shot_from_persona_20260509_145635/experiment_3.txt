# Clinical Vignette: Kofi, Age 55

Kofi has spent the better part of three decades making order out of chaos. As a senior bomb disposal
technician, he has built his identity around the capacity to stay calm when everything around him is
falling apart — to read a situation with precision, to act with deliberate control, and to walk
away. It is a profession that has shaped not only his skills but the way he understands himself as a
man. He is someone who does not flinch.

That story broke open four years ago, when flooding devastated the region where he had grown up and
where his elderly mother still lived. He had traveled home to help with the response effort, lending
his skills to a volunteer rescue operation in the days after the floodwaters rose. What he
encountered there was nothing like the controlled danger of his professional life. There were no
procedures to follow, no protocols to contain the situation. The water moved without logic. On the
third day, he pulled two children from a submerged vehicle, but the driver — a young woman who
looked briefly, horribly, directly at him — did not survive. He held her hand in the water for
longer than he can account for. He does not speak about this easily or in full.

In the weeks that followed, Kofi returned to work and told himself he was fine. He is practiced at
returning to work. But something had shifted in the architecture of how he moved through the world.
He began sleeping poorly, waking at two or three in the morning with his heart already racing,
certain that something is flooding — the room, the house, the street outside. His wife, Abena, tells
him he sometimes calls out in his sleep, though he rarely remembers what he was dreaming. On the
nights he does remember, he is always back in the water, and the young woman's face is always
turning toward him.

He has become hypervigilant in ways that feel foreign even to him. At work, colleagues have noticed
that he startles at sounds he would previously have catalogued without reaction. He finds himself
scanning drainage channels when he drives, calculating water levels, noting where the land dips.
During last year's rainy season, he could not bring himself to cross a particular bridge over a
swollen river and drove forty minutes out of his way for three weeks rather than address it
directly. He told Abena it was road maintenance.

He has grown quieter at home. His two adult children have commented that he seems distant, and Kofi
knows this is true but cannot easily explain the distance even to himself. He feels, much of the
time, as though he is watching his own life from slightly outside it — present at the dinner table,
present at family gatherings, but separated from the warmth of those moments by something he cannot
name or dismantle. This troubles him deeply, because his family is what he has always worked to
protect.

What brings him to the present moment is a conversation with his occupational health officer after a
routine review flagged changes in his performance documentation. He was not referred; he came
voluntarily, which cost him something. He sits in the chair with his hands resting on his knees,
composed in the way of a man who has spent decades composing himself under pressure. He says,
carefully, that he does not understand why a flood has undone him when explosives never did. He has
been trying to solve this problem the way he solves every problem — through knowledge, through
control, through sheer professional competence — and it has not worked.

He is fifty-five years old. He has a wife who loves him and children who are watching him carefully.
He has a career built on the belief that the right training can prepare a person for anything. He is
sitting here, quietly asking why that turned out not to be true.
