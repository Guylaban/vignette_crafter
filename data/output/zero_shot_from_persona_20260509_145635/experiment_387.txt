**Vignette: Amara, Age 33**

Amara grew up watching her younger brother Kofi chase lizards through the red dust of their family's
compound, always fearless, always laughing. When they both eventually settled in the same city as
adults, that closeness held. They spoke by phone nearly every evening, a ritual so ordinary she
never thought to name it as precious. Then, fourteen months ago, Kofi was killed in a road accident
on his way home from a night shift — struck by a driver who ran a red light at speed. He was twenty-
nine. The police called Amara first because her number was saved in his phone as *Sister
(Emergency)*. She stood in her kitchen holding the receiver, and something in her, she says, simply
went very still.

In the weeks that followed, she returned to work as a park ranger because she did not know what else
to do with her body. The forest, which had always felt like a place of order and continuity, began
to feel treacherous in ways she could not explain to her husband or her colleagues. She would be
marking a trail or conducting a wildlife count, moving quietly through the trees, and without
warning the image of Kofi's face — not as she last saw it, but as she imagined it at the moment of
impact — would arrive with a force that stopped her mid-step. Her chest would tighten, her vision
would narrow, and she would have to grip the nearest tree trunk and breathe until the ground felt
solid again.

She stopped answering calls after dusk. The evening phone ritual she and Kofi had shared for years
now sits like a wound in the architecture of her day. Her husband, Emeka, tries to reach her during
those hours, and she sees his calls flash across the screen and feels something close to panic — not
because she doesn't love him, but because the ringing itself has become unbearable, a sound that
carries the memory of the call that changed everything. She answers eventually, always apologizing,
never quite explaining.

Sleep has become a negotiation she rarely wins. She wakes at two or three in the morning with her
heart hammering, sometimes mid-dream, sometimes from nothing she can identify. In the dreams, she is
always arriving somewhere too late — to a hospital, to an accident scene, to a crossroads where Kofi
is standing and then isn't. She lies awake afterward cataloguing the things she did not say to him,
the visit she postponed the month before he died, the way she had ended their last call quickly
because dinner was burning on the stove.

At work, she has developed a reputation for being withdrawn. She used to lead school groups through
the park with warmth and enthusiasm, pointing out bird calls, crouching down to show children animal
tracks. Now she requests the solitary patrols — the early morning boundary checks, the solo
monitoring shifts — because being alone in the trees is easier than managing her own face around
people. She is hypervigilant in ways that read as professionalism but feel, to her, like something
rawer: she scans roadsides when she drives, calculates the stopping distances of approaching
vehicles, mentally traces escape routes in rooms. She cannot stop preparing for the thing she could
not prepare for.

Amara describes herself as someone who has always been strong, and she says this with a kind of
exhaustion, as though strength has been a garment she has worn so long it has begun to chafe. She
loves Emeka. She loves the park, the smell of earth after rain, the slow work of conservation. But
she moves through her days now as though behind glass — present in outline, unreachable in some
deeper way that she cannot yet find the words for. She says she is waiting to feel like herself
again. She is not sure, anymore, who that person is.
