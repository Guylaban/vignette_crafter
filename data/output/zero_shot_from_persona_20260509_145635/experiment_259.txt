**Vignette: Dr. Mere Tuilagi, Age 77**

Mere has spent the better part of five decades with her hands inside other people's bodies,
repairing what is broken, restoring what disease has taken. She trained in Auckland, built a
surgical career that spanned three continents, and retired only two years ago from a vascular
practice she ran for over thirty years in Christchurch. She has always understood the body as a
system to be read and managed. What happened to her own body three years ago has shattered that
understanding in ways she is still struggling to name.

It began with fatigue she dismissed as the ordinary accumulation of a long career. Then came the
weight loss, the night sweats, the lymph nodes swelling along her neck like stones settling beneath
the skin. The diagnosis — aggressive non-Hodgkin's lymphoma — arrived on a Tuesday afternoon,
delivered by a colleague she had known for twenty years. She remembers sitting in his office and
watching his mouth move, and thinking, with the detached clarity of a surgeon, *this is what my
patients feel*. She had never truly known until that moment.

The months of chemotherapy that followed were, she says quietly, the most terrifying of her life.
Not the nausea, not the hair loss, not even the infections that twice sent her to intensive care.
What frightened her most was the sensation of lying in a hospital bed, unable to control a single
thing happening inside her own body — she, who had spent her career being the one who controlled.
During one particularly severe episode of sepsis, she became convinced she was dying. She remembers
the ceiling tiles above her bed with a precision that disturbs her, the particular quality of the
light at three in the morning, the sound of a monitor alarming somewhere down the corridor. She
survived. The lymphoma went into remission. Her oncologist called it an excellent outcome.

Mere does not experience it as excellent. She lives alone now — her marriage ended years ago,
amicably but finally, under the weight of a career that left little room for anything else. Her two
adult children are in Australia and call regularly, but she finds she cannot tell them what her days
are actually like. Most nights she sleeps no more than three or four hours before waking abruptly,
heart hammering, certain that something is wrong inside her. She lies in the dark cataloguing her
body's sensations with the obsessive precision of a diagnostician who has lost her objectivity.
Every ache becomes a relapse. Every fatigue becomes a harbinger.

She has stopped reading surgical journals, which once gave her genuine pleasure. She finds she
cannot tolerate descriptions of disease, of bodies failing, of outcomes that go the other way. She
declined an invitation to speak at a conference last spring and told the organizers she was
traveling. She was, in fact, sitting in her garden in Christchurch, unable to make herself get
dressed. She does not tell people this.

What she does tell people is that she is enjoying retirement, that she is gardening, that she is
finally reading the novels she never had time for. This is partially true. She is gardening. But she
gardens with a kind of joyless urgency, as though stillness is dangerous. She rarely reads for long
before her concentration fractures and she finds herself somewhere else entirely — back in that ICU
room, back in that particular light, listening to that particular alarm.

She came to therapy, she explains in their first session, because she is a scientist and she
recognized, with clinical precision, that what she is experiencing is not adjustment. She says this
with a dry, self-deprecating smile that does not reach her eyes. She spent fifty years understanding
other people's suffering from the outside. She is finding it considerably harder, she admits, from
the inside.
