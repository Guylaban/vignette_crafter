**Vignette: Priya**

Priya is a 37-year-old South Asian woman who has been married for eleven years and runs a small
clothing boutique with her husband in a busy commercial district. She is soft-spoken and deliberate
in how she chooses her words, often pausing mid-sentence as though she is deciding, in real time,
how much of herself to offer. She came to therapy at her primary care physician's urging, describing
her reason for coming simply as "I haven't been myself for a long time."

Two years ago, Priya was sexually assaulted by a vendor she had known professionally for several
years. He had come to the shop after hours to drop off inventory, something that had happened dozens
of times before. She had trusted him. After the assault, she drove herself home, showered, and did
not tell her husband. She went to the shop the next morning and opened on time.

For the first several weeks, she functioned on what she now calls "automatic." She managed
inventory, greeted customers, balanced accounts. She told herself it was over and that she was fine.
But the body kept its own record. She began waking at three or four in the morning, heart hammering,
certain that someone was in the room. She started sleeping with the bedroom light on, which her
husband noticed but initially attributed to stress. She stopped eating dinner with any real
appetite. She lost nearly eight pounds in six weeks.

The shop, which had always been a source of pride, became a source of dread. The sound of the back
door opening — the same door the vendor had used — sends a cold shock through her chest every single
time, even now. She rearranged the store layout twice, ostensibly for aesthetic reasons, though she
knows she was trying to change the geography of what happened. She no longer schedules any vendor
meetings after 4 p.m. and insists her husband or a part-time employee be present for all deliveries.
She has never fully explained why.

Priya has not told her husband about the assault. The weight of that silence is enormous, and she
describes it as a second wound she carries separately. She comes from a family and community where
sexual violence is rarely named, and she carries a deep, internalized fear that disclosure would
bring shame onto her marriage and her family's reputation. She sometimes wonders whether she would
even be believed. These thoughts do not feel irrational to her — they feel like facts.

Intrusive memories arrive without warning. A particular cologne, the creak of a storage shelf, a man
standing too close in a crowded market — any of these can pull her back into the room where it
happened. She describes the experience not as remembering but as *returning*, as though time
collapses and she is briefly, terrifyingly, there again. She has learned to excuse herself to the
shop's back office when this happens, where she sits until her breathing steadies, sometimes for
twenty minutes or more.

Her marriage has quietly suffered. She flinches at physical touch she did not initiate, and intimacy
with her husband has become nearly impossible. She has constructed careful, plausible explanations —
fatigue, hormones, stress from the business — and her husband has accepted them with the patience of
a man who loves her and does not know what he is actually navigating. She describes watching him
from across a room sometimes and feeling a grief she cannot name, a sense that something between
them has been altered in ways she does not know how to repair.

She presents in sessions as composed, even gracious, occasionally apologizing for "taking up time."
Beneath that composure is a woman who is exhausted from two years of managing an internal world that
has become relentless and consuming. She says she does not feel like the person she was before. When
asked to describe that person, she is quiet for a long moment before saying, simply, "She wasn't
afraid."
