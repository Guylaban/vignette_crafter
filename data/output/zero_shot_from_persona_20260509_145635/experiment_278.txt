# Vignette: Harold

Harold is a 71-year-old man who has spent the better part of four decades selling insurance policies
from a modest office on the outskirts of a mid-sized Ohio city. He comes to sessions dressed neatly,
collar buttoned to the top, hands folded in his lap — a man who has made a life's work of projecting
calm and reliability to others. It takes several meetings before he begins to speak about his
childhood with any real specificity, and even then he chooses his words with the careful precision
of someone who has long practiced keeping the worst of it at a manageable distance.

Harold grew up in rural Pennsylvania in the 1950s and 60s, the second of four children. His father
was a volatile man who drank heavily and who beat Harold with a belt, a wooden spoon, or whatever
was within reach, often for reasons Harold could not then and cannot now identify. The beatings were
not occasional — they were a rhythm the household organized itself around. Harold learned to read
his father's footsteps on the porch, the particular sound of a truck door slamming, the silences at
dinner that meant something was coming. His mother, he says quietly, "did what she could," but she
was afraid too. He does not elaborate on that, and for now it seems important not to push.

Harold left home at seventeen and never returned. He built a life that, from the outside, looks
stable and self-sufficient. He earned his insurance license in his late twenties, built a small
client base through sheer persistence, and has maintained it ever since. He never married, though he
came close once in his late thirties. He ended the relationship himself, though he struggles to say
exactly why. "I just couldn't let her all the way in," he says, looking at his hands.

What brings Harold in now, at seventy-one, is a combination of things that have converged in the
past two years. He retired partially — still sees a few longtime clients, more out of habit than
necessity — and the sudden absence of structure has left him unmoored in ways he didn't anticipate.
Without the daily scaffolding of work, the past has moved closer. He wakes most nights between two
and four in the morning, heart hammering, the details of specific incidents from childhood vivid and
immediate, as though no time has passed at all. During the day he finds himself scanning — a door
opening too quickly, a raised voice in a parking lot, a man with a particular build — and his body
responds before his mind can catch up, flooding with adrenaline, his chest tightening.

He describes a persistent low-grade sense that something bad is about to happen, a vigilance he has
carried so long he assumed it was simply his personality. He avoids family gatherings, of which
there are few now, and has declined invitations from a neighbor who has tried repeatedly to include
him in a weekly card game. Being around people who knew his father, or who remind him of that world,
feels intolerable in a way he cannot fully articulate. He describes himself as "not much for
closeness," though there is something in how he says it — a flatness, a resignation — that suggests
this is not a preference but a wound.

Harold is a man who has survived by being useful, competent, and contained. He spent his career
helping other people plan for loss, mitigate risk, protect what they had built. There is a quiet
irony in this that he himself seems to sense, though he has never put it into words. He is not a man
given to self-pity, and he resists any framing that positions him as a victim. What he will say,
after a long pause in a recent session, is this: "I think I've been waiting my whole life to feel
safe. And I'm running out of time to figure out how."
