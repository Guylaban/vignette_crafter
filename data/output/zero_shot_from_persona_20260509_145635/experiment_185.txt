# Carmen

Carmen is a 55-year-old Colombian-American woman who spent eight years serving in the United States
Army Reserve before transitioning to civilian life in her early thirties. She works as a bank teller
at a regional branch in a mid-sized city, a job she has held for nearly fifteen years. She is
divorced, lives alone in a modest apartment, and describes herself as someone who has always
preferred to keep things orderly and predictable. For a long time, she believed she had put the past
behind her.

The event that changed everything happened during her second deployment to Iraq in 2004. Her unit
was traveling in a convoy when the vehicle ahead of hers struck an improvised explosive device.
Carmen was close enough to feel the concussive force of the blast before she fully understood what
was happening. She watched two of her fellow soldiers — men she had eaten with, laughed with, argued
with — burn inside that vehicle. She was among those who ran toward the wreckage. She remembers the
heat on her face, the smell, and the sound of someone screaming that eventually stopped. She has
never spoken about what she saw in those final moments at the vehicle. She says only that there are
things she cannot put into words, and she leaves it at that.

After her discharge, Carmen built a life that looked, from the outside, like recovery. She learned
English fluently, earned her GED, got the bank job, married, and for a while seemed to be moving
steadily forward. But the marriage frayed under the weight of things she could not explain to her
husband — the nights she sat awake in the kitchen because sleep brought images she refused to
entertain, the way she would go cold and distant without warning, the anger that sometimes rose in
her like something pressurized. They divorced seven years ago, and Carmen tells this with very
little emotion, as though it were simply an administrative outcome of facts already in motion.

At the bank, Carmen is known as reliable and precise. She counts cash with practiced calm, greets
customers by name, and rarely makes errors. But certain moments at the teller window cost her more
than her colleagues could guess. When a customer slams a hand on the counter, when a car backfires
in the parking lot outside, when someone raises their voice even in ordinary frustration — her body
responds before her mind can intervene. Her heart races, her vision narrows, and for a few seconds
she is not in the bank at all. She has learned to breathe through it, to smile, to finish the
transaction. No one notices. She notices everything.

She sleeps poorly and wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, her body convinced
something is wrong. She does not always remember dreaming, but she wakes with a residue of dread
that takes hours to dissolve. She has stopped watching certain kinds of films and avoids the news
when it covers conflict overseas. She tells herself this is a reasonable preference, not avoidance,
though somewhere she understands the distinction is thinner than she admits.

Carmen does not think of herself as someone who was damaged by the war. She is proud of her service,
proud of her citizenship, proud of the discipline the military gave her. What she struggles to
reconcile is the gap between the person she believes herself to be — competent, self-sufficient,
resilient — and the person who sits in her car for ten minutes after a hard shift, unable to explain
why she feels so exhausted by the simple act of existing in the world. She came to therapy not
because she believes something is deeply wrong, but because a younger colleague at the bank
mentioned it offhandedly, and Carmen, who has spent two decades managing everything alone, found
herself thinking about it for weeks before finally making the call.
