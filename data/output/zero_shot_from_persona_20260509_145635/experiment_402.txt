# The Weight of the Waiting Room

Marcus is a sixty-five-year-old Māori man who has spent most of his working life building things —
coordinating teams, managing timelines, solving problems before they become crises. He is good at
this. He has always believed that with enough preparation and the right people in the right places,
you can control most outcomes. That belief shattered eleven years ago in a pediatric intensive care
unit in Auckland, and he has never quite reassembled it.

His daughter Aroha was seven when she was rushed to hospital with bacterial meningitis. What began
as what Marcus assumed was a bad flu turned critical within hours. He remembers standing in a
corridor while nurses moved with urgent, quiet efficiency around his daughter's small body, and
understanding for the first time in his life that he could do absolutely nothing. No plan. No
contingency. He was a man whose entire professional identity rested on managing uncertainty, and
here was something entirely beyond his reach. Aroha survived, but she spent three weeks in intensive
care, and Marcus spent those weeks in a state of suspended terror he has never fully left.

His wife, Hinerangi, died of a stroke four years ago. He speaks of her gently but briefly, as though
her loss is a room he can only stand in the doorway of. Since her death, he lives alone in the house
they shared in Tauranga, and the silence there has a particular quality he finds difficult to
describe.

Aroha is now eighteen and healthy. She is studying in Wellington, and by any measure she is
thriving. Marcus knows this. He tracks it carefully — calls her every second day, asks precise
questions about her eating and sleep, monitors her Instagram with the focused attention he once gave
project risk registers. When she doesn't answer his calls within a few hours, something tightens in
his chest that he recognizes as irrational but cannot dismiss. He imagines, in vivid and unwanted
detail, the scenarios in which she does not answer because she cannot.

Sleep is difficult. He wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, his heart already
moving fast, arriving conscious mid-fear rather than drifting into it. The images that come are not
always memories exactly — sometimes they are composites, his daughter's face overlaid with hospital
sounds, the particular blue of the ICU lighting, the moment a doctor placed a hand on his shoulder
and he thought it was over. He gets up and makes tea and sits at the kitchen table until the feeling
passes, which it usually does, slowly, by dawn.

At work, Marcus functions well in most respects. His colleagues describe him as thorough, steady,
experienced. He is all of these things. But he notices that he has become increasingly uncomfortable
delegating tasks that involve any element of unpredictability. When a project enters a phase of
genuine uncertainty, he finds himself working longer hours, checking in more frequently than
necessary, running scenarios in his head at night. His team is patient with him. He is not always
patient with himself.

He avoids hospitals when he can. He has not visited a friend in hospital in eleven years. He knows
this is not reasonable. He goes anyway, he tells himself, when it truly matters — but so far he has
managed to find reasons why it has not truly mattered.

What Marcus finds hardest to articulate is the way the experience changed his relationship with the
future itself. He used to be comfortable projecting forward — five-year plans, retirement goals,
things to build toward. Now the future feels like a surface he cannot trust his weight on. He loves
his daughter with an intensity that sometimes frightens him, and loving her means knowing, in a way
he cannot unknow, exactly how quickly everything can change.

He sits in the consulting room with his hands folded in his lap, composed and precise, and says he's
not sure why he's here, really. Things are fine. Aroha is fine.

He says it like a man who has been saying it for eleven years and is very, very tired.
