**Karim**

Karim is a thirty-year-old tax inspector of Lebanese descent who sits across from his therapist with
a careful stillness, the kind that looks composed until you notice his hands resting flat on his
thighs, pressing down slightly, as though steadying himself against something invisible. He chooses
his words the way he works through a financial audit — methodically, double-checking before
committing — and yet even that precision occasionally fails him when the conversation turns to the
evening fourteen months ago that split his life into a before and after.

It was a Tuesday in late autumn. Karim was driving home from a late site visit, a route he had taken
hundreds of times, when a lorry ran a red light at speed and struck the driver's side of his car.
The vehicle spun and rolled. He remembers the sound — a tearing, compressing roar — and then a
strange, suspended silence before he understood he was upside down, glass in his hair, unable to
move his left arm. He was conscious throughout. Paramedics took forty minutes to cut him free. He
kept thinking, with odd bureaucratic clarity, that he hadn't filed a particular report before
leaving the office.

He recovered physically within three months. A fractured clavicle, lacerations, bruised ribs. His
marriage, already strained, did not survive the aftermath. His ex-wife, Nadia, found his withdrawal
unbearable — the way he stopped sleeping in their bed because lying flat made him feel trapped, the
way he flinched at ordinary sounds, the way he was simply no longer present even when he was
standing in the same room. They divorced eight months ago. Karim speaks of this without apparent
bitterness, as if it, too, was an outcome he had audited and accepted.

He drives now only when he has no alternative, and even then he takes routes that avoid the
intersection where the collision happened, adding twenty minutes to his commute. On the motorway,
his chest tightens and his vision narrows. Twice he has pulled over and sat on the hard shoulder,
breathing, waiting for his heart rate to slow. He has not told his supervisor why he prefers to take
the train to client visits.

At work, Karim functions. He is thorough and well-regarded, and the linear logic of tax codes offers
him something reliable to hold onto. But concentration frays without warning. He will be cross-
referencing figures and suddenly he is back inside the car, upside down, and the numbers on the
screen mean nothing. He saves his work compulsively now, every few minutes, as though afraid
something will be lost before he can secure it.

Sleep is the worst of it, he says quietly. He wakes between two and four in the morning with his
pulse hammering, the dream always the same — not a replay, exactly, but a version in which no one
comes. The paramedics never arrive. He is still there. He lies awake afterwards in his flat,
listening to the street outside, and feels a loneliness he describes as physical, like a pressure
behind the sternum.

He has stopped calling his parents as often. His mother asks why he sounds tired and he tells her he
has been busy with year-end assessments. He does not want her to worry. He does not want, he admits,
for anyone to see how much effort ordinary life currently requires.

What strikes his therapist most is the gap between how Karim presents — measured, self-sufficient,
almost formal — and what he occasionally lets slip: that he sometimes sits in his parked car outside
his building for ten or fifteen minutes before he can make himself go inside, that he has not
listened to the radio since the accident because music makes him feel unexpectedly close to crying,
that he misses, more than anything, the feeling of moving through the world without bracing for it.

He is here, he says, because he is tired of managing. He would like, at some point, to simply live.
