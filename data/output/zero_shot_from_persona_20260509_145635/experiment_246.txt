# The Fabric of What Remains

Kofi is thirty-three years old, and he still flinches at the sound of a car backfiring. He has
learned to pass this off as nothing — a slight tightening of the jaw, a pause in whatever he is
doing — because in his neighborhood, a man who startles easily is a man who invites questions he
does not want to answer.

Three years ago, Kofi was working at his uncle's market stall in the city when a bomb detonated two
streets away. He remembers the sound before anything else — not an explosion the way he had imagined
explosions, but a pressure, a wall of air that hit him in the chest and knocked him off his feet.
When he stood, the world had gone the color of ash. He walked toward the smoke because his cousin
Ama had gone that way minutes before to buy fabric. He found her. He wishes, with a desperation that
has not dulled, that he had not.

What Kofi carries from that afternoon is not one memory but many, and they do not stay where he
tries to put them. He is measuring a customer's inseam and suddenly he is on that street again, his
hands covered in something he does not name out loud. He is threading a needle in the quiet of his
shop and the thread becomes the wrong color and his chest locks. He has learned to keep the radio on
— music, voices, anything — because silence is where the images live.

He runs his own tailoring shop now, a small room with good light and a hand-painted sign his mother
made for him. He is skilled. His customers trust him. But he works alone, and he has arranged it
that way deliberately. The shop is orderly in a way that feels less like preference and more like
necessity — every spool in its place, every seam allowance the same. Control over small things is
the only kind that feels available to him.

Kofi has not been back to a crowded market since the attack. He tells people he prefers to order his
materials online or through a supplier. This is true, and it is also a story he tells so that no one
asks the real reason. Buses trouble him. Restaurants trouble him. Any space where he cannot see the
exits and account for the people near the door costs him something he cannot afford to spend.

At night, he sleeps badly and briefly. He wakes between two and four in the morning most nights,
heart already running, the dream dissolving before he can hold it but leaving its residue behind. He
lies in the dark and runs through the steps of a jacket he is constructing — collar, lapel, lining,
buttons — because concentrating on craft is one of the few things that can slow his mind down to
something manageable.

He does not talk about Ama. When her name comes up, which it does less and less as people learn not
to raise it, he goes somewhere behind his eyes that is not quite present. He has told himself that
grief and what he carries are separate things, but they have become so tangled he can no longer find
where one ends and the other begins.

What Kofi says, when he says anything at all, is that he is tired. Not sleepy — tired in a way that
rest does not touch. He says he used to enjoy his work in a way that felt easy, and now he enjoys it
in a way that feels like effort, like holding something together with both hands. He is good at
that. He has been doing it for three years. He is not sure how much longer he can keep the seams
from showing.
