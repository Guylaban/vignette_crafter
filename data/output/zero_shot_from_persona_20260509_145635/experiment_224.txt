# The Weight of the Room

Elena is a sixty-six-year-old Colombian-born woman who has spent nearly four decades working as a
paramedic in a mid-sized American city. She is married, the mother of three grown children, and by
most outward measures, a woman who has built a sturdy and purposeful life. She speaks with quiet
authority, the kind that comes from having held people together at their worst moments. It is only
when the conversation turns to a particular stretch of seventy-two hours in 2019 that her hands go
still in her lap and her eyes move somewhere far away.

Elena was taken during a family visit to her hometown outside Medellín. She and her brother-in-law
were stopped at an unmarked checkpoint on a rural road, and within minutes, armed men had separated
them and placed Elena alone in the back of a vehicle. She was held for three days in a small
concrete room with a single barred window too high to see through. She was not physically harmed in
the ways she had feared most, but she was kept in near-total silence, given almost no information
about whether she would be released or killed, and denied contact with her family. The uncertainty,
she says, was its own kind of violence. She was eventually released after her family paid a ransom,
and she returned to the United States within the week. She told almost no one what had happened.

For the first several months, Elena threw herself back into work with a focus that felt, to her,
like recovery. She was good at her job — she had always been good at her job — and the structure of
a shift gave her somewhere to put herself. But things began to shift in ways she could not explain.
She started arriving at calls and finding herself momentarily frozen, not from incompetence, but
from a sudden and overwhelming sense that the walls of a room were closing in. Enclosed spaces —
elevators, back rooms of houses, even the supply closet at the station — began to feel unbearable.
She started taking the stairs exclusively and leaving doors open behind her whenever possible.

At home, her husband notices that she rarely sleeps through the night. Elena wakes in the early
hours with her heart hammering, sometimes certain she can hear a lock turning. She does not always
remember what she was dreaming, but the feeling lingers for hours — a dense, animal alertness that
makes ordinary morning life feel surreal. She has stopped accompanying her family on trips to visit
relatives in Colombia, offering practical reasons each time. She has not told her husband the full
truth of what she carries.

What troubles Elena most is the way the past intrudes on the present without warning. A patient's
fear-stricken face in the back of the ambulance can pull her, without transition, into that concrete
room — the smell of damp cement, the sound of footsteps she could never predict. She returns to
herself seconds later, functioning, professional, but shaken in a way she cannot show. She describes
these moments as being "pulled through a hole in time." She finds herself scanning exits when she
enters any building, a habit so automatic now that she barely notices it until someone points it
out.

Elena is not a woman who readily identifies as someone who needs help. She has spent her career
being the person who arrives when things have gone terribly wrong, and there is something deeply
disorienting about sitting on the other side of that equation. She came to therapy, she says,
because her youngest daughter noticed the changes in her and asked her, plainly, to try. Elena
agreed, she explains with a small and complicated smile, because she has learned over forty years of
emergency medicine that waiting too long always makes things worse.

She knows what damage looks like. She is only beginning to recognize it in herself.
