# The Man Behind the Chair

Kwame is fifty-eight years old, a man who has spent the better part of three decades building a life
around other people's comfort. His barbershop sits on a corner in a neighborhood he has watched
change around him, and for years he took quiet pride in being the kind of place where men came not
just for a cut but to sit, to talk, to feel known. He and his wife, Adwoa, raised two sons in the
flat above the shop. He was, by his own description, a man who felt safe in the world he had made.

That sense of safety ended on a Tuesday evening fourteen months ago.

Kwame had stayed late to close up alone, something he had done hundreds of times before. He was
locking the front door when two men came from behind him. One pressed something hard into the small
of his back. They took his phone, his wallet, the day's cash float from the bag he was carrying, and
his watch — a gift from Adwoa on their twenty-fifth anniversary. The whole thing lasted perhaps
three minutes. They left him face-down on the pavement. He lay there for a long time before he could
make himself stand up.

He tells the story quietly, without dramatic inflection, which somehow makes it harder to hear. What
he keeps returning to is not the fear in those three minutes but the absolute certainty he felt that
he was about to die. He had said goodbye to himself on that pavement. Everything that came after has
been colored by the fact that he crossed that threshold and could not entirely cross back.

In the weeks that followed, Kwame reopened the shop because he did not know what else to do. But
something fundamental had shifted in how he occupies his own body. He stands behind the chair now
with his back to the door and finds himself unable to concentrate on the conversation in front of
him, his attention constantly pulled toward the entrance. When the bell above the door rings, he
startles so visibly that his clients have begun to notice and say nothing. He has nicked more
customers in the past year than in the previous twenty combined. He has started leaving early,
locking up while there is still light, and he has hired a young man named Darius partly so he is
never in the shop alone.

At home, he sleeps in fragments. He wakes between two and four in the morning with his heart already
racing, the details of the robbery rearranged by his sleeping mind into something even worse — the
men's faces closer, the object at his back no longer ambiguous. He gets up and sits in the kitchen
rather than disturb Adwoa, though she has told him she hears him go. They talk around the subject
carefully, both of them trying to protect the other.

He has stopped walking to the evening market he used to enjoy. He drives now, even for short
distances, and he parks directly outside wherever he is going. He does not take the shortcut through
the alley behind the shop anymore. He has mapped his neighborhood into safe routes and unsafe ones,
and the unsafe ones are multiplying.

What troubles Kwame most, and what he articulates with a kind of bewildered sorrow, is that he no
longer feels like himself inside the shop. The barbershop was always an extension of who he is — a
place of ease, of story, of belonging. Now he stands in it like a man standing in someone else's
life. He listens to his clients talk and smiles at the right moments, and he is good at it still,
the muscle memory of thirty years carrying him through. But inside, he says, he is somewhere else
entirely. He is still lying on that pavement, still waiting to find out what happens next.
