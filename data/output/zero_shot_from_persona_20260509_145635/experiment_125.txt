# The Weight of the Bush

Kwame is a 46-year-old man who has worked as a park ranger in a national reserve for the past eleven
years. He is married, the father of three children, and by most outward measures, he is a steady and
capable man — the kind colleagues rely on when something goes wrong in the field. But there are
things that happen inside him now, quietly and without warning, that he has never quite been able to
explain to his wife, or to himself.

Before he became a ranger, Kwame served in a peacekeeping unit deployed to a conflict zone in
Central Africa in his late twenties. He does not speak about that time in specific terms, even in
the sessions where he has come closest to opening up. What he will say is that there was an ambush.
That it lasted several hours. That men he knew — men he had eaten with and laughed with — did not
come back from it. He was twenty-nine years old. He came home with no visible injuries, and for a
long time he believed that meant he had come home whole.

For years, the strategy seemed to work. He threw himself into training, then into the rangership,
where the long days in open country and the physical demands of the work gave his body somewhere to
put everything it was carrying. He married his wife, Abena, and built a life that felt, if not
entirely peaceful, at least manageable.

The trouble began in a way that felt almost mundane. About two years ago, poachers opened fire on a
colleague near the northern boundary of the reserve. Kwame was a kilometer away when he heard the
shots. His colleague survived, but something in Kwame did not survive the sound of that gunfire. He
found himself on the ground before he understood what he was doing, his heart hammering, his hands
shaking so badly he could not key the radio. The ambush from nearly two decades ago came back to him
not as a memory but as a present reality — the smell of smoke, the shouting, the particular quality
of silence that followed.

Since then, he has not been the same man he was before that afternoon. He sleeps poorly, waking in
the early hours convinced he hears movement outside the house, lying rigid until dawn. When he does
sleep, he is sometimes back in the bush of the conflict zone, and he wakes with his jaw clenched and
his chest tight. During the day, he finds himself scanning the tree lines when he patrols, his
attention catching on shadows and sounds that his rational mind knows are nothing. He has startled
badly twice in front of junior rangers and felt the shame of it for days afterward.

At home, Abena notices that he has become quieter. He sits through family dinners present in body
but somewhere else entirely. He has stopped attending the church gatherings he once looked forward
to. He tells her he is tired. This is true, but it is not the whole truth, and some part of him
knows she senses that.

What troubles him most, he says, is not the sleeplessness or even the intrusive moments in the
field. It is the feeling that something essential has been severed — that the man who once felt
genuinely at ease in the landscape he loves, who could stand at the edge of a waterhole at dusk and
feel something close to peace, is no longer reliably available to him. The bush was the place he
came to after the war to recover himself. Now the bush is where he hears gunshots in the wind.

He came to his first session at the suggestion of the reserve's occupational health coordinator. He
sat with his hands folded and said he was not sure he believed in talking about things. Then he
talked for forty-five minutes without stopping.
