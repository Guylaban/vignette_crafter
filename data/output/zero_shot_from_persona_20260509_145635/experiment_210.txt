**Vignette: Fatima, Age 34**

Fatima grew up in a small farming village where the rhythm of life was tied to the land — planting
seasons, harvest festivals, the smell of soil after rain. She had known that land her whole life,
and when she eventually settled into agricultural work as an adult, it felt less like a job and more
like a continuation of something inherited. She was thirty-one when everything changed.

It was an ordinary afternoon during the harvest season when a coworker — a young man named Tariq,
barely twenty years old — became entangled in the machinery of a large mechanical harvester. Fatima
was working only a few meters away. She heard the sound before she understood what it was. By the
time she turned and registered what was happening, it was already too late to intervene. She watched
him die. She stood there, frozen, for what she later describes as both a second and an eternity.
Other workers ran toward him. Fatima could not move.

In the days that followed, she told herself she was fine. She went back to work within the week
because she needed the income and because she did not know what else to do with herself. But the
fields had become a different place. The same harvesting equipment that had been unremarkable
background noise for years now seemed to fill all available space in her mind the moment she came
within sight of it. Her chest would tighten. Her hands would shake. Some mornings she would stand at
the edge of the field for ten or fifteen minutes before she could make herself walk in.

Fatima's marriage, already strained, did not survive the following year. Her ex-husband grew
frustrated with what he called her "distance" — the way she would go quiet at dinner, the way she
flinched at unexpected sounds, the way she had stopped sleeping in any meaningful sense. She does
not blame him entirely, though she carries the divorce as another layer of loss. She now lives alone
in a small apartment, and the solitude, while sometimes a relief, more often amplifies everything
she is trying not to feel.

Sleep is perhaps where Fatima suffers most visibly. She rarely sleeps more than three or four hours
at a stretch. When she does dream, she is often back in that field, and in the dreams she sometimes
reaches Tariq in time, and sometimes she does not, and both versions leave her waking in the dark
with her heart pounding and her nightgown damp. She has developed a habit of leaving the television
on through the night — not to watch it, but to have a voice in the room.

During the workday, she functions, but only barely. She has quietly arranged to work in sections of
the farm that use manual tools rather than heavy machinery, telling her supervisor it is a
preference. She has never told him the real reason. She is intensely private about what happened,
partly from shame and partly because she does not believe that putting it into words would change
anything. She carries a particular guilt — a recurring, intrusive conviction that she should have
shouted a warning sooner, that a fraction of a second might have mattered, even though she knows, in
the rational part of herself, that it would not have.

She describes herself as someone who used to enjoy people — who would linger after work to talk, who
hosted her cousins for meals, who laughed easily. She recognizes that person as herself and also as
someone she no longer quite is. She is hypervigilant in ways that exhaust her: scanning the
perimeter of rooms when she enters them, sitting with her back to walls, startling violently at the
sound of engines or loud mechanical noise.

Fatima comes to sessions with her arms crossed and her answers careful, as though she is rationing
what she lets out. She is not resistant — she is exhausted. There is a difference, and it matters.
Beneath the guardedness is a woman who misses the version of herself that felt safe in the world,
and who is not yet sure that version is retrievable.
