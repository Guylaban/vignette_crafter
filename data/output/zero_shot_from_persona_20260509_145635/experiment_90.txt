# The Weight of What Was Done to Him

Miguel is twenty-six years old, and he has learned to make himself invisible. It is a skill that
serves him well in his work — moving quietly through hotel corridors with his cart, straightening
rooms while guests sleep or eat or forget he exists. He is good at this. He is good at being unseen.
But invisibility, he has discovered, does not protect a person from himself.

Miguel grew up in a mid-sized city in Guatemala. At nineteen, he became involved at the edges of a
political organizing movement in his neighborhood — distributing pamphlets, attending meetings,
believing, in the way that young men do, that the world could be changed by people willing to stand
in it. One evening, returning home from a meeting, he was taken off the street by men who did not
identify themselves. What followed lasted three days. He does not speak of the specific details
easily, but he has described being kept in a dark room, beaten repeatedly, burned on his forearms
and chest with something he never saw clearly, and told, with calm and methodical certainty, that no
one was looking for him and no one would come. They were right. No one came. He was released — left
on a roadside outside the city — without explanation, the same way he had been taken.

He left Guatemala within weeks. The journey north was its own ordeal, but it is the three days in
that room that live in his body now, seven years later, in ways he is only beginning to name.

Miguel arrived in the United States at twenty, found work in hotel housekeeping, and threw himself
into the labor of surviving. He married a woman he met through a cousin, and for a time the
structure of that life — the routine, her presence, the noise of a shared apartment — kept something
at bay. But he was not well, and he did not know how to say so. He startled violently at sounds,
could not sleep without the television on, could not tolerate being in enclosed spaces without the
door cracked open. He had nightmares so severe that he sometimes woke on the floor, having thrown
himself out of bed. His wife tried to reach him. He did not know how to let her. They divorced two
years ago.

Now he lives alone in a single room he rents by the week. He works the morning shift, which means
rising at four-thirty in the dark, and he is grateful for this — grateful, he says, that there is no
window of time in the early morning where he has nothing to do but lie there and remember. The rooms
he cleans sometimes stop him cold: a particular smell of bleach, a drain in a bathroom floor, the
sound of a door locking from the outside. In those moments he is not in a hotel in this American
city. He is somewhere else entirely, and his heart is pounding, and his hands have gone cold, and he
has to grip the edge of a sink and remind himself of his own name.

He describes feeling like a fraud in his own life — as though the person who walks and works and
nods good morning to his supervisor is a performance, and the real Miguel is still in that room,
still waiting to find out what happens next. He feels contaminated by what was done to him, ashamed
in ways he cannot fully articulate, certain that if people understood what had happened, they would
see him differently. He has not told his family in Guatemala the truth of why he left.

Some evenings he sits on the edge of his bed and cannot account for the hours that have passed. He
is not sure, he says quietly, that he believes the worst is over. Some part of him is still
listening for footsteps in the hall.
