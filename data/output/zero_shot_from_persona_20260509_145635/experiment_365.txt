# The Weight of a Misstep

Marcus is a fifty-six-year-old professional dancer and choreographer who has spent the better part
of four decades making his living through movement. His body has always been something he trusted
absolutely — an instrument of precision, of beauty, of control. That certainty ended three years ago
during a rehearsal for a contemporary ensemble piece he was directing in Amsterdam.

It was a lift sequence he had choreographed himself, one he had run through dozens of times with the
same two dancers. On that particular afternoon, something shifted in the timing — a fraction of a
second, a miscommunication in the music cue, his hands not quite where they needed to be. His young
partner, a twenty-three-year-old woman named Lena, came down wrong. The sound of her spine meeting
the stage floor is something Marcus describes as living permanently inside his ears. She sustained a
serious spinal injury that left her with partial paralysis in her left leg and ended her performing
career entirely.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus functioned on the surface. He visited Lena in the hospital, sat
with her family, answered questions from the theater's insurance investigators. He returned to work
because he did not know what else to do with himself. But something had fundamentally broken in the
architecture of how he moved through the world. He stopped sleeping more than two or three hours at
a stretch. When he did sleep, he was back on that stage — sometimes the dream was faithful to what
happened, sometimes Lena fell from greater and greater heights, sometimes she did not survive. He
would wake with his heart slamming against his ribs, his wife Anneke reaching for him in the dark
while he sat rigid at the edge of the bed, unable to explain what was happening inside him.

He has not choreographed a lift since. When he watches other dancers rehearse partnering sequences,
his vision narrows and his hands begin to shake. He has quietly restructured his choreographic
vocabulary around this avoidance, and none of his colleagues have questioned it — they assume it is
an aesthetic choice. Only Anneke knows the real reason, and even with her, Marcus speaks about it in
the most minimal terms. He describes a persistent sense of contamination, a feeling that his hands
are dangerous, that the body he spent a lifetime disciplining and trusting has become something that
cannot be relied upon.

His concentration, once extraordinary, now fractures easily. He loses his place in music he has
known for thirty years. He finds himself replaying the moment of the accident not only at night but
in the middle of teaching, sometimes stopping mid-sentence while students wait, puzzled, for him to
continue. He is irritable in ways that embarrass him — short with Anneke over small things, cold
with students who make careless errors. He recognizes this is not who he is, or was, and that
recognition adds a layer of shame to everything else he carries.

He has largely withdrawn from the social world of the dance community, declining invitations to
performances and opening nights he once attended enthusiastically. Watching other companies work
fills him with a dread he cannot fully articulate — something between grief and the persistent,
irrational certainty that he will witness another catastrophe.

Marcus speaks about Lena with a grief that is not diminished by time. He knows, intellectually, that
accidents happen in physical performance, that he was not reckless, that the theater's investigation
found no negligence. He has been told these things by colleagues, by lawyers, by his own wife. None
of it touches the place in him where the guilt lives. He describes feeling as though he stole
something from Lena that can never be returned, and that continuing to dance — to use the same body
that hurt her — is a kind of ongoing offense he has not yet figured out how to justify or forgive.

He is still performing. He is still teaching. From the outside, Marcus looks like a man who has
simply grown quieter with age.
