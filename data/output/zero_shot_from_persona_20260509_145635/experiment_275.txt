# Vignette: Malee

Malee is a fifty-seven-year-old Thai woman who has been married for thirty-one years and runs her
own interior design studio in the city. She is soft-spoken and precise in the way she describes
things — colors, textures, the quality of light in a room — and it is clear that beauty has always
been her language. That changed three years ago, on an afternoon when she was meeting a client at a
hotel lobby café in the business district. A bomb detonated in the adjacent corridor. Malee was
thrown from her chair by the blast. When she opened her eyes, the room was filled with smoke and
screaming, and the man sitting two tables away from her was no longer moving. She crawled toward the
exit over broken glass, certain she was about to die. She did not die. Fourteen people did.

In the weeks that followed, Malee told herself she was fine. She had survived. She went back to work
within ten days because she did not know what else to do with herself, and because stillness felt
dangerous. But her body had not agreed to move on. She began waking at three in the morning with her
heart slamming against her ribs, drenched in sweat, the smell of smoke so vivid she would get up and
check every room in the apartment. Her husband, Somchai, would find her sitting at the kitchen table
in the dark, gripping a cup of tea she had forgotten to drink.

The flashbacks came without warning. She could be standing in a client's living room, holding a
fabric swatch up to the light, and then the sound of a car backfiring on the street below would pull
her completely out of the present. Suddenly she is on the floor of the café again, ears ringing,
tasting blood, watching a woman she does not know press her hands against a wound on her own leg.
These episodes leave Malee disoriented and ashamed, and she has become skilled at excusing herself
to the bathroom until she can reassemble herself.

Her work, which once gave her deep satisfaction, now feels like a performance she is watching from a
distance. She used to walk into an empty room and immediately feel its potential — the way the
afternoon sun could be coaxed through a particular window, the conversation a specific arrangement
of furniture might encourage. Now she moves through spaces feeling nothing, generating ideas she no
longer believes in. She has turned down three major commissions in the past year, telling herself
she is too busy, knowing the truth is that she cannot bear to be in crowded public buildings.

She avoids the business district entirely. She plans her routes through the city the way a general
plans troop movements — accounting for every possible exposure. She no longer takes clients to hotel
lobbies or restaurant spaces with hard surfaces that might carry the echo of a blast. When Somchai
suggested they celebrate their anniversary at a rooftop bar downtown, she agreed and then spent the
preceding week in a state of mounting dread, finally inventing a headache the night of the
reservation. He did not press her. She suspects he is frightened of her now in some quiet way he
would never admit.

What troubles Malee most is not the fear, which she has come to expect, but the numbness that has
settled over the parts of her life she used to love. She cannot feel moved by a beautiful room. She
cannot feel fully present with her husband. She lights incense at the small spirit house on her
balcony every morning, as she has done since childhood, but the ritual that once grounded her now
feels like a gesture made by someone she used to be. She is not sure where that woman went, or
whether the search for her is something she is still permitted to undertake.
