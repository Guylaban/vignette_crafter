# Case Vignette: Harold

Harold is a 78-year-old divorced man who has spent the better part of four decades working with data
— organizing it, protecting it, making sense of it. Even now, well past the age when most of his
peers have retired, he continues to work part-time as a database administrator, logging in from his
home office each morning with a discipline that has defined most of his adult life. He lives alone
in the house he kept after his divorce, and until two years ago, he would have described himself as
a man who had things under control.

That sense of control ended on a Tuesday afternoon when Harold collapsed in his kitchen. He had been
ignoring a tightening in his chest for several days, attributing it to stress and poor sleep. By the
time the paramedics arrived, he was in full cardiac arrest. He was clinically dead for nearly four
minutes. The resuscitation was prolonged and physically violent — ribs cracked, paddles applied
twice. He regained consciousness in the ICU three days later, confused and terrified, with no clear
memory of how he had arrived there, only a fragmented and suffocating sense that something had torn
through him.

The physical recovery took months. The cardiologists were pleased with his progress. Harold was not.
He returned home to find that the house felt different — smaller, somehow, and threateningly quiet.
He began sleeping with the television on. He started checking his pulse dozens of times a day,
pressing two fingers to his wrist at his desk, in the kitchen, in the middle of the night when he
startled awake from dreams he couldn't fully remember but that left him drenched in sweat and
certain, for several disorienting seconds, that he was dying again.

At work, Harold finds himself unable to concentrate the way he once could. He stares at lines of
code and queries that would have taken him minutes to resolve, and his mind slides away to the
kitchen floor, to the ceiling of the ambulance, to a darkness he cannot describe but cannot stop
returning to. He has begun saving redundant backups of everything he works on — triple, sometimes
quadruple copies — driven by a compulsion he recognizes as irrational but cannot resist. Losing
data, he says, feels like losing proof that he existed.

He avoids his doctor's office unless absolutely necessary. He cancelled his last two follow-up
cardiology appointments and rescheduled them only after his daughter called, worried. The waiting
room, the smell of antiseptic, the sound of a blood pressure cuff inflating — any of these can send
his heart racing in a way that he then catastrophizes into evidence of another impending arrest. He
has stopped going to the gym. He has stopped walking the neighborhood route he used to take each
evening. Movement feels dangerous. Rest feels dangerous. His world has grown very small.

Harold speaks about the experience with a kind of careful flatness, as though he has rehearsed the
words enough times to drain them of feeling. But then something will shift — a pause, a tightening
around his eyes — and the flatness cracks. He describes lying in the ICU and realizing that no one
had been called for three days. His ex-wife, his adult children in other cities, his colleagues —
none of them had known. He had nearly vanished without anyone noticing in time. That thought, more
than the cardiac arrest itself, seems to be the one that won't leave him alone.

He is still working. He shows up to his sessions on time. He answers questions precisely and
thoroughly, the way a man trained to think in systems does. But there is something exhausted behind
his eyes, a vigilance that never fully rests. Harold is a man who has spent his career ensuring that
nothing important gets lost, and he is now living inside the unbearable knowledge that he almost
was.
