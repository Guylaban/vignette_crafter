# The Body Remembers

Kofi is sixty-eight years old, a man who has spent most of his life in motion. For decades, he
taught traditional West African dance at a community arts center, his body a precise and expressive
instrument. Even now, in what others might call retirement, he still leads two classes a week,
moving between students with a grace that belies his age. Dance, he has always said, is how he
thinks. It is how he grieves. It was how he survived the death of his wife, Abena, six years ago —
he danced through that loss until something in him found its footing again.

Then, fourteen months ago, everything changed.

He was driving home from the arts center on a Thursday evening in November, the sky already dark by
five o'clock, the road slick with early rain. A delivery truck ran a red light at speed and struck
his car on the driver's side. The impact was catastrophic. His door crumpled inward. He was
conscious throughout — trapped, unable to move, hearing the hiss of something beneath the hood and
smelling what he was certain was fuel. For nearly forty minutes, he lay pinned in the wreckage,
convinced he was going to burn to death. He remembers the sound of his own breathing, shallow and
strange, and thinking of Abena, wondering if he was about to see her again. He was not seriously
injured — bruised ribs, lacerations, a mild concussion — but the forty minutes he spent waiting in
that car have not left him.

In the weeks after the accident, Kofi told himself he was fine. He had survived worse, he reasoned.
He had buried a wife. He returned to his classes within a month, but something had shifted. Standing
in the studio, he would suddenly lose the thread of what he was doing — not forgetting the steps,
but finding himself somewhere else entirely, back in that car, the smell of rain and gasoline
flooding him without warning. He would stop mid-demonstration and have to grip the barre, waiting
for the sensation to pass, hoping his students hadn't noticed the change in his face.

He no longer drives after dark. He has found elaborate reasons to justify this — he is tired in the
evenings, the roads near his home are poorly lit — but the truth is that the thought of nighttime
driving produces a panic so physical and immediate that he once had to pull over and sit on the curb
for twenty minutes before he could compose himself. He avoids the intersection where the accident
occurred entirely, adding fifteen minutes to every journey. When he rides as a passenger, he braces
himself against the door and watches the road with a vigilance that exhausts him.

Sleep has become unreliable territory. He wakes most nights between two and three in the morning,
heart pounding, the details of the accident vivid and immediate — the truck's headlights, the sound
of metal, the smell. He lies in the dark of the house he shared with Abena and feels a loneliness
that is different from grief. It is the loneliness of a man who cannot explain to anyone what it is
like to be afraid of a thing that is already over.

What troubles him most, he says quietly, is what has happened to his dancing. He still moves
beautifully — his students see to that — but he no longer feels it the way he once did. The joy that
used to rise in him when the drums began has been replaced by something flat and watchful. He goes
through the motions with technical mastery, but the interior experience is muted, as though someone
has drawn a curtain between him and himself. For a man whose entire identity is rooted in the felt
life of the body, this numbness is its own kind of devastation. He describes it as losing his mother
tongue.
