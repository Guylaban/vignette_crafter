**Vignette: Hemi, Age 51**

Hemi has spent the better part of his adult life working with fire and steel. For nearly three
decades, he has welded structural frames on commercial construction sites across the North Island, a
trade he took up in his early twenties and came to love for its precision and the satisfaction of
watching something permanent take shape under his hands. He is broad-shouldered and deliberate in
the way he moves, a man who has always prided himself on being steady.

Fourteen months ago, Hemi was driving home from a late shift on a wet Thursday evening when a truck
ran a red light and struck his ute on the driver's side at high speed. The impact sent his vehicle
spinning across two lanes before it came to rest against a concrete barrier. He was conscious
throughout — conscious enough to smell petrol, to feel the door pressing into his left side, to hear
the hiss of something beneath the hood and think, with absolute certainty, that he was going to die
there on that road. He was cut free by emergency services and airlifted to hospital with fractured
ribs, a punctured lung, and a laceration to his left arm that required surgery. Physically, he
recovered. He returned to work after twelve weeks, cleared by his doctor, his arm healed, his lung
functioning.

But something else did not heal.

In the weeks after the accident, Hemi began waking at two or three in the morning with his heart
hammering and his sheets soaked through. He would lie rigid in the dark, certain he could still hear
the sound of metal folding around him. His wife, Aroha, would reach for him and he would flinch
before he could stop himself. He has not told her how bad the nights are. He does not want her to
worry, and he does not entirely have the words for what happens to him in those hours — the way the
darkness of the bedroom becomes the darkness of the crushed cab, the way ordinary sounds, a car
backfiring, a truck reversing on the street outside, can drop him back into that intersection so
completely that he loses track of where he is.

At work, Hemi functions, but only just. He has become the welder who checks his surroundings
constantly, who positions himself so that he always has a clear line of sight to the site exit. When
a forklift moves too quickly nearby, his body floods with adrenaline before his mind catches up. He
has nearly walked off a job twice — once when a reversing truck clipped a barrier and the sound sent
him to his knees behind a steel beam, pretending to check a connection until he could breathe again.
His apprentice has noticed that he is quieter than he used to be. Hemi knows this. He does not offer
an explanation.

He has stopped driving at night entirely. He tells Aroha it is because he is tired after long
shifts, and she accepts this because she trusts him. On the rare occasions he must drive during the
day, he grips the wheel with both hands and scans every intersection twice, his jaw tight, his body
braced for an impact that does not come. He has taken longer routes to avoid the particular stretch
of road where the accident happened, adding twenty minutes to a journey he once made without
thinking.

At home, Hemi sits with his family at dinner and is present in body but somewhere else in mind. He
has less patience than he used to — sharp with his teenage son over small things, then ashamed of
himself afterward. He drinks more than he did before, two or three beers most evenings, telling
himself it helps him sleep, knowing on some level that it only delays the waking.

He has not spoken to anyone about what happened beyond the basic facts. He is fifty-one years old.
He built his life on being the kind of man who holds things together. He is not sure what it means
that he can no longer do that quietly.
