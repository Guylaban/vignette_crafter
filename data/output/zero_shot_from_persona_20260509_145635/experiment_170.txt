# Vignette: Elena, Age 78

Elena has spent the better part of fifty years arranging other people's stories on shelves. As a
librarian at a small branch library in a mid-sized American city, she has always found comfort in
the orderliness of catalogued things — the way every book has a place, a number, a home. It is a
comfort that stands in sharp contrast to the story she carries inside her own body, one that has no
neat classification and refuses to stay shelved.

Elena was twenty-six years old when she and her husband, Marcos, fled their home country in Central
America during a period of intense political violence. The details shift slightly depending on the
day she tells them, but the core remains the same: soldiers came to their neighborhood in the night.
She remembers the sound of boots on packed dirt, the smell of smoke from a neighbor's burning house,
and the moment she grabbed her infant daughter and ran without shoes into the darkness. They walked
for three days through mountainous terrain, hiding during daylight hours, before crossing into a
neighboring country. Her younger brother, who had been separated from the group in the chaos, was
never found. For years, she did not speak of him by name.

Elena and Marcos eventually resettled in the United States, built a life, raised three children, and
by most visible measures thrived. She earned a library science degree at night while working days.
She became known at the library for her warmth with elderly patrons and her patience with children
learning to read. Colleagues describe her as steady and composed. What they do not see is what
happens when the library's emergency alarm is tested on the first Friday of each month. Elena's body
responds before her mind can intervene — her heart hammers, her vision narrows, and she finds
herself gripping the edge of the reference desk until her knuckles pale. Afterward, she feels
embarrassed, then exhausted in a way that sleep does not fix.

At home, she sleeps poorly. She wakes between two and four in the morning and lies still, listening
for sounds she cannot name. Marcos, now seventy-nine and in good health, has learned not to touch
her shoulder when she wakes this way; early in their marriage, she struck him once without meaning
to, and neither of them has forgotten it. She does not watch the news. When her grandchildren play
games involving soldiers or chase, she leaves the room quietly and stands in the kitchen until her
breathing slows.

What has brought Elena to therapy now, after so many decades, is a combination of things. Her
youngest granddaughter recently asked about the family's history for a school project, and Elena
found herself unable to speak — not unwilling, but physically unable, her throat closing around the
words. That same week, a documentary about Central American migration played in the library's
community room, and Elena sat outside on a bench in the cold rather than walk past the open door.
She told Marcos that night that she was tired of being afraid of her own past. He held her hand and
said nothing, which was the right thing.

She describes her life as a good one, and she means it. She loves her work, her grandchildren, her
husband's particular laugh. But she also describes a persistent sense of being slightly outside
herself, as though she is watching her own life through a window — present but not entirely arrived.
She thinks often about her brother, imagines him at various ages, wonders whether he had children.
She keeps a photograph of their mother tucked inside a book of Chilean poetry on her desk at the
library, in a volume she knows no patron ever requests. It is the closest thing she has to a grave
to visit.

She says she does not understand why, after fifty years, her body still believes it is running
through the mountains in the dark. She says this not with anger, but with a kind of bewildered
sorrow, as though she is waiting for an explanation that has simply been misfiled somewhere.
