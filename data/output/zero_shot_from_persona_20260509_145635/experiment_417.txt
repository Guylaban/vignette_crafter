# The Weight of the Gavel

Eleanor is a 78-year-old woman who has spent the better part of five decades standing between the
state and those who cannot afford to stand alone. She is still practicing — three days a week now,
down from five — and she carries herself with the kind of measured composure that comes from arguing
before hostile judges and grieving families for most of her adult life. Her husband of forty-four
years, a retired schoolteacher, drives her to the office on mornings when she asks him to, which is
more often now than it used to be.

Fourteen months ago, Eleanor was walking to her car in the parking structure adjacent to the
courthouse when a man she did not recognize grabbed her from behind, threw her against a concrete
pillar, and demanded her bag. She gave it to him. He hit her anyway — twice, with a closed fist,
once across the jaw and once on the side of her head — before running. She lay on the ground for
several minutes before a court officer found her. She had a hairline fracture of the cheekbone and a
concussion. The man was never identified.

In the weeks following, Eleanor told herself she was fine. She had seen violence in photographs, in
police reports, in the faces of clients who had survived far worse. She understood trauma
intellectually in the way that a cardiologist understands grief — thoroughly, and from a distance.
She returned to work after ten days. She took the stairs instead of the parking structure elevator,
then stopped using the structure altogether, then began asking her husband to drop her off at the
front entrance, then began arriving later in the morning, when the streets were fuller.

She does not sleep well. She wakes at two or three in the morning with her heart already racing, the
sensation of hands on her shoulders so vivid that she has to sit up and turn on the lamp to be
certain of where she is. Her husband has learned not to touch her shoulder to wake her; once, when
he did, she cried out in a way that frightened them both. She does not speak much about that.

At work, Eleanor finds herself doing something she has never done in fifty years of practice: she
flinches. When a client raises his voice, when a door slams in the hallway, when someone steps into
her peripheral vision too quickly — her body responds before her mind can intervene. She is aware of
this, and the awareness humiliates her. She has spent her career insisting that her clients'
reactions to violence were reasonable, human, and deserved to be understood. She does not extend
herself the same courtesy.

She is irritable in ways she cannot always account for. She snapped at a young public defender she
mentors, a woman she is genuinely fond of, over something trivial, and then sat in her office
afterward feeling a cold, unfamiliar shame. She has stopped attending the monthly dinners she and
her husband used to host. The thought of people in her home, the noise, the unpredictability of an
evening, exhausts her before it begins.

Eleanor does not think of herself as someone who was traumatized. She thinks of herself as someone
who had something bad happen to her and has not yet fully recovered. She makes this distinction
carefully and often. When she does allow herself to sit with what occurred in that parking
structure, what rises is not fear exactly but a kind of furious bewilderment — that her body, which
has carried her through courtrooms and crises and decades of other people's worst days, could be
reduced so quickly, so completely, to something helpless on a concrete floor. That is the part she
returns to without meaning to. That is the part she cannot yet put down.
