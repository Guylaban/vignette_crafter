**Vignette: Aroha, 33**

Aroha grew up in a small coastal community in New Zealand, the kind of place where everyone knew
everyone and children ran between houses without knocking. She carried that sense of safety with her
into adulthood — into her marriage, into her work as a network engineer, and most of all into
motherhood. When her son Tama was born, she felt certain she knew how to build things that would
hold.

Tama was two and a half when he stopped eating. At first Aroha told herself it was a phase, the kind
of thing her mother would have waved away with a laugh. But within three weeks he was grey-faced and
lethargic, and a routine blood test sent them straight to the children's hospital in Auckland. The
word the doctors used was leukemia. Aroha remembers standing in the corridor outside the ward, her
phone in her hand, unable to remember her husband's number — a number she had dialed a thousand
times.

What followed was fourteen months of chemotherapy, infections, hospital admissions, and nights when
the monitors beside Tama's bed would alarm and nurses would rush in and Aroha would press herself
against the wall and go completely still, as though stillness might keep him alive. There was one
night in particular, when Tama developed a severe infection and his temperature spiked to a point
where the registrar spoke to her very quietly and very carefully, that she understood he might die
before morning. He did not die. He is now six years old and in remission. But something in Aroha did
not recover alongside him.

Her marriage ended about a year after Tama's treatment finished. Her ex-husband has said, not
unkindly, that she came home from the hospital as someone different. She does not disagree. She
lives alone with Tama now in a flat in Tāmaki Makaurau, and she works long hours managing network
infrastructure for a telecommunications company — a job she is good at precisely because she
understands how systems fail and how to prevent it. She monitors. She checks. She builds redundancy
into everything.

At home, the monitoring does not switch off. She checks Tama's colour when he wakes up. She feels
the glands in his neck when she hugs him goodnight. She has a folder on her phone with photographs
she takes of him weekly, though she cannot fully explain why. When he gets a cold, she does not
sleep. She sits outside his bedroom door and listens to him breathe, and her body is back in that
hospital corridor, certain that the worst is still coming.

She does not talk about this with many people. At work she is known for being calm under pressure,
the person others call when a system goes down at 2 a.m. She is precise and methodical and she does
not panic. Inside, she feels like a network running on a single point of failure — like one alert
away from collapse. She has intrusive images of Tama in the hospital bed that arrive without
warning: during team meetings, while she is driving, once while she was presenting to a client and
had to excuse herself to stand in the bathroom for several minutes. She avoids the street the
hospital is on. She has not been able to watch any television programme that involves a sick child,
even briefly.

She sleeps poorly and wakes most nights between three and four, heart already racing, the dream
dissolving but the dread remaining. She describes feeling detached from other parents at Tama's
school — people whose biggest worry is lunchbox contents — and she is aware this is not entirely
fair, but she cannot bridge the distance. She loves her son with an intensity that frightens her.
She knows, rationally, that he is well. Her body has not received that message.
