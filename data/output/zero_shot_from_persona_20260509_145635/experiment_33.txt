# The Weight of Water

Maria is a 39-year-old Salvadoran-born woman who has served in the United States Army for fourteen
years. She carries herself with the practiced stillness of someone who has learned to take up
exactly the right amount of space — no more, no less. She speaks carefully, choosing her words the
way a person might choose footing on uncertain ground.

Three years ago, Maria was deployed as part of a domestic disaster relief operation following
catastrophic flooding in the Gulf Coast region. What she had expected was logistics — moving
supplies, coordinating evacuations, the structured work she was trained for. What she found was
something the Army had never quite prepared her for. On the third day of the operation, her unit
responded to a neighborhood where floodwaters had risen faster than anyone anticipated. They reached
a house where a family had taken refuge on a second-floor rooftop. Maria was the first to reach
them. She pulled two children and an elderly man into the rescue boat before the current shifted
violently. The mother, who had been waiting at the roof's edge, was swept away before Maria could
reach her. Maria watched her disappear into the brown, churning water. They never recovered the
body.

In the weeks that followed, Maria returned to her regular duties at her base in Texas. She told her
commanding officer she was fine. She told her husband, Rodrigo, the same. She believed it, mostly.
But at night, she began waking at 2 or 3 a.m., her heart slamming against her ribs, her nightgown
soaked through. In her dreams, the water is always the same color — the particular brown-gray of
that afternoon — and the woman's face is always turned toward her, mouth open, before she goes
under.

Maria has stopped drinking coffee in the mornings because the sound of water running through the
coffeemaker is enough to make her chest tighten and her hands go cold. Last spring, Rodrigo
suggested they take their daughters to the community pool. Maria sat in the parking lot for eleven
minutes before driving home alone. She told him she had a headache. She has not told him what she
actually saw that day on the water, not the full version. She carries a private shame about this — a
soldier who cannot sit beside a swimming pool.

At work, Maria is still competent, still respected. She leads her unit with the same precision she
always has. But she has noticed herself scanning exits in the mess hall, sitting with her back to
walls, startling badly when a door slams. A junior soldier dropped a metal tray last month and Maria
was halfway across the room before she understood what had happened. She laughed it off. She is good
at laughing things off.

What troubles her most, she says, is not the nightmares or the hypervigilance — she can name those
things, she has some language for them. What troubles her is the flatness that has settled over her
life. She describes watching her daughters open birthday presents and feeling as though she is
observing it through thick glass. She loves them, she is certain of that, but the feeling arrives
muted and delayed, like a signal from far away. Rodrigo has asked her more than once what is wrong.
She says she is tired. She is always tired.

Maria grew up in a family where endurance was the primary virtue — her mother crossed two borders
pregnant, her father worked construction through injuries that would have sidelined other men.
Asking for help has always felt, to Maria, like a kind of betrayal of who she is supposed to be. She
sits in the chair across from the desk and holds her hands very still in her lap, and when she
finally begins to talk about the woman in the water, her voice does not waver. But her knuckles,
pressed together, go white.
