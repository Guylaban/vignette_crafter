# The Weight of the Parking Garage

Maria is thirty-nine years old, the kind of woman who has spent most of her adult life showing up
for other people. She coordinates outreach programs for a nonprofit that serves immigrant families
in her city — work she chose deliberately, work that feels like a calling. Her colleagues describe
her as warm and unshakable. She has spent years sitting across from people who have survived things
most people cannot imagine, holding space for their fear without flinching. She thought she
understood trauma. She did not understand it the way she understands it now.

Fourteen months ago, on a Tuesday evening in late October, Maria was walking to her car in the
parking garage beneath her office building. It was just past seven. She had stayed late to finish
grant paperwork. The garage was mostly empty. A man she had never seen before came up behind her
between the third and fourth levels, grabbed her by the hair, and slammed her against a concrete
pillar. He demanded her bag, her keys, her phone. When she hesitated — not out of resistance, she
says, but out of pure, animal shock — he hit her twice across the face and shoved her to the ground.
He took everything and ran. The entire incident lasted less than two minutes.

She drove herself home that night with a split lip and a fractured cheekbone, shaking so hard she
could barely grip the wheel. She told her husband, Rodrigo, what had happened, and he held her while
she cried. She filed a police report. She told herself she was fine. She went back to work four days
later.

The not-being-fine arrived quietly at first, then all at once. She stopped using the parking garage
and began parking six blocks away, even in the rain. She started checking behind her constantly — in
grocery stores, in hallways, at her own front door. She began waking between two and four in the
morning, heart hammering, certain that someone was in the apartment. Rodrigo would find her sitting
at the kitchen table in the dark, unable to explain what she was waiting for.

The nightmares are specific and relentless. She is always in the garage. The lighting is always
wrong — too yellow, too dim. She always hears the footsteps before she sees him, and in the dream
she runs but the levels keep multiplying, the car keeps disappearing, and she wakes before anything
resolves. She has not slept more than four consecutive hours in months.

What troubles her most, she says, is what has happened to her work. She sits across from her clients
— women who fled violence, families who crossed borders in fear — and she finds herself somewhere
else entirely. She is in the garage. She is on the ground. She feels a shame she cannot fully
articulate, a sense that she has lost the right to hold other people's pain when she cannot manage
her own. She has started canceling meetings, delegating cases she would once have taken without
hesitation. A colleague asked recently if she was all right, and Maria smiled and said she was just
tired.

At home, she and Rodrigo move around each other carefully. She startles when he enters a room
without speaking first. She knows this frightens him, and that knowledge makes her feel worse. She
has become irritable in ways that feel foreign to her — snapping at small things, then retreating
into long silences. She describes herself as someone standing just outside her own life, watching it
happen through glass.

She came to therapy, she says, because she realized she had started crossing the street to avoid men
walking behind her, and she thought: *this is not who I am.* She says it quietly, without drama. She
wants to find her way back to herself — to her clients, to Rodrigo, to the woman who believed she
was capable of being present for the hardest things.

She still believes that woman exists somewhere. She just cannot find her in the parking garage.
