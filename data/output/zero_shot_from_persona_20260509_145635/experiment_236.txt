# Clay and Silence

Martin is a 62-year-old potter who has worked with his hands his entire adult life. His studio sits
behind his house in rural Vermont, a converted barn that smells of wet earth and wood smoke. For
thirty years, the rhythm of the wheel — the centering, the pulling, the slow transformation of
formless clay into something whole — has been the organizing principle of his days. His wife, Carol,
used to bring him coffee there every morning at nine. She did this without fail, until the morning
she didn't.

Fourteen months ago, Carol collapsed in their kitchen while Martin was twenty feet away in the
studio. By the time he heard the sound — a single, dull impact he initially mistook for something
falling off a shelf — she had already lost consciousness. He found her on the tile floor, her coffee
mug intact beside her, still steaming. She was gone before the ambulance reached the end of their
driveway. A massive cerebral hemorrhage. She was 59 years old and had seen a doctor three weeks
prior for what everyone believed was a tension headache.

Martin describes those first moments with a precision that suggests he has replayed them thousands
of times. He remembers the exact position of her hand. He remembers that the radio was on. He
remembers thinking, in some dissociated corner of his mind, that the coffee was going to get cold.
He has not been able to drink coffee since.

In the months that followed, Martin stopped going into the studio. He tells himself he simply has no
ideas, but when pressed, he admits that he cannot stand the view of the kitchen window from the
workbench — the angle from which he would have seen Carol collapse, had he only looked up. He did
not look up. This thought visits him constantly, arriving without invitation at the breakfast table,
in the grocery store, in the shallow hours of early morning when he wakes at 3 a.m. and cannot
return to sleep. He lies in the dark and reconstructs the sequence of events, searching for the
moment he could have intervened, though he understands intellectually that no intervention was
possible.

Martin is not a man who startles easily, or wasn't. Now, any sudden sound — a door closing, a car
backfiring on the road — sends a jolt through him that takes long minutes to subside. His son
visited last Thanksgiving and dropped a cast-iron pan, and Martin found himself gripping the
counter, heart hammering, certain for one irrational second that something catastrophic was
happening again. He felt ashamed afterward, which is a feeling he has grown accustomed to carrying.

He has become watchful in a way that exhausts him. He monitors his own body obsessively, convinced
that a headache signals something fatal. He has visited his physician four times since Carol's death
with neurological concerns, each time embarrassed to be there and unable to stay away. He worries
about his adult children with an intensity he recognizes as disproportionate but cannot modulate.

His marriage of thirty-four years has left a silence in the house that is not peaceful. Martin
describes it as a pressure, something that presses against him when he sits in the living room in
the evening. He has rearranged the furniture twice. He has not rearranged the studio.

Occasionally he forces himself to sit at the wheel. He centers the clay, begins to pull, and then
stops. He says he doesn't know what he's making anymore, or who he's making it for. Carol had been
his first audience for everything — every bowl, every vase, every failed experiment. Without her
eyes on his work, he is not sure the work means anything. He covers the clay with a damp cloth to
keep it from drying out, and he goes back inside the house, and he waits for a morning that feels
different from this one.
