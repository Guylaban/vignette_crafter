# The Weight of a Moment

Margaret is sixty years old, a state legislator with nearly two decades of public service behind
her. She is composed in the way that experienced politicians learn to be — measured in her speech,
deliberate in her movements, careful about what she allows others to see. But three years ago, on a
rainy October evening after a campaign fundraiser, something happened that quietly dismantled the
woman she had spent a lifetime building.

She had not been drinking. That is the first thing she tells people, when she tells anyone at all,
which is almost never. She had been tired — the kind of bone-deep exhaustion that accumulates across
a campaign season — and she had been distracted, replaying a difficult conversation she'd had with
her campaign manager. She pulled out of the venue's parking garage and simply did not see the
cyclist. The impact was immediate and terrible. The young man, twenty-three years old, sustained a
severe traumatic brain injury. He survived, but he did not survive whole.

In the days that followed, Margaret moved through a kind of mechanical numbness. There were police
reports, insurance calls, a quiet legal settlement. Her staff managed the press carefully, and the
story never became the headline it might have been. She returned to her legislative duties within a
week. She told herself that was the right thing to do — that her constituents needed her, that
collapsing would help no one. What she did not acknowledge, not then, was that returning to work was
also a way of outrunning what had happened.

The outrunning has not worked. She cannot drive after dark. This is simply a fact of her life now,
one she has constructed elaborate explanations to conceal from colleagues. She takes car services,
citing environmental commitment. She schedules evening events with unusual precision, always
ensuring a driver is arranged before she agrees to attend. When she is occasionally a passenger in a
car at dusk or at night, she grips the door handle without realizing she is doing it, and her mind
floods with the sound — a sound she cannot fully describe but cannot stop hearing, somewhere between
a thud and a silence that followed it.

She sleeps poorly. She wakes between two and four in the morning and lies in the dark of her
apartment — she and her husband divorced six years before the accident, and she lives alone —
running through the moment in a loop she cannot interrupt. She changes small details in her mind, as
though revision is still possible. What if she had left five minutes earlier. What if she had taken
the other exit. The loop never resolves. It simply exhausts itself until dawn.

In her professional life, Margaret has become, paradoxically, more effective in certain ways and
more brittle in others. She has thrown herself into transportation safety legislation with an
intensity that her staff finds impressive and slightly alarming. She does not tell them why. She has
become a fierce advocate for traumatic brain injury funding, and she visits rehabilitation
facilities with a regularity that her office frames as constituent engagement. What she is actually
doing, she understands somewhere beneath the surface, is looking for the young man in every patient
she meets — or looking for a version of the story in which she can do something useful with what she
did.

She is irritable in ways she was not before, quick to anger in closed-door meetings, then ashamed of
it afterward. She has stopped attending her book club, stopped returning calls from her two closest
friends, gradually narrowed her world to the dimensions of her work. It is easier, she has found, to
be a public person than a private one. In public, there is a role to perform. In private, there is
only the parking garage, the rain, the moment she cannot take back, and the young man whose name she
knows but cannot bring herself to say aloud.
