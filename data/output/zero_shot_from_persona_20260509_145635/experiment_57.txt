# Case Vignette: Marcus

Marcus is a 42-year-old Māori electrician who has been with his partner, Lena, for eleven years. He
is a broad-shouldered, quietly spoken man who chooses his words carefully, as though each one costs
him something. He came to therapy reluctantly, at Lena's urging, after she told him she no longer
recognized the person he had become.

Two years ago, Marcus was attacked leaving a pub on a Friday night after a work drinks. He had
stepped outside to call Lena when two men approached him from behind. What followed was brief but
savage — he was knocked to the ground, kicked repeatedly, and left with a fractured orbital socket,
three broken ribs, and a deep laceration along his jaw that required fourteen stitches. The men were
never found. The police took a statement, handed him a case number, and the file went quiet. Marcus
was discharged from hospital after two days and went back to work within three weeks because, as he
puts it, *"the bills don't care how you're feeling."*

What he did not go back to was himself.

In the months that followed, Marcus began waking at two or three in the morning with his heart
hammering, drenched in sweat, certain that someone was in the room. He would lie rigid beside Lena,
listening to the house settle, unable to convince his body that the danger had passed. During the
day, he found he could not stop scanning — every car park, every alleyway, every stranger walking
behind him on a footpath. On job sites, the ordinary sounds of construction — a sudden bang, a shout
across a warehouse floor — would send a bolt of adrenaline through him so sharp it left his hands
trembling. He began making excuses to avoid working in underground car parks or confined plant
rooms, places where exits were limited. His apprentice has quietly started covering those jobs
without either of them naming why.

Marcus does not talk about the night of the assault in any direct way. When Lena has tried to ask,
he changes the subject or leaves the room. He describes his own mind as *"a room I don't want to go
into."* He has stopped watching certain television programmes, avoids the suburb where the attack
occurred, and no longer goes to the pub with workmates — something that has cost him friendships he
doesn't know how to grieve. He tells himself he simply doesn't enjoy those things anymore, though
somewhere beneath that he knows the truth is different.

What troubles him most, and what he speaks about with a kind of bewildered shame, is the anger.
Marcus grew up in a household that valued manaakitanga — care, respect, generosity toward others —
and he has always thought of himself as a patient man. He is no longer patient. He snaps at Lena
over small things and has twice put his fist through walls in their home. He describes these moments
as coming from nowhere, as if something overtakes him that he cannot predict or control. Afterwards
he feels a deep, hollow shame that he cannot articulate to her, only apologize around.

He sleeps poorly, concentrates poorly, and has begun drinking two or three beers each night to take
the edge off what he calls *"the hum"* — a constant low-level alertness that never fully switches
off. He has lost a significant amount of weight without trying. Lena tells him he looks hollowed
out. He thinks she is right.

Marcus sits in the therapy room with his arms crossed and his jaw set, a man doing his best to hold
a shape that used to come naturally. He says he is not sure what he wants from this process. He says
he just wants to feel like himself again — to walk through a car park without his pulse spiking, to
lie next to Lena without bracing for something that never comes, to be the kind of man he recognizes
in the mirror.
