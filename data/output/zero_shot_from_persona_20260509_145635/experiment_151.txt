# The Weight of What Was Carried

Marcus is a sixty-year-old warehouse worker who moves through his days with the particular economy
of motion that comes from decades of physical labor. He has worked the same distribution center for
eleven years, loading and unloading freight in the pre-dawn hours when the building is quiet and the
other men haven't yet arrived. He prefers it that way — the stillness, the predictability, the fact
that nothing unexpected tends to happen at four in the morning in a warehouse on the edge of a small
Belgian town.

He was nineteen when he enlisted. What he describes as the defining period of his life unfolded over
fourteen months deployed with a peacekeeping force in the Balkans during the early 1990s, when the
word "peacekeeping" turned out to mean something far more complicated than anyone had told him. He
witnessed mass civilian casualties in a village his unit was ordered to secure but could not
protect. He watched a close friend bleed out in a ditch while radio communications failed. He
carried bodies. He was ordered to stand down when every instinct he had was screaming to move
forward. He came home at twenty-one and told almost no one what he had seen, because the country he
returned to seemed to have no language for it, and because he himself had no language for it either.

For years he managed. He married, worked, raised two daughters. The marriage eventually ended — he
describes this without much elaboration, only saying that he was "not easy to be with" during those
years. There were periods of heavy drinking. There were jobs lost. He found his way to the warehouse
work and to Elise, his current partner, who he says is the most patient person he has ever known.

What brought him to the clinic was not a crisis exactly, but an accumulation. A news segment about
the Balkans aired without warning one evening six months ago, and something in Marcus came loose. He
stopped sleeping in any meaningful way. He lies beside Elise each night and watches the ceiling,
moving through the same scenes again and again — the ditch, the village, the faces — with a clarity
that disturbs him precisely because it feels more real than the room around him. When he does sleep,
he wakes shouting, which distresses him almost as much for Elise's sake as for his own.

At the warehouse, he has begun to struggle in ways he finds humiliating. A forklift backfiring sends
him flat against the shelving before he is conscious of having moved. A colleague who raised his
voice during a scheduling dispute found Marcus gripping the edge of a pallet rack, white-knuckled,
heart hammering, certain for a fractured moment that he was somewhere else entirely. He has started
arriving even earlier than his usual shift to avoid the noise of the loading bay at full operation.

He describes a persistent sense of being hollowed out — present in his body but absent from his
life. He sits at dinner with Elise and understands that he loves her, but the feeling arrives at a
remove, as though observed through glass. He has stopped seeing his daughters as often as he once
did, finding the noise of his grandchildren — whom he adores — almost unbearable some days. He feels
guilt about this that he carries like freight.

What strikes Marcus most, and what he returns to repeatedly in session, is not the fear or the
sleeplessness but the shame — the sense that forty years is too long to still be standing in that
ditch. He is a practical man who has spent his life moving things from one place to another, and he
cannot understand why he is unable to move this. He sits with his large, work-roughened hands folded
in his lap and speaks carefully, as though measuring each word for weight, trying to find the one
that might finally be heavy enough to name what he has been carrying since he was twenty years old.
