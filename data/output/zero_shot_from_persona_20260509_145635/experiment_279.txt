# The Weight of Water

Boon-Nam is a fifty-one-year-old Thai man who works as a senior policy analyst for a regional
environmental agency, a role he has held for nearly fifteen years. He is soft-spoken and precise in
the way he chooses his words — a habit that serves him well in his professional life, where language
shapes legislation. He lives with his wife, Malee, in a modest apartment on the outskirts of the
city, and by most outward measures, he appears composed, even reserved. It is only in the careful
pauses between his sentences that the shape of what he carries begins to show.

In December 2004, Boon-Nam was thirty-two years old and visiting his family in a coastal village in
southern Thailand when the Indian Ocean tsunami struck. He remembers the morning with a clarity that
has never softened — the strange withdrawal of the sea, the uncanny quiet that preceded it, and then
the wall of water that moved through the village with a sound he describes not as a roar but as a
kind of erasure. He survived by clinging to the upper branches of a large mango tree, watching the
water take everything below him. He watched a neighbor he had known since childhood disappear into
the surge. He watched the house where his parents had lived for forty years dissolve. He held onto
that tree for nearly three hours before the water receded enough for him to descend into a landscape
he no longer recognized.

His parents survived. Several relatives did not. In the weeks that followed, Boon-Nam helped
coordinate relief efforts in the area, channeling his shock into action. He returned to his work in
Bangkok within a month, telling himself and others that he was coping, that he was fortunate, that
others had lost far more. For years, this narrative held.

But the water has never fully receded from his mind. Now, nearly two decades later, Boon-Nam finds
himself struggling in ways he can no longer rationalize away. The rainy season in his city has grown
more intense in recent years, and his work — which increasingly involves drafting policy responses
to climate-related flooding — places him in daily contact with exactly the imagery and language that
undoes him. Satellite photographs of inundated coastlines, flood modeling projections, reports from
disaster zones: each one pulls at something deep and involuntary. He will sometimes stop mid-
sentence during a briefing, his chest tightening, a cold sweat moving across his back, certain for a
moment that he can hear the sound of that water again.

He sleeps poorly. He wakes between two and four in the morning, sometimes from dreams he cannot
fully remember, sometimes simply alert and rigid, listening for something he cannot name. Malee has
grown used to finding him at the kitchen table in the early hours, staring at nothing, a cup of tea
gone cold beside him. He rarely speaks to her about what he is experiencing, in part because he does
not want to burden her, and in part because he genuinely struggles to find words for something that
feels pre-verbal, almost physical in its nature.

He avoids the coast entirely. He has declined two work assignments that would have required travel
to flood-affected regions, offering bureaucratic reasons each time. He has not returned to his
family's village since 2005. When his daughter, now grown, mentioned wanting to take a beach holiday
together, he changed the subject and later felt a shame he could not fully account for.

What troubles Boon-Nam most is the sense that he is becoming unreliable to himself — that the
careful, methodical man he has always understood himself to be is increasingly at the mercy of
something he cannot analyze or contain. He prides himself on his ability to assess risk clearly, to
weigh evidence, to remain steady. The fact that his own nervous system will not cooperate with this
self-image is, for him, its own particular wound.
