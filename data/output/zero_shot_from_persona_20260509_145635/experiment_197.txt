# Vignette: Roberto

Roberto is a 65-year-old Latin American man who spent three years in military service in his early
twenties, including a deployment that placed him in the middle of sustained ground combat. He
watched men in his unit die. He carried a friend — barely twenty years old — off a hillside after an
ambush, knowing before they reached the medic that the young man was already gone. Roberto never
spoke about any of this when he came home. He married, found work, built a life. He told himself
that was simply what you did.

For decades, the strategy more or less held. He became a special education teacher, working with
children who had learning disabilities and behavioral challenges — kids who needed someone patient
and steady, someone who didn't flinch. He was good at it. His students responded to him. He threw
himself into the work with a kind of devotion that, if he is honest now, also served the purpose of
keeping him too busy to sit quietly with himself. His marriage frayed over the years under the
weight of his emotional distance, his difficulty being truly present, his tendency to go somewhere
else in his mind even when he was physically in the room. His wife eventually left. He understood
why, even as it devastated him.

The trouble that brought Roberto here began about two years ago, after he retired. Without the
classroom to anchor him, the days became unstructured and the silence grew loud. He started sleeping
poorly, waking at three or four in the morning with his heart pounding, the details of the ambush
returning with a vividness that surprised and frightened him. He had assumed those memories had
faded. They had not. They had simply been waiting.

Now he finds himself moving through the world with a low, persistent wariness he cannot entirely
explain to himself. He sits with his back to the wall in restaurants. He avoids the Fourth of July
entirely — the fireworks send a spike of alarm through his body before his mind has time to catch
up. He knows, intellectually, that he is in a suburban neighborhood and not a hillside, but his
nervous system does not seem to have received that information. He describes a feeling of being
permanently slightly braced, as though something is always about to happen.

He still volunteers two mornings a week at a local school, helping a young boy with dyslexia learn
to read. He says this is the part of his week he looks forward to most. With the child, he is calm
and focused, present in a way he struggles to be elsewhere. But on the drive home afterward, he
sometimes finds his thoughts drifting back — to his friend's face, to the weight of carrying him, to
the particular quality of light on that hillside. He will arrive home without remembering the last
ten minutes of the drive.

Roberto does not describe himself as someone who suffers. He is matter-of-fact, even slightly
apologetic, as though he worries about taking up too much space with his own history. He mentions,
almost in passing, that he rarely talks to his adult children. Not because of any conflict, he says,
but because he doesn't want them to see that something is wrong. He has spent forty years being the
steady one, and he is not sure who he is if he isn't that.

What he does say, quietly, near the end of a session, is that he is tired. Not sleepy — though he is
that too — but tired in a deeper way. Tired of managing something he has never named, tired of the
vigilance, tired of the way the past keeps insisting on being present. He says he thought by sixty-
five a man would have some peace. He is still waiting for it.
