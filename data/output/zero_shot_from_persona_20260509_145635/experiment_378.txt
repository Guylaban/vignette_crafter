# The Weight of Old Code

Martin is a 66-year-old software engineer who has spent the better part of four decades building
systems designed to be predictable, logical, and safe — the kind of ordered world his childhood
never offered him.

He grew up in a small town in rural England, the eldest of three children. His father was a volatile
man who drank heavily and whose moods shifted without warning. From the time Martin was around seven
or eight, beatings were a regular feature of life at home — delivered with a belt, an open hand, or
whatever was nearby. There was rarely a clear reason. Martin learned early to read the atmosphere of
a room the way a sailor reads weather: the particular way his father set down a glass, the sound of
footsteps on the stairs, the quality of silence before a storm. He became very good at it. He had to
be.

He left home at seventeen, earned a scholarship, and threw himself into mathematics and computing
with a focus that impressed his professors. He met his wife, Carol, in his late twenties, and by
most external measures built a stable and successful life. They have two grown children and a home
in Wiltshire. He has been, as he puts it, "functional."

What Martin did not expect was retirement. He stopped working eighteen months ago, and without the
structure of deadlines, problem-solving, and the clean logic of code, something old has begun to
surface. He finds himself sleeping poorly, waking at two or three in the morning with his heart
already racing, a formless dread settled in his chest before he is even fully conscious. He lies
still and listens to the house, straining for sounds he cannot name.

During the day, he is irritable in ways that confuse him. Carol has mentioned it carefully, more
than once. Small things — a raised voice on the television, the unexpected slam of a door — send a
jolt through him that he feels in his stomach before he feels it in his mind. He is quick to snap at
her and then ashamed of it afterward, recognizing in his own sharpness something he spent his life
trying not to become.

He does not think about his father often, or so he has told himself. But the memories arrive
uninvited: the particular smell of his father's breath, the way he used to brace himself against the
wall of his bedroom at night, listening. These are not dramatic flashbacks so much as sudden,
unwanted presences — a texture of feeling that drops over him without warning and takes time to
lift. He finds himself avoiding the pub in the village where he and Carol sometimes go with friends,
because the sound of men laughing loudly across a bar makes something in him go very quiet and
watchful.

What troubles Martin most, and what he finds most difficult to articulate, is a persistent sense of
emotional distance. He loves Carol, he is certain of that, but there are moments when he feels as
though he is watching his own life through glass — present but somehow not quite reachable. He has
always been private, but this feels different from privacy. It feels like a door he cannot find the
handle to.

He came to therapy at Carol's gentle insistence, and he arrived skeptical but polite. He describes
his childhood with a kind of careful precision, the way he might document a legacy system — here is
what it did, here is what it failed to do. But occasionally, mid-sentence, something crosses his
face that is not precision at all. He pauses. He looks at his hands.

He says he is not sure what he expected retirement to feel like, but it was not this — this sense of
having finally stopped moving fast enough to outrun something that had been keeping pace with him
all along.
