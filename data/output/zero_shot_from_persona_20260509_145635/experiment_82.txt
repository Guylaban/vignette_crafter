**Vignette: Maya, Age 37**

Maya has always understood the world through sound. Growing up in Toronto, she learned to read a
room by its acoustics — the way a crowd breathes before applause, the particular silence that falls
when an audience is genuinely moved. Music wasn't just her profession; it was the primary language
through which she made sense of being alive.

Three years ago, Maya was performing at an outdoor festival in the city's east end when a vehicle
drove into the crowd near the main stage. She was mid-song when she heard what she initially mistook
for equipment feedback, then a collective scream that moved through the audience like a wave. She
remembers dropping her guitar and freezing at the microphone for what felt like a very long time but
was probably only seconds. People ran in every direction. She was pulled backstage by a roadie she
barely knew, and from there she watched — through a gap in the stage curtain — as emergency
responders began working on people on the ground. Four people died that afternoon. Dozens more were
injured. Maya was physically unharmed.

In the weeks that followed, she told herself she was fine. She gave interviews. She posted a tribute
online. She tried to go back into the studio, where she sat in front of a microphone for two hours
and recorded nothing. The silence in the booth felt different now — pressurized, like something was
about to happen inside it.

She stopped performing live. She told her label it was burnout, which wasn't entirely untrue. What
she didn't tell them was that she had started avoiding any situation involving large gatherings —
festivals, concerts, even crowded restaurants. When her partner, Daniel, suggested they attend a
friend's outdoor wedding last summer, she agreed and then spent the entire ceremony scanning the
perimeter of the venue, tracking every car that passed on the road beyond the fence. She didn't hear
a word of the vows.

At night, she often wakes between two and four in the morning with her heart already racing, the
images from that afternoon rearranging themselves into slightly different versions — sometimes she
is in the crowd instead of on the stage, sometimes she doesn't make it backstage in time. She lies
still in the dark beside Daniel and works to slow her breathing without waking him. She has become
careful not to let him see how bad the nights can get, worried that her fear will begin to feel like
a burden he didn't sign up for.

During the day, Maya works mostly from home now, composing for film and television — work that suits
her current life because it requires no audience, no stage, no open-air venues. She is good at it,
and she finds genuine satisfaction in the craft. But she notices that she gravitates toward scoring
quieter scenes, and when a director asks for something with crowd noise or ambient festival sound in
the background, she finds ways to route the request to a colleague. She tells herself this is a
reasonable division of labor. She doesn't entirely believe it.

What troubles her most, she says, is not the fear itself but the way it has quietly reorganized her
sense of who she is. She used to feel most herself in front of people, feeding off their energy,
trusting the shared space between a performer and a room full of strangers. That trust is gone now,
and she isn't sure how to grieve something so specific — not a person, not a relationship, but a way
of being in the world that she can no longer access. She still writes music every day. She still
loves it. But she writes it now from behind something, and she is only beginning to admit, sitting
in a therapist's office for the first time, that she has been doing so for quite a while.
