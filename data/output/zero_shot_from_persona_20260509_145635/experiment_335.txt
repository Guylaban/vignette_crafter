**Vignette: Amara, Age 20**

Amara grew up in a mid-sized city in West Africa, the eldest daughter of a pharmacist father who
taught her to love medicine before she could properly read the labels on the bottles. She was bright
and determined, and by nineteen she had earned early admission to a pharmacy program, following her
father's footsteps with a precision that made her family proud. That future felt certain and close —
until the night soldiers came to the house.

She does not talk about what happened in full, not yet. What she has shared comes in fragments,
offered carefully, like something breakable being passed across a table. There were several men.
There was a room with no windows. What they did to her body over the course of two days was
methodical in a way that still makes her feel, even now, that it was somehow meant to erase her. She
was released without explanation, left on a road outside the city in the early morning. She walked
until someone stopped.

Within months, through a combination of family connections and an asylum process she barely
remembers navigating, Amara arrived in a new country. She was nineteen. She had her transcripts, a
small bag, and a boyfriend, Kofi, who had been studying abroad and who she credits — quietly, with
visible ambivalence — with keeping her from disappearing entirely into herself. She resumed her
studies. She is now twenty, completing her first year of pharmacy training, and from the outside she
appears to be managing extraordinarily well.

She is not managing well.

At work in the dispensary, she functions with a competence that surprises even her supervisors. She
knows the drug interactions, the contraindications, the proper protocols. But when a male colleague
stands too close to reach past her for a bottle, her body goes rigid before her mind can intervene.
She has learned to disguise this as concentration. She has become very good at disguising things.

Sleep is where the architecture of her control collapses. She wakes most nights between two and four
in the morning, heart hammering, the smell of that room still somehow present in the air of her
apartment. She does not scream — she learned early to suppress that — but she lies awake for hours,
running through medication classifications in her head as a way of anchoring herself to something
ordered and safe. Some mornings she arrives at the dispensary having not slept at all, and she
counts tablets with the focused desperation of someone holding onto a rope.

She startles at doors. She cannot tolerate the sound of boots on a hard floor. She has stopped
watching the news entirely, and she avoids any film or television that involves confinement or
uniforms, a category she has had to map out carefully and painfully through accidental exposure.
Once, a patient came in wearing military-style clothing and she had to excuse herself to the back
room, where she stood gripping the edge of a shelf until her hands stopped shaking.

With Kofi, she is present in the ways she can manage and absent in the ways she cannot explain to
him. He is patient and loving and she is grateful for this in a way that sometimes feels like grief,
because she knows he is loving someone who is only partially there. Physical intimacy is fraught
with a complexity she has not yet found language for. She holds him at night sometimes and feels
nothing, and other times feels everything at once and has to pull away. He does not always
understand, and she does not yet know how to tell him why.

What Amara carries most visibly is not rage or grief but a kind of hypervigilant exhaustion — the
look of someone who has been on watch for so long she has forgotten what it felt like to simply
rest. She is twenty years old. She counts pills with great precision. She checks the locks on her
apartment door three times before she can sleep, and even then, she does not really sleep.
