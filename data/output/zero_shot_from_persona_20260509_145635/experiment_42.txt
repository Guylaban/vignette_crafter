**Vignette: Leilani, Age 20**

Leilani grew up in a small community in Samoa, where family loyalty and keeping peace within
relationships were values she absorbed long before she could name them. She married young — at
eighteen — to a man she had known since secondary school. In the beginning, she describes their
relationship as full of warmth and possibility. She had just begun an apprenticeship with a design
firm, and she remembers feeling, for a brief window of time, that her life was opening up in all the
right directions.

The violence started gradually. A shove during an argument. A grip on her wrist that left bruises
she covered with long sleeves. By the time the incidents escalated into something she could no
longer explain away, Leilani had developed an intricate internal system for managing her own fear —
staying quiet when he came home late, rearranging her schedule around his moods, reading the
atmosphere of a room the way she had learned to read a floor plan. She left him eight months ago,
after an evening when he struck her across the face hard enough to split her lip. A neighbour called
the police. She has not returned to the home they shared.

Now living with her aunt in Auckland, Leilani presents as composed and articulate, though she often
pauses mid-sentence as though she is listening for something in the silence. She is back at work,
which she describes as the one place she still feels like herself. She loses herself in fabric
samples and colour palettes, in the logic of space and light. But she admits that even at work,
concentration slips away from her without warning. She will be measuring a room and suddenly find
herself back in the hallway of their apartment, her back against the wall, her hands raised. These
intrusions arrive without invitation and leave her shaken in ways she does not always know how to
explain to her colleagues.

Sleep is unreliable. She wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, heart pounding,
certain she has heard the front door. She checks the locks. She checks them again. Her aunt has
mentioned this to her gently, and Leilani feels ashamed in a way she finds difficult to articulate —
as though her body is betraying something she would prefer to keep private. She was raised to
believe that what happens between a husband and wife is not for the outside world to witness, and
even now, separated and safe, that belief sits heavily in her chest.

She has stopped attending her cousin's family gatherings, partly because her ex-husband's relatives
are sometimes present, and partly because she no longer trusts herself to hold it together in a room
full of people. Loud voices — even cheerful ones — cause her to startle. She has noticed herself
scanning exits whenever she enters a new space, a habit she recognises as strange for someone whose
job is to make spaces feel welcoming and beautiful. There is a painful irony in that, she says,
almost to herself.

Leilani does not describe herself as angry, though anger surfaces in careful, controlled bursts when
she speaks about how long she stayed. Mostly she describes feeling numb, then guilty, then numb
again. She worries about what her parents in Samoa would think if they knew the full extent of what
happened. She has told them only that the marriage did not work out.

What she returns to most often, in the quiet of her aunt's spare room, is a question she cannot yet
answer: how she became someone who did not recognise danger until it had already marked her. She is
twenty years old. She is trying to rebuild a life inside an apartment that does not yet feel like
hers, in a city that is familiar but not quite home, carrying something she has not yet found the
words to put down.
