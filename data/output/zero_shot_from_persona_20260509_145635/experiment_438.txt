**Vignette: Fatima, Age 59**

Fatima has spent thirty-one years on the force. She came up through the ranks in an era when female
officers were rarities, and she earned her place through sheer determination and an unshakable
belief that the work mattered. Her colleagues describe her as steady, methodical, someone you want
beside you when things go sideways. That reputation is part of what makes the past fourteen months
so difficult for her to reconcile.

It happened during what should have been a routine welfare check. A call came in about a disturbance
at a residential address — the kind of call Fatima had responded to hundreds of times. When she
entered the apartment, a man she had briefly spoken with at the door turned on her with sudden,
explosive violence. He struck her repeatedly before her partner reached her, and for a stretch of
time she cannot fully account for, she genuinely believed she was going to die in that hallway. She
remembers the texture of the linoleum against her cheek. She remembers the sound of her own
breathing, strange and wet. Then she remembers nothing until the hospital.

Her physical injuries healed within weeks. What has not healed is harder to name, and Fatima resists
naming it. She returned to desk duty after six weeks, then back to active patrol, because that is
what she does — she returns. But patrol is different now. When she drives through certain
neighborhoods, her body decides things before her mind does. Her hands tighten on the wheel. Her
chest constricts. She finds herself running exit calculations in every building she enters,
cataloguing threats in rooms that hold none.

At home, her husband Tariq notices that she sleeps lightly now, or not at all. She wakes at two or
three in the morning and sits in the kitchen with the lights on, drinking tea she doesn't taste. She
has told him she is fine, and she says it with enough conviction that he mostly believes her, or at
least stops asking. What she doesn't tell him is that when she closes her eyes she is back in that
hallway, not as a memory but as something happening right now, with the same cold certainty and the
same helplessness. She has not told anyone that.

She has become short with her adult children in ways that embarrass her afterward. She has stopped
attending the monthly dinners her sister hosts, offering excuses that have grown thin. Her faith has
always been a source of structure in her life, but lately she finds herself sitting through Friday
prayers feeling sealed off from everyone around her, present in body and absent in every other way.

At work, she functions. She files her reports, she responds to calls, she mentors younger officers
with the same patient authority she always has. But she has quietly stopped volunteering for
anything that might take her back to that building or that part of the city. A colleague made a joke
last month about the welfare check call — something offhand, not malicious — and Fatima walked to
the bathroom and stood over the sink for ten minutes, breathing, before she could return to the
room.

What troubles her most, she admits in quieter moments, is not the fear itself but what the fear
implies. She has built her identity around competence and composure, around being the person who
does not flinch. She is fifty-nine years old. She has outlasted skeptics and survived a career that
has asked a great deal of her. The idea that a single afternoon in a stranger's hallway could undo
something so foundational feels, to her, like a betrayal — not by the man who attacked her, but by
herself. She carries that judgment closely, and it sits heavier than most things she has carried.
