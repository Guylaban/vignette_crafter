# The Weight of What She Carried Home

Margaret is fifty-eight years old, silver-haired and precise in the way she moves through a room —
the kind of woman who has always known how to take up exactly the right amount of space. She has
served on the city council for eleven years, championing housing policy and community development
with a reputation for being unflappable under pressure. Her colleagues describe her as measured,
competent, and difficult to rattle. What they don't know is that she hasn't slept through the night
in nearly four years.

Before her political career solidified, Margaret spent several months as a logistics coordinator
with a humanitarian relief organization operating in a conflict zone in Eastern Europe. She had done
similar work before — shorter deployments, lower intensity — and believed she understood what she
was walking into. She did not. Within her first weeks on the ground, she was organizing supply
convoys through active shelling corridors, making decisions about which communities received food
shipments when there wasn't enough for all of them, and watching the consequences of those decisions
play out in real time. One afternoon, a convoy she had cleared to depart was struck. Three local
drivers she had worked alongside daily were killed. She was not in the vehicle. She was standing at
a checkpoint perhaps four hundred meters away, watching.

She finished her deployment. She is proud of that, and also not entirely sure it was the right thing
to do.

When Margaret returned home, she told herself she was tired and needed time to decompress. She took
two weeks, went back to work, and found that the council chambers felt both familiar and somehow
translucent — as though the ordinary business of zoning disputes and budget allocations existed
behind a thin membrane she could see through but not quite touch. She threw herself into her work
with even greater intensity than before, staying late, filling her calendar, becoming the colleague
who always had an answer. Busyness, she has since come to understand, was her first and most
reliable defense.

The intrusions began quietly. A particular quality of afternoon light — low, amber, the kind that
falls in autumn — would pull her back to the checkpoint without warning. She would be mid-sentence
in a committee meeting and find herself briefly, viscerally elsewhere: the sound of the impact, the
way the smoke moved, the strange administrative calm that descended on her afterward as she began
making phone calls and filling out forms. She has learned to pause, take a breath, and continue
speaking. No one has ever noticed, as far as she knows.

She avoids the news coverage of the region where she served. She has declined two invitations to
speak at humanitarian fundraising events, citing scheduling conflicts. She does not watch films
involving war or emergency response, and she has quietly stopped socializing with a former colleague
from the deployment who sends occasional messages she leaves unanswered. Each avoidance feels
reasonable in the moment and leaves a faint residue of shame afterward.

At night, the defenses she maintains so carefully during the day become harder to sustain. She lies
awake replaying decisions — the convoy clearance, the resource allocations, the choices that felt
necessary and still feel wrong. She wakes from fragmented dreams with her heart already racing,
orientating herself to her apartment ceiling, reminding herself where she is. She is more irritable
than she used to be, though she manages this privately. She lives alone, which makes management
easier.

What brings Margaret in now is not a crisis but an accumulation. A younger council member recently
commented, warmly and without any harmful intent, that Margaret seemed like someone who had never
once doubted herself. Margaret went home that evening and sat in her kitchen for a long time, unable
to identify what she was feeling. She made an appointment the following morning. She tells her
therapist, in their first session, that she isn't sure she's the kind of person who needs this. She
says it with enough self-awareness to suggest she already suspects otherwise.
