# Margaret

Margaret is a 76-year-old radiologist who spent most of her career reading films in the quiet,
darkened rooms she always loved — a world of gray gradients and careful looking, where she felt most
at home. She had built a life of precision and calm. Then, in her late sixties, she traveled with a
medical humanitarian organization to a conflict region in the Middle East, intending to volunteer
for six weeks. She stayed for four months, because there was always another body that needed someone
to look at the films.

The event she cannot leave behind happened on a Tuesday afternoon. A missile strike hit a
residential block two streets from the field hospital where she worked. She was in the imaging tent
when the building shook. In the hours that followed, she read scan after scan of children —
fractured skulls, shrapnel lodged against small spines, lungs full of concrete dust. She worked for
nineteen hours without stopping. At one point she called a scan clear and the child died on the
table twenty minutes later from an injury she had missed, buried in artifact on a damaged machine.
She has never forgiven herself for that read.

She returned home to her husband and their house in a suburb of Toronto, and for a while she
convinced herself she was simply tired. But the tiredness did not lift. She began waking at two or
three in the morning with her heart pounding, certain she could smell smoke, certain the bedroom
window was letting in the sound of something burning. Her husband would find her standing in the
hallway, not quite asleep, not quite awake, her hands moving slightly as though she were still
tracing structures on a lightbox.

At work — she has continued practicing part-time, refusing to retire — she finds the reading room
unbearable in ways she cannot fully explain to her colleagues. The darkness that once felt like
sanctuary now feels like a held breath. When she pulls up a pediatric chest film, her hands
sometimes stop moving over the keyboard. She has developed a habit of over-reading, requesting
additional sequences, flagging findings that more junior radiologists tell her are within normal
limits. She knows, rationally, that she is compensating. She cannot stop.

She avoids the news entirely. Her husband has learned not to leave the television on when she comes
home. She declined an invitation to speak at a medical conference about humanitarian radiology
because the program listed photographs from the field. She threw the brochure away without opening
it fully. When her granddaughter, who is nine, was brought to the emergency room last spring with a
minor head injury, Margaret sat in the waiting room and could not bring herself to look at the
scans, though the attending offered to show her as a courtesy. She told them she was too close to
it. What she meant was something she has not yet found words for.

She describes her inner life now as living in two places at once. She is in the kitchen making tea,
and she is also always slightly in that tent, waiting for the next image to load, knowing before it
appears that it will be a child. She feels a persistent guilt that she describes as structural — not
something that comes and goes, but something load-bearing, something she has built her current self
around. She wonders sometimes whether the careful, methodical person she was before the trip was
naive in a way she can no longer afford to be, and whether that naivety was, in fact, a kind of
grace she has permanently lost.

Her husband says she is still the same person. She looks at him with something that is not quite
disagreement and not quite belief, and she changes the subject. She has not told him about the child
whose scan she misread. She has not told anyone, until now.
