# The Weight of a Small Chest Rising

Marcus is a sixty-nine-year-old Filipino man who has spent the better part of four decades caring
for other people's children. He is soft-spoken and precise in the way he moves through a room,
someone who has learned to be present without being intrusive. He has worked as a live-in nanny for
three different families over the years, and he speaks about the children he has raised with the
particular tenderness of someone who understood early that love does not require biology to be real.

Three years ago, Marcus was caring for a four-year-old boy named Daniel when the child collapsed
during breakfast. One moment Daniel was pushing cereal around a bowl, and the next he was on the
kitchen floor, his lips going the color of a bruise, his small body seizing in a way that Marcus had
never seen and cannot stop seeing. Marcus called emergency services and stayed on the phone,
following instructions, pressing his fingers to Daniel's wrist to check for a pulse, watching the
boy's chest for movement. He remembers the dispatcher's voice, flat and professional, asking him to
describe what he was seeing. He remembers thinking that if he described it calmly enough, it would
not be happening.

Daniel survived. A previously undetected cardiac arrhythmia, the doctors explained. He required
surgery and a lengthy hospitalization, but he recovered fully and is now a healthy seven-year-old
who reportedly still asks about Marcus. Marcus, however, has not recovered.

In the weeks after the incident, Marcus found himself unable to sleep in the room where he had been
living in that household. He resigned from the position, telling the family it was time to retire,
though he did not believe that himself. He moved into a small apartment and, after several months of
isolation, accepted a position with a new family. He thought work would help. It has not helped in
the way he hoped.

Marcus now cares for an infant and a toddler, and he describes his days as a sustained emergency.
Every nap is a vigil. He checks breathing so frequently that the infant's parents have gently asked
him whether something is wrong. He cannot explain to them what is wrong. When either child coughs or
stumbles or simply grows quiet, something seizes in his chest — the same sensation he felt standing
in that kitchen, the same absolute certainty that he is about to watch a child die and there will be
nothing he can do. His hands shake. He has to leave the room to breathe.

He lost his wife to cancer eight years ago, and he describes that grief as something he was able to
carry, something that had edges. This is different. This has no edges. He dreams about the kitchen
several times a week — sometimes the dream follows what actually happened, and sometimes it does
not, and the versions where Daniel does not survive are the ones that stay with him into the
afternoon. He wakes from these dreams already sitting upright, already listening for sounds he
cannot name.

Marcus rarely speaks about any of this. He was raised, he says, to be useful, and he worries that
talking about his own distress is a kind of indulgence. He has begun to wonder whether he is suited
for this work anymore, and that question frightens him more than he can articulate, because caring
for children is the only life he has built since his wife died. It is the structure that holds him.

What he says, when he finally says it, is this: he knows Daniel is fine. He has been told Daniel is
fine. But some part of him — the part that was standing in that kitchen with the phone pressed to
his ear — does not believe it yet, and he does not know how to reach that part of himself to tell it
that the boy is still breathing, that the morning ended differently than it felt like it would.
