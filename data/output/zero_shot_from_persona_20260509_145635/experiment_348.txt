**Vignette: Moana, 46-year-old Māori Woman**

Moana has always understood her body through sound. As a professional musician — a guitarist and
vocalist who has performed traditional Māori waiata alongside contemporary compositions for over two
decades — she has lived inside rhythm and breath, knowing instinctively when something is in or out
of tune. So when she first noticed the fatigue three years ago, she told herself it was the touring
schedule, the late nights, the accumulated weight of a busy life. She ignored it the way she
sometimes ignored a slightly flat string, certain she could correct it later.

She could not correct it later. What the doctors found was a rare and aggressive form of lymphoma.
Within two weeks of diagnosis, Moana was in hospital, beginning a course of chemotherapy that her
oncologist described, with careful understatement, as intensive. What Moana remembers from those
early weeks is less the nausea and the hair loss than a particular moment — lying alone in a
hospital bed at two in the morning, watching a monitor track her heart rate, and understanding with
absolute clarity that she might not leave this building alive. Her husband, Tama, was home with
their teenage children. The ward was quiet. She felt, she says, as though she had stepped off the
edge of the known world.

She survived. The treatment worked. Fourteen months after diagnosis, her oncologist used the word
*remission*, and Moana sat in his office and smiled and thanked him, and then walked to her car and
sat there for forty minutes unable to move. Something had not come back with her from that edge.

Now, two years into remission, Moana presents as a woman who looks, from the outside, like someone
who has rebuilt her life. She is performing again, recording a new album, present at school events
for her children. Tama describes her as "almost herself." But Moana knows the distance between
almost and fully, and it haunts her.

She cannot sleep past four in the morning. She wakes with her heart hammering, certain something is
wrong in her body — a new lump, an ache that wasn't there yesterday, a fatigue that feels familiar
in the wrong way. She spends the early morning hours cataloguing her physical sensations, running
her fingers along her neck checking lymph nodes, a ritual she knows is excessive and cannot stop. By
the time her family wakes, she has usually managed to compose herself, but the composure costs her
enormously.

In the recording studio, she finds herself unable to sustain concentration for more than twenty
minutes before a wave of dread moves through her — not attached to anything specific, just a cold
certainty that the cancer is returning, that this period of health is temporary and borrowed. She
has cancelled two sessions in the past month, telling her producer she has a cold. She has not told
him the truth, which is that she sat in the car park outside the studio and could not make herself
go in.

She avoids conversations about illness — in the news, in casual conversation, anywhere. When a close
friend was recently diagnosed with breast cancer, Moana found herself unable to visit her in
hospital, and the shame of this sits heavily on her. She has become irritable with Tama in ways she
doesn't fully understand, snapping at him when he asks how she is feeling, because the question
itself feels like a threat.

What Moana grieves most is her relationship with her own body. Music always required her to trust it
— her breath, her hands, her voice. Now her body feels like a place where catastrophe lives, quiet
and waiting. She plays, still, because she doesn't know who she is without it. But she plays, she
says, like someone who is always listening for the wrong note.
