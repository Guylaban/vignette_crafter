**Vignette: Amara**

Amara is a 54-year-old married woman of African origin who has served as a judge in her country's
high court for nearly two decades. She carries herself with a composure that colleagues describe as
unshakeable — precise in her language, measured in her manner, authoritative in her presence. What
they do not see is what happens when the courtroom falls silent, or when a defendant's raised voice
echoes off the marble walls, or when she returns home in the evening and cannot bring herself to
enter a room that is fully dark.

Eleven years ago, during a period of acute political instability in her home country, Amara was
abducted by a paramilitary faction that viewed her judicial rulings as a threat to their interests.
She was held for nine days. During that time she was subjected to systematic torture — prolonged
stress positions, sleep deprivation, beatings, and repeated mock executions in which she was made to
kneel with a weapon pressed to the back of her head. She was released without explanation and
without acknowledgment, as though the nine days had simply been erased from the official record. She
was told, implicitly and explicitly, that speaking about what had happened would place her family in
danger.

In the weeks that followed, Amara returned to work. She had no choice, or felt she had none. She
presided over cases, wrote judgments, attended ceremonial functions. She told her husband that she
was fine, and she believed, at the time, that this was something she could manage through
discipline. She had survived. She was home. She focused on the work.

But the work began to change. Sentencing hearings became almost unbearable. When a defendant stood
before her in restraints, something would shift inside her — a sudden, vertiginous sense that she
was not the one behind the bench. She learned to grip the edge of the desk beneath her robes, to
breathe slowly through her nose, to keep her face still. She became known for her long, deliberate
pauses before speaking, which colleagues attributed to careful legal reasoning.

At night, the discipline fails her. She sleeps for two or three hours before waking in a cold sweat,
her body convinced she is still kneeling on a concrete floor. She cannot always locate herself in
the dark — cannot immediately feel her husband beside her, cannot trust the silence of the bedroom.
She has quietly moved a lamp to the nightstand and leaves it on. Her husband does not ask about it
anymore.

She finds herself scanning every room she enters. She notices exits, assesses who is standing near
them, calculates distances. In her courtroom she has rearranged the layout of who stands where,
citing procedural concerns. She does not fully acknowledge, even to herself, that she did this
because she needed her back closer to the wall.

She has become increasingly withdrawn from the friends she once kept. Social gatherings feel unsafe
in ways she cannot articulate — too loud, too unpredictable, too full of people whose intentions she
cannot read. Her husband describes her as having "gone somewhere far away" in the years since her
abduction, even when she is sitting directly beside him. She knows this is true and does not know
how to come back.

What troubles her most, she says, is the anger. It rises in her without warning — at the defendants
who lie to her face, at the attorneys who waste her time, at her husband when he touches her
shoulder without warning. She has never raised her voice on the bench. She is afraid of what it
would mean if she did. She is afraid of what it would mean if she stopped being afraid.

She describes her life now as a performance of continuity — each day constructed carefully to
resemble the one before it, the structure of her role as a judge serving as the scaffolding that
keeps everything upright. She is not sure what would remain if that scaffolding were removed.
