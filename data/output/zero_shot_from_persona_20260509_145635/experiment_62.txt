# The Weight of What He Saw

Kofi is a 45-year-old man who works the land with the same quiet steadiness he has carried his whole
adult life. He tends crops on a large farm in the region where he was born, rising before dawn most
mornings, finding in the rhythm of soil and seasons something that has always felt safer than the
inside of a house.

He grew up in a home where safety was never guaranteed. From the time he was about six years old
until he left at seventeen, Kofi watched his father beat his mother with a regularity that became,
in the cruelest way, predictable. He learned to read the signs — the particular silence after his
father came home from drinking, the way his mother's shoulders would rise toward her ears. He would
position himself near his younger siblings, not knowing exactly what he intended to do, only knowing
he could not look away. He felt that if he watched carefully enough, he could somehow hold things
together. He never could. His mother survived, but the marriage, and something in Kofi, did not
fully.

He rarely speaks about those years. When he does, it is in fragments — a sentence offered and then
quickly withdrawn, like a hand pulled back from a flame. What he describes most readily is not the
violence itself but the sounds: his father's voice dropping to a certain register before things
escalated, furniture scraping across a concrete floor, his mother's attempts to muffle her crying so
the children would not hear. Those sounds did not stay in the past.

Today, Kofi lives with his partner, a woman he loves and trusts. Yet the relationship carries a
weight she did not put there. When they argue — even mildly, even about something as ordinary as
money or plans — something shifts inside him that he cannot fully explain. His chest tightens. His
mind goes somewhere else, or perhaps somewhere old. He becomes either very still or leaves the room
entirely, walking outside to stand among the rows of crops until his breathing slows. His partner
has learned not to follow him in those moments. He has never raised a hand to her, and the fear that
he might — a fear he holds privately, that he has never fully voiced — is one of the things that
keeps him awake at night.

Sleep is difficult. He wakes in the early hours, sometimes from dreams he cannot quite hold onto,
sometimes from a formless dread that has no name. He lies still in the dark, listening to the sounds
of the house, parsing them the way he parsed sounds as a child. On the farm, he is competent and
calm. It is the evenings that undo him.

He has trouble concentrating when he is not working. He describes a kind of flatness that settles
over him in quieter moments — not sadness exactly, but an absence, as though certain feelings have
simply gone offline. He does not talk much about his childhood with friends or family. He has told
himself for years that it is finished, that he moved on, that his father is dead and the house is
gone. But the body, as he is slowly beginning to understand, does not sort time the way the mind
insists it does.

What brought him in was not a crisis but an accumulation — his partner gently suggesting, more than
once, that something was wrong that she could not reach. He agreed to come, though he sat for the
first session with his hands folded and his eyes on the floor, as if he were still that boy near the
doorway, watching and waiting and trying not to take up too much space.

He is a man who has built a life with genuine care and effort. The farm, the relationship, the early
mornings — these are not nothing. But underneath all of it, something old is still standing guard,
still listening for a sound that signals danger, still not quite convinced the worst is over.
