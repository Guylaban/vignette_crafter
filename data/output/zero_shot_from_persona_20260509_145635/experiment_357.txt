# The Weight of Hands

Dr. Nadia Suwarno is a fifty-one-year-old Indonesian-born internist who has spent the better part of
two decades building a quiet, ordered life in her adopted country. She is the kind of physician her
patients describe as calm and thorough — someone who listens longer than she needs to, who remembers
the names of grandchildren. That steadiness, she will tell you now, feels like something she is
performing rather than something she possesses. It has felt that way for fourteen months.

Fourteen months ago, Nadia was walking to her car after a late evening shift at the hospital. The
parking structure was poorly lit, a fact she had noted before and never reported. A man she did not
recognize came from behind the concrete pillar near her car. What followed lasted perhaps four
minutes. He grabbed her by the throat, slammed her against the driver's side door, demanded her bag,
her keys, her phone. When she hesitated — not from bravery, she insists, but from simple shock — he
struck her twice across the face and shoved her to the ground. He took what he wanted and ran. A
security guard found her eleven minutes later, sitting on the oil-stained concrete, pressing her
white coat against her bleeding lip.

She filed a police report that night. She told her husband, Benny, that she was shaken but fine. She
returned to work in three days because her patients needed her and because returning felt, at the
time, like the correct thing to do.

The nights became the problem first. Nadia stops sleeping deeply and starts sleeping in fragments,
waking at two or three in the morning with her heart hammering and the absolute physical certainty
that there are hands around her throat. She lies still in the dark beside Benny and does not wake
him, because she cannot explain something that makes so little logical sense to her. She is a
physician. She understands the stress response. Understanding it does not stop it.

By the third month, the days begin to fracture as well. She is reviewing a patient's chart when the
overhead lights flicker, and suddenly she is not in the examination room — she is on the concrete,
and the chart in her hands means nothing. She excuses herself, stands in a bathroom stall, and waits
for the sensation to pass. These episodes happen two or three times a week. She has begun to
schedule fewer evening patients so she does not have to walk to her car after dark. Then she stops
parking in structures altogether, paying more to park on the street, closer to the hospital
entrance, where there are always people.

She finds herself watching. In every waiting room, every corridor, every restaurant where she and
Benny try to have a normal dinner, she tracks the exits and monitors the men near the door. She
knows this is exhausting. She cannot stop. Her concentration, once one of her great professional
strengths, now skips and stutters. She reads the same paragraph of a journal article four times and
retains nothing.

What troubles her most — more than the nightmares, more than the hypervigilance she can at least
name and categorize — is what has happened to her hands. She uses her hands to examine patients
every day: palpating abdomens, pressing gently on lymph nodes, resting a palm on a frightened
person's forearm. Since the assault, she sometimes feels, mid-examination, a sudden revulsion at
being touched in return. A patient reaching up to squeeze her hand in gratitude can send a cold wave
through her entire body. She smiles through it. She always smiles through it.

She has not told Benny how bad it has become. She has not told her colleagues. She chose medicine,
she explains quietly, because she wanted to be the person who holds things together. She is still
trying to be that person. She is just not sure, anymore, who is holding her together in return.
