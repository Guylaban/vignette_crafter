**Vignette: Mara, Age 19**

Mara grew up on the water. From the time she was small, she followed her uncle onto his fishing boat
before dawn, learning to read the currents and the color of the sky the way other children learned
to read books. By the time she was seventeen, she was working the boats herself — one of the few
young women in her coastal community to do so, and proud of it. The sea was not something she
feared. It was the place she felt most like herself.

That changed fourteen months ago.

It was an ordinary morning when it happened — the kind Mara had lived through hundreds of times. She
and two older crew members were hauling nets about three kilometers offshore when a vessel nearby
capsized in a sudden swell. What followed happened fast and did not happen fast at all, the way Mara
describes it. She watched a man she recognized, a father of four from the neighboring village,
struggle in the water. She watched the others try to reach him. She watched him go under. By the
time anyone pulled him to the surface, he was gone. Mara stood at the railing the entire time, rope
in her hands, unable to move. She has never fully forgiven herself for that stillness.

In the weeks after, she told herself she was fine. She went back to work because stopping felt like
surrender, and because her family depended on her income. But the boat became a different place. She
began scanning the water constantly, not for fish, but for shapes beneath the surface — things that
weren't there. The sound of a wave breaking against the hull could send her heart into her throat.
Some mornings she sat in her truck in the harbor parking lot for twenty minutes before she could
make herself walk to the dock. She never told anyone this.

At home, things were not easier. Mara shares a small house with her boyfriend, Tane, who is patient
and steady and who she describes as the best thing in her life. But she wakes most nights before
three in the morning, heart hammering, the image of the man's hands disappearing below the surface
replaying with a clarity that daylight never quite erases. She doesn't always wake Tane. She goes to
the kitchen and sits with a glass of water and waits for her body to remember where she is. Some
nights it takes a long time.

She has become quieter in ways people have noticed. Her mother has asked if something is wrong. Tane
has asked. Mara deflects, says she's tired, says the season has been hard. She is not lying,
exactly. She is just unable to explain that the tiredness lives somewhere deeper than sleep can
reach.

At work, she functions. She is competent and experienced beyond her years, and her crew relies on
her. But she has started avoiding the particular stretch of water where it happened, rerouting
without explanation, telling herself the fish are better elsewhere. When another crew member jokes
about rough seas or close calls — the ordinary dark humor of people who work in dangerous conditions
— Mara feels something tighten in her chest and has to look away. She has stopped joining the others
for meals at the end of long days. It is easier to drive home alone.

What troubles Mara most, she says quietly in her first session, is not the nightmares or the fear.
It is the feeling that she is watching her own life from a slight distance — that the girl who loved
the ocean without reservation is still out there somewhere on the water, and that the person sitting
in this chair is someone who only remembers being her.

She is nineteen years old. She is still trying to find her way back.
