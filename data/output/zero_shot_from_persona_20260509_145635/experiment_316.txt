# The Weight of Numbers

Boon-Nam is a 51-year-old Thai man who has worked for the national revenue department for nearly two
decades. He is precise by nature — meticulous with figures, careful with language, someone who built

occasion, and once thrown against a kitchen counter hard enough to fracture two ribs. He told his
colleagues he had fallen. He told himself the same thing for a long time. He ended the relationship
eleven years ago and, within two years, married his wife, a woman he describes with genuine warmth.
He has not spoken to anyone in his personal life about what happened during those six years.

What Boon-Nam notices now, in his fifties, is that the past does not stay in the past. He sits
across from taxpayers every day — auditing accounts, asking questions, watching people's faces when

his hands go still on the desk, and he finds himself calculating the distance to the door. He has
become known among colleagues as unusually calm under pressure, and he lets them believe that. What
they cannot see is that the calm is something he is holding in place with both hands.

At home, he sleeps poorly. He wakes between two and four in the morning with a physical alertness
that feels like danger, though his bedroom is quiet and his wife is beside him. He rarely tells her
he has woken. He lies still and waits for morning, reviewing tax codes in his head as a way of
steadying himself — numbers as anchor. He has stopped sleeping on his left side because that is the
side he was lying on during one of the worst nights, and his body has apparently filed that

He avoids certain parts of the city where he and his former partner used to go together. He has
