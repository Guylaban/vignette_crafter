**Vignette: Marcus, Age 48**

Marcus has owned the same hardware store for nearly two decades. He knows his regulars by name,
knows which bolts fit which brackets, knows the particular creak of the shop door that tells him
someone has walked in. The store has always been the steadiest thing in his life — predictable,
manageable, his. That steadiness began to fracture three years ago, on an otherwise unremarkable
Tuesday afternoon, when his seventeen-year-old son was struck by a distracted driver while cycling
home from school.

Marcus was behind the counter when his wife called. He remembers the exact sound of her voice — not
the words, but the register of them, something he had never heard from her before and has never been
able to unhear since. He drove to the hospital with no memory of the route. What he does remember is
arriving to find his son on a gurney, unconscious, his face barely recognizable beneath the swelling
and blood. His son survived, after two surgeries and a long rehabilitation, and is now largely
recovered. By most accounts, the story has a good ending. Marcus cannot find that ending from where
he stands.

In the weeks after the accident, he told himself the anxiety made sense. He stayed close to home,
called his son constantly, watched him sleep some nights from the doorway. His wife noticed but said
little, relieved, perhaps, to have someone else nearby who understood the fear. But the fear did not
ease as his son healed. It calcified into something permanent and structural, like a load-bearing
wall Marcus cannot see but cannot stop feeling.

At the store, he finds himself unable to concentrate the way he once could. Customers ask him
questions he should know the answers to and he stands there, momentarily blank, as if the words are
arriving from a great distance. He has begun leaving the shop to his part-time employee on days when
the anxiety rises too high, which means days are becoming unpredictable in a way that troubles him
deeply. He used to be the kind of man who prided himself on never missing a day.

The intrusions come without warning. He will be cutting a key or stacking shelves and suddenly he is
back in that hospital corridor — the smell of antiseptic, the fluorescent light, the particular way
his son's hand felt limp inside his own. His chest tightens and his vision narrows and for a moment
the hardware store ceases to exist. He does not tell his wife about these episodes. She has moved
forward in the way he cannot, and he does not want to burden her with the information that he is
still living, in some essential part of himself, in that corridor.

He avoids driving past the intersection where the accident happened, adding seven minutes to his
commute without ever discussing why. He has stopped watching the local news, which once played in
the background at dinner, because any story involving a vehicle or a hospital sends him into a state
of vigilance that lasts for hours. At night he sleeps lightly and poorly, waking at small sounds,
listening for his son's breathing even though his son is twenty now and away at college.

Marcus describes himself, when pressed, as "not a complainer." He was raised to work through things,
to keep moving. He is embarrassed by the degree to which he has not kept moving. His wife has
suggested he speak to someone and he has agreed, in principle, several times. He finally made an
appointment after a morning when he locked himself in the back office of the store for forty minutes
because a teenager on a bicycle rode past the front window and he could not stop shaking. He sat on
the floor among the inventory boxes and thought, with a clarity that frightened him: *something is
wrong with me that isn't going to fix itself.*

He arrived at his first session quiet, composed, and three years overdue.
