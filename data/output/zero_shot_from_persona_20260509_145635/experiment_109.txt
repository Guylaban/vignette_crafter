**Vignette: Maya**

Maya is a 22-year-old Filipino woman who works as a call center agent on the overnight shift at a
telecommunications company in Manila. She has been married for just under a year. To her colleagues,
she is known as the one who always answers the phone with a warm, steady voice — patient with
difficult callers, quick to laugh during breaks. What they do not know is that she spends most of
her commute home in the early morning hours sitting rigid on the bus, watching the faces of the men
around her, calculating the distance between herself and the nearest exit.

Fourteen months ago, Maya was sexually assaulted by an acquaintance after a gathering at a friend's
apartment. She had known him peripherally — a coworker of her then-boyfriend, now husband — and had
not thought to be afraid of him. The assault happened quickly, in a back room, while music played
loudly enough that she understood no one would hear her. She did not scream. She has spent
considerable time since then trying to understand why she did not scream, and has arrived at no
answer that satisfies her.

In the weeks that followed, Maya told no one. She married her husband as planned, two months later,
and tried to fold the event into something she could leave behind. She threw herself into her new
job, grateful for the structure of scripts and headsets, for the way the night shift kept her busy
during the hours when sleep would otherwise be expected of her. Sleep, by then, had become its own
problem. When she does manage it, she often wakes abruptly — heart pounding, sheets damp — with the
sensation that someone is holding her down. She lies still afterward, sometimes for an hour, waiting
for her body to believe it is safe.

Her husband knows something is wrong. She has not told him what. Physical intimacy between them has
become nearly impossible — not because she does not love him, she is clear about that, but because
certain touches, certain angles of light, certain smells trigger a lurch of nausea and a desperate
need to be anywhere else. She has become skilled at finding reasons to avoid these moments, and she
carries a quiet shame about this that she describes as feeling like a debt she cannot repay.

At work, Maya manages better than she expects to, most days. The anonymity of a headset helps. But
when a male caller becomes aggressive or raises his voice, something shifts in her body before her
mind catches up — her chest tightens, her vision narrows, and she finds herself reading the same
line of her script over and over without absorbing it. She has learned to put such callers briefly
on hold, to breathe, to return. Her supervisor has noticed she sometimes goes quiet mid-call and has
asked if she is alright. She always says yes.

She has stopped going to social gatherings almost entirely. The friend whose apartment it was — she
no longer answers her messages. Any space that resembles that night in texture or feeling: the low
lighting, the particular mix of voices and music, the smell of certain colognes — sends her into a
state of hypervigilance that can last for hours afterward. She describes it as her body making
decisions without consulting her.

What Maya finds hardest to articulate is the way she no longer fully trusts her own perceptions of
people. She had not been afraid of him. She had read the situation as safe. That failure of instinct
— or what she interprets as failure — has left her second-guessing nearly every interaction,
exhausted by the effort of constant reassessment. She is 22 years old, newly married, working
nights, and carrying something she has not yet found words to give to another person. She came to
therapy, she says, because she is tired. Not sleepy — though she is that too — but tired in a way
that goes much deeper than rest can reach.
