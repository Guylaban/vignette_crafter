# Vignette: Fatima, Age 77

Fatima was fifty-three years old when the shelling began in earnest. She had lived in her city her
entire life — had raised her children there, buried her mother there, built a small but steady life
tutoring secondary school students in mathematics and Arabic literature. She knew every sound the
neighborhood made: the call to prayer, the vendor carts, the particular way wind moved through the
alley beside her building. Then those sounds were replaced by others she could not unhear.

Over the course of eighteen months, she witnessed things that no framework she had — religious,
cultural, or personal — could fully absorb. She saw a young man she had tutored since he was twelve
years old die in the street below her window. She spent eleven days in her building's basement with
neighbors, rationing water, listening to the structure above them take damage. When she finally
fled, she carried almost nothing. She did not say goodbye to her apartment, her books, or the graves
of her parents. She simply left, because staying meant dying.

She eventually settled abroad, where two of her adult children had relocated years earlier. She is
now seventy-seven, living in a small flat near her daughter's family. On paper, she is safe. Inside,
she does not feel safe, and she hasn't for a very long time.

Fatima still works — she tutors students in Arabic language and mathematics, mostly children of
immigrant families in the area. She finds the work grounding in the mornings, when her mind is
quietest. But by afternoon, something shifts. The effort of concentrating, of holding space for a
child's confusion over fractions or verb conjugations, begins to cost her more than it once did. She
sometimes loses the thread of what she is explaining mid-sentence, pulled suddenly into a memory she
cannot name quickly enough to dismiss — rubble, a particular smell, a color of smoke.

She sleeps poorly. She wakes between two and four in the morning almost every night, heart already
racing before she is fully conscious. She lies still and tells herself she is in her daughter's
city, in her small flat, and that the walls are not going to fall. Some nights this works. Other
nights she gets up and sits at her kitchen table until dawn, drinking tea she doesn't taste,
watching the street below for nothing in particular.

Loud or sudden noises — a car backfiring, a door slammed in the hallway — produce a physical
response she cannot suppress: her body drops before her mind catches up. She is embarrassed by this,
particularly in front of her grandchildren, who sometimes startle her by accident and then look
frightened themselves by her reaction. She has begun to avoid situations where noise is
unpredictable — markets, family gatherings that grow too loud, the school where one of her
grandchildren performed in a concert last spring. She stood in the back and left before it was over.

She does not speak much about what she experienced. When her daughter asks, Fatima redirects the
conversation or makes a quiet joke about old women and bad memories. She carries a particular
conviction, not quite conscious, that describing those years would make them real in a new way —
more contagious, somehow. She worries about burdening her children, who have their own griefs from
that time.

What she does speak about, in the careful way she has, is a feeling of distance — from the present
moment, from her own pleasure, from the person she was before. She mentions that she used to love
poetry, that she once read Mahmoud Darwish aloud to her students with something she can only
describe as joy. Now the books sit on her shelf and she does not open them. She is not sure, she
says quietly, whether that person still exists, or whether she left her behind in the basement, in
the rubble, in the city she could not say goodbye to.
