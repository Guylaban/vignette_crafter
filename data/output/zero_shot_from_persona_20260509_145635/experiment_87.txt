# The Weight of the Badge

Maya is a 28-year-old police officer who has worn her uniform for five years with something close to
pride. She grew up in a small town in the Czech Republic, the daughter of a firefighter, and came to
believe early that public service was a kind of calling. She moved to the UK at twenty-two, joined
the force at twenty-three, and for the first two years she felt she had found exactly the life she
was meant to live.

Eighteen months ago, that life fractured.

During a crowd control operation at a protest in the city centre, Maya witnessed a senior colleague
beat a restrained man with a baton — repeatedly, methodically, long after any threat had passed. She
was standing close enough to hear the sounds. She did not intervene. She has not forgiven herself
for that stillness, and she replays those seconds constantly, editing them, imagining the version of
herself who stepped forward and did not freeze. That version feels like a stranger. The man was
hospitalised. Her colleague faced an internal review, was quietly reassigned, and continues to work.
Maya gave a witness statement. Nothing came of it.

She returned to duty two weeks later because she did not know what else to do with herself. Almost
immediately, she noticed that something had shifted in the way she moved through the world. Routine
calls that once felt manageable — a domestic dispute, a crowd outside a pub — now trigger a flooding
sensation in her chest that she can only describe as her body trying to leave the room without her.
She has learned to keep her face neutral while this happens. She is very good at keeping her face
neutral.

At night, she does not sleep so much as wait. When sleep does come, it brings the same narrow
corridor of images: the sound of the baton, the man's voice, her own feet planted on the pavement.
She wakes with her jaw clenched and her heart already running. Her partner, Daniel, has started
sleeping in the spare room on the nights she won't settle, and she has told him this is fine, that
she prefers the space. She does not prefer it. She is simply unable to explain what is happening to
her without feeling that she is accusing herself.

She has become careful about which streets she walks down off-duty, which news stories she lets load
on her phone. She no longer watches anything on television that involves crowds or uniforms. These
feel like reasonable precautions to her, not avoidances — a distinction she holds tightly. At work,
she has requested desk assignments where possible, citing administrative backlog, and her sergeant
has accommodated her without asking questions. She is grateful for this and ashamed of it in equal
measure.

What troubles Maya most is not the fear itself but the way it has curdled into something she cannot
name cleanly. She no longer trusts the institution she joined. She no longer entirely trusts
herself. She finds herself watching her colleagues differently — assessing them, cataloguing small
moments of roughness or impatience — and then feeling sick at her own vigilance, as though suspicion
is a betrayal of the people she works alongside. Some days she believes she is the problem. Other
days she believes the problem is much larger than her, and that belief is somehow worse.

She came to therapy, she says, because Daniel asked her to, and she wanted to give him something.
She sits with her hands folded in her lap and speaks carefully and precisely, the way she was
trained to give statements. She is articulate about the facts of what happened. When asked how she
felt in the moment, she pauses for a long time and then says, *small*. She says it like it surprises
her — like she expected a different word to come out, something harder and more defensible than
that.
