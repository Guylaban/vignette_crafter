# The Weight of an Empty Studio

Kwame is a fifty-five-year-old Ghanaian-born painter who has lived in the same modest house in the
northeast for nearly two decades. His wife, Adwoa, describes him as a man who used to hum while he
worked — low, rhythmic tones that drifted out of his studio and into the kitchen where she cooked.
She has not heard him hum in fourteen months.

It began on an ordinary Tuesday. Kwame's younger brother, Kofi, called to say he was coming by the
studio that afternoon to see the new canvases. He never arrived. A driver ran a red light at the
intersection three blocks away, and Kofi was killed on impact. He was forty-eight years old. Kwame
learned of his death not from a phone call but from a police officer standing at his front door,
still holding his brushes, his hands smeared with cadmium yellow.

In the days that followed, Kwame moved through the rituals of grief — the phone calls to family in
Accra, the arrangements, the funeral — with what his wife describes as an eerie calm. He did not cry
at the burial. He stood very still and stared at the casket as though he was trying to memorize it.
When the family returned home, he went directly to his studio and locked the door. Adwoa found him
there the next morning, sitting in the dark, the canvases Kofi had wanted to see still facing the
wall.

Now, more than a year later, Kwame rarely paints. He will sometimes stand in the studio for an hour,
brush in hand, and then set it down and leave without making a mark. The smell of linseed oil, which
he once loved, has become almost intolerable to him. It was the smell in the air the afternoon Kofi
was supposed to come. His mind returns to that moment compulsively — not to the death itself, which
he did not witness, but to the ordinary waiting. The way the afternoon light fell across the
unfinished canvas. The sound of a car he thought might be Kofi's. He replays the sequence and tries,
irrationally, to find the place where he could have changed it.

He sleeps in fragments. When he does sleep, he dreams of Kofi standing just outside a door that
Kwame cannot open, or of receiving the phone call that never came — the one where his brother would
have said he was running late. He wakes most nights between two and three in the morning and lies
rigid beside Adwoa, listening to the house. He has begun checking the locks three and four times
before bed. He cannot explain why, only that the world now feels like a place where things simply
stop without warning.

He has withdrawn from the community of artists he once gathered with on Friday evenings. When
friends invite him out, he declines, citing work, though he is barely working. He has stopped
attending his church, where he and Kofi used to sit together in the third pew from the front. He
tells Adwoa he is fine, and she does not argue, but she watches him the way one watches someone
standing too close to an edge.

What troubles Kwame most, in the quiet moments he will admit to, is the guilt that has settled into
him like a stain he cannot identify the source of. He had been the one to suggest Tuesday. He had
been the one who wanted Kofi to see the new work. He turns this over and over, not as a thought
exactly, but as a physical sensation — a heaviness across his chest that is there when he wakes and
there when he finally, fitfully, sleeps.

His studio is still full of light in the afternoons. The canvases remain facing the wall.
