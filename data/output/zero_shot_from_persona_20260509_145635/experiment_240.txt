# Adaeze

Adaeze is twenty-three years old, recently hired as an environmental scientist at a regional
conservation agency, a job she worked toward since she was a child collecting water samples from the
river near her family's home in Nigeria. She arrived in the country eighteen months ago, full of
ambition, carrying her mother's pride like something fragile and precious in her chest. Her younger
brother, Chukwuemeka — Emeka, she always called him — had been her closest companion across the
distance. They spoke every evening. She was saving money to bring him to visit.

Eight months ago, Emeka died. He was nineteen. A road accident on a market road, the kind that
happens without warning and without reason. A lorry. A moment. Someone called her at six in the
morning and she remembers standing in her kitchen in her socks, the phone against her ear, and then
she was on the floor, though she cannot account for how she got there. She did not make it home in
time for the burial.

That detail — not making it home in time — has become a kind of wound inside the wound. She replays
it constantly, a loop she cannot interrupt: the flight she almost booked, the extra shift she had
worked that week, the twenty-three hours of travel that would have still been too late. She tells
herself she should have known. She does not know what she should have known, only that she feels
certain she failed him.

In the months since, Adaeze has continued to go to work. She is meticulous and capable, and her
colleagues describe her as quiet but dedicated. What they do not see is that she spends long
stretches in the field unable to look at the river without her throat closing. Water was something
she and Emeka shared — his letters were full of her descriptions of her work, and she had been
teaching him the names of things. Now the river feels like a place that belongs to a version of her
life that no longer exists. She completes her measurements. She writes her reports. She does not
tell anyone why she sometimes sits in her car for twenty minutes before she can go inside.

She sleeps poorly, waking most nights between two and four in the morning with her heart already
racing, sometimes from a dream she cannot hold onto and sometimes from nothing she can name. She
lies there running through the timeline of that day — what she was doing when the accident happened,
whether she felt anything, whether she should have. She is exhausted in a way that sleep does not
fix.

She has withdrawn from almost everyone. She attends the small Nigerian church near her apartment
because she feels she owes it to her mother, but she sits near the back and leaves quickly. A
colleague invited her to a birthday gathering last month, and Adaeze agreed and then sat outside in
the car park for the entire hour, unable to open the door. She told the colleague she had come and
gone early. The lie felt easier than the truth, and the truth is that she does not entirely know
what the truth is — only that being around people who are happy feels like standing on the wrong
side of a glass wall.

She carries guilt that is specific and relentless. She was the one who encouraged Emeka to be
independent, to travel, to take the market road instead of waiting for a safer ride. She does not
know if this is accurate. She knows it feels absolutely true. She finds herself bargaining with
versions of the past in quiet moments — at her desk, in the field, in the shower — as though
repetition might eventually produce a different ending.

Adaeze does not describe herself as traumatized. She describes herself as someone who has not yet
figured out how to keep living at the same speed as everyone else.
