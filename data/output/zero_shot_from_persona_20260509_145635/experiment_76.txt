# The Weight of What He Saw

Miguel is twenty-two years old, the youngest product manager at a mid-sized tech startup in Austin,
Texas. His colleagues know him as sharp, driven, and unusually composed for someone his age. What
they do not know is that he spent three weeks two years ago trapped in his grandmother's house in a
northern region of Venezuela while armed conflict between government forces and paramilitary groups
tore through the neighborhood around him.

He had gone back to visit family during a long holiday weekend — a trip he had made before without
incident. Within days of his arrival, the streets outside became impassable. He remembers the
specific afternoon it started: the sound of automatic gunfire so close it rattled the windowpanes,
his grandmother pulling him to the floor of the kitchen, the smell of her hair and the tile cold
against his cheek. Over the following weeks, he watched a neighbor's house burn. He saw a man he did
not recognize lying motionless in the road for two full days before anyone came for him. He heard
things at night he still cannot name. When he finally made it to the airport and boarded a plane
back to the United States, he told himself it was over.

It was not over.

For the first several months after returning, Miguel threw himself into work with an intensity that
his supervisors mistook for ambition. He was the first one in, the last to leave, filling every
quiet moment with tasks and Slack messages and roadmap spreadsheets. Stillness, he discovered, was
dangerous. When he sat still, the sounds came back — not as memories exactly, but as physical
sensations, a tightening in his chest, a sudden certainty that something terrible was about to
happen. He stopped sleeping in his bedroom and began falling asleep on his couch with the television
on, needing the noise to hold the silence at bay.

Now, two years later, the coping strategies that once worked are fraying. He lives in a one-bedroom
apartment alone and keeps the blinds drawn most evenings. He has not been back to visit his family,
and he deflects his mother's questions about when he will return with vague talk about deadlines and
product launches. He avoids the news entirely — not just Venezuelan news, but all of it. A coworker
played a video clip of a protest in the break room last month and Miguel left the room so abruptly
that he had to spend ten minutes in the bathroom talking himself back to baseline before returning
to his desk.

He is not sleeping well. He wakes at two or three in the morning, heart already racing, reaching for
something to orient himself. He rarely remembers the dreams, only the feeling they leave behind — a
residue of dread that follows him into the morning. He has become irritable in ways that embarrass
him, snapping at colleagues during sprint reviews over small things, then spending the rest of the
day quietly ashamed of himself. He is aware that something is wrong. He is not sure he deserves to
call it that.

This last part is perhaps what weighs on him most. He is alive. He is employed. He has a future laid
out in front of him in a country where he is safe. He was not a soldier. He was not a refugee. He
was a visitor who got caught in something terrible and then got out. He tells himself this often, as
though repetition might dissolve what he carries. It does not. He came to his first therapy session
wearing a collared shirt, speaking carefully and precisely, the way he might present a product
roadmap. But when the therapist asked him what it had been like to finally board that plane out of
Venezuela, he went quiet for a long moment, looked down at his hands, and said, very softly, that he
still does not quite believe he made it.
