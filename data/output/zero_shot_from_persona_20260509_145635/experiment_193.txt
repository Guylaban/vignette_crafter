# The Weight of an Ordinary Tuesday

Margaret is a 68-year-old retired schoolteacher who spent thirty-one years shaping the minds of
fourth-graders in a small Ohio town. She describes her life before as "full" — a word she uses
carefully now, aware of how hollow everything has felt since the morning two years ago when her son
David called from the hospital to tell her that her daughter, Claire, had been killed by a drunk
driver on her way to work. Claire was forty-one. It was a Tuesday in March, unremarkable in every
way until it wasn't.

Margaret had spoken to Claire the evening before. They had talked about a recipe. She returns to
this detail often — the recipe, the ordinariness of it, the way she had said *I love you* without
knowing she was saying it for the last time. She wonders now whether some part of her knew, and she
tortures herself with the idea that she should have said more.

The weeks after Claire's death are largely lost to her. She remembers the funeral home, the smell of
lilies she now cannot tolerate, and her husband Gerald's hand in hers. What she does not remember is
eating, sleeping, or how she moved through those days at all. She describes it as watching herself
from a great distance, a woman going through motions that belonged to someone else's grief.

In the months that followed, the distance collapsed. The grief became physical — a pressure in her
chest she wakes with each morning, a body that feels older than its years. She sleeps fitfully,
rarely more than three or four hours before something jolts her awake: a sound, a shadow, the sudden
and complete certainty that something else terrible is about to happen. She lies in the dark
cataloguing the people she loves, running through them like a rosary, checking whether they are
still alive.

Margaret no longer drives on the highway. She tells herself it is a preference, but she knows it is
fear — not for herself exactly, but a visceral, nauseating dread that rises in her throat when she
sees a merge ramp or a semi-truck in an adjacent lane. She takes back roads everywhere, adding
twenty minutes to trips she used to make without thinking.

She had been looking forward to retirement. She and Gerald had talked for years about traveling,
about mornings without alarm clocks, about the grandchildren. Now she finds the days shapeless and
threatening. She moves through the house she has lived in for thirty-five years feeling like a guest
in it. She avoids Claire's old bedroom, where some of her things are still stored, and she has not
opened the box of photographs David brought over after the estate was settled. The box sits in the
hall closet. She walks past it several times a day.

What troubles her most, she says, is who she has become to the people she loves. Gerald is patient
with her in a way that breaks her heart — careful, gentle, watching her with an expression she
recognizes as worry. She snaps at him sometimes and hates herself for it. She has pulled back from
her book club, from her neighbor Ruth, from her grandchildren's school events. Being around people
requires a performance of normalcy she no longer has the energy to sustain. She says she doesn't
want to contaminate anyone with what she carries.

There are moments, she admits, when she wonders whether she will feel this way for the rest of her
life. She is not suicidal — she is clear about that — but she is tired in a way that sleep does not
touch. She misses Claire with an intensity that has not diminished with time, only changed shape.
She misses the woman she was before that Tuesday in March. She is not sure, she says quietly, that
woman is coming back.
