**Case Vignette: Marcus, Age 36**

Marcus is a 36-year-old man of Polish descent who has been working at a large home improvement
warehouse on the outskirts of the city for the past eight years. He is married to his wife,
Agnieszka, and they have two young children together. By most outward measures, Marcus was a steady,
reliable man before the night everything changed — the kind of person his supervisor trusted to
close up the store, the kind of husband who came home and helped with bath time without being asked.

Three years ago, Marcus was working a closing shift when a catastrophic structural failure occurred
in the warehouse's storage annex. A poorly maintained industrial shelving system — later found to be
years overdue for inspection — gave way under the weight of stacked inventory. The collapse was
sudden and violent. Marcus was in the adjacent aisle when it happened. A coworker, a young man named
Darek who had started only months earlier, was buried beneath the falling racks. Marcus heard him
calling out. He tried to reach him, pulling at metal and debris with his bare hands until other
staff pulled him back. Darek survived but sustained severe spinal injuries. Marcus walked away with
lacerations and a fractured wrist. Physically, he healed within weeks.

Everything else has taken much longer.

In the months that followed, Marcus returned to work at the insistence of his manager, who assured
him the shelving had been replaced and the store was safe. He lasted six weeks before he stopped
being able to walk down certain aisles. The sound of something heavy shifting on a shelf — a bag of
cement settling, a pallet being moved by a forklift — would send his heart slamming into his chest.
He would freeze mid-step, convinced the ceiling was about to come down. He transferred to a smaller
retail outlet across town, a garden center, where the ceilings are lower and the inventory lighter.
He tells himself this is practical. He does not fully admit that he cannot be in large enclosed
spaces anymore without watching the walls.

At home, Marcus is present in body but often somewhere else entirely. Agnieszka has learned not to
approach him from behind without speaking first. He startles violently, then feels ashamed of it. He
sleeps badly — most nights he is awake by three in the morning, lying rigid in the dark, replaying
the sound of the shelving giving way, the metallic scream of it, Darek's voice underneath. Some
nights he dreams of not reaching him in time. He wakes from these dreams convinced, for a few
disorienting seconds, that Darek is dead.

Marcus has stopped watching the news. Stories about building collapses, industrial accidents,
anything involving structural failure — he cannot tolerate them. He scrolls past quickly, jaw tight,
and feels a low-grade nausea for the rest of the evening. He has quietly declined two family
gatherings held at a large event hall, offering excuses about the children's schedules. Agnieszka
knows the real reason. They do not talk about it directly.

What Marcus finds hardest to explain — even to himself — is the guilt that sits underneath
everything. He was not the one responsible for the shelving. He knows this. But he also knows he was
the one who walked away, and Darek was the one who did not walk the same way again. Marcus goes
through the shift in his mind regularly, recalculating. If he had been in that aisle. If he had
checked on Darek earlier. If he had been faster pulling at the debris. The logic of these thoughts
does not hold up, but logic has very little authority over them.

He describes himself, with a kind of flat resignation, as someone who used to be easygoing. His
coworkers at the garden center find him quiet and a little distant. He is competent, punctual, and
polite. He does not tell them why he chose somewhere with no industrial shelving.
