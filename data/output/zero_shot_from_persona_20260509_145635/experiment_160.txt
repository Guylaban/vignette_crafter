# The Weight of Being Watched

Marcus is a fifty-year-old Filipino guitarist who has spent most of his adult life performing in
jazz clubs and recording sessions across the city. Music has always been the organizing principle of
his world — the thing that made sense when little else did. He describes his pre-trauma self as
someone who loved lingering after gigs, talking to strangers, staying until the last chair was
stacked. That version of himself feels very far away now.

Three years ago, a woman he had briefly dated began appearing at his performances with a regularity
that first flattered him, then unsettled him, and finally terrified him. Over the course of eight
months, she sent hundreds of messages, left notes on his car, showed up at his apartment building,
and contacted his bandmates, his booking agent, and his ex-wife. When Marcus tried to establish
distance, the contact escalated. One evening she followed him from a venue on foot for six city
blocks. He ducked into a convenience store, hands trembling, watching through the glass door as she
stood on the pavement outside, waiting. He remembers thinking, with strange clarity, that he could
not remember the last time he had felt safe.

A restraining order eventually brought the physical contact to an end, but Marcus will tell you —
quietly, without drama — that the stalking did not really stop inside him. He still scans every room
he enters before he can settle into it. At gigs, he positions himself on stage so that he can see
the entrance at all times, and he has left performances mid-set when a face in the crowd seemed too
familiar or a woman stood too long near the same spot. His bandmates have learned not to comment on
this. He has lost two steady engagements because venue owners grew frustrated with his
unpredictability.

Sleep is a negotiation Marcus rarely wins. He wakes most nights between two and four in the morning
with his heart already pounding, certain he has heard something — a footstep in the hallway, a knock
that may not have happened. He lies still and listens until his body convinces itself the apartment
is secure, a process that can take an hour. He is exhausted in a way that rest does not touch.

His divorce, which was finalized eighteen months before the stalking began, had already left him
living alone in a one-bedroom apartment in a neighborhood he chose partly for its anonymity. That
solitude, once manageable, has since curdled into something heavier. He does not date. He has
difficulty explaining to people why he cannot simply relax, why a text from an unknown number can
ruin an entire afternoon, why he checks his deadbolt three times before bed. He has stopped trying
to explain. Most of his close friendships have thinned through his withdrawal.

What troubles Marcus most, he says, is what has happened to his relationship with music itself. He
used to compose late at night, when the city quieted and his mind opened. Now the silence feels
threatening rather than creative. He sits at his guitar and finds himself listening past it instead
of through it. There are evenings when he cannot play at all — when the instrument in his hands
feels like a prop from someone else's life. He grieves this loss with a specificity that is almost
harder to witness than his fear. The hypervigilance he can explain as a reasonable response to
something real. The silence where the music used to live, he cannot.

He came to therapy at the suggestion of a fellow musician who noticed that Marcus had begun
declining session work — studio recordings where he once thrived — because he could not tolerate not
knowing who else would be in the room. He arrived punctual, polite, and visibly alert, taking the
chair nearest the door without appearing to realize he had chosen it.
