**Clinical Vignette: Marcus, 32**

Marcus is a 32-year-old man of Polish descent who works as a private tutor, primarily teaching
mathematics and sciences to secondary school students from his home study. He is married to his wife
of four years, Joanna, and by most outward appearances, their life together is quiet and organized.
But beneath that surface, Marcus carries something that has fundamentally altered the way he moves
through the world.

Three years ago, a former tutoring client—a young woman in her early twenties whom he had worked
with briefly at a learning centre—began contacting him outside of professional channels. At first,
the messages seemed like misplaced gratitude. Then they became more frequent, more personal, and
eventually threatening. She began appearing outside his home. She sent packages to his address. She
contacted Joanna, his colleagues, and his students' parents with fabricated stories about him. For
nearly eight months, Marcus lived in a state of sustained, unpredictable threat, never certain where
she would appear next or what she would do. Despite a restraining order eventually being granted,
the sense of danger did not simply dissolve when the legal process concluded. The woman moved away,
but Marcus did not.

In the months that followed, he found himself unable to sit in his home study with the blinds open.
He began checking the locks on the front door multiple times each evening—sometimes five, sometimes
twelve. He installed additional cameras around the property, which he monitors from his phone
throughout the day, often mid-session with students. He tells himself this is reasonable. He is not
entirely convinced.

The intrusions are perhaps the most exhausting part. Without warning, he will be explaining a
quadratic equation to a teenager and suddenly find himself back at the moment he opened his front
door to find her standing on his step, smiling. His heart rate spikes. His hands go cold. He has
learned to pause, take a breath, and continue—his students rarely notice—but internally, the
disruption is total.

He sleeps poorly. He wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, certain he has heard
something outside. He lies still, listening, sometimes for an hour before he accepts that the house
is quiet. Joanna has stopped trying to reassure him in those moments; she has learned that her
voice, however kind, does not reach him when he is in that state.

What troubles Marcus most, in the quieter moments when he can reflect, is how much smaller his life
has become. He used to travel across the city to work with students in person. Now he teaches almost
exclusively online or from home, and he has constructed elaborate justifications for
this—efficiency, convenience, the environment—that he repeats to family and friends. He knows they
are justifications. He avoids crowded places, delays grocery runs until late at night when the shops
are nearly empty, and has quietly withdrawn from the social circle he and Joanna once shared. He
tells her he is fine. He says it often enough that he sometimes wonders if he is trying to convince
her or himself.

There is a particular kind of shame Marcus carries that he has not yet found words for fully. He is
a man, he says—though he does not finish the sentence. The implication hangs there. He waited longer
than he should have before telling anyone what was happening, worried about how it would sound,
worried about being disbelieved. Some people, when he has told them, have responded with a kind of
bemused curiosity that felt worse than silence. He has largely stopped telling people.

He came to therapy, he says, because Joanna asked him to. But in the second session, he admitted
quietly that he had been searching for a therapist for six months before she said anything. He just
hadn't been able to make the call.
