# The Weight of What She Saw

Nadia is eighteen years old, recently graduated from a specialized secondary program in
architectural design, and already enrolled in a prestigious university program where she spends her
days drawing structures meant to stand for centuries. She is precise, gifted, and quietly exhausted
in a way that most of her professors mistake for intensity.

Two years ago, Nadia was walking home from school through a busy market district in her city when a
car mounted the pavement without warning and struck a group of people gathered near a fruit stall.
She was close enough to feel the displaced air. She watched a man thrown forward, watched a woman
fold to the ground, watched a child who had been standing one moment simply not be standing the
next. Nadia did not run. She stood frozen at the edge of the chaos for what felt like an eternity —
people screaming, someone calling her name from somewhere she couldn't locate, blood spreading
across the pale stone pavement in a shape she still cannot unsee. She was physically unharmed. That
fact has haunted her ever since.

In the weeks that followed, Nadia told herself she was fine. She went to school. She drew her plans.
She told her mother it had been frightening but that she was managing. What she did not say was that
she had stopped sleeping more than two or three hours at a time, that she woke most nights with her
heart slamming against her ribs, certain she was back in that street, certain the sound she had just
heard in her sleep was real. She did not say that she had begun taking long, elaborate detours to
avoid not just that market but any crowded outdoor space — any place where people gathered near the
edge of a road.

Now, at eighteen, Nadia carries the trauma into every part of her life in ways she is only beginning
to name. In her studio, she is drawn obsessively to questions of structural safety — exits, load-
bearing walls, the distance between a person and a point of impact. Her classmates admire what they
see as a sophisticated preoccupation with human-centered design. Nadia knows it is something else.
She is trying, in blueprint after blueprint, to build a world where what she saw cannot happen.

She startles badly at sudden sounds — a chair scraping, a door slamming — and then feels a hot flush
of shame at her own reaction. She has difficulty concentrating during lectures, not because she
lacks intelligence but because some part of her mind never fully leaves the market. She finds
herself scanning rooms for exits, watching windows, tracking the movement of anyone who enters a
space. Crowds produce a particular dread that rises in her chest like water, and she has quietly
declined several social invitations from classmates, offering excuses that are easier than the
truth.

What troubles Nadia most is a feeling she struggles to articulate: she no longer quite believes the
world is the kind of place she once thought it was. Before that afternoon, she had understood danger
as something abstract, something that happened elsewhere. Now she understands it as random and
sudden and entirely indifferent to who is standing in its path. This knowledge sits inside her like
a stone. She finds it difficult to feel genuinely happy, even in moments that used to come easily —
sitting with her sketchbook, drinking tea with her mother, watching the city from a high window. She
feels at a distance from her own life, as though she is watching herself move through it from
somewhere slightly removed.

She has not spoken to anyone about the full weight of what she carries. She worries that because she
was not physically hurt, her suffering will seem disproportionate — that someone will point out,
gently or not, that she walked away. She walked away. She knows this. She cannot explain why that
feels less like relief and more like its own kind of wound.
