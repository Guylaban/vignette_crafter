# The Weight of What She Carried Home

Amara is twenty-five years old, a pediatric nurse who moves through the ward with quiet competence,
her hands steady even when the rest of her is not. She came to this country three years ago from a
region of West Africa where, for nearly eight months, the town she grew up in became a battlefield.
She does not talk about that time easily, and when she does, she tends to look slightly past the
person she is speaking to, as though the words belong to somewhere else.

She was seventeen when the fighting first reached her neighborhood — distant at first, a rumble she
and her mother tried to explain away as storms. Then it wasn't distant anymore. For the better part
of a year, Amara lived with the sound of gunfire as a kind of weather, unpredictable and total. She
saw things she has no clean language for: a neighbor's house reduced to rubble overnight, children
she had known since infancy displaced and hungry, a morning when she stepped outside and understood,
from the smell alone, that something irreversible had happened nearby. She helped her mother tend to
wounded people in their home because there was nowhere else for them to go. She was not yet
eighteen. She had wanted to be a nurse since she was small, but she had not wanted to learn this
way.

She eventually left with an aunt, a journey she describes as "just moving, just walking, just
getting through." She does not elaborate. She arrived in Europe, then later here, and she built
something — nursing school, certifications, a small apartment, a relationship with a man she
describes as kind and patient. From the outside, her life looks like a success story. She knows
this. It makes her feel, she says, like a fraud.

The nights are the hardest part. She sleeps lightly and poorly, waking at sounds that turn out to be
nothing — a car door, a neighbor's television, the building settling. When she does sleep deeply
enough to dream, she is often back there, and the dreams are not symbolic or distorted; they are
simply replays, faithful and merciless. She wakes from them with her heart already running.

At work, she is skilled and well-regarded, but certain moments reach through her professional
composure and grab something older. A child brought in after a car accident, blood on his forehead,
will sometimes stop her mid-motion — not for long, just a second or two, but long enough that she
has to breathe through it, recalibrate, remind herself where she is. The smell of certain
antiseptics, the sound of a monitor alarm, the particular quality of fluorescent light in a trauma
bay — any of these can drop her briefly into a state she struggles to name. She calls it "going far
away."

She has become selective about what she lets herself feel. Her boyfriend notices that she is
sometimes unreachable even when she is sitting right beside him. She does not like crowded spaces
and has quietly reorganized her life to avoid them. She does not watch the news. She does not go to
certain parts of the city where there are loud weekend markets because the noise accumulates in a
way that becomes unbearable. She is not reckless or self-destructive; she is, if anything, overly
controlled, as though she is managing something that could spill.

What she says she wants most is to stop bracing. She describes her body as always slightly clenched,
always half-expecting something. She has carried this tension so long that she is no longer sure
what it would feel like to be without it. She chose nursing, she says, because she wanted to help
people survive things. She is only beginning to consider that she might need to apply some of that
care to herself.
