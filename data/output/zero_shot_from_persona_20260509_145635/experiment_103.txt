# Vignette: Rohan

Rohan is a 39-year-old South Asian man who works as an interior designer, a career he built quietly
and deliberately over fifteen years. He is married, soft-spoken, and precise in the way he describes
things — a habit, he says, that comes from spending his days translating what people feel into what
they can see and touch. He came to therapy three months ago, at his wife's gentle insistence, after
she told him she felt like she was living with a stranger.

Eighteen months ago, Rohan's younger brother Vikram died suddenly of a cardiac event at thirty-four.
There had been no warning. Vikram had been healthy, or so everyone believed. On a Tuesday morning in
November, Vikram collapsed while jogging near his apartment. By the time the paramedics arrived, he
was gone. Rohan received the call from his sister-in-law while he was standing in a client's half-
finished kitchen, holding a tile sample up to the light. He remembers the specific color of that
tile — a warm terracotta — and now finds it almost impossible to specify earth tones in his work
without his hands going cold.

In the weeks following Vikram's death, Rohan moved through the rituals of grief that his family
expected of him. He organized, he coordinated, he stood at the center of a mourning household and
held it together. He did not cry at the funeral. He told himself this was strength. He told himself
he would process it later, when things quieted down. Things quieted down. The processing never came.

What came instead were the nights. Rohan began waking at two or three in the morning with his heart
pounding, convinced for a disorienting moment that something catastrophic had just happened — or was
about to. He would lie in the dark running through a mental checklist: his wife's breathing, his
parents' health, his own chest. He started monitoring his heart rate obsessively, pressing two
fingers to his wrist at his desk, in the car, in client meetings. He downloaded three different
health apps and deleted them all, then downloaded them again.

During the day, he functions — but only just. He describes a persistent sense of flatness, as though
a pane of glass has been installed between him and everything he used to care about. Work, which
once gave him genuine pleasure, now feels mechanical. He moves through showrooms and site visits
with professional competence, but the joy of it — the way a well-lit room used to feel like a small
act of creation — is simply absent. He catches himself wondering, mid-presentation, what any of it
matters.

He avoids calling his parents as often as he used to, because their voices remind him of Vikram, and
because he is afraid of what he might hear in them — some new emergency, some fresh loss. He has
stopped going to the gym where he and Vikram occasionally worked out together. He has not told his
wife why.

What troubles Rohan most, and what he finds most difficult to say aloud, is the guilt. He replays
the last time he saw Vikram — a Diwali gathering six weeks before his death — and searches the
memory for signs he should have caught. He wonders whether Vikram said something he didn't listen to
carefully enough. He knows, intellectually, that there was nothing to catch. He cannot make himself
believe it.

His wife describes him as irritable in a way he never used to be — short with her over small things,
then distant and apologetic afterward. Rohan agrees this is true. He says he feels as though he is
perpetually braced for something, though he cannot say exactly what. He is not sure he remembers
what it felt like to simply be in a room without waiting for it to change.
