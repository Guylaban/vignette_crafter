# The Weight of What He Carried Home

Marcus is a 36-year-old tax consultant who lives with his wife, Dana, in a quiet suburb outside of
Columbus, Ohio. From the outside, his life looks composed — a tidy house, a steady career, a man who
wears pressed shirts to client meetings and speaks with careful precision about depreciation
schedules and capital gains. But inside, Marcus is living in two places at once: the present, and a
road in Kandahar that he cannot leave behind.

He served two tours in Afghanistan in his mid-twenties, working as part of a convoy security unit.
On his second deployment, the vehicle ahead of his was struck by an IED. Marcus watched it happen in
real time — the eruption of earth and metal, the silence that followed, and then the sounds he still
cannot name without his jaw tightening. Three men from his unit died that afternoon. Marcus pulled
one of them — a 22-year-old from Georgia named Darnell — from the wreckage. Darnell did not survive.
Marcus held him anyway.

He came home eight months later, separated from the Army, and tried to step back into ordinary life.
He enrolled in school on the GI Bill, met Dana, built something that resembled stability. For a
while, the strategy of keeping busy seemed to work. Tax season, in particular, became a kind of
refuge — the long hours, the numerical order of it all, the way a spreadsheet never asks anything
unexpected of you. Marcus threw himself into the work with an intensity his colleagues admired,
though none of them fully understood it.

But the nights were different. Sleep became something Marcus approached with dread. He would lie
awake for hours, and when he finally did sleep, he would wake up mid-dream, heart hammering, certain
he could smell burning. Dana learned early in their marriage not to touch him while he slept. Once,
she reached for him in the dark and he was across the room before he was fully conscious, pressed
against the wall, breathing hard. He apologized for twenty minutes afterward. She told him it was
fine. Neither of them believed it.

During the day, Marcus manages by controlling his environment with meticulous care. He takes the
same routes to work. He sits with his back to the wall in restaurants. He has stopped going to
Fourth of July events entirely — the fireworks are non-negotiable. Last summer, a car backfired in
the parking garage at his office building and he found himself crouched behind a concrete pillar,
hands shaking, before he could locate himself in time and place again. He told no one.

His concentration, once his professional strength, now fragments without warning. During client
meetings, a particular quality of light through a window or the distant sound of a truck on the
street can pull him out of the room entirely. He compensates with meticulous preparation — notes,
outlines, redundancies — because he no longer trusts his own mind to stay present. His clients see a
thorough, serious professional. His wife sees a man who goes somewhere she cannot follow.

Marcus rarely talks about any of this. He carries a particular belief, formed somewhere between the
Army and adulthood, that naming what is wrong is the same as surrendering to it. He describes
himself as "a little on edge" and "not great with crowds." He tells Dana he is fine more often than
either of them finds convincing. When she asks about Darnell, or about that day, he changes the
subject with a quiet efficiency that has become its own kind of wall between them.

What Marcus knows, on some level, is that he never fully came home. Part of him is still on that
road, still holding Darnell, still waiting for something to make sense of what happened. He goes to
work. He loves his wife in the ways he is able to. He keeps the spreadsheets in order. And he
carries, without a word, what he cannot yet put down.
