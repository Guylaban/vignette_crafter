# Clinical Vignette: Tariq

Tariq is a 27-year-old married physical therapist of Lebanese descent who presents with a quiet,
careful manner that takes several sessions to soften into something more candid. He chooses his
words deliberately, often pausing mid-sentence as though weighing the cost of each one. He is well-
regarded at the rehabilitation clinic where he works, known among colleagues for his patience with
difficult cases and his ability to put anxious patients at ease. There is an irony in this that he
himself has begun to notice.

Two years ago, Tariq was sexually assaulted by a male acquaintance at a gathering he attended while
his wife, Nadia, was visiting family abroad. The assault occurred in a private room after Tariq had
been drinking, and he spent the weeks that followed convincing himself it was something other than
what it was. He tells himself he is a man. He tells himself men do not experience this. He did not
report it, did not tell Nadia, and returned to work the following Monday as though the weekend had
been unremarkable.

For the first several months, he functioned through sheer momentum. Then the momentum began to fail
him. He started waking between two and four in the morning, heart already loud before his eyes
opened, certain that something in the room had shifted. He would lie still for an hour or more,
listening. He stopped sleeping on his back entirely because the position triggered a physical
response he cannot fully articulate — a tightening across his chest, a sensation of being held down
— and so he curls on his side now, close to the edge of the bed, which Nadia has quietly noticed and
quietly said nothing about.

His work has become the most complicated terrain. Physical therapy requires sustained, purposeful
touch — hands on shoulders, on hips, on the muscles of the lower back — and Tariq is skilled at
this. But there are moments, particularly with male patients who are larger than him or who make
unexpected movements, when something shifts beneath his professionalism. His jaw tightens. He finds
a reason to step back, to adjust equipment, to create distance. He has not hurt anyone. He has not
made an error. But he finishes some shifts feeling wrung out in a way that has nothing to do with
physical fatigue, and he sits in his car in the parking garage longer than he used to.

He has become someone who scans rooms. He notes exits. At the clinic's holiday party last December,
he stood near the door for forty minutes before telling Nadia he had a headache and asking to leave.
She believed him, and her believing him made him feel worse.

He describes his relationship with Nadia as loving but increasingly distant, though the distance is
something he is constructing from his side of the wall. She reaches for him and he finds ways to
redirect — he is tired, he has an early patient, he will be better on the weekend. He loves her
without question. The thought of her knowing what happened to him produces a shame so acute he
describes it as physical, like something pressing on his sternum.

He carries a particular belief that has settled into him over these two years: that what happened
was a failure of his own judgment, his own body, his own masculinity. He has read enough about
trauma to know, intellectually, that this is not accurate. Knowing it intellectually has not
loosened its grip. He came to therapy not because he believed he deserved help, but because he had
begun to notice himself becoming someone he did not recognize — short-tempered, hollowed out, going
through the careful motions of a life he feels increasingly outside of.

He is still showing up. To work, to his marriage, to these sessions. He describes this as
stubbornness. It may also be something else.
