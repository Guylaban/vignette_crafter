# Vignette: Rohan

Rohan is a 29-year-old South Asian man who runs a small convenience store that his family helped him
establish several years ago. He is soft-spoken and careful with his words, and it takes him several
sessions before he begins to speak openly about what brought him here. When he finally does, he
starts not with the worst night, but with how things began — how charming she was at first, how
quickly they moved in together, how he told himself the first time she hit him that it was a one-
time thing, that she was under stress, that it would not happen again.

It happened again. Over the course of fourteen months, the physical violence escalated from shoves
and slaps to incidents that left him with a fractured wrist and, on one occasion, a laceration above
his eye that he told a concerned customer he got from a shelf bracket in the stockroom. He did not
tell anyone the truth. He says he was ashamed — not just of what was happening, but of how long he
stayed. He grew up understanding that men in his family did not speak about things like this, and he
could not find language for what was being done to him that did not feel somehow diminishing.

The relationship ended approximately eight months ago after a particularly violent episode in which
she threw a glass object at him. He left that night and has not returned. He is now living with a
cousin and has been in a new relationship for about three months, with a woman he describes as kind
and patient. He wants very much for this relationship to work, but finds himself unable to fully
inhabit it.

Most mornings, Rohan wakes before his alarm, heart already racing, a residue of dread sitting in his
chest that he cannot always trace to a specific dream. He opens the shop by seven and finds that the
routine of it — counting the till, restocking shelves, greeting the regulars — gives him something
to hold onto. But the store has its own ambushes. A customer raising her voice at him over a pricing
error last month sent him into a state of frozen alertness that lasted the rest of the day. He could
not explain to his part-time employee why he had gone so quiet. He simply said he had a headache.

He describes his mind as a place that is no longer fully private. Intrusive images of specific
incidents arrive without invitation — her face contorted in rage, the sound of something breaking,
the particular helplessness of covering his face with his arms. These images come when he is
driving, when he is trying to fall asleep beside his partner, when he is standing at the register
doing nothing more threatening than waiting for a card payment to process. He does not tell his
partner about them. He worries that if she understood what had happened to him — that a woman had
done this to him — she would think less of him, or worse, that she would not believe the full weight
of it.

His partner has noticed that he flinches when she moves quickly near him. She has noticed that he
sometimes goes somewhere else in the middle of a conversation. She has asked, gently, whether
something is wrong. He tells her he is tired. He is always telling people he is tired, because it is
the one true thing he can say without having to explain anything further.

Rohan carries a particular kind of loneliness — the loneliness of a person whose suffering does not
fit neatly into the stories his culture tells about who gets hurt and who does the hurting. He is
trying, quietly and with considerable effort, to remain present in his own life. He shows up for the
shop every day. He answers his mother's calls. He holds his partner's hand. But there is a part of
him that is still standing in that apartment, arms raised, waiting for it to be over.
