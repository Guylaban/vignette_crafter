**Vignette: Miriam**

Miriam is a 66-year-old Guatemalan-born woman who has worked as a literary editor for nearly three
decades, first in Mexico City and now in a small publishing house in Chicago where she has lived
since her late thirties. She is soft-spoken and precise with language in the way that people who
have spent a lifetime caring about words tend to be. She chooses what she says carefully, and she
pauses often, as though she is editing herself in real time.

She grew up during the height of Guatemala's civil conflict. In 1981, when she was twenty-two and
newly married, the violence reached her mid-sized city in a way it had not before. One afternoon,
returning from the market with her younger sister, she witnessed the killing of several civilians in
the street — men from her neighborhood, people she recognized — carried out by paramilitary forces
in broad daylight. She and her sister pressed themselves against a wall and did not move. Her sister
was trembling so violently that Miriam held her by the shoulders and told her to look at her face,
only her face. She does not remember how long they stood there. What she remembers is the smell —
something metallic and thick — and the quality of the light, which she describes as very flat and
very wrong. Within months, she and her then-husband fled the country.

For years, she functioned. She built a career she is proud of, raised a son largely on her own after
her divorce in the mid-nineties, and immersed herself in literature with a devotion that was, she
now understands, partly protective. Books kept her at a safe distance from her own interior life.
She rarely spoke about Guatemala, even to close friends, and when the topic arose she would redirect
the conversation with practiced ease.

The difficulties that brought her to therapy began approximately two years ago, after she accepted
an editorial project — a novel set during a Latin American civil conflict. Reading the manuscript in
her apartment, alone at her desk, she found herself unable to continue past the third chapter. Her
hands were shaking. She was not sure, at first, what was happening to her. She closed the laptop and
sat on the floor of her kitchen for an hour. That night she could not sleep, and when she did drift
off briefly she woke from a dream that was not quite a dream — more like a return, vivid and
physical, to that street in 1981.

Since then, sleep has been unreliable. She wakes between two and four in the morning with her heart
pounding, though she often cannot identify a specific nightmare, only a residue of dread that takes
hours to lift. During the day she finds herself scanning her surroundings in ways she didn't used to
— noting exits in restaurants, startling at sudden sounds on the street, feeling inexplicably unsafe
in crowds. She has declined several social invitations and has become, she says with some
embarrassment, "a person who prefers to stay home."

Her work has suffered in ways that distress her deeply. She cannot return to the manuscript she
abandoned, and she finds that certain passages in other texts — descriptions of violence, of
helplessness, even of particular kinds of light — pull her out of her editorial focus and into
something she struggles to name. She describes it as being dragged underwater. She surfaces, but it
takes time, and she feels ashamed of what she perceives as a professional failure.

She speaks about her sister, who still lives in Guatemala City, with particular tenderness and a
guilt she has carried quietly for decades — the guilt of having left, of having built a life
elsewhere. She does not fully connect this guilt to the afternoon they stood against that wall
together, but the connection surfaces in the way her voice changes when she speaks of her sister,
dropping lower, as though she is trying not to be heard.

She describes herself as a private person. She says she has never talked about this before, not
really. She seems both relieved and frightened to be doing so now.
