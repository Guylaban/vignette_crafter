# The Weight of Hands

Maria is twenty-seven years old, a digital animator at a mid-sized studio in Austin, and she sits in
session with her fingers often moving against her thighs — small, rhythmic tapping, as though she is
sketching something invisible just to keep herself grounded in the present moment.

She grew up in Guadalajara before moving to the United States at nineteen for art school, carrying
with her a talent for bringing still images to life. It is a bitter irony she has quietly noticed
herself: she makes things move, and yet for the past two years she has felt utterly frozen.

The relationship with Damián began when she was twenty-four. He was charming and attentive, the kind
of man who remembered small details — her favorite color, the way she took her coffee — and she
mistook his intensity for devotion. The violence did not arrive all at once. It crept in slowly, a
shove after an argument, an apology so sincere she convinced herself it was an aberration. By the
time his hands were around her throat on a Tuesday evening in her own apartment, she had already
spent months minimizing, explaining, and forgiving. She left him that night, bleeding from a cut
above her eyebrow, and drove herself to an urgent care clinic where she told the nurse she had
walked into a cabinet door.

She has not spoken those words aloud — *I lied to the nurse* — until recently, and saying them seems
to cost her something physical. Her jaw tightens. She looks at the wall.

In the months that followed, Maria threw herself into work with a ferocity that her colleagues read
as ambition. She was the first one in and the last to leave, filling her screen with characters in
motion because motion meant she did not have to be still with her own thoughts. But the nights were
brutal. She stopped sleeping in her bedroom after she realized that the angle of light from the
streetlamp through the curtains looked, in a half-awake state, exactly like the shadow he used to
cast in the doorway. She sleeps on her couch now, the television running low, because silence feels
like a held breath waiting to become something terrible.

Loud sounds undo her in ways she cannot always predict or explain. A door slamming in the hallway of
her apartment building can send her heart into a sprint that lasts for twenty minutes. At the
studio, when a colleague raised his voice during a production meeting — not at her, not even
angrily, just with enthusiasm — she found herself unable to breathe and had to excuse herself to the
bathroom, where she sat on the tile floor until her hands stopped shaking. She told no one why.

She does not date. She has tried, once, agreeing to coffee with someone a coworker introduced her
to, and spent the entire hour cataloguing exits and measuring the distance between her chair and the
door. She came home and did not leave her apartment for three days.

What troubles Maria most, she says, is not the fear — she has made a kind of uneasy peace with the
fear — but the way she no longer recognizes herself. She used to be someone who laughed easily, who
called her mother every Sunday, who found genuine joy in the small miracle of a character blinking
on a screen. Now her mother's calls feel like obligations she endures, and when she watches her own
animations she feels detached from them, as though someone else made them, as though she is watching
her life from a slight remove, through smudged glass.

She describes a recurring image that comes to her uninvited while she is working: his face very
close to hers, his expression not angry but calm, which was always the most frightening thing. She
pushes it away. She keeps drawing. She keeps the television on. She taps her fingers against her
thighs and waits, though she could not say with certainty what she is waiting for.
