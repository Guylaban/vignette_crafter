# The Weight of the Road

Minh is a 33-year-old Vietnamese-American physical therapist who works at an outpatient
rehabilitation clinic in a mid-sized city. He is married to his wife of four years, and by most
outward measures, he presents as composed, competent, and quietly dedicated to his work. But
eighteen months ago, something happened on a rain-slicked highway that changed the architecture of
his inner life in ways he is only beginning to understand.

It was a Tuesday evening in late autumn. Minh was driving home from a continuing education seminar,
traveling in the right lane of the interstate when a semi-truck lost control on the wet pavement and
crossed into his lane. The impact was catastrophic. His car was pushed into the guardrail and spun
nearly one hundred and eighty degrees before coming to rest in the emergency lane. Minh was
conscious throughout — he remembers the sound of metal folding, the smell of something burning, and
the strange stillness that followed, during which he was convinced he was about to die. He sat
trapped in the crumpled vehicle for nearly forty minutes before first responders arrived. He
sustained a fractured clavicle, three broken ribs, and a mild traumatic brain injury. The truck
driver survived as well, though Minh did not learn this until days later.

The physical injuries healed on a predictable timeline. The other things did not.

In the weeks after the accident, Minh found himself waking at two or three in the morning, heart
hammering, certain he could still hear the grinding of metal. He would lie still in the dark beside
his wife, unwilling to wake her, running his hands along his own ribcage as if to confirm he was
intact. During the day, he began taking longer routes to work — routes that avoided the interstate
entirely — adding nearly thirty minutes to his commute each way. He told his wife it was for
traffic. He told himself the same thing for a while.

At work, Minh remains meticulous and present with his patients. He is skilled at reading bodies in
pain, at understanding the slow, frustrating work of recovery. But there are moments — when a
patient describes a car accident, or when someone mentions hearing a loud bang in the parking lot —
when something shifts behind his eyes. He excuses himself to the supply room, stands there for a
minute or two with his hands braced on the counter, and then returns. His colleagues have noticed he
seems quieter than he used to be, though no one has said anything directly.

His wife, Lan, has noticed more. She describes a version of Minh that startles easily, that declines
invitations to evening events because driving after dark now fills him with a dread he cannot fully
articulate. He has stopped watching the television dramas they used to enjoy together, particularly
anything involving crashes or emergency scenes. When she asks how he is doing, he typically says
*fine* and changes the subject — not out of dismissiveness, but because he genuinely struggles to
find language for what lives inside him now. He grew up in a household where difficulty was managed
quietly, where endurance was its own form of dignity, and that inheritance runs deep.

What troubles Minh most, perhaps, is the intrusion of the accident into the very work that once
grounded him. When he guides a patient through gait retraining after a motor vehicle accident, he
sometimes finds himself back inside that crumpled car — the rain on the windshield, the silence
after the impact, the certainty of death. He pushes through it, professionally and without
complaint. But the effort costs him something, and by the end of certain days he arrives home
hollowed out in a way that rest alone does not seem to repair.

He has not spoken to anyone professionally about any of this. He scheduled an appointment once and
cancelled it the morning of, telling himself he was too busy, that others had it worse, that he was
managing fine. He is still telling himself that now.
