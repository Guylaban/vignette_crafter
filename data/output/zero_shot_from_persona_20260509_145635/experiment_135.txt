# The Weight of Water

Kofi is a 65-year-old Ghanaian man who has worked the floor of a large home goods store for the past
eleven years. He is the kind of employee customers seek out — patient, unhurried, with a quiet
dignity that makes people feel they are in capable hands. His manager calls him the backbone of the
department. Kofi accepts the compliment with a modest nod and returns to straightening shelves.
Routine, he has learned, is something to be protected.

Three years ago, Kofi traveled back to his home village in the Eastern Region of Ghana to visit his
aging mother and attend a family gathering. He had not been back in nearly a decade. The rains that
season had been heavier than anyone could remember, and on his fourth night there, a flash flood
tore through the valley without warning. The water came in the dark, fast and indifferent. Kofi
remembers the sound before anything else — not a roar, but a low, building pressure, like the earth
itself was exhaling. He scrambled onto the roof of his mother's house with two of his nephews. His
mother did not make it out of the house in time. A neighbor pulled her from the water nearly a
quarter mile downstream. She survived, but Kofi watched that happen. He watched his mother's body
turning in the current before hands reached her. He was on the roof. He could not get down. He could
not move.

The flood receded by morning. Four people in the village died. Kofi flew home to his wife, Abena,
ten days later. He told her it had been frightening. He did not tell her about the roof, or about
his mother in the water, or about the particular stillness that had overtaken him while he watched.

Back at the store, colleagues noticed he seemed quieter than usual, but attributed it to jet lag, to
grief, to the natural weight of seeing the old country in distress. Kofi let them believe that. What
he could not explain — what he could barely acknowledge to himself — was that he had stopped
sleeping through the night. He wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, heart already
pounding, the sound of water filling a room that is perfectly dry. Sometimes he sits at the kitchen
table until dawn rather than return to a bed where the dreams are waiting.

He avoids the garden hose. He changes the channel when news footage shows flooding anywhere in the
world. Last spring, during a heavy rainstorm, he sat in his parked car in the store's lot for forty
minutes, unable to make himself walk through the rain to the entrance, watching water sheet across
the windshield. A younger colleague eventually came out with an umbrella. Kofi told him his knee was
bothering him.

At work, the overhead speakers sometimes play a sound-effects track near the seasonal display —
ocean waves, rainfall, ambient nature sounds meant to sell outdoor furniture. The first time Kofi
heard it, he knocked a display lamp from a shelf and stood gripping the edge of a table, certain,
for a moment, that the floor was moving beneath him. He requested a transfer to a different section
of the store. He did not explain why.

Abena knows something is wrong. She is a perceptive woman and she loves him carefully. She has
asked, more than once, what happened on that roof. Kofi tells her he is fine. He tells her he is
just getting older, that sleep becomes difficult at his age, that he has always been a private man.
He believes some of this. He sits across from her at dinner most evenings, present in body, and
thinks about his mother's hands above the waterline, reaching for something that wasn't there. He
passes the bread. He asks about her day. He keeps his face very still, the way he has learned to do,
the way that has always served him — until it no longer does.
