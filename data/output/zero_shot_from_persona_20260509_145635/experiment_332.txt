# Clinical Vignette: Amara, Age 43

Amara grew up in a household where love and terror arrived through the same hands. Her father, a man
she describes as "two different people depending on the day," beat her regularly from the time she
was about six years old until she left home at seventeen. The beatings were unpredictable —
triggered by a spilled cup, a wrong look, a grade that wasn't high enough — and that
unpredictability may have been the most damaging part. She learned early to read the air in a room,
to scan her father's posture and voice before she'd even crossed the threshold of the front door.
She became, as she puts it, "a very good detector." She did not know then that this skill would
follow her into adulthood and reshape every relationship and environment she would ever inhabit.

Amara came to West Africa from Ghana in her mid-twenties, married a man she describes as gentle and
patient, and built what looks, from the outside, like a stable life. She works in IT support for a
mid-sized logistics company, a job she is good at and genuinely enjoys. There is something she finds
comforting in the logic of systems — a problem has a cause, and the cause can be found and fixed.
People, she has learned, are far less cooperative than servers.

At work, however, the comfort has limits. When her supervisor raises his voice — even at a
situation, not at her — Amara's body responds before her mind can intervene. Her heart accelerates,
her vision narrows, and she finds herself calculating the fastest route out of the room. She has
excused herself to the bathroom on multiple occasions simply to sit in a stall and breathe until her
hands stop shaking. She has never told anyone at work why.

At home, she is vigilant in ways her husband cannot fully understand. She sleeps lightly, waking at
sounds that don't disturb him at all. She has positioned her side of the bed closest to the door — a
choice she made without consciously realizing what she was doing. When he reaches for her
unexpectedly, even affectionately, she flinches. She sees the hurt that flickers across his face and
hates herself for it. She knows he is not her father. Her body has not received that memo.

The memories themselves do not always arrive as memories. Sometimes they are sensory — the
particular smell of a leather belt, the sound of footsteps in a hallway — and suddenly she is not
forty-three years old in a comfortable home. She is small again, and waiting. She has learned to
call these episodes "going somewhere else," and she can sometimes feel herself beginning to drift
before it happens fully, though she cannot always stop it.

She carries a persistent, low-grade shame that has been her companion so long she rarely identifies
it as shame anymore. It presents as a conviction that she is fundamentally difficult, that her
reactions are excessive, that she should, by now, be over this. She works hard, she is competent,
she is a good wife — and yet she feels, in some private and unexamined place, that she is held
together with considerably less than everyone around her seems to be.

She came to therapy not because of the childhood abuse, which she initially presented as "something
that happened, but a long time ago," but because her sleep had deteriorated sharply over the past
year and she had begun having what she called "bad patches" — stretches of days where she felt
detached from her own life, watching herself move through it from a slight distance. Her husband
encouraged her to come. She agreed, she says, mostly to reassure him.

She sits in the chair with her back straight and her hands folded, and she answers questions
thoroughly and thoughtfully. She is, even here, scanning the room.
