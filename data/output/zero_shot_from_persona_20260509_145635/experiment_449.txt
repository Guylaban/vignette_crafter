**Vignette: Aroha, Age 27**

Aroha grew up on the outskirts of Auckland, the youngest of four siblings in a large Māori family
where the front door was rarely locked and neighbors moved through the house as freely as relatives.
That sense of open, easy safety felt like a birthright — until about two years ago, when a former
classmate from their undergraduate teaching program began showing up in places where Aroha had no
reason to expect them.

It started quietly enough. A few too many comments on social media posts. A text message that
referenced something Aroha had mentioned only in passing, weeks earlier. Then the classmate appeared
at the café where Aroha spent Sunday mornings grading lesson plans. Then outside the school where
Aroha had recently taken a position teaching students with complex learning needs. Each encounter
was framed as coincidence, offered with a smile that Aroha felt in their chest long after the person
had gone. Over the following four months, the pattern intensified — notes left on Aroha's car, a
bouquet delivered to the school front office, and eventually a series of messages that made explicit
what Aroha had already understood in their body: this person was watching them, and had been for
some time.

The formal intervention — a police report, a restraining order, the classmate eventually relocating
— resolved the external situation. What it did not resolve was the architecture of fear that had
quietly rebuilt itself inside Aroha during those months.

Now, two years on, Aroha moves through the world with a vigilance that exhausts them. Walking from
the school car park to the building each morning, they scan faces, read postures, catalogue exits.
They have rearranged their classroom so that their desk faces the door. Students and colleagues
experience Aroha as warm and attentive — and Aroha genuinely loves the work, the particular intimacy
of helping a child find a way into learning that fits the shape of their mind — but maintaining that
presence costs something. By the time Aroha arrives home to their partner, Sione, there is very
little left.

Sleep is thin and unreliable. Aroha wakes most nights between two and four in the morning with a
heart rate that belongs to someone running, though they are lying still. The dreams are not always
dramatic — sometimes it is simply the feeling of being observed, a pressure at the back of the neck,
and then waking into a dark room that takes several seconds to recognize as safe. Aroha has stopped
telling Sione about the dreams as often, not wanting to wear out the concern they can see in his
face.

There are stretches of the day when Aroha feels hollowed out, as though the emotional range has been
narrowed without their consent. They notice this most acutely when a student achieves something
meaningful — a breakthrough with a child who has been struggling to communicate — and the joy Aroha
expects to feel arrives muted, like a sound heard through a closed door. This troubles them perhaps
more than the nightmares do. Teaching was supposed to be the thing that stayed whole.

Aroha has become selective about social plans, canceling more often than not when gatherings involve
unfamiliar venues or crowds. They have not returned to the café where they used to grade on Sundays.
The route they take to work changes regularly, a habit that began as a practical precaution and has
since become compulsive — they feel genuinely uneasy on the days when circumstance forces them to
drive the same road twice in a row.

What Aroha finds hardest to articulate, even to Sione, is that the threat is gone and the fear is
not. The restraining order is real. The classmate is in another city. Aroha knows this. And yet the
body has not received the news, or has received it and chosen not to believe it — holding its
breath, watching the door, waiting for the coincidence that proves it was right all along.
