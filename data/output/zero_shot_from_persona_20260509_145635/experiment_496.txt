**Vignette: Aroha, Age 31**

Aroha grew up in a small coastal town in New Zealand, the eldest of four children in a household
that, from the outside, looked ordinary enough — a modest house, a garden, a father who worked on
the fishing boats. But inside, the walls held a different kind of life. From as early as she can
remember, perhaps five or six years old, Aroha would lie still in her bed at night listening to the
sounds from her parents' room — her mother's voice rising and then breaking, the thud of something
hitting a wall, her father's low, controlled anger that somehow felt more frightening than shouting.
She learned to read the silence between her parents the way other children read picture books,
scanning for danger in a look, a shift in posture, the particular way her father set down his glass.

She never spoke about what she saw. She became instead the child who held things together — watching
her younger siblings, making sure they stayed in their rooms, sometimes standing in doorways she
knew she shouldn't, unable to look away and unable to leave. One night, when she was nine, she
watched her father strike her mother across the face hard enough to send her into the kitchen bench.
Aroha stood frozen in the hallway, her hand pressed flat against the wall, and did not move or make
a sound. That image — her mother's face turned away, the particular angle of her body as she fell —
has never left her.

Dance found Aroha in secondary school, and she found in it something she couldn't name then but
understands better now: a place where her body could move through feelings that had no other
language. She trained seriously through her late teens and twenties, eventually becoming a
contemporary dancer with a small but respected company. She married her partner, Daniel, at twenty-
eight, and by most measures her adult life looks like one of genuine flourishing.

But the past does not stay past. Aroha describes a persistent sense of being braced for something, a
low-level vigilance that never fully lifts. In her marriage, she finds herself watching Daniel's
moods with an intensity that exhausts her — cataloguing the way he holds his jaw, whether he seems
quiet, whether quiet means something is wrong. When they argue, even mildly, her body responds
before her mind can intervene: her heart hammers, her vision narrows, and she goes somewhere else
entirely, back to that hallway, back to that wall. Afterward she feels ashamed and cannot always
explain to Daniel what happened.

Sleep is difficult. She wakes in the early hours with a racing heart and a certainty that something
is terribly wrong, though the bedroom is quiet and Daniel is beside her. The dreams are not always
literal — sometimes it is simply a feeling of paralysis, of being small and unable to move while
something terrible unfolds nearby.

In the studio, things are more complicated than they once were. Movement has always been her refuge,
but certain choreographic sequences — particularly those involving physical contact with male
collaborators, or scenes that require her to portray submission or fear — can pull her suddenly and
completely out of the present. She has learned to manage this professionally, to push through, but
it costs her. She describes standing in the wings before a performance and feeling a dissociation so
complete that she has to press her fingernails into her palm to locate herself in her own body.

What troubles Aroha most, she says, is not the memories themselves but the way they have shaped her
sense of what love looks like. She loves Daniel and believes he loves her, and yet she cannot fully
trust the quietness of their home. Some part of her is still waiting — still nine years old, still
standing in that hallway — for the ordinary to become dangerous. She wants, more than anything, to
stop waiting.
