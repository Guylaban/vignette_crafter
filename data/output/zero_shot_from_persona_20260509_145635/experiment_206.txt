# Elena

Elena is a 74-year-old Colombian-born woman who has worked as a pharmacist for nearly four decades.
She is currently married to her second husband, a quiet and gentle man she describes as "nothing
like him." She sits with her hands folded tightly in her lap during sessions, occasionally glancing
toward the door, a habit she seems unaware of until it is gently reflected back to her.

For eleven years, Elena was married to her first husband, Roberto. What began as occasional cruelty
— a shove against the kitchen wall, a hand closed around her wrist until she cried out — escalated
steadily through the late 1970s and into the 1980s. The worst night came when Elena was 38. Roberto
came home from a work gathering having had too much to drink, and when Elena asked him to lower his
voice so their daughter could sleep, he beat her severely enough to fracture two ribs and leave her
unconscious on the bathroom floor. Their daughter, then nine years old, found her. Elena remembers
waking to the child's screaming and thinking, with strange clarity, *I have to get up. I have to get
up or she will never recover from this.* She did get up. She pressed a towel to her face, drove
herself to the hospital, and told the admitting nurse she had fallen down the stairs. She left
Roberto six months later, after carefully hiding money in a coffee tin at the back of the pharmacy
stockroom where she worked.

Elena describes those years as something she "put away in a box." She remarried in her late forties,
raised her daughter, built a career she is deeply proud of. For long stretches of time, the box
seemed to stay closed. But since her retirement three years ago, the structure that kept it shut has
dissolved. Without the daily rhythm of the pharmacy — the counting of tablets, the counseling of
patients, the precise and purposeful work that had organized her mind for so long — the memories
have come flooding back with a force that frightens her.

She wakes most nights between two and three in the morning, her heart slamming, certain she can hear
Roberto's footsteps in the hallway. She lies rigid beside her husband, unable to wake him because
she does not want him to see her this way, and waits for a danger that never arrives. During the
day, she is startled by doors slamming, by raised male voices on the television, by the particular
sound of ice in a glass. Last winter, her son-in-law raised his voice during a family argument —
nothing directed at her, nothing threatening — and Elena found herself in the bathroom, sitting on
the cold tile floor, unable to explain to herself why she had gone there.

She feels a persistent sense of shame that she describes as heavier than the fear. She is a woman
who dispensed medications, who counseled patients through their worst moments, who prided herself on
competence and composure. That she "allowed" what happened to her is a conviction she holds with
quiet, immovable certainty, no matter how many times it is gently challenged. She has told almost no
one — not her daughter, not her current husband, not the friends she has known for thirty years. The
secret has become its own kind of weight.

She reports little pleasure in things she once loved: her garden, cooking, the telenovelas she and
her husband used to watch together on Sunday evenings. She describes herself as feeling "behind
glass" — present in her life but unable to fully touch it. She worries she is becoming a burden,
then worries about worrying, cycling through the night in a loop she cannot find her way out of.

What brings her to therapy now, she says, is her granddaughter — a girl of seven with Roberto's dark
eyes and none of his cruelty. Elena wants to be present for her. She wants, at 74, to finally put
the box down rather than simply carry it more quietly.
