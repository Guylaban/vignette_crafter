# Vignette: Amara

Amara is a 77-year-old African woman who has spent the better part of four decades in public life.
She sits in the regional assembly as a senior legislator, known to colleagues and constituents alike
for her measured voice and her unwillingness to be interrupted. What very few people know — what she
has protected as carefully as she has protected her public reputation — is that for eleven years,
between the ages of thirty-one and forty-two, her first husband beat her.

It began gradually, the way she now understands these things often do. A shove after a disagreement.
A slap that was followed by tears and flowers. By the time the violence became regular and severe —
fists, a belt, once a ceramic lamp broken across her back — she had already learned to arrange her
schedule around his moods, to wear certain fabrics, to make sure she was never the last one
laughing. She left him only after he fractured two of her ribs during an argument about a
constituency meeting she had attended without his permission. She packed a single bag and drove
herself to her sister's house, and she never went back.

She has been married to her second husband, a retired physician, for thirty-one years. By any
external measure, her life since leaving is a story of remarkable resilience. She raised three
children, built a legislative career, and has been celebrated at national women's conferences as a
symbol of strength. She accepted those tributes graciously and believed, for a long time, that she
had simply moved on.

Then, two years ago, a junior female colleague disclosed to the assembly that her own partner had
been abusing her. Amara was asked to lead the parliamentary response. She agreed immediately,
because of course she did — it was the right thing, the necessary thing. But sitting in the chamber
during the first formal hearing, listening to the younger woman describe the particular sound of a
door being locked from the inside, something in Amara came loose. She drove home that evening and
sat in her car in the driveway for two hours, unable to explain to herself why she could not open
the door.

Since then, sleep has become unreliable and hostile. She wakes most nights between two and four in
the morning with her heart already pounding, the details of dreams she cannot fully recall leaving a
residue of dread that takes hours to lift. During assembly sessions, if a male colleague raises his
voice sharply or moves his chair suddenly, she feels a jolt of fear so physical it embarrasses her —
a tightening in her chest, a narrowing of her vision, the impulse to become very still and very
small. She finds this intolerable. She is not, she tells herself, a woman who becomes small.

She has begun avoiding certain corridors in the assembly building, certain colleagues whose
mannerisms remind her of nothing she can name precisely. She has stopped attending the women's
advocacy dinners she once chaired. When her husband reaches across the bed in the night, she
sometimes flinches before she can stop herself, and she watches him absorb that with a quiet sadness
that she finds harder to bear than the flinching itself.

She describes her emotional life in the present tense as "flat most of the time, and then suddenly
not." She is irritable with her staff in ways that are new and that she notices with dismay. She has
difficulty reading legislation she has been reading for decades, finding that her attention slides
away from the page. She feels, she says — choosing the word carefully, as she chooses all her words
— hollowed. As though something that had been sealed for a long time has been opened, and what was
inside was not the nothing she had assumed, but something that had been waiting with considerable
patience.

She is 77 years old. She has survived a great deal. She is not entirely sure what it means that this
is happening now.
