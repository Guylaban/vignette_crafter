# Vignette: Moana

Moana is a 57-year-old Māori architect who has spent the better part of three decades designing
buildings meant to last — structures she describes as "things that hold people safe." There is a
quiet irony in that phrase now, one she seems to recognise without quite being able to say it aloud.

Four years ago, Moana's daughter, Aroha, was involved in a serious motor vehicle accident on a rural
highway outside Auckland. Aroha survived, but sustained a traumatic brain injury that left her with
permanent cognitive and physical impairments. Moana was not present at the accident. She received
the call at her office while reviewing blueprints for a community centre — a detail she has never
been able to shake. She drove to the hospital alone, and what she found when she arrived — Aroha
unconscious, swollen beyond recognition, surrounded by machines — became the image that now lives
permanently behind her eyes.

In the months that followed, Moana took leave from her firm and moved into a rhythm of hospital
visits, rehabilitation appointments, and the grinding administrative work of navigating disability
services. She did what she has always done: she organised, she planned, she held herself together
with the precision of someone accustomed to managing complex systems. Her marriage, already
strained, did not survive the weight of it. Her ex-husband, she says without particular bitterness,
"couldn't find a way to stay."

What Moana did not do, for a very long time, was stop.

Now, four years on, Aroha is living in supported accommodation and has reached a plateau in her
recovery. The crisis, by most external measures, has passed. But Moana finds herself in a kind of
wreckage she cannot blueprint her way out of. She sleeps poorly, waking most nights between two and
four in the morning with her heart already racing, the hospital corridor vivid and immediate in the
dark. She does not always know, in those first seconds, that she is in her own bed.

During the day she functions — she has returned to work, leads a small team, meets deadlines — but
she describes moving through her days as though behind glass. Colleagues comment that she seems
distracted. She is. She is also hypervigilant in ways that exhaust her: she checks her phone
compulsively for missed calls, avoids driving on motorways, and cannot sit in waiting rooms without
her chest tightening into something close to panic. She has stopped watching television dramas
involving hospitals or accidents entirely, and she recently declined a project that would have
required designing a rehabilitation facility, telling her partners it was a "conflict of interest."
She did not explain further.

What she finds hardest to articulate — and what she circles around in session with careful,
architectural language — is the guilt. Not survivor's guilt exactly, but something adjacent: a
mother's guilt, rooted in the conviction that she should have been able to prevent what happened, or
at minimum, to absorb it herself. She knows this is not rational. She is a precise and intelligent
woman, and she knows. But knowing has not helped. She finds herself replaying not the accident
itself, which she did not witness, but every conversation she had with Aroha in the weeks before —
looking, she says, for the thing she missed.

Moana describes her relationship with her culture as something she has "let slide" since the
accident. She used to attend hui, to weave, to visit her marae on the coast north of the city. She
has not been back in two years. When asked why, she is quiet for a long moment. "I don't know how to
be there," she finally says, "when I'm still not sure who I am now." She pauses. "Everything I built
— the career, the family, the sense of myself as someone who could hold things together — it just
doesn't look the same anymore."

She comes to sessions punctually, notebook in hand, as though she is still hoping that if she
documents enough, a pattern will emerge that explains how to find her way back.
