# The Weight of the Dark Room

Marcus is a 53-year-old Māori photographer from Auckland, married for twenty-six years to his wife,
Hine. For most of his adult life, he has made his living documenting the natural world — coastlines,
forests, the faces of people in remote communities across the Pacific. His work has always required
a particular kind of patience, a willingness to wait in stillness for the right light. That
stillness, he says, has become something he can no longer trust.

Seven years ago, Marcus was on assignment in a remote region of Papua New Guinea, photographing a
story on isolated village life. What began as a straightforward documentary project ended when he
and his local guide were detained by a armed militia group operating in the area. They were held in
a small, windowless structure — a storage shed, he thinks, though he was never certain — for eleven
days. His guide was taken from him on the third day and not returned. Marcus spent the remaining
eight days alone, in near-total darkness, not knowing whether he would be released, ransomed, or
killed. He was given minimal food and water. He heard gunfire on several occasions. He was
interrogated twice, with a weapon pressed against his head. He was eventually released without
explanation, left on a roadside at dawn.

He flew home. He told Hine he was fine. He went back to work within three weeks.

For the first year, Marcus functioned on momentum. He accepted assignments, processed images in his
darkroom, attended his children's events. But the darkroom — a space he had once loved for its quiet
and its alchemy — began to feel like the shed. The smell of the chemicals, the red safety light, the
particular quality of enclosed silence: his body would respond before his mind could intervene. His
heart would accelerate. His hands would sweat. He began to find reasons to avoid it, switching
increasingly to digital workflows, telling clients it was about efficiency.

He stopped sleeping through the night. He would wake at two or three in the morning, certain that
something was wrong, lying rigid beside Hine and listening to the house. He began checking the
locks. Then checking them again. He started sitting with his back to walls in restaurants and
leaving if spaces felt too enclosed. He declined an assignment in Fiji — a place he had photographed
a dozen times — because the travel felt impossible to contemplate.

Hine noticed. She is a quietly perceptive woman, and she began asking careful questions. Marcus
deflected them for two years before he told her, one evening on the back porch, what had actually
happened in that shed. He had never told anyone the full account. As he spoke, he found himself
dissociating — hearing his own voice from a distance, watching the garden as though it belonged to
someone else's life.

He describes the guilt about his guide as a weight that never lifts. He does not know what happened
to the man. He has searched, made inquiries, found nothing. He returns to those eight days of
solitude and asks himself what he could have done differently, though he knows, on some level, that
there was nothing. The knowing does not help.

In the present, Marcus is a man who moves carefully through his own life. He is present with Hine
and their adult children in the ways that matter to him, but he describes feeling like he is
watching them through glass — close, but separated by something he cannot name. He still
photographs. He is skilled and his eye has not left him. But he avoids low light. He avoids enclosed
spaces. He shoots outdoors almost exclusively now, always with an exit visible, always knowing where
the open air is.

He says the Pacific light used to feel like a gift. Now he needs it simply to breathe.
