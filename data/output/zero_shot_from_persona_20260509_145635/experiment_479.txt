# Vignette: Marta, Age 76

Marta has spent the better part of her life reading water. Fifty years as a ship captain — first on
the Baltic ferries, later commanding cargo vessels through the North Sea — taught her to trust her
own steadiness above all else. She read weather systems the way other people read faces, and her
crew trusted her because she never flinched. That version of herself feels very distant to her now.

Three years ago, Marta attended a public demonstration in the city center, not far from the
apartment where she has lived alone since her husband Pieter died. She had not gone to make trouble.
She had gone, she says quietly, because she believed in what people were saying and because she was
tired of sitting in a quiet flat. What followed has rearranged something fundamental inside her. The
police moved into the crowd without warning. Marta was pushed to the ground before she understood
what was happening. An officer stood over her, and she remembers the baton and she remembers the
pavement against her cheek and she remembers, most of all, that no one stopped it. She was seventy-
three years old and she was on the ground and she could not get up. She sustained a fractured wrist
and bruised ribs. She was released without charge.

In the weeks afterward, Marta told herself she was fine. She had survived North Sea storms. She had
pulled crew members from danger. This was a bruise, she said. It would fade. But the nights would
not cooperate. She began waking at two or three in the morning with her heart hammering, certain
someone was in the flat. She would lie rigid, listening, until the light came. She stopped going out
in the early evenings when the streets filled with people, and then she stopped going out much at
all.

When she did sleep, she dreamed of the pavement. Sometimes she dreamed she was on the bridge of a
ship and looked down to find she was pinned and could not move the wheel. She would wake gasping,
her wrist aching even though it had healed.

What troubles her most, she says, is what happened to her sense of authority. On a vessel, she was
the final word. Her judgment kept people alive. Now she stands in a grocery queue and feels a
crawling alertness if someone in a uniform walks past — a security guard, a transit officer, anyone
with a badge. Her body responds before her mind can intervene. Her chest tightens. She moves to the
edge of the space. She has left shops without buying anything simply to get outside.

She does not talk about it easily. Her generation, she explains, did not talk about being
frightened. And there is something else beneath that — a shame she finds difficult to name. She is a
woman who commanded vessels in international waters, who once coordinated a rescue in force-eight
conditions, and she cannot ride a city tram without scanning every face near the doors. That
contradiction sits in her like ballast shifted wrong.

She has become irritable with her daughter, who calls from Rotterdam twice a week. She knows she is
short with her and cannot fully explain why. She finds herself unable to read for long, which once
gave her great pleasure. She keeps the television on for noise but rarely watches it. She has
declined two invitations to attend maritime association dinners she would previously have looked
forward to — rooms full of people, noise, no clear exit.

Marta still keeps her captain's logbooks on the shelf above her desk, forty years of entries in her
careful hand. She looks at them sometimes and feels something she cannot quite reach — not pride
exactly, more like grief for a person who knew, without question, that she could handle whatever
came.
