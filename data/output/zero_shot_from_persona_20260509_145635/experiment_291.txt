# The Weight of Hands

Marcus is fifty-five years old, trim and carefully put together, the kind of man who enters a room
and is immediately noticed — not because he seeks attention, but because everything about his
appearance is deliberate. He has been designing women's ready-to-wear for nearly three decades,
building a modest but respected name for himself in the industry. He works out of a studio in his
home, surrounded by fabric swatches and dress forms, and he prefers it that way. Alone, mostly.
Controlled.

He grew up in a small city in Ontario, the second of three boys, in a house that looked ordinary
from the outside. His father worked in manufacturing and came home most evenings with a particular
kind of exhaustion that turned, reliably, into rage. Marcus learned early to read the sound of a car
in the driveway — the speed of it, the slam of the door — the way other children learn to read
weather. When the signs were wrong, he would go still, make himself small, try to disappear into the
wallpaper. It didn't always work. His father's hands were large, and the beatings were not random;
they had a ritual quality to them, a terrible patience, that Marcus found in some ways worse than
the blows themselves. This went on from the time he was about six until he left for college at
seventeen. His mother knew. She said nothing.

He built a life that looked, for a long time, like escape. He moved to Toronto, then Montreal, then
briefly New York. He married a woman named Claire in his late thirties; they divorced after nine
years, quietly, without children. He tells himself the marriage ended because of the long hours, the
travel, the consuming nature of the work. He does not often examine this too closely.

What brings him in now is something that happened eight months ago. His elderly father — estranged
for years, living in a care facility outside Hamilton — had a stroke and was briefly hospitalized.
Marcus visited once, out of what he calls obligation. Standing in the doorway of that hospital room,
looking at his father's diminished body, he felt nothing he expected to feel. What he felt instead
was a flooding, physical terror, as though he were six years old again and the door was about to
open. He left after four minutes. He has not slept properly since.

The nights are the worst of it. He wakes between two and four in the morning with his heart
hammering, the sheets damp, fragments of old scenes playing behind his eyes with a vividness that
feels less like memory than presence. He gets up and works — draping fabric, sketching, anything to
keep his hands moving — because lying still feels dangerous in a way he cannot fully articulate. He
is exhausted most days, and the exhaustion has a particular quality, like something has been opened
that he does not know how to close.

He finds himself flinching at sounds he should be able to tolerate: a door closing too hard, a
raised voice from the street below his studio. A colleague touched his shoulder unexpectedly during
a fitting last month and Marcus had to excuse himself to the bathroom to collect himself. He is
aware that this is not entirely new — that he has always been this way, to some degree — but the
hospital visit seems to have stripped away whatever management strategies he had quietly assembled
over the decades.

He describes his emotional life as "narrow." He is good at appreciating beautiful things — a well-
cut sleeve, a particular quality of autumn light — but intimacy of any kind feels like standing at
the edge of something with no railing. He keeps people at a distance he has learned to make look
like self-sufficiency. His ex-wife once told him she never felt like she could reach him. He
thought, at the time, that this was her problem. He is less certain of that now.

He sits in the chair across from the therapist with his legs crossed and his hands folded, and he
speaks carefully, choosing words the way he chooses fabric — for what they reveal, and for what they
conceal.
