**Vignette: Priya**

Priya is a thirty-year-old South Asian woman who works as a software engineer at a mid-sized tech
company. She is married to a man she describes as gentle and patient, and from the outside, her life
looks composed and successful. Inside, it rarely feels that way.

Growing up in a household that prized discipline and academic achievement, Priya was raised
primarily by her father, whose anger was unpredictable and physical. The beatings were not constant,
but they were severe enough and arbitrary enough that she never knew when they were coming. A poor
grade, a spilled glass of water, a tone of voice he didn't like — any of these could shift the
atmosphere in the house within seconds. She learned to read rooms the way other children read
storybooks, scanning her father's face the moment she walked through the door, calculating the
safest thing to say or do or be. By the time she was twelve, she had become very good at
disappearing into herself.

She left for university at seventeen and told herself she had left all of it behind. For a while,
she almost believed it. She built a career she is genuinely proud of, married someone who has never
once raised his voice at her, and moved far from the city where she grew up. But the past did not
stay where she left it.

The first clear sign that something was still wrong came three years ago when her team hired a new
manager whose communication style was blunt and occasionally dismissive. He never said anything
overtly threatening, but the way he would lean over her desk to review her code, the way he sighed
when something wasn't right — her body responded as though she were twelve years old again. Her
heart would slam against her chest. Her mind would go blank. She began staying late to triple-check
her work before submitting it, terrified of making an error, though she could not always articulate
what she was afraid would happen if she did.

At home, she finds it difficult to be fully present. Her husband will reach for her hand in the
evening and she will feel herself go somewhere else — not consciously, just suddenly distant,
watching herself from a slight remove. She doesn't always know how to explain this to him, and so
she often doesn't try. She worries that her quietness reads as coldness, that he will eventually run
out of patience, though he has given her no reason to believe this.

Sleep is its own battleground. She frequently wakes in the early hours from dreams she can't always
remember but that leave her heart racing and her jaw aching from clenching. Some mornings she sits
at her desk before her screens come to life and feels a heaviness that she can't name or shake, a
sense that something terrible is about to happen even when the day ahead holds nothing more
threatening than a stand-up meeting and a code review.

She has never spoken to her mother about what happened. In her family, what her father did was
framed, when it was acknowledged at all, as discipline — as love expressed in the only language he
knew. Priya does not fully accept this framing, but she has not entirely rejected it either, and the
ambivalence sits in her like a splinter she can't quite reach. She loves her father in a way that
confuses and exhausts her. She has not visited her parents in over a year.

What brings Priya to therapy now is not one specific event but a slow accumulation of exhaustion.
She is tired of flinching when a colleague raises his voice across the office. She is tired of the
hypervigilance that hums constantly beneath her concentration, making it harder to do work she
otherwise loves. She is tired, most of all, of feeling like a stranger in a life she has worked very
hard to build. She wants, as she puts it quietly in the first session, to finally feel safe
somewhere — and to believe that she deserves to.
