# The Weight of an Empty Chair

Marco is a 28-year-old Colombian American man who works as a corrections officer at a medium-
security state facility. He has been married for three years to his wife, Daniela, and by most
outward measures, he presents as steady and composed — qualities that have served him well in a
profession that demands emotional control. Beneath that composure, however, something has been
quietly fracturing since the night, fourteen months ago, when his younger brother Sebastián was
killed in a car accident at the age of twenty-four.

It happened on a Friday evening. Marco had declined Sebastián's invitation to go out that night, too
tired after a long shift, and told him they'd catch up that weekend. Sebastián was struck by a drunk
driver on his way home. He died before the ambulance arrived. Marco received the call from his
mother at 11:47 p.m. — a time he knows precisely because he has replayed that moment so many times
that the image of his phone screen, glowing in the dark bedroom, has become almost architectural in
his mind. He remembers thinking, for a split second before answering, that Sebastián was probably
calling to tease him for bailing on the night out.

In the weeks following the accident, Marco returned to work quickly, telling himself and Daniela
that staying busy was the right thing to do. His colleagues noticed little change in him. He was
professional, punctual, reliable. But inside the facility, certain things began to feel unbearable
in ways he couldn't explain. The sound of a heavy door slamming — a sound he has heard thousands of
times — now sends a jolt through his chest that takes minutes to settle. He finds himself scanning
the cell block with a hypervigilance that goes beyond professional caution, as though something
catastrophic is always just about to happen.

At home, the difficulties are harder to hide. Marco sleeps poorly, waking between two and four in
the morning with his heart racing, sometimes from dreams in which he is driving toward Sebastián and
cannot stop the car, sometimes from no dream at all. He has begun avoiding the route he used to take
to his mother's house because it passes near the intersection where the accident occurred. He
stopped watching the television show he and Sebastián used to talk about every week, and he has not
touched the guitar that sits in the corner of the spare room — an instrument Sebastián had convinced
him to start learning just months before his death.

What troubles Marco most, and what he finds most difficult to articulate, is the guilt. He is a
rational man who understands, intellectually, that he could not have prevented what happened. But
rationality has little purchase on the thought that visits him most nights: that if he had gone out
with his brother, Sebastián would have been somewhere else, or left later, or taken a different
road. He carries this thought the way he carries his keys — always present, always within reach.

Daniela has noticed that he is different. She describes him as "far away," and she is not wrong.
Marco finds it difficult to feel genuinely close to her, or to feel much of anything that resembles
joy. He attends family gatherings because he believes he should, but he sits at the edges of them,
watching his relatives laugh and eat, feeling as though he is observing through glass. His mother
has stopped mentioning Sebastián's name around him, sensing that it costs him something. This, too,
grieves him — the way grief has made his brother unspeakable.

Marco describes himself as someone who has always been the strong one in his family, the one others
lean on. The idea of being undone by something invisible, something that leaves no mark anyone can
see, sits uneasily with his sense of who he is supposed to be. He comes to sessions with his hands
folded in his lap, speaking carefully, as though choosing each word is itself a form of holding
himself together.
