# The Weight of Borders

Marcus is twenty-one years old, and he has already lived several lifetimes. He grew up in a small
town in Eastern Europe, the kind of place where everyone knows your name and the summers smell like
cut grass and wood smoke. When he was sixteen, that world ended. Political violence swept through
his region with terrifying speed, and Marcus fled with his mother and younger sister over the course
of three weeks that he describes, when he can bring himself to describe them at all, as "just
walking and not dying." They crossed four borders. They slept in fields, in the back of a stranger's
truck, in a church basement that smelled of mildew and fear. At one crossing, soldiers separated
Marcus from his mother for six hours. He was certain, with the absolute conviction that only a
teenager in darkness can feel, that he would never see her again.

He did see her again. They made it. He tells himself this matters.

Marcus is now a junior public defender in a mid-sized European city, having fast-tracked through law
school on scholarship, driven by something he struggles to articulate but that his colleagues
recognize as ferocity. He is brilliant in the courtroom — composed, precise, relentless on behalf of
clients who have no one else. His supervisor has flagged him as exceptional. What his supervisor
does not see is that Marcus sometimes sits in his car for twenty minutes before entering the office,
waiting for a wave of nausea to pass that arrives without warning, without apparent cause, and
leaves him gripping the steering wheel until his knuckles whiten.

The nausea is one thing. The sleep is another. Most nights, Marcus wakes between two and four in the
morning with his heart hammering and the absolute, cellular certainty that something terrible is
happening right now, in this room, to the people he loves. He gets up, checks the locks, sometimes
texts his girlfriend Lena — who is patient and bewildered and trying — just to see the three dots
appear and know she is alive. He rarely goes back to sleep. He functions on five hours and coffee
and the particular adrenaline of a man who has learned that rest is a luxury the world does not
reliably offer.

His work brings its own complications. When he sits across from clients who are asylum seekers —
which is often, given the nature of his practice — he feels something crack open in his chest that
he cannot close again for days. He is effective for them, fiercely so, but afterward he finds
himself unable to eat, replaying their testimony alongside his own memories in a loop that blurs the
boundaries between their stories and his. He has started avoiding certain case files. He hates
himself for it.

Crowds trigger something animal in him. Loud, sudden noises — a car backfiring, a colleague slamming
a door — send a jolt of electricity through his body so acute that he has to excuse himself to the
bathroom and stand with cold water running over his wrists. He is hypervigilant in a way that
exhausts him: always facing the door in restaurants, always knowing where the exits are, always
half-listening for something that never quite arrives.

Lena knows something is wrong. She is warm and she loves him and she does not fully understand why
he sometimes goes somewhere unreachable in the middle of an ordinary evening, staring at nothing,
returning slowly like a man surfacing from deep water. He has not told her about the crossing, about
the six hours without his mother, about the things he saw on the road. He has not told anyone. He
carries it the way he carries everything — quietly, efficiently, and entirely alone, as if the act
of surviving obligates him to make it look easy.

He is twenty-one years old, and he is very, very tired.
