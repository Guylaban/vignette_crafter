# The Weight of What He Set in Motion

Youssef is a 78-year-old Egyptian-born environmental scientist who spent the better part of five
decades studying the slow, patient rhythms of ecosystems — how damage accumulates, how recovery is
possible, and how sometimes it is not. He has always believed that careful observation prevents
catastrophe. That belief collapsed three years ago on a Tuesday morning in early spring.

He had been driving to a wetlands restoration site outside the city, a project he had championed for
years and planned to see through into retirement. He was running slightly late, distracted by a
phone call he had just ended — his wife reminding him about a dinner that evening. He tells himself
he was not speeding. He tells himself he checked the intersection. What he knows for certain is that
a young cyclist, a woman in her late twenties, appeared from nowhere in his peripheral vision, and
then there was a sound he cannot describe except to say it was wrong, fundamentally wrong, in the
way a bone sounds wrong when it breaks. He pulled over immediately. He held her hand until the
ambulance came. She survived, but she lost the use of her right arm permanently.

In the weeks that followed, Youssef found himself unable to return to the wetlands site. He told his
colleagues he was unwell, which was true in ways he did not elaborate on. He began waking at 3 or 4
in the morning with his heart hammering, replaying the sequence of the morning — the phone call, the
turn, the sound — searching for the exact moment he could have changed it. He lies beside his wife
Fatima, who sleeps quietly, and he does not wake her. He has not told her the full weight of what he
carries. She knows he was in an accident. She does not know that he sometimes sits at the kitchen
table before dawn, staring at his hands.

He retired from fieldwork six months after the accident, earlier than planned. He tells people his
knees were going. He has not driven on that road since, and he has reorganized his entire life to
avoid it — adding twenty minutes to every journey across the city. When he passes cyclists anywhere,
his chest tightens and he grips the wheel until his knuckles pale. Some days he avoids driving
altogether, asking Fatima to take him to appointments, offering no reason she finds convincing.

The intrusions are not always dramatic. Sometimes it is simply the quality of spring light — that
particular pale gold of an early morning — that returns him instantly to the intersection. He will
be standing in his garden, examining a patch of soil, and suddenly he is there again, hearing that
sound. He has stopped working in the garden in the mornings. He does not fully understand why until
he is asked.

He describes himself as a man who has become, in his own words, "smaller." He no longer attends the
environmental consultancy meetings he once led with quiet authority. He declined an invitation to
speak at a conference honoring his career contributions. He told the organizers he was traveling. He
was at home. He sat in his study surrounded by forty years of field journals and felt like a fraud —
a man who preached the ethics of environmental stewardship and could not manage the stewardship of a
single intersection.

What troubles him most, he says, is not the nightmares or the sleeplessness, though those are
terrible. What troubles him is the feeling that his entire professional identity — built on the
premise that careful, responsible action protects the vulnerable — has been revealed as a story he
told himself. He watched ecosystems heal for fifty years. He is not certain the same is true of
people. He is not certain it is true of him.

He comes to sessions on time. He is courteous, precise with language, and deeply reluctant to cry,
though sometimes he does anyway, and looks briefly startled by himself each time.
