# The Weight of the Platform

Tariq is twenty-nine years old and has worked as a train conductor for the past four years. He
describes the job as one he fell into almost by accident — a cousin knew someone at the transit
authority, there was an opening, and the pay was steady. He liked the rhythm of it at first: the
routes, the regularity, the feeling of moving people from one place to another without incident.
That sense of order is something he no longer takes for granted.

Fourteen months ago, on a Tuesday evening in late autumn, Tariq was working the westbound commuter
line when a man fell — or jumped, the investigation never fully resolved it — from the platform
directly in front of the train. Tariq was not the engineer; he was in the passenger car managing
ticketing toward the rear. But he heard the impact, felt the shudder move through the floor beneath
his feet, and was one of the first crew members to reach the front of the train. What he saw on the
tracks he describes only in fragments, and only once, early in treatment. He says he stood there for
what felt like an hour but was probably less than two minutes before emergency services arrived. He
remembers a woman on the platform screaming. He remembers not being able to move.

In the weeks that followed, Tariq returned to work because he believed he should. He told himself it
was an isolated incident, that people who work in transit accept certain realities. He did not tell
his family what he had seen. His mother called regularly from their family home and he answered
cheerfully, asking about his younger siblings, deflecting her questions about whether he was eating.

He was not, particularly. Sleep became difficult almost immediately. He would lie down and find
himself back on that platform, the shudder moving through his feet again. He began waking at two or
three in the morning, heart pounding, certain something terrible was about to happen without being
able to name what. He started checking the locks on his apartment door multiple times before bed and
again before he could fall back asleep.

At work, Tariq moves through his shifts in a state of sustained vigilance that exhausts him by
midday. He positions himself near the doors when the train approaches stations and finds himself
scanning the platforms for anyone standing too close to the edge. When a passenger stumbles or a bag
drops suddenly, his body responds before his mind can — heart rate spiking, hands going cold. He has
developed a habit of counting the passengers in his car, a ritual he knows is irrational but cannot
stop performing. He has called in sick eleven times in the past six months.

Outside of work, Tariq has withdrawn from most of the friendships he maintained before the incident.
He used to play football on weekends with a group of men from his neighborhood. He stopped going in
January and has not returned. He tells himself he is tired, which is true, but he also notices that
being in crowds now produces a low-grade dread he cannot fully explain. He does not like situations
he cannot quickly leave.

What troubles Tariq most, and what he admits to with some reluctance, is that he no longer feels
like himself. He describes a flatness — not sadness exactly, but an absence of the feeling he used
to have when something good happened. His cousin got married in the spring and Tariq attended and
smiled and said the right things, but inside, he says, it was like watching through glass. He
wonders sometimes if something was permanently rearranged in him that night on the platform, if the
man he was before is simply gone.

He sits across from his therapist each week with his hands folded in his lap, speaking carefully, as
though the right words might finally make sense of what he cannot stop carrying.
