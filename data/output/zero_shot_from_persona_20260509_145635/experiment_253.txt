# The Weight of Numbers

Marco is twenty-four years old, and he spends his days surrounded by figures that are supposed to
mean something — quarterly projections, risk assessments, variance reports. He is good at his job.
His supervisor says he has a gift for finding patterns in noise. What his supervisor does not know
is that Marco learned to read chaos long before he ever opened a spreadsheet.

He grew up in a close-knit Colombian-American family in South Florida, the eldest son of parents who
had sacrificed everything to give their children stability. At nineteen, partly out of duty and
partly out of a young man's hunger for purpose, Marco enlisted in the Army. He deployed to
Afghanistan at twenty-one. For the first several months, the deployment felt manageable — tense,
unfamiliar, but survivable. Then, during a patrol in a rural district outside Kandahar, his unit was
ambushed. The firefight lasted less than twelve minutes. His closest friend, a twenty-two-year-old
from Texas named Darnell, died beside him. Marco held him. He remembers the exact quality of the
light that afternoon — flat and white, the way light gets before a storm — and he has not been able
to tolerate that particular shade of brightness since.

He was honorably discharged at twenty-three, came home, enrolled in a finance program, and threw
himself into building the life his parents had imagined for him. On paper, he is succeeding. In
practice, he is barely holding together.

Marco sleeps in short, broken stretches. When he does fall deeply asleep, he wakes shouting, certain
that he is back in that dry corridor of road, that the ground is shaking, that Darnell is still
reachable if he moves fast enough. He has stopped telling his roommate about the nightmares because
he ran out of ways to explain them. He keeps a lamp on all night now. He tells himself it is a
preference.

At work, the open-plan office is its own kind of minefield. A chair scraping across the floor, a
door slamming down the hallway, a colleague dropping a stack of binders — any of it can send his
heart into a full sprint. He has learned to position himself with his back to the wall and a clear
view of the exit. He frames this to himself as good professional habit. He knows it is not.

The intrusions come without warning. He will be reviewing a client portfolio and suddenly he is not
in the office — he is crouched behind a compound wall, his hands are wet, and Darnell is asking him
something he cannot make out. These episodes leave him disoriented for the rest of the afternoon,
his concentration shattered, his chest tight. He has started declining team lunches and after-work
drinks, finding reasons to leave before anyone can get too close. Closeness feels dangerous now in a
way he cannot fully articulate.

He carries a persistent, low-grade conviction that he should not have made it home. He does not talk
about this with anyone. His family sees a young man who is quiet and driven and perhaps a little
serious. His mother worries that he works too hard. She does not know that the work is the only
thing that keeps the other thoughts at bay — that numbers, unlike memory, stay where he puts them.

He has not been back to Florida in eight months. He tells his parents the job keeps him busy. The
truth is that his childhood bedroom has a window that faces west, and in the late afternoon the
light comes in at an angle that reminds him of Kandahar, and he is not ready for that yet.

Marco sits at his desk each morning, pulls up his models, and does what he was trained to do: he
finds the signal inside the noise. He is still trying to figure out whether that skill works in the
other direction — whether there is any order to be found inside what he carries home each night.
