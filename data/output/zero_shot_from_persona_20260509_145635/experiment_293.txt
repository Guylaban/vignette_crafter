# Vignette: Meena

Meena is a 73-year-old South Asian woman who has worked as an interior designer for nearly four
decades. She built her practice slowly and deliberately, the way she does everything — with
attention to proportion, to light, to the way a space can make a person feel safe or exposed. She is
proud of this work. Or she was, before the evening two years ago when everything she understood
about safety was rewritten.

It happened outside a community center in her neighborhood, where she had gone to attend a meeting
about a proposed development that would displace several local families she knew. The protest that
followed was peaceful, she insists — people with signs, people with voices. But the police response
was not. In the confusion of dispersal, Meena was pushed to the ground by an officer, her face
pressed against the pavement, her wrists zip-tied behind her back. She remembers the weight of a
knee against her back, the inability to breathe fully, the sound of her own voice asking for help
and receiving no answer. She was held for several hours before being released without charge. She
was 71 years old. She had never been in trouble with the law in her life.

In the weeks afterward, Meena tells herself she is fine. She is a practical woman. She has survived
things before — the death of her husband six years earlier, the disorientation of immigration
decades before that. She keeps her appointments. She answers her emails. But she notices that she
cannot take the route past the community center anymore, that she crosses to the other side of the
street when she sees police vehicles, that her body does something she cannot quite control — a
flooding heat, a tightening in her chest, a sudden and total certainty that something terrible is
about to happen.

Sleep becomes unreliable. She wakes at two or three in the morning with her heart pounding, the
sensation of the pavement still present in her body, the weight still on her back. She does not
always know, in those first moments of waking, that she is in her own bedroom. Her daughter, who
calls every Sunday, notices that her mother sounds flat, distant, like someone speaking from behind
glass.

The work suffers in ways Meena finds humiliating. She has always had an instinct for space — for
knowing when a room feels constricting and when it breathes. Now she sits in clients' homes and
feels only the walls. She cancels two consultations, something she has never done in her career. A
third she attends but cannot concentrate; she keeps losing the thread of what the client is saying,
pulled back without warning into a moment she cannot stop replaying.

She has begun to believe, in a way she cannot entirely reason herself out of, that what happened to
her was not an accident or an error but a message — that at 73, South Asian, a woman alone, she is
not the kind of person the world is arranged to protect. This belief sits in her quietly and changes
the color of everything. She no longer attends community meetings. She has withdrawn from a
neighborhood design collective she helped found. She tells herself she is simply tired, that this is
what getting older looks like, but she knows that is not entirely true.

What she finds hardest to explain, even to herself, is the shame. She was not doing anything wrong.
She knows this. And yet the memory of lying on that pavement, helpless, visible to everyone passing,
carries with it a humiliation she cannot seem to put down. She is a woman who has spent her
professional life making spaces dignified, livable, considered. She cannot reconcile that person
with the one whose face was pressed against the concrete while people watched and no one came.

She comes to therapy because her daughter asked her to. She sits very straight in the chair.
