# The Weight of the White Coat

Roberto is a 67-year-old Colombian-born internist who has practiced medicine in the United States
for nearly four decades. He is soft-spoken, precise in his language, and carries himself with the
composed authority that comes from a lifetime of being the person in the room who is supposed to
have answers. He has never married, though he came close once. He lives alone in a modest apartment
near the hospital where he still works three days a week, surrounded by medical journals he no
longer reads with the same appetite he once did.

Eighteen years ago, Roberto's eleven-year-old son, Diego — who lived primarily with his mother in
Bogotá following their separation — was diagnosed with acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Roberto learned
of it over the phone, a Tuesday morning between patients. He remembers the fluorescent hum of the
hallway, the weight of the receiver, the way his own medical knowledge became a kind of weapon
turned against him. He understood, with clinical precision, exactly how bad it could get.

He flew to Colombia within forty-eight hours and spent the next fourteen months navigating a
terrifying middle space — neither Diego's primary caregiver nor a detached observer, but a
physician-father who knew too much and could control almost nothing. He sat beside Diego's hospital
bed watching infusions drip and interpreting every lab value with an expertise that brought him no
comfort. There were two episodes of septic crisis during which Roberto genuinely believed he was
watching his son die. Diego survived. He is now a healthy twenty-nine-year-old living in Medellín,
married, with a daughter of his own.

Roberto will tell you, quietly, that Diego is fine. What Roberto does not say easily is that he is
not.

The intrusions began while Diego was still in treatment and never fully stopped. Roberto wakes most
nights between two and four in the morning with his heart already racing, pulled from sleep by
images he cannot always name upon waking — the blue tint of Diego's lips during one crisis, the
particular sound of a cardiac monitor's alarm, the face of the oncologist delivering a complication
update. He lies in the dark and runs through the memory like a film he cannot switch off, his body
convinced the emergency is still happening.

At the hospital, Roberto is competent and respected. But pediatric cases have become nearly
impossible for him. When he encounters a seriously ill child — or even hears colleagues discussing
one — he feels a physical constriction in his chest, a narrowing of his vision, a desperate urge to
leave the floor. He has quietly arranged his practice to minimize such encounters, a fact he has
never disclosed to his department chief. He tells himself this is a matter of specialization.

He has become, over the years, someone who keeps people at a careful distance. Colleagues describe
him as reserved. He declines most social invitations, finding that gatherings feel effortful in a
way they did not before — as though he is performing a version of himself that no longer quite fits.
He drinks two glasses of scotch most evenings, which he monitors carefully, telling himself that
monitoring it means it is not a problem.

What troubles Roberto most, when he allows himself to examine it, is the guilt that has never
loosened its grip. He was a physician. He understood the disease. And still he felt helpless — worse
than helpless, useless — sitting in that hospital room in Bogotá. He has never been able to
reconcile the doctor he believes himself to be with the father who sat in a plastic chair and wept
in a bathroom stall so his son would not hear him. He carries this as a private failure, tucked
beneath the white coat he still puts on three mornings a week, walking into a hospital where
everyone assumes he is fine because he has always appeared to be.
