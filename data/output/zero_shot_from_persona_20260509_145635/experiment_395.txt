# Vignette: Fatima, Age 68

Fatima has spent the better part of four decades working inside the walls of a women's correctional
facility, and for most of that time she carried herself with the kind of quiet authority that
younger officers came to admire. She was the one who knew how to de-escalate a situation with a
look, who remembered inmates' birthdays, who believed that her presence in that place mattered. That
sense of purpose is something she now struggles to locate.

Three years ago, during what should have been a routine transfer escort, an inmate attacked her from
behind in a corridor. The woman had concealed a sharpened piece of metal, and before backup arrived,
Fatima had sustained lacerations to her left forearm and neck and had been slammed hard against a
concrete wall. She remembers the sound of her own head hitting the surface more vividly than almost
anything else — a dense, hollow crack — and then the strange stillness that followed before the
alarms began.

She returned to work after a medical leave of six weeks. Her supervisors praised her resilience. Her
husband, Karim, brought her tea every morning and told her she was strong. Fatima agreed with all of
them, because agreeing felt like the only sensible thing to do. She is from a family that survived
displacement and loss long before this, and the idea of being undone by a single incident felt, to
her, almost shameful.

But things have not been right since then. The corridor where the assault happened runs through her
dreams several nights a week. In the dreams it is always longer than it actually is, and she is
always walking toward something she cannot see. She wakes before she reaches it, her heart pounding,
Karim's steady breathing beside her doing little to slow her own. She does not wake him. She goes to
the kitchen and sits until the feeling passes.

At work, she has quietly rearranged her routines so that she rarely has to walk that corridor alone.
Her colleagues have not noticed, or if they have, they have said nothing. She is still competent,
still professional, still the person others defer to in difficult moments — and yet she finds
herself scanning every room she enters, tracking the position of every person near her, calculating
exits. It is exhausting in a way she cannot fully explain to Karim, who asks her, gently, why she
seems so far away at dinner.

She does not startle easily in front of others. She has too much pride for that. But alone, a door
slamming or a sudden shout from the yard can send her pulse into a kind of controlled panic that
takes long minutes to subside. She has begun to feel that her body no longer entirely belongs to her
— that it operates on its own threat assessments, independent of what her mind knows to be true.

What troubles her most, though, is something harder to name. She used to feel connected to her work
in a way that sustained her. Now she moves through her shifts with a flatness she finds bewildering.
She watches younger officers with something close to envy — not for their youth, but for their
belief that the place they are standing is basically safe. She no longer shares that belief, and she
is not sure she ever will again.

At home, she is present in the ways that matter: she cooks, she calls her daughters, she sits with
Karim in the evenings and watches television. But there is a pane of glass between her and all of
it. She can see her life clearly from where she stands. She simply cannot seem to get back inside
it.

She has not spoken about any of this at length to anyone, including her physician, who cleared her
for full duty and noted that she was coping well. Fatima did not correct him. She thanked him and
drove herself back to work.
