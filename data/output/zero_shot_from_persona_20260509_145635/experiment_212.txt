**Vignette: Fatima, Age 69**

Fatima came to this country from Lebanon more than thirty years ago, building a quiet life with her
husband in a mid-sized American city. She raised her children, learned English through persistence
and necessity, and when her youngest left home, she took a part-time job at a fast food restaurant
near her neighborhood — not because she needed the income badly, but because she needed the
movement, the routine, the small dignities of being useful. She has worked the morning shift there
for eleven years. Her coworkers are decades younger than she is, and she has always liked that. It
kept her feeling present in the world.

Three years ago, a man began coming into the restaurant every morning and sitting in the same corner
booth. At first Fatima thought nothing of it. Regulars were common. But he began timing his visits
to her shifts specifically, and she noticed. He would watch her. When she moved to wipe tables, he
moved his eyes. When she spoke to other customers, his jaw tightened. He began leaving her notes
folded beneath his coffee cup — first compliments, then declarations, then something that felt like
ownership. When she stopped acknowledging him, the notes became threatening. He began appearing
outside her bus stop. Once, he followed her two blocks toward her home before she ducked into a
pharmacy, hands shaking, and called her husband.

The police were involved. The man was warned, eventually charged. He moved away, or at least
disappeared from her immediate life. Legally, the situation resolved. Inside Fatima, nothing
resolved at all.

She still works the morning shift, though her husband now drives her and waits in the parking lot
until she is inside. She will not take the bus anymore. The corner booth where the man used to sit
remains empty most mornings, and Fatima cannot look at it directly. She routes her cleaning path
around it. When a male customer lingers too long over his coffee, her chest tightens and her hands
go cold and she finds reasons to stay behind the counter. She has never told her manager why she
sometimes needs a moment in the back room, standing with her palms pressed flat against the
stainless steel counter, steadying herself.

At home, she sleeps poorly. She wakes between two and four in the morning and lies still, listening
to sounds in the hallway, certain for a few disorienting seconds that someone is in the house. Her
husband has learned not to reach for her suddenly in the dark. Once, he touched her shoulder to wake
her and she screamed — a sound she says she did not recognize as her own. She has not told her adult
children the full extent of what happened. In her understanding of herself, in the culture she
carries inside her, a woman her age is supposed to have moved past vulnerability. She feels a deep
shame about this that she has difficulty naming.

She describes herself as someone who used to enjoy people. She was the woman who remembered
customers' names, who asked after their children. Now she finds herself scanning faces for threat
before she sees them as human. She has stopped attending her mosque's women's group, which she loved
for years, because the walk there requires passing an alley that reminds her of the pharmacy doorway
where she hid. The memory lives in her body as much as in her mind — a particular quality of cold in
her fingers, a narrowing behind her sternum.

What she says she wants most is to stop feeling like she is still waiting for him to come back. She
knows, intellectually, that he is gone. But some part of her — the part that wakes at three in the
morning and listens — does not believe it yet. She is sixty-nine years old, she says, and she did
not expect to spend these years being afraid.
