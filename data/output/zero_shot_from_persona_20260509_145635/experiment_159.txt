# Vignette: Nadia, Age 51

Nadia grew up in a household where silence was the safest strategy. The eldest of five children in a
conservative Lebanese family, she learned early that her father's moods were unpredictable and that
his hands could move faster than his words. The beatings were not occasional — they were the
architecture of daily life. A misplaced dish, a grade that fell short of his expectations, a glance
he interpreted as defiance — any of these could bring him to her room at night or catch her in the
kitchen before school. Her mother watched from doorways and said nothing. Nadia tells herself she
understood, even then, that her mother was afraid too. She is not sure she fully believes this.

She left Lebanon at nineteen, married a man she describes as "safe but distant," and built what
looked, from the outside, like a stable life. She raised two sons, completed a university degree in
social work, and eventually settled into a position with a community organization that provides
support services to refugee families and survivors of domestic violence. The work, she says, chose
her. She understood something about these families that her colleagues did not — the way a person
can move through a room while bracing for impact, the way a child's eyes track an adult's hands
before they track their face.

For most of her adult life, Nadia managed. She kept busy, kept useful, kept moving. Then, three
years ago, her divorce was finalized, her younger son left for university abroad, and the house went
quiet. The quiet, she says, is when it all came back.

She began waking at three or four in the morning, heart pounding, certain there was someone in the
hallway. She would lie rigid, listening, sometimes for an hour, before she could convince herself to
get up and check. She started sleeping with the bedroom light on. During the day, she found herself
scanning rooms when she entered them — noting exits, positioning herself with her back to walls. She
did not connect this to anything at first. She thought she was simply anxious, perhaps
perimenopausal, perhaps lonely.

The intrusions began more gradually. A client at work — a young woman whose husband had broken her
wrist — described the sound of his footsteps coming down the hall, and Nadia had to excuse herself
to the bathroom, where she sat on the floor for ten minutes, shaking, unable to explain to herself
why. She began to dread home visits. She started calling in sick on days when she knew the caseload
would be heavy with abuse cases, and then felt a crushing shame about it, because these were the
people she had devoted her career to helping.

She has become short-tempered in ways that unsettle her. She snapped at a colleague last month and
afterward sat in her car and wept. She describes a persistent sense of being hollowed out — present
in body but watching herself from some slight distance, as though she is a person she is not quite
sure she trusts. She sleeps poorly, eats irregularly, and has largely stopped calling friends. She
tells herself she has nothing to say that would not burden them.

What brings her in now is a sentence one of her clients said to her during a session: *You deserve
to be safe too.* Nadia had been trying to say something similar to this woman for months. Hearing it
directed back at her, she says, was like being handed something she had no idea where to put. She
sat with it for two weeks before she made the appointment.

She arrives to sessions composed and articulate, professional in her manner, careful with her
language. Only occasionally — when a particular memory surfaces, or when the conversation moves too
close to her father — does something shift in her face, something younger and unguarded, and she
goes very still, the way a person does when they are listening for footsteps in the hall.
