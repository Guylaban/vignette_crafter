# The Weight of the Switch

Marcus is twenty-two years old, and he runs a school.

It is an unlikely thing for someone his age, and he knows it. Every morning he walks through the
front doors of the small community primary school where he was appointed acting principal eight
months ago, and he straightens his tie before the children can see him. He has learned to hold
himself like someone older. He learned that long before he ever set foot in an administrative
office.

He grew up in a rural township in Eastern Africa, the eldest of four children, raised primarily by
his uncle after his mother's illness forced her to send the children away. His uncle was a respected
man in the community — a deacon, a disciplinarian, a man who believed that suffering produced
character. What happened inside the house was understood to be private. Marcus understood this from
the time he was six years old, which is roughly when the beatings began in earnest.

They were not random. That is the part Marcus still turns over in his mind, even now, even at
twenty-two, even when he is supposed to be reviewing the school's quarterly budget. The beatings
followed rules. A poor grade, a slow response, a perceived disrespect — each infraction had a
corresponding punishment, delivered with a switch or a belt or, on certain occasions, an open hand
across the face. His uncle would explain the reason beforehand, calmly, the way a judge reads a
sentence. Marcus learned to stand very still and to make no sound. Making sound made it worse.

He left for boarding school at thirteen and did not go back. He excelled, because excelling was the
only language he had ever been taught to speak. He earned a teaching certificate at nineteen. He was
appointed to his current role at twenty-one. People in his district call him a prodigy. He smiles
when they say it and thanks them and goes home and cannot sleep.

Sleep is where his uncle finds him. Not every night, but often enough that Marcus has begun to dread
the hours between midnight and four in the morning. He wakes with his heart striking hard against
his ribs, the smell of wood smoke in his nose, though there is no wood smoke in his flat. His
girlfriend, Amara, reaches for him when this happens, and he moves to the edge of the bed without
meaning to. He does not want to be touched when he wakes that way. He has tried to explain this to
her. He is not sure he has succeeded.

During the day, the school provides structure, and structure has always been his armor. But there
are moments that break through it. When he raises his voice at a teacher during a staff meeting and
sees the flinch — that small, involuntary drawing inward — he feels something cold move through him.
He apologizes afterward, formally, thoroughly. He cannot stop seeing his uncle in himself in that
moment, and the thought makes him want to walk out of the building and not return.

He monitors the teachers' discipline practices with an attention that colleagues find unusual in
someone so young. He has written three separate policies on corporal punishment. He cannot explain
to anyone why this feels so urgent, why his hands go slightly cold when he hears a child cry
somewhere in the hallways.

He scores seventy-one on the checklist his doctor gave him, though he does not know what the number
means. He knows that he is tired in a way that sleep does not fix. He knows that he loves Amara but
cannot let her fully in. He knows that he became a principal because he wanted to build something
different from what was built inside him — a place where children are not afraid of the people who
are supposed to protect them.

He is not sure, on the harder mornings, whether he has built that place, or whether he has only
built a very convincing version of it.

He straightens his tie. He walks through the doors.
