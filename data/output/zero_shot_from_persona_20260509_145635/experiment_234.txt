# The Weight of Compliance

Roberto is a 53-year-old Colombian-born tax consultant who has lived in the United States for nearly
two decades. He built his life here methodically — first earning his credentials, then his clients,
then a modest but comfortable apartment in a mid-sized city where he raised two daughters alongside
his now ex-wife. He is a careful man by nature, the kind who double-checks figures and keeps
meticulous records, someone who believed deeply that if you followed the rules, the rules would
protect you.

Three years ago, that belief was taken from him.

Roberto had been returning home from a late client meeting when he was pulled over for what officers
later described as a broken taillight. What followed was not a traffic stop. Within minutes, he was
ordered out of the car, thrown against the hood, and restrained on the pavement while two officers
shouted commands he struggled to process through his accent and his terror. A third officer pressed
a knee into his back. Roberto remembers the concrete cold against his cheek, the smell of motor oil,
and the absolute certainty — for several long minutes — that he was going to die. He was released
without charges. No one apologized. He drove himself home with bruised ribs and a split lip, and he
did not tell his daughters.

In the weeks that followed, Roberto kept working. Tax season was approaching and his clients needed
him. He told himself the incident was over. But his body refused to agree. He stopped sleeping
through the night, waking at two or three in the morning with his heart hammering, certain he could
hear someone at the door. He began taking longer routes to avoid the intersection where it happened,
then longer routes to avoid police vehicles entirely, then longer routes that sometimes added forty
minutes to his commute. He noticed he was holding his breath in elevators. He noticed he could no
longer concentrate on a balance sheet the way he once could — numbers blurred, his mind drifted, and
then snapped back to the pavement.

His marriage, already strained, did not survive the year. His ex-wife said he had become
unreachable. He does not entirely disagree. He describes himself now as someone who watches
conversations from a slight distance, as though behind glass. He laughs less. He is quicker to anger
and then ashamed of it. He has stopped attending his church, where several members are law
enforcement officers, and he has not explained why.

At work, Roberto functions — but only through enormous effort. He has developed elaborate systems to
compensate for the concentration difficulties he never had before: color-coded files, redundant
checklists, reminders set for tasks he once completed automatically. His clients notice he is
quieter. One long-standing client asked if he was all right, and Roberto said he was tired. He has
said he is tired many times now.

What troubles him most, he says, is not the fear — it is the shame. He grew up in Bogotá during a
period of significant civil unrest and crossed an ocean specifically to escape the feeling of being
unsafe in his own country. The idea that he has arrived at the same feeling through different
geography is something he circles around and cannot quite touch directly. He wonders, quietly and
persistently, whether he did something wrong that night, whether his accent, his appearance, some
failure of compliance on his part invited what happened. He reviews it the way he reviews a tax
return with an error he cannot locate — methodically, repeatedly, certain the mistake must be his.

His daughters have noticed the changes in him. The younger one, who is studying pre-law, recently
asked him to tell her what happened. He changed the subject. He is not ready, he thinks, to hand
something so unresolved to someone he loves.
