# The Weight of the Badge

Marco is a 54-year-old Colombian-American man who has spent the better part of three decades working
as a parole officer in a mid-sized city in the American Southwest. He is broad-shouldered and
deliberate in the way he speaks, a man who chose his career because he believed in second chances —
a belief that now sits uneasily inside him, like something he swallowed that won't quite go down.

Four years ago, Marco was driving home from a late shift when he was pulled over in a neighborhood
two miles from his house. He had done nothing wrong. He was in civilian clothes, his badge locked in
the glove compartment. Before he could reach for it, two officers had him on the pavement, a knee on
his neck, gravel pressing into his cheek. He remembers the cold of the asphalt, the smell of
exhaust, the sound of his own voice — calm at first, then desperate — telling them he was law
enforcement, telling them his name. He remembers thinking, with absolute clarity, that he was going
to die on that street. The incident lasted eleven minutes. It felt like a lifetime compressed into
something he can no longer fully unfold.

He drove himself home afterward, sat in the driveway for an hour, and said nothing to his wife,
Esperanza, when he came through the door. He told her he was tired. That was true. He has been tired
ever since.

In the weeks that followed, Marco found himself unable to sleep past three in the morning. He would
lie rigid beside Esperanza, listening to the house settle, convinced something was wrong, though he
could never say exactly what. When he did sleep, he woke from dreams that left him drenched and
disoriented — dreams in which the pavement never ends, in which no one comes. He stopped taking the
highway to work, rerouting through surface streets to avoid passing the intersection where it
happened. He still does this, four years later, adding twenty minutes to every commute without ever
consciously deciding to.

At work, Marco moves through his caseload with a kind of mechanical efficiency that his supervisor
mistakes for professionalism. He supervises men and women who have been released from prison, many
of them people of color, and he is good at his job — or he used to feel that way. Now, when he sits
across from a young man who has been roughed up by police, Marco feels something shift behind his
sternum, a recognition he doesn't know what to do with. He sometimes ends these meetings early. He
tells himself he has paperwork.

He has stopped attending the monthly inter-agency meetings where parole officers sit alongside law
enforcement. He submitted a medical excuse the first time. After that, no one asked.

Esperanza knows something is wrong. She has known since the night he came home silent and sat in the
driveway. She has tried to reach him — gently at first, then more directly — and he responds by
reassuring her, by changing the subject, by becoming very busy with small tasks around the house. He
loves her and cannot explain to her what happened to him without also explaining what it means: that
the institution he has given his life to nearly took his. He isn't ready for her to see how much
that has unmade him.

He describes himself, in his more honest moments, as a man who used to believe in systems. He still
goes to work. He still files his reports. He still stops at the same bakery on Thursday mornings and
orders the same coffee. But somewhere beneath all of that routine, Marco is still lying on cold
pavement, still waiting for someone to believe him, still listening for footsteps that may or may
not be walking away.
