# The Weight of Small Hands

Marco is twenty-five years old, a data scientist at a mid-sized tech firm in Austin, and the kind of
person who has always found comfort in numbers. Patterns make sense to him. Probabilities feel like
solid ground. What happened three years ago with his younger sister, Valentina, shook that ground in
ways he is still trying to understand.

Valentina was seven when she was diagnosed with acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Marco was twenty-two,
the oldest of four children, and the one their mother leaned on most heavily when their father was
working double shifts. He remembers the afternoon the diagnosis came clearly — the fluorescent
lights of the pediatric oncology ward, the way the doctor's voice seemed to arrive from somewhere
far away, and Valentina sitting on the examination table swinging her small legs, not yet
understanding what any of it meant. Marco understood enough for both of them.

For the next fourteen months, he drove his mother to the hospital three times a week, sat in waiting
rooms with his laptop open, and tried to keep working while Valentina moved in and out of crises
that the medical team described in statistics he knew how to read but wished he didn't. There were
two nights when the family was told to prepare themselves. He held his mother both times. He did not
cry until he was alone in his car.

Valentina survived. She is now ten, healthy, back in school, obsessed with soccer. Everyone in the
family speaks of her recovery as a miracle, a closed chapter, a reason to be grateful. Marco agrees
with all of this and means it. But something in him did not close when the chapter did.

He notices it most at night. Sleep comes reluctantly and leaves early, interrupted by dreams that
don't always feature Valentina directly but carry the same feeling — the sensation of waiting for
something terrible, of being responsible for preventing something he cannot control. He wakes with
his heart already racing, lying still in the dark of his apartment, running through a kind of
internal checklist until his body believes it is safe.

At work, he is good at what he does, but concentration has become effortful in a way it never used
to be. He finds himself reading the same block of code three times, his mind drifting back to a
particular image — Valentina's hand bruised from an IV line, her hair gone, her eyes still trusting
him completely. He has built small rituals to manage these moments: a specific playlist, a
particular brand of coffee, a desk organized just so. His colleagues find him a little rigid. He
doesn't explain.

He has pulled back from the family somewhat, which confuses him and produces guilt he carries
quietly. Visits to his mother's house, which now include a laughing, healthy Valentina, sometimes
trigger a tightness in his chest he cannot entirely explain. He loves her. Seeing her well should
feel like relief, and it does, but underneath the relief there is something else — a residue of all
those months of dread that his nervous system does not seem to know is over.

He has not been in a relationship since before Valentina's diagnosis. He tells himself he has been
busy, which is partly true. But he also notices that closeness feels risky in a way it didn't
before, as though loving someone creates a vulnerability he is no longer sure he can afford.

He came to therapy after a colleague mentioned offhandedly that Marco seemed "somewhere else"
lately. The observation landed harder than it should have. He sat with it for two weeks before
making the call. In his first session, he described himself as fine, then paused, then said — almost
to himself — that he thought he might still be in that waiting room. That he wasn't sure he had ever
really left.
