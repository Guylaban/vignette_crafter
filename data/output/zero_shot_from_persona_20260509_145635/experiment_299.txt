**Vignette: Mae**

Mae is a 57-year-old married woman of Thai descent who has run a small fabric and sewing notions
shop in the same neighborhood for nearly twenty years. She is soft-spoken and precise in the way she
describes things — the kind of person who folds cloth along exactly the right grain before she cuts
it. That same precision, she says, is what makes it so hard when her mind refuses to stay where she
puts it.

Eight years ago, Mae's youngest son, Decha, then fourteen, collapsed at school during a physical
education class. What followed was three weeks in a pediatric intensive care unit, a diagnosis of
viral myocarditis, two episodes where physicians told Mae and her husband that Decha might not
survive the night, and an emergency transfer to a larger hospital two hours from their home. Mae
slept in waiting rooms. She watched nurses move quickly and quietly around her son's bed in ways
that told her more than any doctor's words. She remembers the particular sound of the cardiac
monitor — a steady, ordinary beep that she learned to listen to with her whole body, terrified of
any change. Decha survived. He is now a healthy young man in university. Mae knows this. She reminds
herself of it often. It does not always help.

In the years since, Mae has rebuilt most of the surface of her life. The shop opens at nine. She
orders inventory, advises customers on thread weight and interfacing, teaches a small alterations
class on Thursday evenings. Her husband notices that she seems fine. She mostly is, in the way that
a person can be fine while carrying something heavy in both hands.

What Mae does not tell many people is that she still cannot hear a certain kind of alarm — a medical
alert tone, the beep of a truck reversing, even the timer on her shop's back-room microwave —
without her chest tightening and her hands going cold. She has replaced the microwave timer with a
silent visual model. She does not fully understand why she did this until she tries to explain it
here.

She avoids the hospital where Decha was first admitted. This means she has not visited a neighbor
who was recently treated there, something that troubles her conscience. She tells herself she is
busy with the shop. She knows that is not the whole truth.

Sleep is where the past finds her most reliably. She wakes between two and four in the morning,
sometimes from a dream in which she is standing outside a glass door she cannot open, watching a
monitor she cannot read. Other times she simply wakes, already frightened, without knowing why. She
lies still so as not to disturb her husband, running through a mental inventory of everyone she
loves, checking that they are accounted for. She has become, she says with a small, self-aware
laugh, "a woman who needs to know where everyone is."

When Decha calls home less frequently during exam periods, Mae finds herself constructing quiet
catastrophes. She will send a casual message and wait for the reply with a vigilance she recognizes
as disproportionate but cannot quite release. She describes this as feeling like standing at the
edge of something, always. She is not depressed, she is careful to say. She loves her life. She
loves her shop, her husband, her son. It is more that the world has shown her, once, in vivid and
undeniable detail, exactly how quickly everything can change, and she has not entirely found her way
back from that knowledge.

She came to therapy, she explains, because Decha recently had a routine cardiac follow-up
appointment, and for the two weeks beforehand Mae could not eat a full meal. She sat behind the shop
counter and cut fabric and smiled at customers and felt, the entire time, as though she were still
in that waiting room — still listening, still watching the door.
