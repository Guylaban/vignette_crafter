# The Weight of the Walk Home

Marcus is a 35-year-old special education teacher who has spent the better part of a decade learning
to read people — to notice the small signals his students send when they are overwhelmed,
frightened, or about to break. He is good at his job precisely because he pays attention. Which is
why it makes a particular kind of cruel sense that what undid him was something he never saw coming.

Fourteen months ago, on a Tuesday evening in November, Marcus was walking home from a late parent-
teacher meeting. It was just past eight o'clock, and the street was quiet but not empty — the kind
of quiet he had walked through a hundred times before without a second thought. Two men approached
him near the entrance to an underpass. One pressed something hard against his ribs. He couldn't say
afterward whether it was a knife or a gun. He only remembers the voice — flat, unhurried, almost
bored — telling him not to move. They took his wallet, his phone, and his bag. The whole thing
lasted less than two minutes. Then they walked away, and he stood alone under the orange wash of a
streetlamp, unable to move his legs for what felt like a very long time.

He filed a police report that night. He told his friends he was fine. He believed it, for a while.

The first sign that something had shifted came a few weeks later, when he noticed he was planning
his routes to and from school with an almost architectural precision — avoiding underpasses, staying
on lit streets, timing his movements around the presence of other pedestrians. He started taking
taxis home even when he couldn't really afford to. He told himself this was just being sensible.

By January, he was waking between two and four in the morning with his heart hammering, the flat
voice from the underpass still somewhere in the room. He would lie in the dark and run through what
he could have done differently, even though he knows — rationally, completely — that there was
nothing. The replaying doesn't feel like a choice. It simply happens to him, the way weather
happens.

At school, Marcus functions. He is still patient with his students, still creative, still present in
the ways that matter most. But the effort it costs him now is enormous. He sits with a boy who
becomes dysregulated during transitions and walks him through breathing exercises, and while he does
this, a part of Marcus is monitoring the classroom door, the hallway, the sounds from outside. He is
always, now, listening for something he cannot name.

He has stopped going out in the evenings almost entirely. He declined an invitation to a colleague's
birthday dinner, then a friend's leaving party, then a date he had been looking forward to. Each
time he constructs a reasonable excuse, and each time the real reason is the same: the thought of
being outside after dark produces a dread so physical it feels like illness. He has not told anyone
this. He lives alone, and the solitude that once felt comfortable now has a different quality — less
chosen, more cornered.

What troubles Marcus most is not the fear itself but the way it has colonized his sense of himself.
He was not, before, someone who was afraid. He grew up in a small town in the Netherlands, moved to
London for university, stayed because he loved the noise and the scale of it. He used to walk
everywhere. Now the city feels like a place that has already shown him what it can do, and he moves
through it braced for the moment it does it again.

He describes himself, in a quiet moment during one session, as feeling like a stranger inside his
own life. He is still going through the motions — teaching, eating, sleeping badly — but something
has come loose from its moorings, and he has not yet found a way to tie it back down.
