# Vignette: Arjun

Arjun is a fifty-eight-year-old South Asian man who came to this country with his wife nearly two
decades ago, building a life that, from the outside, looks like a quiet success story. He works as a
senior product manager at a mid-sized technology firm, where colleagues know him as methodical,
precise, and unusually good at anticipating problems before they surface. What they do not know is
that this vigilance was not cultivated in a boardroom. It was learned in a detention cell.

Before emigrating, Arjun was a civil rights attorney in his home country during a period of severe
political repression. He was detained without charge for eleven days by state security forces.
During that time he was subjected to repeated physical beatings, stress positions, sleep
deprivation, and what he describes in a flat, careful voice as "other things I have not yet found
words for." He was eventually released — no explanation given, no apology offered — and within
months he and his wife had left the country, carrying two suitcases and the understanding that
survival required silence.

For years, the silence held. Arjun threw himself into building a new career, learning a new
industry, becoming the kind of person who is always the first one in the office and the last to
leave. Work gave him structure. Structure kept the memories manageable. His wife, Priya, noticed
that he never slept deeply, that he startled at car horns, that he had stopped being able to
tolerate crowded restaurants — but they did not speak of it directly. In their shared understanding,
endurance was its own form of dignity.

The silence broke approximately eight months ago, when Arjun watched a documentary about political
prisoners in another country. He did not finish it. He turned the television off midway through and
sat in the dark for several hours, unable to move, his body convinced — despite everything his
rational mind was telling him — that he was back in that cell. The smell of damp concrete returned
to him so vividly that he checked the walls of his own living room. He did not sleep that night, or
the next.

Since then, the past has become unreliable in its distance. Arjun finds himself ambushed at work — a
colleague raising his voice in a meeting, the particular sound of a door being locked, the sensation
of sitting in a small conference room with no window — and suddenly he is not entirely in the
present. He describes it as being "split in two," one part of him still running the meeting, the
other part somewhere else entirely, heart hammering, hands braced against the table. He has started
declining travel for work, avoiding any situation that might require him to pass through airport
security. The pat-down, he says quietly, is not something he can do.

At home, Priya tells him he has become distant. He knows she is right. He sits at the dinner table
and hears her voice as though through water. He has difficulty feeling warmth, even when he wants
to. He wakes most nights between two and four in the morning and goes to sit in the kitchen alone,
unwilling to disturb her, unwilling to explain what he has seen in the dark behind his closed eyes.

He came to therapy, he says, because his performance reviews at work have begun to slip. He cannot
concentrate the way he once could. He loses the thread of conversations. He makes lists obsessively
— product timelines, meeting agendas, grocery items — because the lists create the illusion of order
when everything inside him feels disordered and loud. He is a man who has spent thirty years being
competent, and the erosion of that competence frightens him in a way that, he admits slowly, even
the original events did not. Back then, he was surviving. Now, he is not sure what he is doing.
