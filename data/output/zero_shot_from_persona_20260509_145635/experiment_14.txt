# Clinical Vignette: Rosa

Rosa is a 54-year-old Latin American woman who has spent the better part of her adult life in
uniform. She joined the Army at nineteen, drawn by a sense of duty and the practical promise of
stability, and she built a career as a combat engineer — a field she entered when women in that role
were still a rarity, something she carried with quiet, deliberate pride. Over three decades she
became the kind of soldier others leaned on: methodical, unshakeable, good under pressure. She
retired four years ago. Her husband, Marco, a retired logistics officer she met during her second
deployment, describes her now as someone he sometimes doesn't recognize.

The event that fractured something in Rosa happened during her final deployment overseas, roughly
six years ago. Her unit was conducting route clearance when a buried IED detonated beneath the
vehicle ahead of hers. She was out of her truck within seconds — that was training, that was
instinct — and what she found when she reached the wreckage is something she has never fully
described to anyone, including Marco. She knows only that she worked for forty minutes trying to
keep a twenty-two-year-old specialist named Dávila alive, and that she failed. He died with her
hands pressed against him. She completed the mission. She filed the report. She did not cry until
she was alone in a portable latrine, and even then she stopped herself after a few minutes because
there was still work to do.

She tells herself that is when it started, though she suspects it had been building for years.

In the months after she returned home and then retired, Rosa began sleeping badly — waking at two or
three in the morning, heart hammering, convinced she has heard something. She lies still in the dark
and listens until she is certain the house is safe, a process that sometimes takes an hour. Marco
has learned not to touch her when she wakes like that. Once, early on, he reached for her shoulder
and she threw his arm away so hard she bruised his wrist. She was mortified. She apologized for
days. She has not forgiven herself for it.

During the day she keeps herself occupied with rigid routine — coffee at six, a long drive through
rural roads, physical work around their property. She avoids crowds with the kind of deliberateness
that looks, to outsiders, like preference. She does not go to the hardware store on weekends. She
does not attend her nephew's soccer games. Loud, unpredictable spaces make her feel as though her
skin has been turned inside out. She is hyperaware of exits, of the people near her, of anything on
the ground that looks out of place — a habit so deeply grooved now that she cannot turn it off at
the grocery store or a family dinner.

She thinks about Dávila more than she tells anyone. Not always in nightmares, though those come too
— vivid, sensory, leaving her with the smell of burning metal that lingers into the morning.
Sometimes it is simply his face appearing while she is doing something ordinary, washing a dish,
watching television, and the weight of it stops her completely. She has stood at the kitchen sink
for twenty minutes, water running cold over her hands, not knowing how to move forward from that
moment.

Rosa describes herself as someone who has "never been a complainer," and she means it without irony
or self-pity. She has survived things, she says, that most people cannot imagine. She is proud of
her service. She loves her husband. She wants, more than almost anything, to feel like herself again
— though she is no longer entirely sure who that person was before the road, before Dávila, before
the forty minutes that seem to have swallowed so much of what came after.
