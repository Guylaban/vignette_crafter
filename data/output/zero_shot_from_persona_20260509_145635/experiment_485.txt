# The Weight of That Morning

María Eugenia is seventy-eight years old, a small woman with careful posture and silver hair she
keeps pinned neatly at the nape of her neck. She has worked as a call center agent for nearly six
years now — a job she took after her husband Rodrigo died, partly for the income and partly, she
says quietly, because the silence in the apartment had become unbearable. She answers customer
service calls for a telecommunications company, sitting at her kitchen table with a headset on, her
voice warm and deliberate even when callers are impatient with her. Work, she has always believed,
is what keeps a person upright.

What happened to her occurred four years ago, on an ordinary Tuesday morning. She had traveled back
to visit her sister in the city where she was born, a trip she had been planning for months. She was
sitting at a café two tables from the window when a car bomb detonated in the street outside. The
blast shattered the glass inward. A young man seated near her was killed. María Eugenia was thrown
from her chair, her ears filled with a sound she describes not as an explosion but as the world
closing. She lay on the tile floor among broken glass and smoke for what she believes was a very
long time, though witnesses said it was minutes. She was not gravely injured physically — some
lacerations, a fractured wrist, temporary hearing loss in her left ear. She flew home five days
later, telling herself she was fine because she had survived.

For the first several months, she functioned by staying busy. She took extra shifts at the call
center. She reorganized the apartment. She told almost no one what had happened, partly from pride
and partly because she did not yet have language for what was wrong. Then the dreams began — vivid,
suffocating replays in which the window does not shatter outward but collapses inward onto her,
slowly, and she cannot move. She wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, her heart
hammering, the smell of smoke so present she has gotten up to check the stove.

Daytime is its own kind of siege. She cannot sit near windows in restaurants and no longer goes to
cafés at all. When she hears a car backfire or a door slam hard in the hallway of her building, her
body responds before her mind can intervene — she drops, or grabs the nearest surface, her breath
gone, her hands shaking for an hour afterward. She has stopped taking the bus because the press of
strangers and the unpredictability of street noise feel genuinely dangerous to her, not irrational,
she insists — dangerous.

At the call center, she has developed a habit of keeping the volume on her headset low enough that
she sometimes misses what callers say. Loud or angry voices on the line send a current of dread
through her chest that she has to manage silently, professionally, while her leg bounces beneath the
table. She has received two performance notes about response times. She has told no one at work why
she sometimes needs to step away from the headset and stand in the bathroom with the tap running
until her hands stop trembling.

What troubles her most, she says, is not the fear itself but the person the fear has made her. She
was never a woman who avoided things. She raised three children largely alone during difficult
years. She nursed Rodrigo through his illness without complaint. She came to a new country at sixty
and learned to operate a computer. She does not recognize the woman who now checks the locks four
times before bed, who cannot watch the news, who sat through her granddaughter's outdoor birthday
party last spring in a state of rigid, smiling terror, scanning the street the entire time.

She describes herself, with a kind of rueful precision, as a woman who is present in her body but
absent from her life.
