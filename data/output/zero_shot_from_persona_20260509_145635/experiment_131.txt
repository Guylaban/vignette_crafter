# The Weight of Knowing

Marcus is a fifty-year-old psychologist in private practice, a man who has spent the better part of
his career helping others find language for their most unspeakable experiences. He is well-regarded
in his field, known among colleagues for his calm presence and his particular skill with trauma
survivors. He has been married to his wife, Elise, for nineteen years. From the outside, his life
looks exactly as it should.

Fourteen months ago, Marcus was sexually assaulted by a male acquaintance at a professional
conference. He had stayed late at a post-event gathering, shared drinks with someone he had met
through a mutual colleague, and accepted an offer to continue the conversation in the man's hotel
room. What happened there was not ambiguous to Marcus, though he has spent considerable energy since
that night trying to make it so. He has never reported it.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus threw himself into his caseload with a ferocity that alarmed his
administrative assistant, who noticed he was scheduling clients back-to-back with no breaks,
arriving before she did and leaving after she had gone home. He told Elise he was preparing a
conference paper. He told himself he was fine. He is a psychologist. He knows what trauma looks
like, and this, he reasoned, did not look like that.

Except that it does. He knows that too.

He cannot sleep in a way that feels restful. He wakes most nights between two and four in the
morning with his heart already pounding, the sheets damp, the room too small. The dreams are not
always explicit — sometimes it is only a sensation of weight, of being unable to move, of calling
out and producing no sound — but they leave him hollowed out by morning. He sits with clients
through the day, nodding, reflecting, holding space, while something behind his sternum feels like a
fist pressed inward.

He avoids hotel bars. He avoids professional conferences entirely now, which has required careful
management of his professional reputation. He declined two keynote invitations this year, citing
scheduling conflicts. He has stopped attending the peer consultation group he founded twelve years
ago, finding reasons each month to bow out. Being in a room with colleagues — people who might see
something in his face, who know enough to name it — feels intolerable in a way he cannot fully
articulate even to himself.

Intimacy with Elise has quietly collapsed. He has not told her why. She has asked, gently and then
with more worry, and he has offered fragments — stress, fatigue, a difficult period with clients.
These are not lies, exactly. She has accepted them because she loves him and because he is
persuasive and because some part of her may not yet want to know what she senses. He watches her
sometimes across the dinner table and feels a grief so acute it resembles rage, though he would not
call it that.

What Marcus experiences most consistently is a profound and corrosive shame that operates beneath
everything else like a low-frequency hum. He is aware, intellectually, that shame is a near-
universal feature of sexual trauma, that it belongs to the perpetrator and not the survivor, that he
would say exactly this to a client without hesitation. This knowledge does not help him. If
anything, it makes the shame more layered — he is ashamed of the assault, and ashamed that he cannot
apply his own clinical understanding to himself, and ashamed of the gap between the man he presents
to the world and the man who wakes at three in the morning unable to locate a single feeling that is
not dread.

He is hypervigilant in ways he disguises as professional attentiveness. He monitors exits. He
catalogues the body language of every man he does not know. He has become, without quite deciding
to, someone who sits with his back to the wall.

He has not told anyone what happened. He is not sure he believes he is allowed to.
