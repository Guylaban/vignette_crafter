# Clinical Vignette: Aroha, Age 21

Aroha grew up in a small coastal community in New Zealand, the kind of place where everyone knows
your name and the sea is never far from anything. She was nineteen when she enlisted, drawn by a
family tradition of service and a genuine desire to do something that mattered. She completed her
nursing training concurrently with her military preparation, proud to carry both identities at once
— healer and soldier. She was deployed to a forward operating base in a conflict zone eight months
into her service, assigned to a field medical unit. She was twenty years old.

The night that changed everything came without warning, the way those nights always do. A
coordinated attack on the base left multiple casualties arriving at the medical unit in rapid
succession. Aroha worked for hours under fire, her hands moving through blood and noise and the
sounds of men and women calling out in pain or going terrifyingly quiet. She watched a colleague,
someone she had eaten breakfast with that morning, die on the table in front of her despite
everything she did. The attack lasted less than four hours. The weight of it has not lifted in the
fourteen months since.

She is home now, living with her partner in a flat in Auckland, back in a civilian hospital where
she works rotating shifts in the emergency department. On paper, the transition looks manageable. In
practice, almost nothing feels manageable.

She sleeps poorly and wakes often, sometimes sitting upright with her heart hammering before she is
fully conscious, certain that something is wrong, that someone is bleeding out somewhere and she is
already too late. The dreams are not always the same night, but they carry the same feeling —
helplessness dressed up in different images. She has stopped telling her partner about them because
she can see the worry on his face and she does not want to become a burden to him.

At work, the emergency department is both the place she feels most competent and the place that
costs her the most. She is skilled and her colleagues respect her. But certain sounds — the
particular pitch of a trauma alarm, the wet sound of a chest wound being packed — pull her back to
the base in ways that feel less like memory and more like time collapsing. She has learned to finish
the task in front of her first and fall apart later, usually in her car in the hospital car park,
where she sits for twenty minutes before she can drive home.

She avoids the news entirely. She has muted words on her phone and changed the route she walks to
work so she does not pass a particular military recruitment poster that went up near the train
station. These adjustments feel rational to her. She does not name them as avoidance. She calls them
coping.

What troubles her most, and what she has only recently begun to say aloud, is the numbness. She
loves her partner and she knows this in an intellectual way, but the warmth she used to feel sitting
beside him has become harder to access, as though there is a pane of glass between her and her own
feelings. She worries she is becoming someone different from the person he fell in love with. She
worries she is becoming someone different from the person she used to be.

She comes from a culture that values collective resilience, where strength is understood as
something you carry for the people around you, not something you ask others to carry for you. This
shapes how she holds her distress — tightly, and mostly alone. She has not told her family the full
truth of what she saw. She presents as composed because composure is what she has always understood
love to look like.

She is twenty-one years old. She is exhausted in a way that sleep does not touch. And somewhere
underneath the competence and the routine and the careful management of everything, she is still
standing in that field unit, still trying to save someone she could not save.
