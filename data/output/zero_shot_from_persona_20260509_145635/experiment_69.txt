# Vignette: Emmanuel

Emmanuel is a 62-year-old married man who has spent the better part of four decades doing work that
most people cannot bring themselves to imagine. As a bomb disposal technician, he has always
understood that danger is simply part of the contract — something to be managed through training,
discipline, and the kind of quiet courage that never announces itself. He has defused devices in the
field, worked in conditions that would paralyze an ordinary person, and come home each time to his
wife, Amara, and their grown children. For most of his career, he believed that his composure was
something close to permanent.

That belief ended three years ago.

During a routine sweep of a suspected weapons storage facility, Emmanuel and two colleagues
encountered what appeared to be a compromised canister of industrial chemical agent. Before proper
containment protocols could be executed, there was a partial release. Emmanuel was closest to the
source. He remembers the smell first — something sweet and wrong at the same time — and then a
burning sensation across his face and throat that arrived before he had time to reach for his mask.
He was evacuated, decontaminated, and hospitalized for eleven days. His colleagues were treated and
discharged within a week. Emmanuel's respiratory damage was more significant, and the uncertainty
about long-term effects was never fully resolved by the medical team.

He returned to modified administrative duties within three months, insisting to his supervisors and
to Amara that he was fine. He was not fine.

The smell is the thing that undoes him most reliably. Cleaning products, certain perfumes, even the
particular sharpness of cold morning air can send him back to that room with a speed and
completeness that leaves him gripping whatever surface is nearest. During these moments, Emmanuel
does not feel as though he is remembering — he feels as though he is still there, still breathing
something that is trying to kill him. His heart pounds, his throat tightens, and for a few seconds
that feel much longer, he is certain he is dying. He has stopped using the communal kitchen at work
because a colleague's solvent-based hand cleaner triggered an episode so severe that he had to leave
the building and sit in his car for forty minutes before he could drive.

At home, Amara has learned not to light scented candles. She has not said anything about this
directly, and neither has he, but they both know. Emmanuel finds himself checking the windows before
bed — not for intruders, but to make sure nothing is getting in. He sleeps in fragments, waking
between two and four in the morning with his chest tight and his mind already running through the
sequence of events at the facility, searching, as it always does, for the moment where he could have
done something differently. He has not found it. He keeps looking.

He is quieter than he used to be. His eldest son mentioned it at a family dinner, framing it gently
as a question about whether his father was tired, and Emmanuel changed the subject with the
practiced ease of a man who has spent a lifetime managing what others see. What his son does not
know is that Emmanuel had excused himself to the bathroom twenty minutes earlier because the smell
of the gas stove had made his hands shake.

He still goes to work. He still reads the technical briefings, still consults on cases, still
presents himself as a man with thirty-eight years of experience who knows what he is doing. And in
many respects, he does. But he also knows that he has not been inside an active disposal site since
the incident, and that when his supervisor recently suggested he might return to light field work,
Emmanuel agreed immediately and then spent the following night awake until dawn, watching the
ceiling and telling himself that he would be ready.

He is not sure he believes it.
