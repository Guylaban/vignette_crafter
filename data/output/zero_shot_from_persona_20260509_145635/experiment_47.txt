# The Weight of Being Watched

Marcus is nineteen years old, lean and soft-spoken, with the kind of quiet intensity that makes
sense once you learn he spends his days thinking about how people move through digital spaces — what
they notice, what makes them feel safe, what drives them away. He works as a junior UX designer at a
small tech startup, a job he landed straight out of a coding bootcamp, and one he was genuinely
proud of until about eight months ago, when pride became the least of his concerns.

It started during his first semester of independent living. He had moved into a shared apartment in
the city, newly arrived from Lagos two years prior, still building his footing. A neighbor — a man
in his mid-thirties who lived one floor above — began paying attention to Marcus in ways that felt,
at first, like ordinary friendliness. Small talk in the hallway. A knock on the door to return a
parcel. Then came the texts, sourced from a number Marcus never gave him. Then the notes slipped
under his door. Then the waiting — the man positioned at the building entrance when Marcus left for
work, at the coffee shop Marcus frequented, outside the office building one afternoon, hands in his
pockets, watching.

Marcus reported it twice to building management and once to the police. Each time, he was told there
was not enough to act on. The stalking continued for four months before the man abruptly moved out.
No explanation. No resolution. Just absence — which, Marcus will tell you, is not the same thing as
safety.

Now, eight months later, Marcus sits in session with his shoulders pulled slightly inward, a posture
that looks like someone perpetually bracing for impact. He sleeps poorly and lightly, waking at
sounds that turn out to be nothing — a pipe, a door down the hall, wind. When he does sleep, he
dreams of footsteps that stop just outside wherever he is. He keeps his blinds closed even during
the day, which makes his apartment feel like a cave he cannot quite bring himself to leave.

At work, the hypervigilance that once kept him safe has become a kind of static he cannot turn off.
He is good at his job — genuinely talented at imagining how a user experiences a digital environment
— but lately the skill feels corrupted. He finds himself building in escape routes during design
reviews, mentally cataloguing where every person in the open-plan office is sitting, tracking who
moves and when. He has started wearing headphones not to listen to music but so that no one will
approach him without him first catching their reflection in his monitor.

He has pulled back from the two friends he had made since arriving in the city. He cancels plans,
then feels ashamed of canceling, then cancels again. He tells himself he is tired, which is true but
incomplete. The fuller truth is that being in public feels like standing in the open, and standing
in the open feels like an invitation.

What troubles Marcus most — what he brings up with a particular kind of careful quietness — is how
the experience has reorganized his understanding of other people. In Lagos, in his family home, he
was known. Here, he had been working to build something: a career, a life, a sense of belonging in a
city that did not yet know his name. The stalking interrupted that project in a way that feels
almost architectural, like a load-bearing wall was removed. He does not know, anymore, how to read a
stranger's attention as anything other than a threat. Friendliness looks like surveillance. Interest
looks like the beginning of something he will not be able to stop.

He is nineteen. He designs spaces meant to make people feel at ease. And he cannot remember the last
time he felt that way himself.
