# Vignette: Ernesto, Age 75

Ernesto has spent the better part of five decades making sure things don't fall apart. As a QA
engineer, his entire professional identity has been built around anticipating failure — reading
systems carefully, catching the flaw before it becomes a catastrophe. It is a cruel irony, then,
that the event that broke something inside him was one no checklist could have prevented.

Three years ago, a major earthquake struck the region of Colombia where Ernesto had returned to
visit extended family. He and his wife, Claudia, were staying in the home of his elderly aunt when
the ground began to move at just past two in the morning. The shaking lasted under a minute, but in
that time the back wall of the house collapsed, a neighbor's home across the street pancaked
entirely, and Ernesto found himself pinned under a fallen ceiling beam in complete darkness, calling
out to Claudia and hearing nothing back for what felt like an hour. She was eventually found
uninjured in a doorway, following the instructions they had been given years ago. He had not
remembered to do the same. He had simply frozen.

Ernesto was physically unharmed, aside from bruising across his shoulder and back. He flew home to
the United States within a week, returned to his part-time consulting work, and told almost no one
what had happened. He described the earthquake to colleagues as "a close call" and moved on — or
tried to.

Within a month, the sleep problems began. He wakes most nights between two and four in the morning,
heart pounding, the darkness of the bedroom briefly indistinguishable from the darkness under that
beam. He does not always remember a dream, but the dread is physical and immediate, and he often
cannot return to sleep. He has taken to leaving a lamp on in the hallway, something Claudia has
accepted without much comment, though he has never fully explained it to her.

During the day, Ernesto is watchful in ways that exhaust him. He scans the structure of buildings
when he enters them — noting ceiling materials, proximity to load-bearing walls, the distance to
exits. At family gatherings, he positions himself near doorways. He is aware this behavior is
excessive. Knowing that does not stop it. His engineering mind catalogs threats with the same
systematic attention he once gave to product defects, except now the system being audited is every
room he walks into.

He has become quieter with Claudia and with their adult children. He describes a persistent sense of
flatness — that the warmth he used to feel at Sunday dinners or during calls with his grandchildren
is somehow muted, as though received through glass. He still shows up. He still participates. But he
notices the distance between himself and the moment, and it troubles him.

What troubles him most, he says, is the freezing. In his professional life, Ernesto has always been
the person who stays calm and methodical when others panic. He has built a reputation on it. But
when the ceiling came down, he did not move toward Claudia. He did not move at all. He returns to
those seconds regularly — not in a way that feels like reflection, but in a way that feels like
being pulled back against his will, replaying the moment he believes he failed the most basic test
of who he is.

He has mentioned the earthquake to his primary care physician only in passing and has not framed
what he is experiencing as something requiring attention. He came to his current sessions at
Claudia's insistence, after she noticed him gripping the doorframe one evening during a minor tremor
from a distant minor quake. He sat with his eyes closed for several minutes afterward and could not
explain to her what was happening inside him. He is not sure, even now, that he fully can.
