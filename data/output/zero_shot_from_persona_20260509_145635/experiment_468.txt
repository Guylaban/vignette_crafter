# Vignette: Kwame

Kwame is twenty years old, one of the youngest doctors anyone at the regional hospital has ever
seen. He finished medical school accelerated, driven by a quiet intensity that his professors
mistook for ambition. What they didn't know — what almost no one knows — is that before he ever held
a stethoscope with any authority, he held a rifle. At seventeen, in the northeastern corridor of his
country where the insurgency had swallowed three provinces whole, Kwame was conscripted into a civil
defense unit. He spent fourteen months in the bush before a ceasefire allowed him to return to his
studies.

The worst of it happened on a Tuesday morning in the dry season. His unit was moving through a
village that was supposed to have been evacuated. It hadn't been. What followed lasted less than
four minutes by any reasonable clock, though Kwame has never been able to make his body believe
that. He watched two men he had eaten breakfast with that morning die within arm's reach of him. He
watched other things too — things involving civilians — that he has not yet found words for, even to
himself.

He returned home, enrolled in university, and moved through the years with extraordinary discipline.
Medicine felt like penance and purpose at once. Now he works long shifts in the emergency
department, and his colleagues describe him as calm under pressure, almost preternaturally so. What
they are actually observing is a man who has already seen the worst thing he can imagine and has
organized his entire life around never being still long enough to feel it.

Sleep is where the discipline fails him. Most nights he lies in his small apartment and waits, with
a dread he cannot fully articulate, for the moment his mind goes quiet enough for the images to
return. They always do. The smell of burning. A particular quality of light through smoke. The sound
— and this is the one that undoes him most reliably — of a child crying somewhere he could not
locate. He wakes most mornings before four, heart slamming, sheets damp, and he sits on the edge of
the bed and counts his own breaths the way he once counted ammunition.

At work, certain things reach him in ways he cannot predict or control. A patient brought in after a
road accident, bleeding from the head, will sometimes stop being a patient entirely. For a few
seconds Kwame is not in the emergency department. He is back in the bush, and his hands are doing
something he cannot stop. His colleagues notice that he occasionally goes rigid at the trauma bay,
that he sometimes steps outside between cases for longer than seems necessary. He tells them it's
the heat.

He has stopped going to the part of the city where street vendors burn charcoal in open drums. He
has stopped watching the news. He declined an invitation to his cousin's wedding because it was in
the north, and he cannot make himself go north. The perimeter of his life is shrinking in ways he
notices but feels powerless to reverse.

What troubles him most, sitting across from anyone willing to listen, is not the nightmares or even
the hypervigilance he recognizes clinically in himself with a kind of grim irony. It is the guilt.
He is a doctor. He saves people. He tells himself this every morning like a prescription. But some
part of him — the part that is still seventeen and standing in that village on a Tuesday in the dry
season — is not convinced that saving people now is the same as what he did then, or that it cancels
anything out. He carries both versions of himself to work each day, and the weight of that, he says
quietly, is something he did not learn about in medical school.
