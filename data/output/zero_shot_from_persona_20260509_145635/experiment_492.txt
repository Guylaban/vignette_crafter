# Clinical Vignette: Tane

Tane is an eighteen-year-old Māori man who carries himself with a composure that reads, at first
glance, as confidence. He works as a junior project manager for a small construction firm — a role
he landed through sheer determination and a reputation among his supervisors for being unflappably
organized, the kind of person who keeps every moving part accounted for. His girlfriend, Aroha,
describes him as steady. What she doesn't yet fully see is how much effort that steadiness costs
him.

Tane grew up in a household where the walls held their breath. From the time he was around five
until he was fourteen, he watched his father hurt his mother — not once, not occasionally, but in
cycles that had their own terrible rhythm. He learned to read the atmosphere of a room the way other
children learned to read books. A certain tone of voice, the sound of a beer can opening late at
night, his father's truck pulling into the driveway — each became a signal that flooded his small
body with dread. He would lie in his bed listening, muscles rigid, waiting to hear whether tonight
would be one of those nights. Often, it was. He remembers pressing his back against the hallway
wall, watching through a cracked door, unable to move, unable to stop what was happening to his
mother. That helplessness — the absolute, paralysing helplessness of it — is something that never
quite left him.

His parents separated when he was fourteen. On paper, the danger ended. Inside Tane, it did not.

At work, Tane is meticulous to the point of anxiety. He monitors every detail of a project not
because he is ambitious, but because disorder feels genuinely threatening to him. When a timeline
slips or a subcontractor doesn't show, he experiences a spike of alarm that is disproportionate and
physical — tight chest, shortened breath, a sudden need to fix everything immediately. His
colleagues find him impressive. He finds himself exhausted.

At home, the difficulties are quieter but more personal. He and Aroha argue sometimes, the way young
couples do, and when her voice rises even slightly in frustration, something shifts in Tane that he
cannot always control. He goes cold and distant, or he leaves the room entirely, heart hammering. He
has never raised a hand to her — the thought horrifies him — but he is haunted by a fear that he
cannot fully articulate: that he is his father's son, that violence lives somewhere inside him
waiting. This belief is not rational, but it is persistent, and it shapes how close he allows
himself to feel.

He sleeps poorly. Several nights a week he wakes from dreams he can't always remember clearly, but
that leave him with a residue of shame and fear. Some nights he hears sounds — a raised voice from a
neighboring apartment, a door slamming — and he is instantly, completely awake, scanning the dark
room, heart already running ahead of his thoughts. He doesn't tell Aroha why. He tells her he's a
light sleeper.

There are stretches of the day when Tane feels oddly detached from himself, as though he is watching
his own life from a slight distance. He is present in meetings, present with Aroha, present in all
the ways that are visible — but something underneath runs on a separate track, alert and unquiet. He
has become skilled at keeping the two tracks from crossing. He thinks this is just who he is.

What brings him to therapy is not a crisis. It is Aroha, gently, persistently, telling him that she
loves him but that she can feel a wall. He agreed to come mostly to reassure her. In the first
session he speaks about his work, his goals, his plans. It takes time before he speaks about the
hallway, the cracked door, and the boy who stood there unable to move.
