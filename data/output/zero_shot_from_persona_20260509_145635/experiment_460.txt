**Vignette: Tama, 48-Year-Old Married Roofer**

Tama grew up in a close-knit Samoan community where men were expected to be strong, dependable, and
silent about anything that threatened that image. For most of his adult life, he lived up to that
expectation. He built a reputation as one of the most reliable roofers in his region — steady hands,
a good eye for pitch and angle, someone the younger guys on the crew looked up to. He married his
wife Leilani twenty-two years ago, raised three children, and took quiet pride in the life he had
constructed, much like the roofs he laid — solid, protective, built to last.

Three years ago, at a work function held at a colleague's home, Tama was sexually assaulted by a man
he had considered a friend. He had been drinking, though not heavily, and found himself alone in a
back room when the assault occurred. He told no one. He drove himself home that night, showered
until the hot water ran cold, and went to bed beside Leilani without saying a word. In the weeks
that followed, he convinced himself it was something he simply needed to move past. Men in his
family did not speak of things like this. There was no language for it that felt safe.

But the silence did not protect him. Within months, Tama began waking at two or three in the
morning, heart hammering, the details of that night pressing against the inside of his chest like
something trying to get out. He started avoiding the smell of the particular cologne the man had
worn — a scent that could stop him cold in a hardware store aisle, leaving him gripping a shelf,
waiting for the wave to pass. He began declining social events, telling Leilani he was tired from
work, which was always at least partly true. Roofing is physically punishing, and Tama had always
used the exhaustion of the job to justify early nights and withdrawn moods. Now it became cover for
something much heavier.

On the roof, however, things changed in a way that frightened him. Heights had never bothered Tama —
he had always moved across ridgelines with the ease of someone born to it. Now he sometimes freezes
near the edge, not from dizziness but from a strange, intrusive thought that flickers through his
mind: that he deserves to fall. He never acts on it, but the thought visits him, and afterward he
feels ashamed and confused by its presence.

His relationship with Leilani has grown strained in ways neither of them fully understands. He
flinches from her touch sometimes, not because he doesn't love her, but because his body has
developed a hair-trigger startle response that embarrasses him deeply. He has become irritable with
the younger roofers — snapping at small mistakes, then feeling guilty for hours afterward. He drinks
more than he used to, usually alone on the back porch after Leilani has gone to bed, not enough to
be obvious, but enough to dull the edges of the evening.

Tama eventually came to therapy not because he sought it out, but because his GP, who had noticed
elevated blood pressure and disrupted sleep over several appointments, gently suggested it. He sat
in the first session with his arms crossed and his jaw set, and said only that he had been "going
through a rough patch." It took three more sessions before he disclosed what had happened, and even
then he spoke in careful, minimizing language — *something happened*, *a situation*, *not a big
deal, really*. But his hands were shaking as he said it.

He is a man who has spent his life building things meant to shelter others. He has not yet found the
structure that might shelter him.
