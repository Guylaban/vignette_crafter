# The Weight He Carries in His Holster

Marcus is eighteen years old, and he has been wearing a uniform for three months. He stands at just
under six feet, broad-shouldered in the way of someone who spent his teenage years trying to build a
body that felt safe to live in. He grew up in a small city in Ghana, the eldest of four children, in
a house where the walls were thin and the nights were long.

From the time Marcus was about six until he was nearly twelve, he watched his father beat his
mother. Not occasionally. Regularly. With a kind of terrible rhythm that the household organized
itself around — the drinking, the silence, the explosion, the aftermath. Marcus remembers standing
in the doorway of his parents' bedroom, frozen, while his mother's cries filled the room and he
could not make his legs move. He remembers the sound most of all: the specific crack of an open hand
against skin, his mother's sharp intake of breath, the way the sound seemed to travel through the
walls and settle somewhere inside his chest, where it has never fully left.

His mother eventually left when Marcus was twelve, taking the children to live with her sister. She
never spoke about what had happened. Neither did Marcus. He became quiet in a way people described
as serious, focused, mature. He did well enough in school. He decided young that he wanted to become
a police officer — he has told himself it was about justice, about protecting people, and he
believes this is true. What he has not examined as closely is how much of that decision was shaped
by a six-year-old boy who could not protect his mother.

He joined the force at eighteen, one of the youngest recruits in his cohort. He was proud. His
mother cried when she saw him in uniform.

The trouble began almost immediately. On his third week of duty, Marcus responded to a domestic
disturbance call. He heard the woman screaming before he reached the door. By the time he and his
partner entered the apartment, the husband had his wife pinned against the kitchen wall by her
throat. Marcus froze in the doorway — the same doorway, the same frozen body — for what his partner
later said was only a few seconds but what felt to Marcus like falling through the floor of his own
life. His partner handled the situation. Marcus stood and shook.

Since then, he has not slept more than three or four hours on any given night. When he does sleep,
he is back in that childhood bedroom, watching, unable to move, and he wakes with his heart slamming
against his ribs and his sheets soaked through. During the day he is hypervigilant in a way that
exhausts him — scanning every room he enters, startling violently at raised voices, sitting with his
back to walls in the station canteen. His colleagues find him intense. He finds himself incapable of
explaining why.

He has begun to dread going to work, which fills him with a shame he cannot articulate. He became a
police officer to walk toward danger, and now the sound of a dispatcher's voice crackling through
his radio makes his stomach drop. He avoids calls that might involve domestic situations when he
can, and when he cannot avoid them, he functions — but only barely, and at great cost. Afterward he
sits in his patrol car and cannot remember large portions of what happened.

Marcus does not talk about any of this. He is eighteen, he is African, he is a man, he is a police
officer. There is no version of his world in which any of those identities permits him to say: *I am
still six years old in that doorway, and I cannot get out.* So instead he drives, and he patrols,
and he carries everything he has always carried — only now it sits alongside a loaded firearm, and
the weight of all of it is immense.
