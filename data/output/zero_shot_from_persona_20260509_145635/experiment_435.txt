# The Weight of What He Breathed

Hassan is a 78-year-old Lebanese-born man who has spent the better part of six decades working on
rooftops. He came to the United States in his late twenties with calloused hands and a willingness
to climb, and roofing became not just his trade but his identity — the way he provided for his wife,
Fatima, and their four children, and later their grandchildren. Even now, well past the age when
most men have retired, he still consults on jobs, still drives past worksites and watches the
younger men work, still measures his worth in square footage sealed against the weather.

Three years ago, Hassan was supervising a commercial re-roofing project when a storage unit on the
roof was breached during tear-off. What was released into the air that afternoon — a combination of
industrial solvents and chemical residue from decades of improper storage — was not immediately
identified. Hassan remembers the smell first: something sharp and sweet, wrong in a way he couldn't
name. He remembers his eyes beginning to water, then burn. He remembers telling the crew to move
back, but by then he had already been standing in it for nearly twenty minutes. Two younger workers
were hospitalized briefly. Hassan drove himself home.

In the weeks that followed, he developed severe respiratory symptoms, neurological tingling in his
hands and feet, and persistent headaches that his doctors struggled to fully explain. The physical
symptoms have partially stabilized, but something else did not. Hassan began waking at two or three
in the morning, sitting upright in bed with his heart slamming against his ribs, convinced that the
smell was back — that sweet, burning wrongness filling the bedroom. Fatima would reach for him in
the dark and he would flinch before he recognized her touch. He has never told her how frightening
that flinch is to him.

He stopped going to worksites entirely for nearly a year. When he eventually returned, he found he
could not tolerate the smell of any chemical product — roofing tar, solvents, even certain cleaning
products Fatima uses in the kitchen. These smells send him somewhere he cannot easily return from:
his chest tightens, his vision narrows, and a certainty descends on him that something invisible is
poisoning him again and he will not know it until it is too late. He has left grocery stores mid-
aisle. He has asked Fatima to change her cleaning products three times.

Hassan does not speak easily about fear. In his culture, in his generation, in his trade, fear is
something you work through or work around. He describes the nightmares as "bad sleep" and the
hypervigilance as "being careful now." But when pressed, quietly, over several sessions, he admits
that he no longer trusts his own body to tell him when he is safe. The exposure took something from
him that he cannot name precisely — some fundamental confidence that the air around him is not
trying to kill him.

He worries about becoming a burden to Fatima, who is 74 and manages her own health concerns. He
worries that his sons will think he has gone soft. He sits in sessions with his hands folded in his
lap, a man who has worked at heights his entire life now afraid of something he cannot see or climb
away from. He still prays five times a day, and he says that helps, though some mornings the prayers
feel very far away.

What strikes the clinician most is the particular cruelty of this trauma for a man like Hassan: he
survived wars, immigration, poverty, and forty years of physical labor, only to be undone by twenty
minutes of invisible air. He does not understand why his mind will not let that afternoon go. He
keeps returning to the moment before the smell — when he was still just a man on a roof, doing what
he had always done, certain of his footing.
