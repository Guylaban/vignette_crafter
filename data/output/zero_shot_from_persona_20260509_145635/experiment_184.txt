# The Weight of Walls

Boon-Nam is a sixty-nine-year-old Thai man who has lived in the same modest house for over thirty
years, a house he chose specifically because it has large windows and an open floor plan. His wife,
Malee, knows not to close the bedroom door at night. She learned this early in their marriage, not
from any conversation they had, but from the first time she shut it and found him sitting rigid on
the edge of the bed at two in the morning, drenched in sweat and staring at nothing she could see.

When Boon-Nam was twenty-three, he was detained by authorities during a period of political unrest
in Thailand. What began as a brief detention stretched into eleven weeks of confinement in a small,
poorly ventilated cell. He was held without formal charges, interrogated repeatedly, and given
little reliable information about when, or whether, he would be released. He was not physically
tortured in the way that leaves visible scars, but the uncertainty was its own kind of violence —
the not knowing, the darkness, the sound of other men being taken away in the night. When he was
finally released, he was told simply to go home. No explanation. No apology. He walked out into
daylight that felt almost hostile in its brightness and took a bus back to his village.

He emigrated to Australia in his early thirties, married Malee, and built a life through physical
work. Plumbing suited him in ways he has never fully articulated — the work is concrete, problems
have solutions, and most of it happens in spaces he can exit whenever he chooses. He still works
part-time, mostly maintenance jobs for a property management company. His apprentices have noticed
that he will not work inside crawl spaces or service confined utility rooms without propping the
access panel fully open first. He frames it as a safety habit, and no one questions it.

What Boon-Nam does not easily talk about is how the past keeps finding him in the present. Several
nights each week he wakes from dreams that are not quite memories — the smell of damp concrete, a
door being bolted from the outside, voices speaking in tones that never resolve into words he can
understand. He lies still afterward, listening to Malee breathe, counting the streetlight patterns
on the ceiling until his heart slows. He rarely goes back to sleep.

During the day he manages. He keeps himself busy, which has always been his preferred strategy. But
there are moments — a car backfiring, a news segment about political prisoners, once a job that
required him to replace pipes in a building basement with no exterior windows — when something
shifts inside him that he cannot name. His chest tightens. His thoughts scatter. He finishes the
job, drives home, and sits in the driveway for a while before going inside.

He describes his emotional life to his therapist with characteristic understatement. He says he is
"not a complainer." He says the young men detained alongside him had it worse. He says he is
grateful for everything he has built. All of this is genuinely true. And yet he also acknowledges,
carefully, that he has never quite felt safe inside enclosed spaces the way other people seem to.
That he keeps his distance from news about Southeast Asian politics because something in it "gets
into him." That Malee has sometimes said, gently, that he is somewhere else even when he is sitting
right beside her.

He came to therapy not because he identified himself as someone struggling with the past, but
because his GP suggested it after he mentioned the sleep problems during a routine check-up. He sat
in the first session with his hands folded in his lap and looked around the room before he spoke,
noting, almost to himself, that the door was ajar.
