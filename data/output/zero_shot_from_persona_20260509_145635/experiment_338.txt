# The Weight of What He Carried

Dang is forty-one years old, a professional dancer and choreographer who has spent the better part
of two decades using his body as a language. He is soft-spoken in conversation but commanding in the
studio, a man who has always believed that movement could say what words could not. His wife
describes him as someone who used to fill every room he entered. She uses the past tense without
seeming to realize it.

Three years ago, Dang took a leave of absence from his dance company to volunteer with a
humanitarian relief organization operating in an active conflict zone in Southeast Asia. It was not
an impulsive decision. He had family connections to the region, a deep sense of obligation, and the
physical stamina that his training had given him. He spent seven months distributing food and
medical supplies in areas where the sound of shelling was ordinary, where the line between the
living and the dead was crossed with terrible regularity. He helped carry bodies. He translated for
medics. He held a child who did not survive the night.

The incident that broke something open in him happened in the fifth month. A supply convoy he was
traveling with was ambushed. Two colleagues were killed in the vehicle directly ahead of his. Dang
was uninjured physically, but he spent nearly four hours pinned down in a drainage ditch, certain he
was going to die, listening to sounds he has never been able to describe to anyone in a way that
felt adequate. When the shooting finally stopped, he helped pull one of his colleagues from the
wreckage. He remembers the weight of her, and the way the light looked that afternoon, and he wishes
he did not.

He returned home six months later, quieter than he had left. For a time, he told himself he was
simply readjusting. But the nights became a problem almost immediately. He rarely sleeps more than
three or four hours before something drags him back to the surface — the sound of a car backfiring
on the street below, his wife shifting beside him, nothing at all. When he does sleep, he is often
back in the ditch, or watching the convoy, or holding his colleague, and he wakes with his heart
already running.

The studio, once his sanctuary, has become complicated. Certain pieces of music — anything with a
particular rhythm, anything that builds to a sudden silence — can send him somewhere he does not
want to go. He has stopped choreographing work that involves the floor, without fully acknowledging
to himself why. He watches his students with a vigilance that exhausts him, scanning the room in
ways that have nothing to do with their technique. He has cancelled workshops, declined residencies,
offered explanations that are partially true.

He is irritable in ways that shame him. He snapped at his wife last month over something trivial and
watched her face go careful and still in a way that frightened him more than he expected. He has
become someone who sits with his back to walls in restaurants, who maps exits, who cannot watch the
news without his chest tightening into something close to rage. He feels cut off from the person he
was before — not grieving exactly, but estranged, as though that man left for the conflict zone and
someone else came back wearing his face.

What troubles Dang most, and what he finally said aloud in session after several weeks of circling
it, is that he no longer trusts his own body. His body was always his instrument, his certainty, the
one thing he understood completely. Now it startles, it freezes, it floods with fear in the middle
of an ordinary afternoon. He stands at the front of his studio some mornings and looks at his
reflection in the mirror and feels, he says, like a stranger watching a stranger. He is still
searching for a way back to himself, though he is not yet sure the path exists.
