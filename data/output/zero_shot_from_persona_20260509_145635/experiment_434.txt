**Vignette: Marta, Age 31**

Marta grew up in a small town in Poland before relocating to the United Kingdom in her mid-twenties,
drawn by the prospect of work and a fresh start. She trained as a paramedic and found the role
suited her — she was methodical under pressure, good with frightened people, and proud of the
stability the job gave her during the years her marriage was quietly unravelling. By the time she
and her husband divorced two years ago, the work had become something she could count on when little
else felt solid.

The incident happened eighteen months ago, during a street protest in the city centre. Marta and her
partner had been called to treat a man who had collapsed on the periphery of the demonstration.
While she was crouched over the patient, inserting an IV line, a line of officers in riot gear moved
through the crowd. In the chaos and noise, she was struck twice across the shoulders and back with a
baton before an officer registered her uniform and pulled back. She was not seriously injured in a
medical sense — bruising, a mild concussion — but she lay on the pavement for several seconds unable
to move, certain, in those moments, that she was about to be beaten badly or killed. The patient she
had been treating was separated from her in the surge. She never found out what happened to him.

In the weeks that followed, Marta told herself she was fine. She returned to work after four days
and said nothing to her supervisor beyond the minimum required for the incident report. But she
began noticing things she couldn't explain away. Driving to a call, she would sometimes see police
vehicles in the rearview mirror and feel her chest lock up, her hands tighten on the wheel until her
knuckles whitened. The sound of crowd noise — even from a television in a neighbouring flat — would
send a cold wave through her body that took an hour to subside. She started sleeping with the light
on.

At work, the changes are more subtle but persistent. She is sharper with colleagues than she used to
be, quicker to read situations as dangerous. On calls involving law enforcement, she finds herself
watching the officers more than the patient, tracking their movements with a vigilance that exhausts
her. There have been two occasions where she has frozen briefly — a second or two, no more — at
scenes that felt suddenly loud and crowded, and both times she was able to push through, but the
memory of those moments frightens her.

She does not sleep well. She wakes between two and four in the morning with a residual sense of
dread she cannot always attach to a specific dream, though sometimes the dream is vivid: she is on
the pavement again, and this time no one stops. She lies awake cataloguing the sounds of her
building, reassuring herself room by room. In the mornings she is tired and short-tempered with
herself.

Marta has largely withdrawn from her social life. She declined a friend's birthday gathering last
month because it was near the city centre, and she has stopped running her usual route through the
park, which passes a police station. She tells herself these are practical decisions. She is less
convinced of this than she used to be.

What troubles her most, she says, is the sense of betrayal she carries. She was in uniform, doing
her job, and it made no difference. She had always operated on an unspoken assumption that the
systems around her were, broadly, safe — that wearing the right vest and carrying the right bag
meant something. That assumption is gone now, and she doesn't know how to do her job, which asks her
to trust that assumption every single shift, without it.

She has come to therapy reluctantly, framing it as a practical problem: she needs to function at
work. But underneath that, there is something quieter and more frightened that she is only beginning
to let herself acknowledge.
