# Vignette: Layla, Age 21

Layla was nineteen years old when the shelling started. She had grown up in a mid-sized city that
had always felt, to her family at least, like it sat just far enough from the conflict to remain
untouched. That belief collapsed on a Tuesday afternoon in late autumn, when a series of strikes hit
her neighborhood in rapid succession. She was at home with her younger brother and her grandmother
when the first explosion shook the walls. The second one brought part of the ceiling down in the
kitchen. By the time her mother arrived home two hours later, Layla had been crouched in a corner of
the interior hallway for the entirety of that time, her arms wrapped around her brother, listening
to what she describes as sounds she cannot find words for — a pressure in the air, a quality of
destruction that felt personal, as though the building itself were screaming. Her grandmother did
not survive the third strike.

Layla and what remained of her family eventually made their way out of the country through a process
she remembers in fragments — checkpoints, unfamiliar faces, a bus that smelled of diesel and fear.
Within fourteen months she had been resettled and, because she had always been exceptionally gifted
with computers, she enrolled in a technical program and ultimately secured a position as a database
administrator at a mid-sized logistics company. On paper, her life has stabilized considerably. In
reality, she carries the hallway with her everywhere she goes.

She sits at her workstation now and maintains complex relational databases with a precision that her
supervisors find remarkable. She arrives early and stays late, not out of ambition but because the
structured logic of her work — the clean hierarchies, the predictable queries, the way data either
returns correctly or it does not — provides a containment that her inner life entirely lacks. When
the office is quiet and the screens are bright, she can almost believe she is only a young woman
doing her job. Then a door slams somewhere down the corridor, or a truck backfires in the street
below, and her body makes a decision before her mind can intervene. Her heart is suddenly enormous.
Her hands leave the keyboard. She finds herself staring at the emergency exit sign and calculating,
involuntarily and with great specificity, how long it would take her to reach the stairwell.

Sleep is where the worst of it lives. She rarely falls asleep before two or three in the morning,
and when she does, she frequently wakes within the hour, gasping, convinced she can smell concrete
dust and smoke. She has stopped trying to describe the dreams to anyone. Most nights she simply sits
at her desk at home and runs queries on practice databases, or watches videos about database
optimization — anything that keeps the screen lit and the mind occupied with something that has a
correct answer.

She does not speak about her grandmother directly. When the subject approaches in conversation —
which she carefully prevents — she feels a particular kind of blankness descend, as though a
partition has been drawn across that part of her memory. She knows the loss is there. She does not
go near it. She has told the one friend she has made since resettling that she is "not really a
feelings person," and she believes this is true, though some part of her recognizes it as something
she became rather than something she always was.

She is twenty-one years old. She is efficient, technically skilled, and quietly exhausted in a way
that has no bottom to it. She showers with the bathroom door open so she cannot be surprised. She
sits with her back to the wall in every room she enters. She is doing, by most external measures,
remarkably well. She knows this because people tell her so, and she has learned to receive the
compliment without explaining what it costs her to make it look that way.
