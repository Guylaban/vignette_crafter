# The Weight of Evidence

Margaret is a 71-year-old forensic analyst who has spent the better part of four decades examining
crime scenes with a steady hand and a methodical mind. She has always prided herself on her
composure — the ability to look at what others cannot bear to see and remain functional,
professional, present. That capacity, which served her so well throughout her career, is now
precisely what she believes betrayed her.

Fourteen months ago, Margaret was assaulted in the parking garage beneath her office building. It
was early evening, the kind of ordinary Tuesday that carries no particular weight until it does. A
man she did not know grabbed her from behind, struck her repeatedly, and took her bag. The physical
injuries healed within weeks. What has not healed is everything else.

In the immediate aftermath, Margaret told her husband Gerald that she was fine. She filed the police
report with the same detached precision she would bring to any documentation task, noting details,
correcting the officer's spelling. She returned to work within five days. For a brief time, she
believed she had handled it — processed it the way she processes evidence, catalogued it, and moved
on. She was wrong.

The first sign that something was different came at work. Margaret analyzes trace evidence — fibers,
soil, biological material — and she is very good at it. But now she finds herself freezing at her
bench when a sample arrives from an assault case. Her hands, which have never trembled over the most
disturbing evidence, begin to shake. She has started delegating those files to junior analysts
without fully explaining why, and the shame of that sits heavily in her chest.

At home, she does not sleep through the night. She wakes between two and four in the morning with
her heart pounding, certain that someone is in the house, and lies rigid beside Gerald, listening.
She has asked him to install additional locks on every exterior door. He did so without argument,
but she has seen the worry in his face when he thinks she isn't looking. She is always looking.
Hypervigilance, she knows the clinical term for what she experiences, but naming it does nothing to
quiet the constant low-level alarm that hums through her body from the moment she opens her eyes
each morning.

She avoids the parking garage entirely now. She parks three blocks away and walks to the building in
daylight, regardless of the weather. On the days she cannot avoid enclosed spaces — elevators,
stairwells — she counts her breathing and presses her thumbnail into her palm, a private, silent
ritual she has developed without anyone teaching her to do it. She has not told Gerald about the
nightmares in any detail. She tells him she sleeps fine.

What disturbs her most, and what she finds most difficult to articulate, is the way the assault has
altered her relationship to her own professional identity. For thirty-eight years, Margaret has been
the person who understands violence through its physical residue — the person who can hold a damaged
thing and read its history without flinching. She has testified in court. She has trained
investigators. She has always been certain that knowledge is a form of protection. Now she is not
certain of that at all. The assault did not happen to someone else's evidence. It happened to her
body, and her body is not something she can analyze from the outside.

She sits across from her therapist with her hands folded in her lap, precise and contained, and says
she is not sure why she is struggling so much with something that lasted less than four minutes. She
is 71 years old. She has seen far worse. She knows, intellectually, that duration is not the measure
of damage. But knowing something and feeling it to be true, she is learning, are not the same thing
at all.
