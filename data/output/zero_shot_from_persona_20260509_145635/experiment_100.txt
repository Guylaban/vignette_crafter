# Case Vignette: Daniel

Daniel is a 39-year-old Māori man who has spent the better part of his adult life in the air. He
earned his commercial pilot's license in his mid-twenties and has logged thousands of hours across
both domestic New Zealand routes and long-haul international flights. Flying, he has always said, is
the one place where everything makes sense — where the instruments tell you the truth and the rules
are clear. That certainty is something he has been unable to find on the ground for the past three
years.

The incident occurred on a summer evening when Daniel was driving home from a layover in Auckland. A
routine traffic stop escalated with terrifying speed. What began as a request for his license became
something he still cannot fully piece together — raised voices, a door wrenched open, his body
pulled from the car and forced face-down onto the hot asphalt. He remembers the weight of a knee on
the back of his neck, the sound of his own voice trying to explain who he was, and the absolute
certainty, in those moments, that he was going to die. He was released without charge approximately
forty minutes later. No explanation was given.

In the weeks that followed, Daniel told almost no one. He returned to work, climbed into the flight
deck, and performed his pre-flight checks with the same methodical precision that had always defined
him. From the outside, nothing had changed. Inside, he had become someone he didn't recognize. He
stopped sleeping more than two or three hours at a stretch, jolting awake with his heart hammering,
his body convinced the threat was still present. He began checking the locks on his apartment door
four, five, six times before bed.

The divorce, which had been quietly unraveling for some time, finalized eight months after the
incident. His ex-wife later told a mutual friend that he had become "completely unreachable." Daniel
doesn't dispute this. He knows he withdrew. He knows he stopped being able to tolerate being
touched, stopped being able to sit in restaurants with his back to the door, stopped being able to
explain any of it.

The cockpit became both his refuge and, eventually, a source of its own distress. Air traffic
control communications — the clipped, authoritative voices in his headset — began triggering a
visceral alarm response he couldn't suppress. During one approach into Sydney, a controller issued a
sharp instruction to hold position and Daniel's hands went cold, his vision narrowed, and he found
himself back on the asphalt before his co-pilot's voice brought him back. He filed no report. He is
terrified of losing his medical certificate.

Now, sitting in a therapist's office for the first time, Daniel speaks carefully, the way a man
speaks when he has spent years rationing his words. He describes the incident with a kind of flat
precision, as though reading from a checklist, but his jaw tightens when he reaches the part about
the asphalt, and he stops. He says he has not cried about it. He says he is not sure he is capable
of it anymore. He describes a persistent sense that the world is a place where the rules he believed
in — competence, compliance, professionalism — offer no real protection. That realization has
hollowed something out in him.

He still flies. He shows up, passes his simulator checks, shakes hands with cabin crew. He is good
at his job in a way that has become almost dissociated from any pleasure. He describes the view from
cruising altitude — the particular blue of the Pacific, the curve of the earth at dawn — and for a
moment something moves across his face that might be grief. He used to love it, he says. He is
trying to remember what that felt like.
