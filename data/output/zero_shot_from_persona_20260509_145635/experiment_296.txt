**The Weight of the Room**

Maya is twenty-six years old and has been a firefighter for three years. She is the kind of person
her crewmates describe as steady — the one who moves toward danger without hesitation, who checks on
others before herself at the end of a shift. That steadiness is something she built deliberately,
and it is something she is now struggling to hold onto.

Fourteen months ago, Maya was off duty when she was taken. She had stopped at a petrol station on
the outskirts of the city on her way home from a weekend away. What followed was forty-one hours
during which she was held in the back of a van and then in a locked basement by two men whose
motives she still does not fully understand. She was not physically assaulted in the ways she feared
most, but she was bound, threatened repeatedly, and kept in near-total darkness. She did not know
whether anyone was looking for her. She did not know whether she would leave that room alive. She
remembers counting the ridges on a concrete block near her head to keep herself from disappearing
entirely into the fear.

She was found. She went back to work six weeks later because she thought returning to routine would
fix something. For a while, it almost seemed to.

Now, more than a year on, Maya sits across from her therapist and describes a life that has quietly
fractured. She sleeps badly, waking between two and four in the morning with her heart already
pounding, the darkness of the bedroom briefly indistinguishable from the darkness of that basement.
She keeps a lamp on a timer now, set to turn on at three. Her flatmates think she is just an early
riser.

At the station, she moves through her duties with the same competence she always has, but something
has shifted underneath. Entering confined spaces — stairwells, storage rooms, the cab of the engine
when the door closes — produces a tightening in her chest that she has learned to breathe through
without anyone noticing. Last month, during a training exercise in a smoke-filled corridor, she
froze for approximately four seconds. To her crew it was invisible. To Maya it felt like falling.

She finds herself scanning constantly — exits, windows, the position of strangers. In a café, she
sits with her back to the wall. She has stopped taking the underground. She tells herself these are
reasonable precautions, and part of her believes it.

What she finds harder to explain is the flatness. She used to love her work in a way that felt
almost physical — the purpose of it, the camaraderie. Now she goes through the motions and waits to
feel something. She has pulled back from her friends, cancelling plans with excuses that are
plausible enough that no one has pushed back. She has not told her parents what happened. She told
them she had a difficult call at work and left it there.

There are moments — a particular angle of afternoon light, the smell of diesel, the sensation of her
wrists pressed together — when she is not in the present at all. She is back in the van, and the
forty-one hours are happening again, and the distance between that time and this one collapses
entirely. She comes back to herself within seconds, but the residue lingers for hours.

What troubles Maya most, she says quietly, is not the fear. Fear she understands — fear kept her
alive in that room. What troubles her is the suspicion that the person who counted ridges in the
dark and held herself together is not quite the same person who came out the other side. She is
trying to work out who she is now, in a body that startles at closed doors, in a job that asks her
to be unafraid, in a life that looks, from the outside, entirely intact.
