# Margaret

Margaret is a 65-year-old widowed woman who spent the better part of three decades as a military
medic, a career that took her through field hospitals and forward operating bases where she learned,
as she puts it, "to keep moving no matter what." She was someone who prided herself on steadiness —
the one who stayed calm when everyone else fell apart. That self-understanding shattered on a
Tuesday afternoon three years ago, when the car she was driving was struck by a semi-truck that ran
a red light at highway speed.

The impact was catastrophic. Her vehicle was pushed nearly forty feet before coming to rest against
a concrete barrier. Margaret was conscious throughout — a fact she now describes as a cruelty. She
remembers the sound of the metal folding around her, the smell of something burning, and the
strange, distant awareness that she could not feel her left leg. She also remembers thinking, with
the clinical precision of someone who had triaged dozens of casualties, that she was probably going
to die before anyone reached her. She did not die. She was extracted after twenty-two minutes,
sustained a shattered pelvis, a fractured femur, and a traumatic brain injury, and spent four months
in rehabilitation.

What she did not expect — what she finds deeply humiliating even now — is what came after the bones
healed.

The nights became unbearable almost immediately. She would wake at two or three in the morning,
heart slamming, absolutely certain the truck was still coming. She could hear it. She could feel the
steering wheel in her hands and the helplessness of knowing she had nowhere to go. Some mornings she
would find herself on the floor beside her bed without any memory of getting there. Her late husband
had always been the one she reached for in the dark, and his absence — he died of cancer five years
before the accident — compounds the isolation of those nights in ways she struggles to articulate.

During the day, she functions, but narrowly. She has not driven since the accident and tells herself
this is a practical decision, though she knows it is not. She takes taxis or asks her daughter to
drive her, and even as a passenger she rides with one hand braced against the dashboard and her eyes
fixed on every intersection they approach. The sound of air brakes — common enough in the city where
she lives — stops her cold. She has left a grocery store without her bags twice because a delivery
truck pulled up outside.

She is hypervigilant in ways that feel both familiar and foreign to her. In the field, that
alertness kept people alive. Now it keeps her trapped. She scans parking lots before she crosses
them. She sits with her back to walls in restaurants, which she rarely visits anymore because the
noise and unpredictability feel overwhelming in a way she cannot fully explain to her daughter, who
worries visibly and asks too many questions.

Margaret is also aware of something quieter and more corrosive: a flatness that has settled over her
emotional life. She used to find genuine pleasure in gardening and in the weekly calls she shared
with two close friends from her service years. The garden has gone largely untended. The calls have
become shorter, more effortful. She describes feeling like she is watching her own life from a
slight remove, as though the glass that separated her from the outside world during those twenty-two
minutes never fully came down.

What troubles her most, she says, is the loss of her own identity. She was the one who held the IV
line steady under fire. She was the one who talked frightened young soldiers through the worst
moments of their lives. She does not understand, and cannot yet accept, why she cannot simply apply
that same discipline to herself — why the woman who never flinched in a field hospital now cannot
sit in the passenger seat of her daughter's car without gripping the door handle until her knuckles
go white.
