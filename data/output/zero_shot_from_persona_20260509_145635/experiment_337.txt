**Vignette: Maya, 44**

Maya has spent the better part of two decades turning other people's skin into art. Her studio in
the back of a small shop is cluttered with ink bottles arranged by color, reference sketches pinned
to corkboard, and the quiet hum of machines she knows by sound alone. She is meticulous, patient,
and deeply proud of her craft. It is, she says, the one place in her life where she has always felt
completely in control.

That sense of control shattered three years ago on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon. Maya was working
in a commercial building when a chemical storage room two floors above her malfunctioned — a
pressurized container of industrial solvent ruptured, and the vapor moved through the ventilation
system before anyone understood what was happening. She remembers the smell first: sharp, sweet,
wrong. Then the burning in her throat, the way her eyes filled with tears she couldn't explain, and
the sudden, terrifying realization that her legs were no longer cooperating. She was found collapsed
in a hallway. She spent four days hospitalized, her airways inflamed, her vision intermittently
blurred. For weeks afterward, she wasn't sure whether the damage to her lungs was permanent.

Physically, she recovered. But Maya describes the months that followed as a kind of unraveling she
still hasn't fully explained to herself. She returned to her studio and found she could no longer
tolerate the smell of her own inks. The chemical base in certain pigments — something she had
breathed for twenty years without a second thought — now sent her heart slamming against her ribs
and her hands shaking so badly she had to put down her machine. She began opening every window
regardless of the weather, positioning herself close to the door, watching the ventilation grille on
the ceiling the way someone watches a stranger on a dark street.

Her husband, a quiet and steady man, noticed before she did that she had stopped cooking. The steam
from a boiling pot, the faint chemical smell of certain cleaning products under the sink — these
things sent her back to that hallway floor in a way she couldn't interrupt or reason her way out of.
She would stand at the kitchen doorway, heart racing, convinced on some cellular level that the air
had turned against her again.

Sleep offers little relief. She wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, chest tight,
certain she has been smelling something. She lies still in the dark and runs through a checklist —
windows, husband's breathing, the absence of any real alarm — but the certainty lingers for an hour
or more. She is exhausted in a way that rest doesn't touch.

At work, she has adapted in ways she doesn't fully acknowledge as accommodation. She now works only
in the mornings, when the studio is coolest and freshest. She has quietly switched to water-based
pigments wherever she can, telling clients it's an upgrade. She keeps the door propped open year-
round. A client recently asked if she was cold, and she laughed it off. She is good at laughing
things off.

What she doesn't laugh off, alone with herself, is the grief. She misses the version of herself who
could lose four hours in a piece without once thinking about the air in the room. She misses feeling
safe in her own body. She describes a persistent low-level dread that she can't quite locate — not
fear of anything specific, but a kind of vigilance that never fully powers down, as though some part
of her nervous system received a message three years ago and never got the all-clear.

She came to therapy, she says, because her husband told her she flinches in her sleep. She hadn't
known. She is not sure what to do with that information, but she is here, which feels, to her, like
a beginning.
