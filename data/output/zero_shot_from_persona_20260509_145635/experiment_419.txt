# The Weight of the Stage

Maya is nineteen years old, and she used to love the smell of a theatre — the dust, the rosin, the
particular warmth of stage lights on skin. She trained for most of her life for this, moving from
her small town in the Czech Republic to a dance conservatory in Prague at fifteen, giving everything
she had to the discipline of her body. Dance was the language she trusted most. That was before the
nightclub fire.

It happened fourteen months ago. Maya had gone with several friends from the conservatory to a venue
in the city centre — a rare night off, a chance to simply move without being watched or evaluated.
The fire started in the back of the building near the electrical panel. What followed was a crush of
bodies, smoke so thick she could not find the exit, the sound of screaming she still cannot fully
separate from her own voice. She was pulled out by a stranger whose face she never saw clearly. Two
of her friends were hospitalised with burns. She walked away without a single visible injury, and
this fact has become its own particular torment.

In the weeks after, Maya told herself she was fine. She went back to class. She marked through
rehearsals with the mechanical precision she had always been praised for, and nobody seemed to
notice that something fundamental had changed. She stopped sleeping well almost immediately. When
she did sleep, she returned to the nightclub — not in dreams that made narrative sense, but in
fragments: the heat against her back, a hand grabbing her wrist, the moment she genuinely believed
she would die there on a sticky floor surrounded by strangers. She wakes from these episodes
gasping, her heart already at a sprint, her body convinced the emergency is still happening.

The hypervigilance has reshaped her daily life in ways that exhaust her. She scans every room she
enters for exits. She cannot sit with her back to a door. Crowded spaces — theatres, studios, the
metro — produce a climbing panic that she has learned to manage by counting her breaths, by pressing
her thumbnail into her palm, by whatever small private ritual keeps her functional long enough to
get through the next hour. She has become an expert at performing composure.

What she cannot perform, lately, is dance itself. This is the thing that frightens her most. The
studio, which was once the safest place she knew, has become complicated. Music with a heavy bass
line can trigger a sudden, vertiginous sense that the fire is happening again — not a memory
exactly, but a full-body conviction. She has walked out of rehearsals twice without explanation,
standing in the cold outside until her nervous system quiets. Her teachers have noticed. She has
heard the word *unfocused* used about her, and it sits in her chest like a stone.

She feels almost nothing like herself. The girl who drove toward difficulty, who ran combinations
until her feet bled because she loved the feeling of getting something right — Maya is not sure
where that person went. In her place is someone who feels constantly braced, as though the next
catastrophe is already arriving. She describes a persistent sense of unreality, of watching her own
life from a slight remove, as though she is standing just behind herself at all times.

She carries significant guilt about surviving without physical injury when her friends did not, and
she has not spoken to them much since. She tells herself this is to protect them, but she knows, in
quieter moments, that she cannot bear to be reminded of that night by the people who were also
there.

Maya comes to sessions dressed neatly, her posture trained and upright. She speaks carefully,
choosing words with the same precision she once applied to choreography. Occasionally, mid-sentence,
she will stop and look at her hands — as though checking that they are still hers, that she is still
here, that the ground beneath her is solid enough to hold.
