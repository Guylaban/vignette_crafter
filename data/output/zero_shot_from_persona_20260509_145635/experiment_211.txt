# Sister Margaret

Sister Margaret is a fifty-five-year-old Roman Catholic nun who has served her religious community
for nearly three decades. She is soft-spoken and deliberate in the way she chooses her words, as
though she has spent a lifetime learning that language carries weight. She came to therapy
reluctantly, referred by her congregation's spiritual director, who noticed that something in her
had gone quiet in a way that concerned him.

Three years ago, Sister Margaret's younger brother Daniel was the victim of a violent carjacking
outside a grocery store in the city where he lived. He survived, but just barely — he spent six
weeks in intensive care with a traumatic brain injury and multiple fractures. Sister Margaret was
the family member who arrived first. She walked into that hospital room and saw him connected to
machines, his face swollen past recognition, and something in her, she says, "broke open in a way I
didn't know a person could break."

She returned to her convent after Daniel was stabilized, because the community needed her and
because she believed, genuinely, that prayer and duty would carry her through. For a while, it
seemed they might. She led morning lauds, she taught the younger sisters, she visited the sick in
the parish. But at night she would lie awake replaying the moment she walked into that hospital room
— not just the image of Daniel, but the sound of the ventilator, the smell of antiseptic, the
particular quality of the fluorescent light. She didn't call it a flashback at first. She called it
"being unable to stop thinking."

Within months, she found herself avoiding the news entirely. A report of any violent crime — a
mugging, a robbery, anything — would send her heart racing in a way she describes as "my body going
somewhere my mind doesn't want to follow." She stopped taking her usual evening walks through the
neighborhood near the convent because she began scanning every parked car, every stranger on the
sidewalk, cataloguing threats. She, who had spent thirty years cultivating a contemplative inner
life, found that her interior had become a place she didn't want to be.

Her prayer life, which had always been her anchor, began to feel hollow. She sits in the chapel and
her mind skips like a stone across water — she cannot settle. She has not told her sisters why she
sometimes leaves Compline early. She is ashamed of this, and the shame compounds everything. She has
built her identity around being a steady presence for others, a woman of faith and equanimity, and
she cannot reconcile that self-understanding with the person who woke up screaming twice last week.

Daniel has recovered substantially, though he lives with lasting cognitive effects. Sister Margaret
visits him monthly, and she loves him fiercely and with relief. But she notices that after each
visit she is worse for days — irritable with the younger sisters in ways she immediately regrets,
unable to eat properly, sleeping fitfully. She has begun to wonder, quietly and with considerable
guilt, whether she avoids calling him as often as she should.

What troubles her most, she says, is a creeping sense that the world is not what she believed it to
be — that the order and mercy she has staked her life on are somehow less reliable than she thought.
She does not say she has lost her faith. She is careful about that. But she admits that when she
prays for protection over the people she loves, she no longer feels certain that the prayer does
anything at all. That uncertainty, for a woman whose entire life is built on the architecture of
belief, is its own particular kind of suffering — one she carries quietly, behind her veil, in a
community where she is still the one others come to when they are afraid.
