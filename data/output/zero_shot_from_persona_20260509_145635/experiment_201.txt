# The Weight of What She Saw

Maria is a seventy-year-old Colombian-born fashion designer who has spent the better part of five
decades building a quiet, beautiful life around fabric, color, and form. She and her husband Roberto
have been married for forty-three years. They live in a modest but carefully decorated home, every
room a testament to her eye for beauty — hand-stitched throw pillows, curtains she sewed herself, a
worktable in the sunroom where bolts of silk and linen still wait for her hands. They wait more
patiently now than they used to.

Three years ago, Maria was visiting her younger sister Graciela in Medellín when a man was shot and
killed on the street directly in front of them. They had just stepped out of a café, coffees still
warm in their hands. The gunman was gone in seconds. The man on the pavement was not. Maria knelt
beside him — she does not know why, only that her body moved before her mind could stop it — and she
held his hand while Graciela screamed for help. He died before anyone came. His eyes were open. She
remembers the precise color of his shirt: a dusty terracotta, almost the shade of a fabric she had
once used for an autumn collection. She has not worked with that color since.

In the weeks after returning home to Miami, Maria told herself she was simply shaken, that time
would smooth it over the way it always had. But time has not cooperated. She wakes most nights
between two and four in the morning, her heart hammering, certain she can hear the sound again — not
a bang, she tells her therapist, but something duller, more final. Roberto reaches for her in the
dark and she flinches before she can stop herself. She hates that she flinches. She has been married
to this man for forty-three years.

During the day, the intrusions come sideways. A car backfiring. The particular angle of afternoon
light on terracotta tile at a restaurant. A news broadcast left on too long. Any of these can drop
her back onto that Medellín sidewalk in an instant, kneeling, the man's hand cooling in hers. She
has stopped watching the news entirely, stopped going to the Colombian restaurant she and Roberto
loved, stopped answering calls from Graciela with any real presence — she picks up the phone and
hears her sister's voice and something in her chest closes like a fist.

Her work has suffered in ways that quietly devastate her. Fashion design has always been the place
where Maria felt most fully herself — her intelligence, her sensuality, her stubbornness all
channeled into something purposeful and lasting. Now she sits at her worktable and the ideas will
not come, or they come and feel meaningless, frivolous, almost offensive. Why should anyone care
about the drape of a sleeve? She knows this thought is distorted. She has known beautiful things to
matter. But knowing and feeling have come apart from each other.

She describes herself as hollowed out, and then immediately apologizes for the dramatics. She is not
a dramatic woman, she wants her therapist to understand. She raised three children. She built a
business. She survived things before. But this — watching a stranger die on a sun-bright street on
an ordinary Tuesday — has lodged in her in a way she cannot explain and cannot dislodge. She feels
guilty for being so affected when it was not even someone she knew, when Graciela seems to have
moved on, when Roberto looks at her with a worry she cannot bear to see on his face.

She still goes to her worktable most mornings. She touches the fabric. Sometimes she pins a seam.
She is trying, in the language she has always trusted most, to find her way back to herself.
