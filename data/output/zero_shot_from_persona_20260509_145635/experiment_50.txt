**Vignette: Adaeze**

Adaeze is an eighty-year-old widowed woman of Nigerian origin who spent the better part of five
decades shaping public health policy across two continents. She carried herself, until recently,
with the kind of quiet authority that comes from a lifetime of being the most prepared person in any
room. That changed fourteen months ago, on a Tuesday morning in November, when a delivery truck ran
a red light and struck her vehicle on the driver's side at highway speed.

She was conscious throughout. She remembers the sound — a compression of metal she describes as the
world folding in on itself — and then the strange stillness afterward, her body pinned against the
door, her left arm bent at an angle she understood immediately was wrong. She remembers thinking,
with the methodical clarity that has always defined her, *I am going to die here.* She did not die.
She was extracted by emergency responders, airlifted to a trauma center, and spent eleven days in
hospital with a fractured humerus, three broken ribs, and a lacerated spleen. Physically, she has
largely recovered. In every other sense, she has not.

In the months that followed, Adaeze returned to her consulting work — she still advises a regional
health ministry on aging and chronic disease policy — but the woman who now sits at her desk is not
quite the same one who once drafted frameworks with unhurried confidence. She works from home
exclusively now, having given up the short drive to her office without fully acknowledging to
herself that it was the driving she could not face. She tells herself the commute was never worth
it. She does not tell herself what happens when she hears a car horn outside her window: the sudden
lurch in her chest, the way her hands go cold, the few seconds during which she is entirely back
inside that crumpled car.

Sleep has become its own ordeal. She wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, heart
pounding, with fragments of the collision still vivid — the truck's grille filling her windshield,
the sound, the angle of her arm. She lies in the dark of the bedroom she shared for thirty-one years
with her late husband, Emmanuel, and she feels a loneliness that is different from grief, sharper
and more disorienting. She has not told her adult children how bad the nights are. She was raised,
she says, not to burden people with things they cannot fix.

During the day she moves through her work with competence but at a remove, as though she is
observing herself from a slight distance. Colleagues notice that she is slower to offer opinions,
more hesitant in meetings, quicker to defer — behaviors entirely foreign to the woman they have
known. She notices it too, and this troubles her perhaps most of all. She built her professional
identity on the ability to analyze risk clearly and recommend decisive action. Now she second-
guesses whether it is safe to walk to the corner shop. She checks the locks on her front door three
times before bed. She has stopped attending her church's Sunday service because the parking lot —
the noise of engines, the movement of vehicles — leaves her shaking for hours afterward.

She does not describe herself as frightened. She describes herself as *altered*, which she says with
a precision that suggests she has thought carefully about the distinction. She is a woman who has
outlived a husband, navigated the deaths of close friends, and watched her country transform through
decades of political upheaval. She expected old age to bring a certain earned stillness. Instead she
finds herself startled by sounds, hollowed out by sleeplessness, and quietly estranged from the
capable, unshakeable version of herself she had always assumed she would remain.

She came to therapy, she says, because she is a policy analyst, and policy analysts solve problems
with data. She wanted, she explains carefully, to understand what the data say about why she cannot
simply decide to be well again.
