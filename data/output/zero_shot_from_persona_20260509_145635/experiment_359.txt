**Vignette: Karim, Age 48**

Karim grew up in Beirut, a city he describes as having "beauty and chaos woven into the same
fabric." He built his career there through his twenties, working his way from a small atelier in
Gemmayzeh to running his own modest fashion house. He had a wife, a daughter, a studio full of light
and bolts of silk. Then, on an August afternoon in 2020, the port exploded.

He was in his studio, two kilometers from the harbor, pinning a sample garment to a dress form when
the shockwave hit. The windows didn't just break — they became projectiles. He remembers a sound he
cannot adequately describe, something below sound, and then he was on the floor covered in glass,
his ears ringing, the ceiling partially collapsed, and his assistant — a young woman named Nour —
was screaming somewhere he couldn't see. The building's facade had partially sheared away. When he
finally got outside, the sky was the color of rust and the street looked like a photograph of a war
he had thought was behind his country. He found Nour. Others nearby, he did not find in time.

He relocated to Paris eighteen months later, following a divorce that had been quietly deteriorating
for years and that the disaster finally fractured completely. He tells his therapist that he doesn't
entirely blame the explosion for the marriage ending — "we were already cracked" — but the blast, he
says, "finished the work."

In Paris, Karim has rebuilt professionally with considerable discipline. He consults for a mid-range
European label and has begun a small ready-to-wear line of his own. From the outside, he appears
composed, even polished — the kind of man who chooses his words the way he chooses fabric,
deliberately. But there are things he does not tell most people.

He does not work near windows anymore. His current studio is an interior room, and when colleagues
ask why, he says it is about light control. He has not told them about the glass. He still flinches
at the pneumatic hiss of a bus door, at the sudden slam of a loading dock. Last spring, a car
backfired on the Rue du Faubourg Saint-Antoine and he found himself crouched behind a parked van,
heart hammering, before he fully understood what had happened. He was embarrassed for days
afterward.

Sleep is inconsistent. He wakes between three and four in the morning several times a week, not
always from a nightmare he can name, but with a physical alertness that feels like his body is still
waiting for the next impact. He lies in his apartment — which he chose specifically for its thick
walls — and listens to the building settle. He has become, he admits with some self-deprecation, "an
expert in the sounds of silence."

What troubles him more than the startle responses or the broken sleep is what has happened to his
relationship with his work. Fabric and construction were always, for him, an act of optimism — the
belief that beauty could be made and worn and last. Now he sometimes stands in front of a finished
garment and feels nothing. He describes it as looking at something through smudged glass. He still
produces good work — his eye is intact, his technical skill undiminished — but the pleasure has
thinned. He worries this is permanent.

He speaks of Nour occasionally, and of the people who didn't survive the blast in the surrounding
blocks. He doesn't dwell on them dramatically; he mentions them the way you mention something that
has simply become part of the architecture of your thinking. He has a daughter in Beirut who is
nineteen now, and he calls her every Sunday. He says she sounds fine. He says he hopes that is true.

He came to therapy, he explains, not because he feels he is falling apart, but because he is tired
of managing. "I am holding everything very neatly," he says, smoothing the knee of his trousers as
he speaks. "But I am very tired of holding."
