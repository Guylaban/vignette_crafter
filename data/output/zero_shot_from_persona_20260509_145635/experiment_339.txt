# The Weight of What He Witnessed

Martin is a 55-year-old counselor who has spent the better part of three decades helping other
people find their footing after crisis. He is soft-spoken, precise with language in the way that
therapists often are, and quick to turn questions back on the person asking them. It has taken him
several sessions to stop doing that — to sit with being the one in the chair rather than beside it.

Eleven years ago, Martin was attending a professional conference in a mid-sized European city when a
gas explosion tore through the hotel's lower floors during an evening reception. He remembers the
sound first — not loud, he says, which still confuses him — more like the air being pulled out of
the room before the structure gave way beneath him. He fell through partial flooring into a space
thick with dust and the smell of burning insulation. He was trapped for nearly four hours. Around
him in that dark, compressed space were people he had been laughing with minutes before. Two of them
did not survive. One was a colleague he had known for fifteen years. Martin held her hand in the
darkness while she lost consciousness, calling out to rescuers who could not yet reach them.

He returned to work within three weeks. He told himself, and anyone who asked, that he was fine —
that he understood trauma, that he had the tools. For a while, he believed it. He threw himself into
his caseload with an intensity his supervisor quietly noted but did not challenge, because his
clients were doing well and Martin had always been dedicated.

The cracks showed slowly. He began avoiding enclosed spaces — elevators, underground car parks,
rooms without windows. He rerouted his commute to bypass a construction site where controlled
demolitions occasionally rattled the ground. Sleep became unreliable, then punishing. He would wake
at two or three in the morning with his heart already pounding, the smell of burning plastic so
vivid he would check his flat for a fire. Some nights he did not go back to sleep at all, sitting at
his kitchen table until dawn with a cup of tea he forgot to drink.

What troubles Martin most, and what he finds hardest to say aloud, is what happens when he is with
clients. He is still competent — he knows this, and his outcomes confirm it — but he is no longer
fully present in the way he once was. When clients describe accidents, structural collapses, or
anything involving entrapment, something in him goes very still and very far away simultaneously. He
has learned to manage his expression, but inside he is back in that hotel, listening to the
breathing beside him grow shallow. Afterwards he sits in his car for long stretches, unable to
account for the time.

Martin has become increasingly isolated over the past several years. He ended a relationship shortly
after the disaster — he says it was mutual, but describes it with the flat affect of someone who has
stopped examining the loss. He declines social invitations more often than not, preferring the
predictability of his flat and his routines. He drinks more than he used to, not dramatically, but
steadily — a measure of whisky that became two, that became a reliable nightly anchor.

What finally brought him to seek help was a session six months ago in which a client described being
buried under rubble during a building collapse. Martin excused himself, walked to the bathroom, and
sat on the floor for twenty minutes, shaking, before he could return. He told the client he had
received an urgent call. That lie — small and protective and entirely unlike him — frightened him
more than the shaking had.

He comes to sessions with his notepad, a habit he is slowly learning to leave in his bag. He is a
man who has spent his life holding space for others and has almost no practice being held himself.
He is beginning, cautiously, to try.
