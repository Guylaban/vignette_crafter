**Vignette: Leilani, Age 35**

Leilani grew up in Samoa before immigrating to New Zealand in her mid-twenties, drawn by work
opportunities and eventually building a quiet, stable life with her husband in Auckland. She trained
as an accountant and found genuine satisfaction in the orderliness of numbers — the way a balanced
ledger meant everything was accounted for, everything in its rightful place. That sense of control
felt important to her, though she could not have articulated why until everything changed on an
otherwise unremarkable Tuesday evening fourteen months ago.

She had stayed late at the office to finalize a client's quarterly report and was walking the three
blocks to the carpark just after eight o'clock when a man approached from behind, grabbed her by the
shoulder, pressed something hard into the small of her back, and told her to hand over her bag and
her phone. She remembers the smell of him — cigarettes and something chemical — and the way the
streetlight caught the silver of his rings. She gave him everything without speaking. He shoved her
into a doorway and ran. The whole encounter lasted perhaps forty seconds. She stood in that doorway
for a very long time before she could move her legs.

In the weeks that followed, Leilani told herself she was fine. She had not been physically harmed.
She went back to work. She sat at her desk and opened spreadsheets and tried to locate herself
inside the familiar logic of columns and figures. But the numbers no longer held still for her. She
would stare at a screen and realize twenty minutes had passed and she had no memory of what she had
been doing. Her supervisor asked twice if she was alright. She said yes both times.

She stopped walking anywhere after dark. Then she stopped walking anywhere alone. Her husband began
driving her to work, and she told him it was just easier, that parking was a nightmare, that it
saved time. She did not tell him that the sight of a man walking behind her on a footpath made her
heart seize in her chest, that she had begun scanning every room she entered for exits, that she
kept her office door locked even during the day and felt a flush of shame each time a colleague
knocked and she startled so hard she knocked her coffee over.

Sleep became its own ordeal. She wakes most nights between two and four, certain she has heard
something, her body already rigid and drenched before she is fully conscious. When she does sleep,
she is back in the doorway — not always as a memory but as something more total, more present, as
though time has simply folded and she is there again, feeling the pressure in her back, smelling
cigarettes. She has stopped telling her husband when this happens because she can see the worry
accumulating on his face and she does not want to be the source of it.

What troubles Leilani most, and what she speaks about with a kind of bewildered grief, is the person
she seems to have become. She describes herself before the mugging as someone who was warm, who
laughed easily, who cooked elaborate Sunday meals for her extended family and genuinely enjoyed the
noise and press of people. Now she finds herself irritable at gatherings, hypervigilant in a way
that exhausts her, and emotionally distant from her husband in ways she cannot fully explain or
control. She loves him and simultaneously feels sealed off behind glass. She has declined three
family events in the past two months, citing work. The work is real — she has been taking on extra
projects, filling her hours with tasks that require concentration, because concentration, when she
can achieve it, is the only thing that reliably quiets the noise inside her. But the ledgers no
longer balance the way they once did, and somewhere underneath the spreadsheets and the locked
office door, Leilani knows it.
