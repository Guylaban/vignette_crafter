**Vignette: Aroha, 39**

Aroha grew up on the North Island of New Zealand, the kind of person who felt most herself when she
was outdoors — reading weather patterns, tracking native birds, knowing the land beneath her feet.
She became a park ranger in her late twenties, and for nearly a decade the work felt like a calling.
She was good at managing risk in the bush, good at staying calm when trampers got lost or weather
turned. That steadiness was what led her, at thirty-five, to volunteer with an international
humanitarian organisation during a period of intense conflict in a region she had only known from
news reports. She went as a logistics coordinator, helping to move food and medical supplies through
corridors that were never entirely safe.

She was there for eleven months.

What happened during those months does not arrange itself neatly in her memory. It comes in pieces —
the morning a convoy she had planned was hit and she arrived to find colleagues she had eaten
breakfast with an hour earlier dead in the road. The weeks of working in a field clinic where the
patients were mostly children. The night she spent hiding in a drainage ditch while shelling moved
closer and then, eventually, further away. She did not break down over there. She kept working. She
told herself, and believed it, that she was fine.

She came home to her husband, Tama, and to the park, and for a few months things seemed almost
normal. Then, gradually, they didn't. She began waking at three or four in the morning, heart
already pounding before she was fully conscious, convinced something was wrong — that she had
miscalculated something, forgotten something, left someone behind. She would lie rigid in the dark,
listening to the house, unable to explain to Tama what she was afraid of because she wasn't sure
herself.

At work, the park that once felt like sanctuary has become difficult to navigate. Loud, sudden
sounds — a helicopter passing over the ridge, a hunter's shot echoing through the valley — send a
jolt through her body that takes a long time to settle. She has started avoiding the sections of the
park that require her to drive the service road alone, though she hasn't told her supervisor why.
She tells herself it's inefficiency, a scheduling choice. She knows it isn't.

She finds herself scanning. In the staffroom, she sits with her back to the wall. In the
supermarket, she moves quickly and leaves as soon as she can. She has stopped going to the large
family gatherings her extended whānau holds regularly, the noise and the press of bodies making her
feel trapped in a way she cannot fully articulate and is ashamed of. Her relationship with Tama is
strained in ways neither of them has found words for yet. She is present but not present. She knows
he feels it.

What troubles her most, she says, is the flatness. She used to feel things strongly — joy at a good
sunrise, satisfaction at the end of a long patrol, love that was uncomplicated and close to the
surface. Now those feelings seem to reach her from a distance, filtered through something she can't
name. She wonders sometimes if she left part of herself in that drainage ditch, or on that road, and
whether it is coming back.

She does not think of herself as traumatised. She thinks of herself as someone who saw things that
were hard to see and is taking longer than expected to adjust. She volunteered; no one made her go.
She came home intact, which others did not. These thoughts sit heavily in her, and she returns to
them often, especially at three in the morning when the house is quiet and her body is certain,
despite all evidence, that something is still very wrong.
