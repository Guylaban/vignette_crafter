# Clinical Vignette: Mere

Mere is a 65-year-old Māori woman who has worked in cybersecurity for nearly three decades. She is
methodical, precise, and has built a career on anticipating threats before they materialize. She
knows how systems fail. She knows how to read warning signs. What she cannot reconcile — what sits
at the center of everything that has unraveled since — is that she saw none of it coming.

Eighteen months ago, Mere was driving home from a conference on a wet winter evening when a logging
truck lost control on a coastal highway and crossed into her lane. The collision was catastrophic.
Her car was pushed through a guardrail and came to rest partially over an embankment above the sea.
She was conscious throughout — trapped for over an hour, the vehicle shifting intermittently beneath
her, the sound of metal groaning against rock, water visible below. She remembers thinking, with
extraordinary clarity, that she was going to die there alone. Her husband, Tama, had passed two
years earlier. There was no one to call. She simply waited, and the car moved, and she waited again.

She survived with a fractured pelvis, three broken ribs, and a lacerated forearm. Physically, she
has largely recovered. In every other sense, she has not.

In the weeks following the accident, Mere returned to her apartment and did not leave it for nearly
a month. She told colleagues she was healing. She was, in part. But she was also unable to cross in
front of a window without ducking. She could not hear rain against the glass without her chest
seizing. She began sleeping on the floor beside her bed because the height of the mattress felt, in
some way she cannot fully articulate, dangerous.

She returned to work remotely, and her colleagues noticed little change. Mere is good at performing
competence. She spends her days monitoring network traffic, identifying intrusions, writing threat
assessments — work that requires sustained vigilance, and vigilance is something she now has in
terrible abundance. She is hyperalert at her desk for ten hours at a time, scanning, scanning,
unable to stop. At night, the scanning does not stop either. She sleeps in fragments of ninety
minutes or less, waking to the sound of the truck that is never there.

The dreams are not always the accident itself. Sometimes she is simply in the car again, on the
embankment, and Tama is in the passenger seat, and she knows he is already dead but she cannot tell
him, and the car keeps shifting. She wakes from these dreams and lies on the floor in the dark and
does not know, for a moment, which loss she is grieving.

Mere has begun avoiding the coastal road entirely, adding forty minutes to any drive she takes — on
the rare occasions she drives at all. She has declined two in-person conferences this year. She told
herself this was practical. She is beginning to understand it is not. She has also noticed that she
feels almost nothing when she speaks to her daughter in Auckland, and this frightens her more than
the dreams do. She used to be the one who called. She used to be the one who held things together
after Tama died. Now her daughter calls, and Mere answers, and she is somewhere behind her own
voice, watching herself perform a conversation.

What she describes most often, and with the most difficulty, is not the fear. It is the sense that
the woman who was trapped above the water — who waited, and did not die — is somehow not the same
woman who drove onto that highway. She cannot find the seam between them. She knows how breaches
happen. She knows how systems are compromised from the inside. She just did not expect it to happen
to her.
