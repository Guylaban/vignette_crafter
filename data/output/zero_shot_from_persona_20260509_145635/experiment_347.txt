**Vignette: Rajan, Age 61**

Rajan has spent the better part of three decades working for a small non-profit organization that
provides food security resources to immigrant communities in his city. He is the kind of man who
arrives early and stays late, who knows the names of every volunteer's children, who finds genuine
meaning in the slow, unglamorous work of helping people rebuild their lives in a new country. His
colleagues describe him as steady and warm. He would have described himself that way too, until
fourteen months ago.

It was a Tuesday evening in late autumn. Rajan was driving home from a community fundraising event,
tired but satisfied, replaying the night in his mind. A driver running a red light struck his car at
full speed on the passenger side. The impact spun the vehicle across two lanes of traffic. Rajan
remembers the sound — a sound he says is impossible to describe to anyone who hasn't heard it — and
then a strange, suspended silence before he understood that he was still alive. He sat in the
wreckage for nearly twenty minutes before emergency services arrived, unable to move, certain at
several points that he was dying. He sustained a fractured collarbone, three broken ribs, and a
serious concussion. The physical injuries healed within months. Everything else has not.

In the weeks following the accident, Rajan told his wife, Priya, that he simply needed rest. He
minimized what had happened, partly because minimizing difficulty is something he has done all his
life, and partly because he genuinely believed he would feel normal again soon. But normal did not
come. He began waking at two or three in the morning, heart pounding, drenched in sweat, with the
sensation of impact still reverberating through his body. The dreams were not always about the crash
directly — sometimes he was simply trapped somewhere, unable to move, the world going dark around
him.

Driving became impossible. For the first two months, he told himself it was practical caution. Then
he admitted, quietly, that sitting behind the wheel made his chest close up and his hands shake in a
way he could not control. He now takes the bus to work, which adds forty minutes to his commute each
way, and he has constructed elaborate explanations for colleagues who ask why he no longer drives to
site visits. He rides in cars when he must — when Priya insists on taking him to appointments — but
he sits rigid in the passenger seat, watching every intersection with a vigilance that exhausts him.

At work, Rajan functions, but not as himself. He finds it difficult to concentrate during long
meetings. He loses the thread of conversations. He has caught himself snapping at a young program
coordinator over something trivial and felt a shame afterward that sat with him for days. The work
that once felt like a calling now sometimes feels like a performance he is barely sustaining. He
worries that people can see the difference in him, though no one has said so directly.

He has said very little to Priya about the depth of what he is experiencing. She knows he is not
sleeping well. She does not know that some evenings, when he hears a loud noise from the street
outside — a car backfiring, a dumpster lid slamming — he has to leave the room and stand in the
hallway with his eyes closed, waiting for his body to believe it is safe. He does not want her to
worry. He also, on some level, does not have the language for what is happening to him. Men of his
generation, in his experience, were not taught that the mind could be injured the way the body can.

What brings him to therapy now is not a crisis but an accumulation — a slow recognition, finally
named by his physician at a routine appointment, that what he is carrying has a shape and a name,
and that carrying it alone is no longer working.
