**Vignette: Priya, Age 32**

Priya grew up in a mid-sized city in southern India, the daughter of a schoolteacher and a civil
engineer who raised her with a deep sense of civic responsibility and faith. At twenty-six,
idealistic and newly credentialed as a humanitarian worker, she traveled to a conflict zone in
Southeast Asia as part of an international aid organization. She believed, with the certainty of the
young and principled, that her presence mattered. For eleven days, she was held by a paramilitary
faction that had detained several aid workers. During that time, she was subjected to repeated
physical beatings, stress positions, sleep deprivation, and mock executions. She watched a colleague
endure the same. She was released after a negotiation she was never fully told the details of.

She returned home a different person, though it took her years to understand the dimensions of that
difference.

In the immediate aftermath, Priya threw herself into structure. She completed a graduate degree in
divinity, married a man her family approved of, and found work as a hospital chaplain — a role she
had chosen, she says, because she wanted to sit with people in the worst moments of their lives. She
wanted her suffering to mean something. For a while, the logic of that held.

But the marriage fractured under the weight of what she could not say. Her ex-husband described her
as "absent," a word she finds both unfair and, in her more honest moments, accurate. She could not
be touched without flinching. She could not sleep in a room with the door closed. She would wake at
3 a.m. with her heart hammering, certain — absolutely certain — that someone was standing just
outside the room. She stopped telling him about the nightmares after he started looking at her with
an expression she could only read as helplessness edging toward resentment. They divorced two years
ago.

Now Priya works five days a week in a large urban hospital, moving between oncology wards and the
ICU, sitting with patients and families in grief. She is, by all accounts, extraordinarily good at
her job. Colleagues describe her as calm, present, spiritually attuned. Patients request her
specifically. She carries this with a quiet pride and a private irony she rarely voices: she can
hold space for other people's terror with remarkable steadiness, but she cannot watch a news segment
about political detention without leaving the room and pressing her back against a wall until her
breathing slows.

The symptoms she lives with are not abstract. When a patient's family member raises his voice in the
corridor, her body responds before her mind does — pulse spiking, vision narrowing, hands going
cold. She has learned to excuse herself to a supply closet, where she counts ceiling tiles until the
sensation passes. She avoids the hospital's security staff, not because she distrusts them
rationally, but because the sight of a uniform still triggers something she cannot fully override.
She has not traveled internationally since her release. She has not been able to pray in the way she
once did, which is perhaps the loss she mourns most quietly — the sense that the God she spoke to
before those eleven days is not quite the same one she encounters now, if she encounters one at all.

She describes herself as "functional but fractured." She keeps a meticulous routine. She calls her
mother every Sunday. She has a small apartment with large windows, which she chose deliberately. She
tends to a collection of houseplants with a devotion that she suspects is partly displacement.

There are moments — rare, but real — when she sits with a dying patient and something settles in
her, some convergence of purpose and presence that feels close to peace. She does not know what to
make of those moments, whether they are healing or simply reprieve. She holds them carefully, the
way she holds most things now: with both hands, and with the knowledge that they can be taken.
