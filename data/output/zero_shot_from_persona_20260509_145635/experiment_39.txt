# The Weight of What He Witnessed

Boonsri is sixty-eight years old, a public defender who has spent four decades standing between the
state and the accused. He is a careful man — deliberate in his speech, precise in his thinking, the
kind of person who reads a contract twice before signing it. His colleagues describe him as
unshakeable. He would have agreed with them, once.

Three years ago, his wife of thirty-one years, Niran, was struck by a vehicle while crossing the
street outside their neighborhood market. Boonsri was with her. He was holding the grocery bag. He
watched it happen.

He describes the moment with a strange detachment that breaks apart as he speaks — the sound, he
says, was nothing like what he expected. He had imagined, in the abstract way one sometimes does,
that such things would be loud and dramatic. Instead, what he remembers most is a kind of muffled
thud, and then the silence that followed, and then Niran on the pavement. He ran to her. He held her
head in his hands on the asphalt for eleven minutes before the ambulance arrived. She died at the
hospital four hours later without regaining consciousness.

In the weeks that followed, Boonsri did what he had always done: he worked. He returned to court
within ten days. His supervisor offered leave; he declined. He told himself that routine was
medicine, that stillness would be dangerous. What he did not tell anyone was that he had stopped
sleeping in any meaningful way — that he would lie in the bed he and Niran had shared for decades
and watch the ceiling until three or four in the morning, replaying the crossing, recalculating the
angle of the car, wondering whether a half-second earlier or later would have changed everything.

The intrusions are not confined to the night. During hearings, when a witness describes a sudden
event — a door slamming, a car backfiring, any moment of abrupt violence — Boonsri finds himself
back on that street. He does not lose consciousness or visibly react. He has trained himself over
forty years to maintain composure in front of a judge. But internally, he says, he is gone. He is
kneeling on the asphalt. He can feel the warmth of Niran's head in his palms.

He no longer drives past the market. He takes a longer route home that adds fifteen minutes to his
commute, and he does this without thinking about it now, the way one avoids a hot burner. He has
stopped attending the community dinners he and Niran used to host each Songkran. He tells his adult
children he is tired. This is partly true.

What Boonsri finds most difficult to articulate — and what emerges only slowly, across several
sessions — is the guilt. He was the one who suggested they walk to the market that afternoon. He was
the one who stepped off the curb first and then moved slightly ahead. He has constructed, in the
architecture of his grief, an elaborate case for his own culpability, and he prosecutes it nightly
with the same rigor he brings to his courtroom arguments. He knows, intellectually, that he is not
responsible. He has argued personal injury cases. He understands proximate cause. And yet the
knowledge sits inert, unable to reach whatever part of him is still on that street corner.

He describes himself as hollowed out. He eats because eating is something a person does. He goes to
work because his clients need him, and this — the needs of others — remains perhaps the only thing
that reliably pulls him forward into the present moment. He is good at his job. He has always been
good at his job. But he moves through his days now like a man performing a version of himself, going
through the motions of a life whose center is no longer there.
