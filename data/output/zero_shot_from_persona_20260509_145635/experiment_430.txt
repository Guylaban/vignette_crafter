# The Weight of the Waiting Room

Rajan is a fifty-year-old South Asian man who has worked as an animator for nearly two decades,
spending his days building worlds frame by frame, controlling every detail of movement and
expression. He is soft-spoken and precise, the kind of man who notices things others miss. He sits
in sessions with his hands folded in his lap, and when he talks about his son, he looks just
slightly to the left of wherever he is.

Four years ago, his son Arjun — then fourteen — collapsed during a cricket match in the backyard. By
the time the ambulance arrived, Arjun had had a seizure. The diagnosis that followed was acute
lymphoblastic leukemia. Rajan remembers the oncologist's exact words, the exact angle of light
coming through the hospital blinds, the way his wife Priya gripped his forearm so hard she left
marks. He remembers thinking that he needed to memorize everything, as though paying close enough
attention might give him some control over what happened next.

For eighteen months, Arjun underwent chemotherapy. Rajan took a leave of absence from the studio and
drove his son to treatments twice a week. He watched Arjun become someone smaller — thinner,
quieter, exhausted in a way no teenager should be. There were two hospitalizations for infections,
one of which landed Arjun in the ICU for five days. During those five days, Rajan did not sleep. He
sat in a chair outside the unit and stared at the double doors, certain that if he looked away,
something irreversible would happen. He has never entirely stopped believing that.

Arjun is now in remission. He is eighteen and applying to universities. By every medical measure, he
is well. Rajan knows this. He also cannot fully feel it.

He returned to work two years ago, but the studio is a different place now. He sits at his desk and
draws characters in motion — running, falling, catching themselves — and without warning, his chest
tightens and the hospital comes back. Not as a memory he chooses to visit, but as something that
arrives uninvited: the smell of antiseptic, the sound of an IV alarm, the specific quality of
fluorescent light at three in the morning. He sets down his stylus and waits for it to pass.
Sometimes it takes minutes. Sometimes the rest of the day is lost.

He does not sleep well. He wakes between two and four most mornings and lies still beside Priya,
running through scenarios — what if the remission ends, what if there is a secondary cancer, what if
he misses a sign. He has become hypervigilant about Arjun's health in ways that have begun to strain
their relationship. He texts his son several times a day. He monitors Arjun's energy levels the way
he once monitored vital signs on a hospital monitor. Arjun, who wants to be a normal eighteen-year-
old, has started leaving his phone on silent.

Rajan feels the distance opening between them and does not know how to close it. He and Priya rarely
speak about those eighteen months. She has moved forward in a way that both comforts and bewilders
him. He wonders sometimes if something broke in him during those ICU nights that simply did not
break in her, or whether she is carrying it differently, somewhere he cannot see.

He describes himself as a man who used to love his work. Animation, he explains, is about creating
the illusion of life — making still things move. He says this without irony, but the parallel is not
lost on him. He spends his days animating vitality while feeling, in some fundamental way, frozen in
a hospital corridor four years ago, watching a set of double doors and waiting to learn whether his
son would live.

He is here, he says, because Priya asked him to come. But also, quietly, because he is tired of
being somewhere else every time he is in the room.
