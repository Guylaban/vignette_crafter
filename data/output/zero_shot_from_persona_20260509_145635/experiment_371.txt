# The Weight of a Wrench

Marco is a 22-year-old Latino man who works as an HVAC technician for a commercial contracting
company. He is soft-spoken and tends to sit with his hands folded tightly in his lap during
sessions, as though bracing for something. He came to therapy reluctantly, at the urging of his
older sister, and spent most of the first meeting staring at the floor.

Fourteen months ago, Marco was working a routine maintenance job on a rooftop unit at a mid-rise
office building. He had been on the crew for just over a year and was still learning, eager to prove
himself. He and a senior coworker, a man named Darnell, were working in close quarters on a
compressor unit when Marco loosened a bolt that he believed had already been secured. It had not
been. A pressurized component released violently, and a piece of housing struck Darnell across the
face and neck. Marco watched Darnell collapse. He remembers screaming for help and pressing his
hands against the wound, feeling the blood run warm between his fingers before anyone else arrived.
Darnell survived, but he lost sight in one eye and has not returned to work.

In the weeks after the accident, Marco told himself he just needed to keep moving. He went back to
work within ten days because staying home felt worse — too quiet, too much time to think. But being
on a rooftop again was unbearable. The sound of pressurized systems, the hiss of refrigerant lines,
the ordinary percussion of tools on metal — all of it pulls him back to that afternoon without
warning. He has learned to anticipate certain sounds and will sometimes pause mid-task, jaw tight,
heart hammering, waiting for something terrible to happen. His supervisor has noticed that he works
more slowly now and double-checks connections four or five times before moving on, sometimes longer
than the job requires.

At night, Marco rarely sleeps past three in the morning. When he does sleep, he often wakes from
dreams in which the sequence of events is slightly different — sometimes he reaches out in time,
sometimes the outcome is worse. He wakes with his shirt damp, his chest tight, and lies in the dark
running through the same question he has been asking himself for over a year: *Why didn't I check it
again?* He has no satisfying answer, and the question does not stop coming.

Marco has quietly withdrawn from most of his social life. He used to play fútbol on weekends with
friends from his neighborhood and would spend Sunday afternoons at his mother's house. He still goes
to his mother's occasionally, but he finds it hard to sit at the table and be present. When his
family laughs or the television is loud, he feels a strange irritation that he cannot explain and
does not feel he has the right to express. He has told no one in his family the full details of what
happened. He describes carrying a version of himself at work and another version everywhere else,
and says that neither one feels like him.

He has looked up Darnell's name online more than once, searching for information he is not sure he
wants to find. He has not reached out. He says he has thought about it constantly but cannot imagine
what he would say — that every version of the message he composes in his head sounds hollow against
what Darnell lost. The guilt is not something Marco describes dramatically. It sits in him quietly,
like a stone he has learned to carry without drawing attention to it.

When asked what he hopes for, Marco is quiet for a long moment. Then he says he just wants to stop
feeling like he is still on that rooftop. He wants to pick up a tool without his hands going cold.
He wants to sleep until morning. He says these things plainly, without self-pity, in the tone of a
young man who has already spent a long time waiting to feel like himself again.
