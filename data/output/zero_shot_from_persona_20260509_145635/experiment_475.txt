# The Weight of What Was Taken

Mateo is a 34-year-old Filipino man who works as a chaplain at a mid-sized urban hospital. He is
soft-spoken and deliberate in the way he carries himself — qualities that draw grieving families and
frightened patients to him naturally. People in crisis find him easy to trust. He has spent years
sitting beside the dying, holding the hands of strangers, offering presence when words fall short.
He is practiced at absorbing other people's pain. What he was not prepared for was his own.

Eighteen months ago, Mateo was walking back to his car after a late shift. It was a Tuesday in
November, just past ten o'clock. He had spent the evening with a family whose teenage son had died
after a car accident, and he was still carrying that grief — distracted, moving slowly — when two
men stepped out from between parked cars. One pressed something hard into his ribs. He never saw a
weapon clearly. What he remembers is the voice: low, close to his ear, telling him that if he made a
sound, something would happen to him. He handed over his wallet, his phone, his wedding ring. That
last part was not demanded. He pulled it off himself, instinctively, before they could take it. He
does not know why he did that. It is the detail that haunts him most.

His wife, Clara, had died of ovarian cancer two years before the mugging. The ring was the last
thing he had that felt like a continuous thread to her. When he placed it in a stranger's palm that
night, something in him broke in a way he still cannot fully name.

After the robbery, Mateo told almost no one what had happened. He filed a police report and returned
to work within days. He told himself he was fine — that he had not been physically harmed, that
others had suffered far worse, that faith demanded he not dwell. But he began sleeping with the
lights on. He stopped taking the stairs in the hospital parking garage and started arriving in the
daytime only, rearranging his schedule without explaining why. When his pager sounds unexpectedly,
his heart lurches into a kind of frantic rhythm that takes minutes to slow. He has learned to press
his hand flat against his sternum in those moments, a private gesture no one on the ward has thought
to ask about.

The nights are the hardest. He dreams of the parking lot with variations — sometimes Clara is there,
sometimes it is a patient he could not save, sometimes there is no robbery at all, just the darkness
and the certainty that something terrible is about to happen. He wakes at two or three in the
morning with his undershirt soaked through, and he sits at the kitchen table with the overhead light
on and tries to pray. Mostly he stares. The words that have sustained him through years of ministry
feel distant now, like a language he once spoke fluently and has somehow begun to forget.

He is aware of a creeping numbness that frightens him professionally as much as personally. He sits
with families in the ICU and goes through the motions of comfort — and the motions are good, he is
still skilled — but the felt sense of connection that used to move through him like warmth has grown
thin. He wonders sometimes whether the men in the parking lot took something that cannot be
inventoried. He wonders whether he handed it over himself, the way he handed over the ring.

He has begun to avoid the hospital chapel during his off hours, which he once used as a place of
quiet restoration. The silence there feels dangerous now, too much space for everything he is
holding to expand. He keeps busy instead. He volunteers for extra shifts. He answers every call. He
is, by all external measures, an exceptionally devoted chaplain. Inside, he is a man standing very
still, listening for footsteps.
