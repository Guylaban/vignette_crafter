# Vignette: Priya

Priya is a 66-year-old South Asian woman who has spent the better part of four decades bringing new
life into the world. She works as a senior midwife at a regional hospital, a role she has held with
quiet authority and deep devotion. Her hands have steadied countless frightened women through the
most vulnerable hours of their lives. She is, by every outward measure, a woman of composure and
competence. But there is a version of Priya that her colleagues and even her husband of thirty-eight
years rarely see — the one who wakes at 3 a.m. with her heart hammering against her ribs, certain
that the sound she heard was not the wind but an incoming shell.

Priya grew up in Sri Lanka. When she was in her late twenties, already a trained nurse and newly
married, the civil conflict that had simmered for years erupted into open warfare in her region. For
nearly eight months, she worked in a field medical unit attached to a civilian hospital that found
itself caught between advancing military forces and insurgent positions. She triaged soldiers and
civilians alike, sometimes under direct fire, sometimes in the aftermath of shelling when the
corridors filled with the injured and the dying faster than she could count them. One afternoon in
particular — a Tuesday, she always remembers it was a Tuesday — a mortar strike hit the outer wall
of the ward where she was delivering a baby. The mother survived. The infant survived. A colleague
standing three feet from Priya did not. She finished the delivery with blood on her arms that was
not the mother's and not the child's.

Priya and her husband emigrated to the United Kingdom two years later, and for a long time she
managed. She built a career, raised two children, planted a garden. She told herself the distance —
geographic, temporal — had done its work. She did not speak of the war, not even to her husband, who
had been in a different city during those months and knew only fragments of what she had lived
through.

It is only now, in her mid-sixties, that the past has begun to insist on itself. Her youngest
grandchild was born eighteen months ago, and something about being present in that delivery room —
the particular smell of blood and antiseptic, the fluorescent light, a moment of silence before the
baby cried — cracked something open in her. She found herself back in that ward. Not remembering it.
*In* it. She gripped the bed rail and could not speak for nearly a minute. Her daughter-in-law
assumed she was moved by emotion. She was, in a way she could not explain.

Since then, the intrusions have been relentless. She avoids attending births where complications are
expected. She has started delegating tasks she once performed without hesitation. At night, she
dreams of the colleague who died — sometimes his face, sometimes only his shoes, which she had
stared at while she kept working. She wakes and lies rigid beside her sleeping husband, running
through the logic that she is in England, that it is decades later, that she is safe, and none of it
touches the fear in her body.

During the day she is watchful in ways she cannot fully account for. She sits with her back to the
wall in the hospital canteen. She flinches at the sound of doors slamming on the ward. She has begun
to feel that the world is fundamentally fragile in a way her colleagues seem not to understand, and
this separates her from them in ways she finds difficult to articulate. She is irritable with her
husband, then ashamed of her irritability. She describes herself as feeling "hollowed out" — present
in her life but no longer quite inhabiting it.

Priya has told no one at work what is happening to her. She is a midwife. She is the one who holds
steady. The idea that she might be the one unraveling is something she is only beginning, slowly and
with great reluctance, to allow herself to consider.
