# Vignette: Mae Lin

Mae Lin is a 71-year-old Vietnamese-American woman who has practiced family law for nearly four
decades. She carries herself with the quiet authority of someone accustomed to being the steadiest
person in any room — the one who absorbs other people's crises and transforms them into ordered
arguments, manageable steps, forward motion. For most of her adult life, that steadiness felt like
something she had earned and could rely upon. It no longer does.

Fourteen months ago, Mae Lin was walking from her office to the parking garage two blocks away, as
she had done thousands of times before. It was early evening, still light. A man approached her from
behind, grabbed her by the coat, slammed her against a concrete pillar, and demanded her bag. When
she resisted — a reflex, she says, not a decision — he struck her across the face and threw her to
the ground. He took her bag and ran. The assault lasted less than two minutes. She lay on the
pavement for a moment before a passerby found her, her cheekbone fractured, her hands bleeding from
the concrete.

She told her husband she was fine. She told her colleagues she was fine. She returned to work within
a week, because the alternative — staying home, being still, being alone with what had happened —
felt unbearable in a way she could not yet name.

What she could not have anticipated was how thoroughly the world rearranged itself around that two-
minute event. The parking garage became impossible. She now parks in a surface lot four blocks in
the opposite direction and walks only on streets with heavy foot traffic, even when it adds twenty
minutes to her commute. She cannot tolerate anyone walking closely behind her — in a courthouse
hallway, in a grocery store, anywhere — and will stop abruptly to let them pass, her heart already
hammering before she can reason her way out of it. Evenings have become a particular kind of dread.
She leaves the office before five now, before the light changes, though she would never describe it
that way to a colleague.

Sleep has become a negotiation she usually loses. She wakes between two and four in the morning to
the sensation of being grabbed — not a dream exactly, but something more physical than that, a lurch
of the body that brings her upright and leaves her sitting in the dark for an hour or more,
listening to her husband breathe. She does not wake him. She has not told him how bad the nights
are.

At work, she notices that her concentration fractures in ways it never did before. She will be mid-
sentence in a deposition and lose the thread entirely, distracted by the sound of a door closing too
hard in another room, or by a client who shifts suddenly in his chair. She has begun over-preparing
for hearings she could once have navigated on instinct, filling the extra hours with work as though
competence can be rebuilt through volume.

What troubles her most, she says, is what has happened to her sense of herself. She spent her career
helping women who had been harmed by people who were supposed to protect them, and she had always
believed, privately, that she understood something essential about resilience. Now she is not sure
she understands anything. She feels a low, persistent shame about the fact that she is still
affected — that a man who spent less than two minutes in her presence has taken up permanent
residence in her nervous system. She is angry at herself for being angry. She is exhausted by the
vigilance and embarrassed by the exhaustion.

She sits across from the therapist in a good suit, her hands folded, and speaks in the measured
cadences of someone who has spent a lifetime making arguments. But occasionally her voice catches on
a word — *grabbed*, *ground*, *alone* — and for just a moment the lawyer disappears, and what
remains is simply a woman trying to find her way back to a life that no longer feels entirely safe
to inhabit.
