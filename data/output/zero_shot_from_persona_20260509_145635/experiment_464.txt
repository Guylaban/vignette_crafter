# The Weight of What He Witnessed

Rohan is twenty-four years old, the youngest attaché at his country's embassy in a city that,
eighteen months ago, became a war zone almost overnight. He had arrived full of ambition — pressed
suits, careful notes, the belief that diplomacy could hold the world together with the right words.
He had not yet learned what happens when words run out.

The shelling began on a Tuesday morning. Rohan remembers the exact quality of the light through the
embassy windows, pale and ordinary, before the first explosion shook the building. What followed
were eleven days he does not speak about in sequence, because sequence implies order, and there was
none. He helped carry a colleague whose leg had been torn open by shrapnel. He crouched in a
basement corridor beside a local interpreter named Farid, listening to the sounds of a neighborhood
being dismantled building by building. On the eighth day, Farid left to find his family. Rohan never
saw him again. On the eleventh day, a convoy evacuated the remaining embassy staff. Rohan sat in the
back of an armored vehicle and watched the city disappear through a scratched window, and something
in him went very quiet — a quietness that has not left.

He is back home now, working at the foreign ministry in a glass-and-marble building that smells of
carpet cleaner and coffee. His colleagues admire him. He was *there*, after all. He handled himself.
His supervisors speak of his composure during the evacuation as though it were a credential. Rohan
listens to this and feels nothing he can name, or rather, feels too many things he cannot separate.

Sleep is where the war finds him most reliably. He wakes two, sometimes three times a night, heart
hammering, certain he can smell smoke, certain the building is shaking. In the mornings he is
exhausted in a way that coffee does not touch. His girlfriend, Priya, notices the dark under his
eyes and asks gentle questions he deflects with the same diplomatic fluency he uses at work. He
tells her he is adjusting. He tells himself the same thing.

Certain sounds undo him without warning. A car backfiring on his commute once sent him flat against
a wall, hands raised, before he understood where he was. A construction crew working outside the
ministry produces a low percussive thudding that makes it nearly impossible to think. He has
rerouted his walk to avoid them, adding fifteen minutes to his morning, telling himself this is
practical rather than what it actually is. He has stopped watching the news. He has stopped
answering calls from embassy contacts still posted abroad, because every unfamiliar ringtone briefly
convinces him something terrible is happening.

What troubles him most, in the honest hours, is the numbness. He used to feel things clearly — pride
in his work, pleasure in a good meal, real warmth when Priya laughed. Now those experiences arrive
muffled, as though through the same thick glass he watched the city through. He goes through the
motions of a life that looks, from the outside, entirely intact. He writes cables. He attends
briefings. He takes Priya to dinner on Saturdays and watches himself be present without quite being
present.

He thinks about Farid often — not in a way he can process, but in the sudden, intrusive way of
something unfinished. He does not know whether Farid is alive. He has not tried to find out, because
the trying would require him to sit with the possibility that he is not, and Rohan is not sure he
can do that yet.

He is twenty-four years old. He believes, in the abstract, that he should be fine. He was not a
soldier. He was not even the one who was hurt. He has told himself this so many times that it has
become its own kind of torment — the idea that what broke inside him had no right to break.
