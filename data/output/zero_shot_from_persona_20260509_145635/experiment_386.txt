# The Weight of a Phone Call

Kofi is a sixty-two-year-old Ghanaian man who has worked the floor of a major brokerage firm for
nearly three decades. He is the kind of man his colleagues describe as unflappable — someone who
could watch a market collapse and still speak in measured, deliberate sentences. That steadiness, he
says, was something he built carefully over a lifetime. He does not feel steady anymore.

Fourteen months ago, Kofi received a phone call shortly after midnight. His son, Kwame, thirty-one
years old, had been killed in a car accident on the interstate — a wrong-way driver, alcohol
involved, nothing that could have been anticipated or prevented. Kofi was standing in his kitchen in
his robe when he heard the words. He remembers the refrigerator humming. He remembers setting the
phone down very gently on the counter, as though it were fragile. He does not remember the hours
that followed.

Kofi and Kwame had been rebuilding their relationship after Kofi's divorce from Kwame's mother eight
years earlier. The divorce had been bitter, and Kofi carries considerable guilt about the years of
distance that followed. In the months before the accident, they had begun meeting for dinner on
Sundays. Kofi had started teaching Kwame about investing. He describes those Sunday evenings as the
best part of his week. He had believed, quietly, that they had time.

Now time is the problem. Kofi cannot stop his mind from moving backward. He replays the last Sunday
dinner with a kind of compulsive precision — what Kwame ordered, what he said about a girl he was
seeing, the way he laughed at something on his phone and turned the screen to show his father. Kofi
searches these memories for something he might have missed, some sign he should have recognized,
though he knows rationally there was nothing to find. The searching does not stop.

At work, Kofi sits in front of screens that scroll with numbers and he finds himself staring through
them. He has made two significant errors in judgment over the past year — errors that would have
been unthinkable to his former self — and his supervisor has quietly suggested he consider taking a
leave. Kofi refuses. The trading floor is loud and relentless, and on his best days, its noise
crowds out the silence that waits for him at home. On his worst days, the ringing of his own phone
sends a cold shock through his chest that takes several minutes to subside. He has begun letting
calls go to voicemail.

He sleeps poorly and briefly. When he does sleep, he often wakes at two or three in the morning in a
state of acute alertness, heart pounding, though he cannot always identify a specific nightmare —
only a feeling of tremendous dread, and then the slow, crushing return of knowing. He has stopped
cooking dinner. His apartment, once kept with the precision of a man who values order, has grown dim
and cluttered. He has not spoken to his ex-wife since the funeral, though they stood beside each
other at the grave.

Kofi describes himself as someone who does not believe in falling apart. He was raised to be
capable, to provide, to endure. He tells his story in a careful, controlled voice, but his hands
move restlessly in his lap, and twice he stops mid-sentence and looks toward the window. He says he
is not sure who he is without the version of the future he had been building — the one where he and
Kwame had more Sundays, more dinners, more time to make things right. He says he keeps thinking that
if he had called that evening instead of waiting for the weekend, Kwame might have stayed home. He
knows this is not logical. He is a man who has spent his life trusting logic. He does not know what
to trust now.
