# The Weight of the Gavel

Margaret is fifty-two years old, a circuit court judge in a mid-sized Midwestern city, and by most
outward measures she is the steadiest person in the room. She speaks in measured cadences. She
rarely raises her voice. Attorneys who appear before her describe her as unflappable. What they do
not know is that she keeps a small bottle of antacids in her top drawer and takes one almost every
morning before she puts on her robe, standing in her chambers with her eyes closed, steadying
herself for whatever the day might require of her.

Fifteen years ago, before she ascended to the bench, Margaret spent eight months in a conflict zone
in Central Africa as a legal observer and relief coordinator with an international humanitarian
organization. She had volunteered during a sabbatical from her law practice, drawn by what she
describes as a need to do something that mattered beyond billing hours. What she encountered there
was beyond anything her training had prepared her for. She witnessed mass displacement, severe
malnutrition in children, and on two occasions was present when shelling struck near the
distribution sites where her team was working. During one of those incidents, a young local
interpreter she had grown close to was critically wounded beside her. She held him until the medical
team arrived, his blood soaking through her clothing, not knowing whether he would survive. He did
not.

She returned home and did what she had always done with difficulty — she pushed forward. She
finished her sabbatical, returned to practice, and within four years had been appointed to the
bench. She married during that period and divorced six years later. She does not connect the divorce
to what happened in Africa, though she acknowledges she became "harder to reach" around that time.

The trouble, as she now understands it, has never really left. She sleeps poorly, waking between two
and four in the morning with a sensation she calls "the pressure" — a tightening in her chest and a
vivid, unwanted recall of that afternoon: the sound of the blast, the dust, the specific quality of
the light. Some mornings she wakes already exhausted, as though she has been working through the
night. She has learned to function on five hours, which she frames to herself as discipline.

In the courtroom, she manages well until she doesn't. Cases involving violence — particularly those
with photographic evidence, or testimony describing injuries in precise detail — can pull her
sideways without warning. She has developed a habit of writing on her notepad during graphic
testimony, not notes exactly, but shapes, lines, something to anchor her hand and her attention to
the present room. She has never recused herself. She is not sure she could explain to anyone why she
would need to.

She is hypervigilant in ways she has rationalized as professional thoroughness. She notices exits.
She reads people's hands before their faces. Loud, sudden sounds in the courthouse — a door
slamming, a cart dropped in the hallway — produce a startle response she has learned to absorb
without visible expression, though her heart can be hammering for minutes afterward.

She describes herself as "not a person who dwells," and she means it as a point of pride. She has
not spoken about Africa at length to anyone in years. Her ex-husband knew the outline. Her two adult
children know she went and that it was difficult. She has a few close colleagues on the bench, but
the culture of the judiciary is not one that invites this kind of conversation, and she is not sure
she would want it to be.

What brought her to therapy was not a crisis but an accumulation — a morning when she sat in her car
in the courthouse parking garage for twenty minutes, unable to make herself go inside, watching the
fluorescent light flicker overhead, thinking about a boy she could not save in a country most people
she knows cannot find on a map.
