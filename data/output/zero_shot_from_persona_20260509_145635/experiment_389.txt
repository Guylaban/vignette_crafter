# The Weight of Hands

Maya is a 35-year-old Filipino physical therapist who has been married for three years to her
current husband, a man she describes as "the gentlest person I've ever known." She says this with a
kind of wonder, as if she still cannot fully believe it. Before him, there was Daniel — four years
of a relationship that began with tenderness and ended with her standing in a hospital parking lot
at two in the morning, pressing a bag of ice against her jaw, trying to decide whether to go inside.

She never went inside. She drove herself home instead.

The abuse began gradually, the way she now explains nerve damage to her patients — imperceptibly at
first, then suddenly undeniable. By the third year with Daniel, the violence had become a rhythm she
organized her life around. She learned to read the particular silence that preceded his rages, the
way his shoulders would draw inward before his hands moved outward. She became, she says, an expert
in him. She knew exactly which muscles were tensing before the strike came.

The final incident happened on a Tuesday evening. Daniel slammed her head against the edge of the
kitchen counter, and she lost consciousness briefly. When she came to, she was on the tile floor,
and he was sitting at the kitchen table, eating dinner. She left that night with her car keys and
her work bag. She did not go back.

That was four years ago. By any external measure, Maya has rebuilt her life completely. She has a
career she loves, a husband who treats her with consistent care, and a small home with a garden she
tends on weekends. But the past is not finished with her.

She wakes most nights between two and three in the morning, heart pounding, convinced that someone
is in the room. She lies rigid beside her sleeping husband, listening, cataloguing sounds — the
refrigerator hum, traffic outside, the house settling — until her body slowly releases its
emergency. Some mornings she arrives at the clinic already exhausted, moving through her patient
caseload on discipline alone.

The hardest part of her work, she admits, is her hands. Physical therapy requires constant touch,
and she is good at it — her patients trust her, and she is skilled at reading bodies in pain. But
there are moments, particularly when a male patient flinches or moves unexpectedly, when something
seizes in her chest and her hands go briefly still. She recovers quickly enough that no one notices.
She has become skilled at that too.

She cannot tolerate raised voices. In the break room recently, two colleagues had a loud, laughing
argument about a sports game, and Maya found herself in the supply closet, back against the
shelving, waiting for her breathing to slow. She told no one. She returned to her patient and
finished the session.

cannot stop. When he is quiet, she scans his face for signs she knows intellectually are not there.
She has begun avoiding the kitchen in the evenings. She cannot fully explain this to herself. She

waiting. She has not told her husband the full extent of what happened with Daniel. She has not told
