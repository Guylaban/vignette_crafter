# Vignette: Amara, 35-Year-Old Police Officer

Amara has worn a badge for eleven years. She grew up in Lagos before her family immigrated when she
was a teenager, and she carried with her a fierce sense of justice that eventually pulled her toward
law enforcement. Her colleagues describe her as composed, decisive, someone who moves toward danger
without flinching. That reputation feels like a cruel joke to her now.

Fourteen months ago, Amara was sexually assaulted by a fellow officer — a man she had worked
alongside for three years, someone she had trusted enough to accept a ride home after a late shift.
What happened in that car remade her understanding of the world in ways she is still struggling to
name. She did not report it immediately. She tells herself it was because she understood exactly
what reporting meant — the interviews, the skepticism, the whisper networks inside the precinct. She
is a Black woman in a department where she has always had to work twice as hard to be taken half as
seriously. She knew the math. She stayed silent and went back to work the next morning.

For the first several weeks, she functioned on a kind of autopilot that frightened her in
retrospect. She was efficient, even praised. Then the dreams started. She would wake at two or three
in the morning, heart slamming, the interior of that car so vivid she could smell the air freshener
hanging from the rearview mirror. Her husband, Kofi, would reach for her and she would go rigid. She
began sleeping on the couch and telling him she was restless, that she didn't want to disturb him.
He has stopped asking questions. The distance between them has grown into something neither of them
speaks about directly.

At work, Amara has developed what she privately calls her system — a set of rigid routes through the
precinct that allow her to avoid passing his desk. On the two occasions she has encountered him
unexpectedly, her body has responded before her mind could intervene: a cold flood through her
chest, her vision narrowing, her hand moving without thought toward her holster. Both times she
walked into a bathroom stall and stood there until her breathing slowed. She has not told anyone
what happened in those moments.

She finds herself scanning every room she enters, cataloguing exits, positioning herself with her
back to walls. This is not entirely foreign to her — police training encourages situational
awareness — but she recognizes that what she does now is different. It is not tactical. It is
desperate. She cannot turn it off at home, at the grocery store, at her sister's house during Sunday
dinners where her nieces climb into her lap and she sits there feeling both present and utterly
unreachable.

Amara has started drinking more than she used to. A glass of wine became two, became a habit she
guards carefully, measuring herself against some internal threshold she keeps adjusting. She tells
herself she is managing. She is not managing.

She has begun to believe something is permanently broken in her — not in a metaphorical sense but in
a concrete, cellular way. She was someone who trusted her own instincts, who believed in her ability
to read people and situations. That trust is gone. She second-guesses herself in the field in ways
that terrify her, because hesitation in her line of work has consequences. Last month she froze for
several seconds during a domestic call when a man raised his voice, and the officer beside her had
to move without her. She replayed those seconds for days.

She came to therapy, she says, because she is afraid of herself — afraid of what the freezing means,
afraid of the drinking, afraid of the widening silence in her marriage. She has not yet said aloud
what happened to her. She describes a traumatic event, a situation, something that occurred. She is
not ready for the word. She sits in the chair across from the therapist with her hands folded in her
lap and her posture perfectly correct, and she looks exhausted in a way that has nothing to do with
sleep.
