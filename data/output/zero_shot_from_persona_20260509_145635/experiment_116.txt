**Vignette: Marcus, 34**

Marcus has always understood the sea. He grew up near the water in coastal Virginia, joined the Navy
at eighteen, and spent the better part of his adult life reading weather systems, navigating open
ocean, and trusting that preparation could outpace catastrophe. That belief held until the summer
two years ago, when his vessel was stationed off the Gulf Coast and Hurricane Ida made landfall with
a fury that no amount of training had truly prepared him for.

The storm arrived faster than the updated models had projected. Marcus was on deck coordinating the
securing of equipment when the surge hit — a wall of water that took out two junior sailors and
pinned him against a steel railing for what he later learned was nearly four minutes. He remembers
the sound most vividly: not wind, but a deep, pressurized roar, like the ocean had turned inside
out. He was pulled to safety by a petty officer. The two sailors survived, though one sustained
serious injuries. Marcus walked away with bruised ribs and a laceration along his left forearm that
required sixteen stitches. On paper, he was fine.

He returned to duty within the week. There was paperwork, a brief medical clearance, and the quiet
expectation that officers absorb difficulty and move forward. Marcus did what he had always done —
he moved forward. He told his wife, Dana, that it had been a close call, nothing more.

But the nights told a different story. For months after the storm, Marcus would wake between two and
four in the morning, chest tight, convinced he could hear that pressurized roar somewhere outside
the bedroom window. He would lie still for long minutes, cataloguing sounds — the hum of the
refrigerator, Dana's breathing, a car passing on the street — until he could locate himself in the
present. He rarely went back to sleep.

At work, he began struggling in ways that confused him. He is a methodical, respected officer,
someone his crew describes as unflappable. But during a training exercise six months after the
incident, when an unexpected squall rolled in, he found himself frozen at the helm for nearly thirty
seconds — an eternity in his world. He covered it, barked an order, and no one seemed to notice. He
noticed. He started reviewing weather reports compulsively before every deployment, sometimes
checking the same forecast four or five times in an hour.

At home, Dana has noticed a flatness in him that she struggles to name. He is present but not quite
there — engaged enough at dinner, patient with their seven-year-old daughter, but somehow behind
glass. He has stopped taking his daughter to the beach, offering rotating excuses. He has not told
Dana why. There is a part of him that believes if he says it aloud — that standing near open water
now fills him with a dread he cannot rationalize — it will mean something is permanently wrong with
him.

He does not think of himself as someone who was traumatized. He thinks of himself as someone who had
a bad day at sea and has not quite gotten his footing back. He uses that language carefully, because
the other language feels like a door he is not ready to open. He is aware, in a clinical corner of
his mind, that something shifted that day on the deck. He is aware that the man who believed
preparation could outpace catastrophe no longer fully believes that. He grieves that loss quietly,
the way he grieves most things — privately, and without much ceremony.

He came to therapy at Dana's gentle insistence, framing it initially as stress management. In
sessions, he is thoughtful and articulate, precise in the way officers often are. He chooses his
words carefully. But occasionally, when describing the sound of the water, he goes quiet for a
moment longer than usual, and his hands, resting on his knees, press down just slightly, as if
steadying himself against something only he can feel.
