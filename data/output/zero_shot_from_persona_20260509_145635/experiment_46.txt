# The Weight of the Overpass

Malcolm is a 63-year-old Māori man who has worked as a financial analyst for nearly three decades.
He is the kind of man who has built his life around precision — spreadsheets, forecasts, risk
models. He is married to his wife, Aroha, and they have two adult children. Until eighteen months
ago, he would have described his life as steady, even fortunate.

It was an ordinary Tuesday morning when everything changed. Malcolm was driving to work on the
motorway when a concrete panel from an aging overpass gave way. The slab struck the roof of his car,
partially crushing it. He remembers the sound — a crack like the world splitting — and then
darkness, and then the smell of dust and something burning. He was conscious when the first
responders arrived, pinned behind the steering wheel, unable to move his left arm, blood from a
scalp laceration running into his eye. He spent three weeks in hospital with a fractured clavicle,
three broken ribs, and a traumatic brain injury classified as moderate. Physically, his doctors are
satisfied with his recovery. Malcolm is less certain.

In the months that followed, he returned to his office in the city, resumed his meetings, and sat
once again in front of the dual monitors he knows so well. But something fundamental had shifted
beneath the surface of his competence. He finds it nearly impossible to concentrate. Numbers that
once arranged themselves effortlessly in his mind now seem to slide away from him. He rereads the
same paragraph four and five times, convinced he has missed something critical, that a catastrophic
error is hiding somewhere in the data. His colleagues have noticed he is quieter. He has not told
them why.

He does not drive on motorways anymore. He takes a longer route to work each day, adding forty
minutes to his commute, telling Aroha it is simply a preference. She knows it is not. When he does
drive under any overpass or bridge — even on the slower suburban roads — his hands tighten on the
wheel, his chest contracts, and he is certain, with the full force of physical conviction, that the
structure above him is about to come down. He knows intellectually that this is unlikely. The
knowledge does nothing.

Sleep has become its own ordeal. He wakes between two and four in the morning, heart slamming, often
unsure for a moment whether he is in his bed or still in the car. He hears the crack. He smells the
dust. Some nights he does not go back to sleep at all, and he sits in the kitchen in the dark,
drinking tea, waiting for the sun to make the world feel solid again.

Malcolm has begun to feel a creeping shame about all of this. He is a man who spent his career
calculating risk, who prided himself on clear-headed analysis under pressure. He survived. He tells
himself that repeatedly — *you survived* — as though survival should be sufficient to close the
account. He does not understand why his nervous system has not received the same memo. He worries
that what he is experiencing is a kind of weakness, something he cannot quite name and therefore
cannot fix.

Aroha has gently suggested he speak to someone. He agreed, though he sat in the waiting room for
twenty minutes before his first appointment, nearly talking himself out of it. He is a private man,
and speaking about the accident — about the fear, about the way time seems to collapse without
warning and deposit him back inside that crushed car — does not come naturally.

What he does say, quietly, in one of his early sessions, is this: *I used to feel like I understood
how things worked. Now I don't trust anything to hold.*
