# The Weight of Knowing

Kofi is a 37-year-old Ghanaian psychologist who works at a community mental health clinic in a mid-
sized city, where he has spent the better part of a decade helping survivors of trauma rebuild their
lives. He is well-regarded by colleagues, known for his warmth and his ability to sit with people in
their darkest moments without flinching. What none of them know is that for the past fourteen
months, he has been living inside a darkness of his own.

It happened after a professional conference — a late evening, a colleague he trusted, a hotel room
he did not leave the way he entered it. Kofi does not use the word easily, even in his own mind. He
knows the clinical definition better than most people alive. He has written about it, lectured about
it, held space for others who carry it. And yet when he tries to apply the word to what happened to
him that night, something in him recoils, as though the language belongs to other people — to his
patients, not to him.

In the weeks that followed, he told himself he was processing. He is a psychologist, after all. He
knows the stages, knows the neurobiology, knows that avoidance only deepens the wound. And still he
avoided. He stopped attending conferences. He rearranged his caseload so he no longer worked with
male survivors of sexual violence, telling himself it was a boundary issue, a matter of professional
self-care. He began taking a longer route to work to avoid passing the hotel where it happened,
adding twenty minutes to his commute each morning, rain or shine.

Sleep has become something he negotiates with rather than surrenders to. Most nights he lies awake
until two or three in the morning, his body alert in a way that exhausts him, and when he does
sleep, he often wakes abruptly, heart hammering, the details of that night vivid and immediate, as
though no time has passed at all. He showers in the morning and sometimes again at noon. He is not
always sure why.

At work, he functions. He listens, reflects, asks the right questions. But there are moments — a
patient describes a violation, or uses a particular phrase, or a certain quality of afternoon light
falls through his office window — and he is no longer fully present. He is somewhere else entirely,
and it takes effort to find his way back. He has become skilled at concealing this. Being a
psychologist, he understands exactly which expressions to wear, which responses to offer, to appear
composed. This knowledge feels, at times, like a second betrayal.

He has not told his family in Accra. His mother calls every Sunday, and he answers cheerfully,
asking about his younger sisters, his aunts, the neighborhood. The idea of telling her — of telling
anyone — produces a specific kind of nausea he cannot fully articulate. There is shame woven into
it, though he would challenge that shame fiercely in any patient who brought it to him. He knows
shame is not his to carry. Knowing this changes nothing.

He has grown quieter socially, declining invitations he once would have welcomed, finding reasons
not to attend dinners, birthday gatherings, the informal drinks his colleagues organize on Friday
evenings. He tells people he is busy. He is, in a sense — occupied by something constant and
interior that leaves little room for lightness.

What brought him to therapy, finally, was a session with a patient. The patient — a young man, a
university student — described feeling like a stranger in his own body, and Kofi had to excuse
himself to compose himself in the bathroom. Standing at the sink, looking at his own face in the
mirror, he recognized something he had been carefully not recognizing for over a year. He made an
appointment the same afternoon. He sat in the waiting room with his hands folded in his lap,
feeling, for the first time, like someone who needed to be there.
