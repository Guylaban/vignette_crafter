# Vikram

Vikram is a 32-year-old South Asian man who works as a housekeeper at a mid-sized hotel in the city.
He has been married for six years, and by most accounts, he describes his life before the incident
as quiet and predictable in the way he liked — early mornings, honest work, coming home to his wife
Priya in the evenings. He is a man who takes pride in order, in things being clean and in their
right place. That sense of order was shattered fourteen months ago on a Tuesday night when he was
walking home from a late shift.

It was just past eleven o'clock. Vikram had taken the same route dozens of times without incident.
Two men approached him near a poorly lit stretch of road beside a car park. One of them had a knife.
They demanded his phone, his wallet, and the small backpack he carried. When he hesitated — frozen,
he says, just completely frozen — one of the men grabbed him by the collar and pressed the blade
against the side of his neck. He remembers the cold of the metal and the smell of the man's breath
and the sound of his own heartbeat in his ears. He handed everything over. They ran. He stood on the
pavement for what felt like a very long time before his legs started moving again.

In the weeks that followed, Vikram told himself he was fine. Nothing had actually happened to him,
he reasoned. He still had his life. He went back to work, changed his route home, and said little to
Priya beyond the basic facts. But sleep became difficult almost immediately. He would lie awake
listening to sounds in the flat — a door in the hallway, footsteps on the stairs above — and his
body would respond as though the knife were at his throat again. When he did sleep, he woke from
dreams he couldn't always fully recall but that left him drenched and rigid, heart hammering.

At work, the symptoms quietly colonized his days. Vikram pushes a trolley through hotel corridors,
knocks on doors, enters rooms that are sometimes still occupied. What used to be routine now fills
him with a dread he cannot always explain. When a door opens unexpectedly or a guest moves suddenly,
he startles badly — dropping things, stepping back, apologizing in a rush. His colleagues have
noticed and made comments he tries to laugh off. He has become slower in his work, not from laziness
but because he finds himself checking corridors before he enters them, scanning for exits,
positioning himself near doors. He is exhausted by his own vigilance.

At home, Priya tells him he has become distant. He knows she is right. He finds it difficult to sit
in the living room with the curtains open after dark. He has stopped walking anywhere alone at night
and has declined several social invitations from family members, including a cousin's engagement
celebration that he genuinely wanted to attend. When Priya asks what is wrong, he struggles to find
words that don't sound, to him, like weakness. He comes from a family where men were expected to
absorb difficulty quietly, and he carries that expectation like a second skin.

What troubles Vikram most, he says, is that he no longer trusts his own body. He describes feeling
as though some part of him is permanently waiting for something terrible to happen — scanning,

minutes to his commute. Sometimes he sees a man in a dark jacket on the bus and feels his chest
tighten until the man gets off. He cannot watch certain scenes in films. He finds little pleasure in
food, in conversation, in the small rituals that used to anchor his evenings.

He sits across from the therapist with his hands folded carefully in his lap, speaking in measured
