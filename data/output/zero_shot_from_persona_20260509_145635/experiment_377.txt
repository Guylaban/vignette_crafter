# The Weight of the Room

Marcus is a 45-year-old investigative journalist who has spent the better part of two decades
covering conflict zones across the Middle East and Eastern Europe. His colleagues describe him as
meticulous and fearless — a man who believed that bearing witness was the most important work a
person could do. That belief was tested, and ultimately fractured, three years ago when he was taken
captive while reporting near the Syrian-Turkish border.

He was held for eleven days. In the telling of it, he is precise about the number — eleven days — as
though the exactness of that figure is the one thing he can still control. He was kept in a
windowless room, bound for much of the time, uncertain whether his captors intended to ransom him,
execute him, or simply forget about him. He heard other men being beaten in adjacent rooms. He was
not given consistent food or water. On the fourth day, he was made to kneel in front of a camera,
and he became convinced, with absolute certainty, that he was about to die. He did not die. He was
released through a negotiated exchange that he was not informed of until the moment it happened. He
walked out into blinding afternoon light and felt, he says, nothing at all.

That numbness lasted about six weeks. Then the room came back.

Marcus now lives in Brussels with his wife, Elise, and their two teenage children. From the outside,
he has resumed something resembling his former life. He still works as a journalist, though he has
moved to an editorial role and no longer travels to conflict zones — a decision he frames as a
professional choice rather than a personal one, though he acknowledges, reluctantly, that he cannot
board a plane without his heart hammering against his ribs. He sits with his back to walls in
restaurants. He checks the locks on the doors of his house two, sometimes three times before bed,
and then lies awake anyway, listening to the house settle around him.

Sleep, when it comes, is rarely safe. He dreams of the room — not always the actual room, but rooms
that share its logic: low ceilings, no exits, the sound of footsteps approaching. He wakes most
nights between two and four in the morning, drenched in sweat, briefly unable to remember where he
is. Elise has learned not to touch him in those first moments of waking. Early in the first year,
she reached for him instinctively, and he grabbed her wrist hard enough to leave a bruise. He still
carries the shame of that.

At work, he functions adequately, though his concentration has become unreliable in ways that
frustrate him deeply. He was once known for his ability to hold vast amounts of information in his
head, to construct complex narratives from scattered details. Now he loses threads mid-sentence. He
rereads paragraphs and finds they have left no impression. When a younger colleague recently
returned from reporting in Ukraine and described a detention facility he had briefly been held in —
speaking about it with the casual bravado Marcus once recognized in himself — Marcus stood up from
the editorial meeting and left the room without explanation. He sat in the bathroom for twenty
minutes, breathing carefully, waiting for his hands to stop shaking.

He does not talk about what happened with Elise, not really. She knows the outline. She does not
know that he still, three years later, wakes some mornings and spends several seconds genuinely
uncertain whether the life around him is real or whether the room is the truth and everything else
is the dream. He has started to suspect that the person who walked out into that afternoon light was
not quite the same person who had walked in — and that the distance between those two men may be
permanent.

He is not sure what to do with that suspicion. So mostly, he says nothing.
