**Vignette: Marcus, Age 38**

Marcus is a secondary school history teacher who has spent the better part of his adult life
believing that the world, on balance, is a reasonably safe place. That belief shattered on an
ordinary Tuesday evening fourteen months ago, when he was walking home from a staff meeting through
a part of his neighborhood he had crossed hundreds of times before. Two men approached him quickly
from behind. One pressed something hard into the small of his back — he still does not know whether
it was a knife or a finger shaped to mimic one — and told him, in a low and unhurried voice, that if
he made a sound he would not walk away. They took his wallet, his phone, and his watch, a gift from
his wife on their wedding day. The whole thing lasted perhaps ninety seconds. Then they were gone,
and Marcus stood alone on the pavement, unable to move for what felt like a very long time.

He did not tell his wife, Elise, for three days. When he finally did, he minimized it — *just a
mugging, I'm fine, these things happen* — and he half-believed that himself. He filed a police
report, replaced his phone, and returned to work the following morning. For a few weeks, he managed
to convince himself that he had processed it cleanly and moved on.

Then the dreams began. In them, the scenario shifts and expands in ways that reality did not: the
men catch him when he tries to run, or the hard object at his back turns out to be real, or Elise is
there and he cannot protect her. He wakes most nights between two and four in the morning with his
heart slamming in his chest, the sheets damp, the darkness of the bedroom feeling briefly hostile
rather than familiar. He lies still and waits for the room to become ordinary again, and sometimes
it takes a long time.

During the day, Marcus functions — mostly. He is at school, he teaches his lessons, he jokes with
colleagues in the staffroom. But he has rerouted his walk home to avoid that street entirely, adding
fifteen minutes to his commute regardless of weather. He has also stopped walking after dark
whenever he can avoid it, and when he cannot, he keeps his phone in his hand and his eyes moving in
a way that Elise has noticed and gently questioned. He finds it difficult to explain to her that he
is not being dramatic; that the scanning feels involuntary, like breathing.

In the classroom, concentration has become unreliable. He will be mid-sentence about the causes of
the First World War when a student scrapes a chair loudly or slams a locker in the corridor outside,
and his body responds before his mind can — a sharp spike of adrenaline, a momentary blankness, a
need to pause and collect himself. His students, teenagers who notice everything, have started
asking if he is all right. He finds those questions mortifying.

He describes a persistent sense of being changed in some way he cannot fully articulate — as though
the version of himself that trusted the evening walk home simply no longer exists, and he is not
sure what replaced it. He feels irritable with Elise in ways that seem disproportionate to what
prompts them, and then guilty about the irritability. He has withdrawn from a five-a-side football
group he played with for six years, finding the social effort required too much on top of everything
else. He tells himself he is tired.

What Marcus finds most distressing, sitting in the chair across from the therapist and finally
saying things aloud that he has not said to anyone, is not the fear itself but the sense of its
permanence — the quiet, unsettling conviction that the man who walked down that street fourteen
months ago and the man sitting here now are not quite the same person, and that the distance between
them may not be crossable.
