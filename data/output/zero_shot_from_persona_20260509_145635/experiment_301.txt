# The Weight of What He Witnessed

Tariq is a 34-year-old Lebanese-American man who works as a publisher for a mid-sized literary press
in Chicago. He is married to his wife, Nadia, and by most outward measures, his life carries the
texture of something built carefully and with intention — a home they chose together, a career he
loves, a circle of close friends. But for the past two years, Tariq has been living alongside a
version of himself he does not recognize, one that emerged the night he watched his younger brother,
Rami, get struck by a car while the two of them were walking home from dinner.

It happened quickly and without warning, the way the worst things always do. One moment Rami was
laughing at something Tariq had said, and the next he was in the street, and then he was on the
ground, and Tariq was running toward him, pressing his hands against his brother's head, shouting
for someone to call for help. Rami survived, but he spent three weeks in the ICU and required months
of rehabilitation for a traumatic brain injury that permanently altered his speech and his ability
to work. Tariq was there for all of it — the surgery, the slow and uncertain recovery, the grief of
watching his brother relearn how to be himself.

In the months that followed, Tariq found it increasingly difficult to return to his own life. He
went back to his desk, back to manuscripts and editorial meetings and author calls, but he sat in
those spaces like a man behind glass. He would be reading a submission — something quiet and
domestic, a novel about family — and without warning he would be back on that street, his hands wet,
Rami's face turned at an angle that still makes him sick to remember. He has learned to keep a glass
of water nearby at work because his mouth goes dry when it happens, and he has learned to position
himself near the window in meetings so he can look out if he needs to anchor himself somewhere.

At home, the distance between him and Nadia has grown in ways neither of them fully understands. He
loves her, and he knows she sees his suffering, but when she reaches for him at night he often lies
still and rigid, unable to explain why closeness feels like something he has to brace against. He
sleeps badly. He wakes between two and four in the morning and lies in the dark replaying the
sequence of events, searching — still — for the moment he could have pulled Rami back, the half-
second he believes he missed. Some nights he does not sleep at all and arrives at work hollow-eyed,
running on coffee and the performance of competence.

He has stopped walking on streets without crosswalks. He takes longer routes. He has declined two
invitations to visit Rami because the last time he went, seeing his brother struggle to find a word
in the middle of a sentence sent him to the bathroom, where he stood gripping the sink for several
minutes, breathing through what he now understands was a panic attack but in the moment felt like
his body simply refusing to continue. He has not told Rami any of this. He believes, with a quiet
and stubborn conviction, that he does not have the right to struggle when Rami is the one who was
hurt.

At work, Tariq is known as thoughtful and exacting, someone who reads with unusual care and
remembers details about every author he works with. His colleagues do not know that some days he
reads the same paragraph four times without absorbing it, or that he keeps the blinds in his office
angled so he cannot see the street below. He carries the event everywhere — in his body, in his
sleep, in the way he flinches at the sound of brakes — and he has begun to wonder quietly whether
the man he was before that night is someone he will ever fully find his way back to.
