**Vignette: Priya**

Priya is a fifty-year-old South Asian woman who has spent the better part of her adult life
navigating the careful, measured world of diplomacy. She carries herself with the kind of composure
that comes from decades of representing her country in rooms where every word matters. She is
precise, controlled, and professionally accomplished — qualities that have served her well and that
she now leans on, perhaps too heavily, to hold herself together.

Three years ago, Priya was stationed at a consulate in a mid-sized European city when a cleaning
contractor inadvertently introduced an industrial chemical solvent into the building's ventilation
system. Within minutes of arriving at her desk that morning, she noticed a sharp, acrid smell she
couldn't identify. Before she could act on the instinct that something was wrong, she was overcome —
eyes burning, throat closing, her body dropping to the floor of her office before she could reach
the door. She regained consciousness in a hospital, where she was told she had sustained a
significant chemical inhalation injury. Two junior colleagues were hospitalized alongside her. One
of them did not fully recover.

In the months that followed, Priya returned to work. She told herself she was fine. There were
cables to draft, meetings to attend, a career she had built with extraordinary discipline. But
something had shifted in ways she found difficult to name and more difficult to admit.

She now notices that she cannot enter a room without first scanning for the nearest exit. When she
walks into any enclosed space — a conference room, a restaurant, even her own apartment — there is a
beat of something close to panic before she locates the door. She has rearranged her home office so
that her desk faces the window, which she keeps cracked open regardless of the weather. Colleagues
have commented on it. She laughs it off.

Certain smells stop her completely. Cleaning products, industrial solvents, even the sharp scent of
a colleague's hand sanitizer can pull her back to that morning with a vividness that feels less like
memory and more like reliving. Her throat tightens. Her vision narrows. She has learned to excuse
herself quickly and stand outside until the sensation passes, which she describes as "clearing her
head." She does not call it what it is.

Sleep is fragmented and unreliable. She wakes in the early hours with her heart hammering, the
details of the dream already dissolving but the physical dread fully intact. She lies still and
waits for it to pass, which it does, eventually. She has started keeping the television on through
the night as background noise — something she finds embarrassing and cannot entirely explain.

What troubles Priya most is not the intrusions themselves but what they have done to the version of
herself she has always known. She was someone who moved through the world with confidence and
purpose. Now she feels, beneath the polished surface she presents, a persistent low-grade vigilance
that exhausts her. She is irritable in ways she monitors carefully. She has declined two postings in
the past eighteen months, citing scheduling conflicts. The truth, which she has not said aloud until
recently, is that she was afraid of being far from home, far from hospitals, far from the exits she
has memorized.

She describes the experience of that morning not with dramatic language but with a quiet, almost
clinical detachment that occasionally cracks. She says she felt, for the first time in her adult
life, completely powerless — not over a negotiation or a political outcome, but over her own body,
in her own office, on an ordinary Tuesday. That, she says, is what she cannot seem to get past. The
ordinariness of it. The fact that she never saw it coming.
