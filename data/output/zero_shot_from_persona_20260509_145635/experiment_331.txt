# The Weight of Smoke

Margaret is a seventy-three-year-old widowed painter who lives alone in a small house on the
outskirts of a rural town in northern Ontario. She has spent most of her adult life working in
relative solitude, which she always considered a gift — long mornings in her studio, afternoons
walking the land, evenings reading beside the woodstove her late husband installed the year they
married. That life, the one she had carefully and deliberately built, ended on a Wednesday afternoon
three years ago when a chemical plant eleven kilometers from her home exploded, sending a dense
plume of toxic smoke across the valley where she lives.

She remembers the sound first — a low concussion that she felt in her sternum before she heard it
with her ears. She was standing at her easel when the windows rattled. Within twenty minutes,
emergency alerts were flooding her phone, and the sky outside had turned a sickly yellowish grey.
She was told to shelter in place. She sealed her doors and windows with wet towels the way the radio
instructed, and she sat alone in her kitchen for eleven hours while the air outside turned
poisonous. Her nearest neighbor was half a kilometer away. She did not know if anyone was coming.
She did not know if the air she was breathing was already killing her. She did not know, for a long
time, whether what she was experiencing was survivable.

It was survivable, in the narrowest sense. The plume dispersed. No one in her immediate area died.
Officials declared the situation contained within two days. But Margaret has not felt contained
since.

She sleeps in fragments now, rarely more than three hours at a stretch. When she does sleep, she
often wakes with her heart pounding, certain she can smell something chemical and acrid, though she
knows the windows are sealed. She keeps the windows sealed even in summer. Her neighbors find this
eccentric. She finds their open windows alarming. On days when the wind carries anything — woodsmoke
from a neighbor's fire, exhaust from a passing truck, the sulfurous smell of a struck match — her
body responds before her mind can intervene. Her chest tightens, her hands begin to shake, and she
finds herself calculating exits, counting the steps to her car.

Her painting has suffered in ways she finds difficult to articulate without grief. She used to paint
the landscape outside her studio windows — the treeline, the changing light on the fields, the
particular grey of an overcast sky. She cannot paint those things anymore. The grey sky that once
felt meditative now looks, to her, exactly like it did that afternoon. She has tried painting other
subjects and finds herself unable to concentrate for more than twenty minutes before a restlessness
overtakes her and she has to leave the studio. Several canvases sit unfinished against the wall. She
covers them with drop cloths so she does not have to see them when she passes.

She describes herself as a person who used to feel at home in solitude and now feels imprisoned by
it. The isolation she once chose now feels imposed. She avoids the news, particularly anything
involving industrial accidents or environmental disasters, and has stopped watching television
almost entirely. She keeps a go-bag packed by the front door — a habit she developed in the weeks
after the explosion that she has never been able to give up, even though she understands, on some
level, that the immediate danger has passed.

What troubles her most, she says, is not the fear itself but the loss of the self that existed
before it. She was, for fifty years, a woman who found the world beautiful enough to spend her life
trying to capture it. She is not certain that woman still exists. She sits in her kitchen most
mornings with her coffee, facing away from the window, waiting for something she cannot name.
