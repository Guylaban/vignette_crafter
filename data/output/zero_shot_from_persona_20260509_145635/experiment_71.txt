# The Weight of Iron

Roberto is a 70-year-old Chilean-born man who spent the better part of his working life shaping
metal with his hands. He came to welding in his twenties, drawn to the precision of it — the way a
steady hand and a controlled flame could fuse two separate things into something permanent and
strong. He still thinks about that sometimes, the idea of things being made whole.

What happened to Roberto happened in 1974, when he was twenty-four years old. He was a union
organizer at a manufacturing plant in Santiago, and in the months following the coup, that was
enough. Soldiers came to his apartment before dawn. He was held for eleven days in a detention
facility whose name he still cannot say aloud. During those eleven days he was beaten, subjected to
electrical shocks, deprived of sleep, and made to listen to others being tortured in adjacent rooms.
He was released without explanation. He never learned why he was taken, or why he was let go.

He fled Chile within the year, eventually settling in Houston, where a cousin helped him find work.
He rebuilt his life methodically — learned English, earned his welding certifications, married a
woman named Esperanza, raised two daughters. For decades he managed by moving forward. He worked
long hours. He did not speak about what had happened to him, not to Esperanza, not to anyone. He
told himself that silence was a form of control, and for a long time it felt like one.

Esperanza died four years ago. Her death did not cause his symptoms, but it removed the last
structure that had been quietly organizing his days and keeping the past at a manageable distance.
Since then, Roberto has been living alone in the same house in Houston, now retired, with long
unscheduled hours that he does not know what to do with.

He does not sleep well. Most nights he wakes between two and four in the morning with his heart
pounding, the sheets damp, the specific sensation of restraint still present in his wrists and chest
even as he sits up in the dark of his own bedroom. The dreams are not always narrative — sometimes
they are only darkness and sound, the sounds from the other rooms. He has learned not to try to go
back to sleep after these episodes. He sits in his kitchen and drinks water and waits for morning.

During the day he avoids the news, particularly anything involving political violence or military
imagery. He cannot watch certain films. He is startled badly by unexpected loud noises — a car
backfiring, his neighbor's door slamming — and the startle response is followed by a shame he finds
difficult to articulate, a sense that his body is betraying him. He describes feeling emotionally
flat much of the time, as though he is watching his own life from a slight remove. He has little
interest in the things that once engaged him. His daughters call regularly, and he is glad to hear
their voices, but he finds it hard to be present in the conversations.

He still goes to his former colleague Marcos's garage on Tuesday afternoons, where they work on an
old truck together. Roberto is precise and patient with the metal, the same as he always was. Marcos
does most of the talking. Roberto finds the work grounding in a way he cannot fully explain —
something about the heat, the physical demand of it, the fact that the result is visible and real.
But even there, certain moments catch him without warning. The smell of burning metal sometimes
stops him cold. He sets down his torch and stands very still until it passes.

He has never told his daughters what was done to him. He is not certain he has the language for it,
or that the language exists. He describes himself as a private man, and says this as though it is a
choice he has made freely, though some part of him understands that it is also something that was
made for him, in a room he has spent fifty years trying to leave.
