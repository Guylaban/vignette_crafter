# Marcus

Marcus is a 52-year-old film director who has spent the better part of three decades constructing
worlds for other people to inhabit — controlling light, sound, movement, and meaning with practiced
authority. He is the kind of man who has always felt most comfortable behind the camera, where
everything that enters the frame does so because he has decided it should. That sense of control is
something he no longer entirely trusts.

Fourteen months ago, Marcus was driving home from a location scout in the hills outside the city
when a truck drifted across the centre line and struck his car head-on. The impact was severe enough
to require the fire service to cut him free. He was conscious throughout — a detail he returns to
again and again. He remembers the smell of antifreeze and something burning, the strange quality of
the afternoon light through a shattered windscreen, and the particular silence that followed the
collision before the pain arrived. He walked away with a fractured clavicle, three broken ribs, and
a laceration along his left forearm that required forty-two stitches. The truck driver was
uninjured. Marcus found this fact difficult to process for reasons he cannot fully articulate.

The physical recovery took several months. What has not recovered, or has not recovered in the same
way, is harder to name. In the weeks following the accident, Marcus began waking between three and
four in the morning with his heart already racing, certain — for a few disorienting seconds — that
the impact was happening again. He would lie in the dark beside his partner, Elena, unable to
explain why he could not simply go back to sleep. He has not told her the full extent of what those
nights feel like.

Driving remains a significant problem. Marcus returned to the wheel after about six weeks because
his work demanded it, but he describes highway driving as something he now endures rather than
performs. He sits rigidly, watches the mirrors compulsively, and finds himself rehearsing escape
routes when trucks appear in adjacent lanes. On two occasions he has pulled off the motorway without
being able to say exactly why, sitting in a lay-by until the tightness in his chest subsided. He has
begun delegating more location scouts to his assistant director, framing it as a shift in his
working style rather than an avoidance.

On set, colleagues have noticed that Marcus is less patient than he used to be. He is aware of this
himself and is quietly ashamed of it. He describes a persistent low-level irritability that he
cannot always explain or control, a sense of being faintly braced against something that has not yet
arrived. Loud, sudden sounds — a clapper board snapping, a piece of equipment falling — produce a
startle response that he finds embarrassing in a professional environment where he is expected to
project calm authority. He has become more reliant on meticulous pre-production planning, he says,
though he acknowledges this may be less about craft and more about managing his own anxiety.

There are stretches of days, sometimes longer, when things feel almost normal. He can lose himself
in the editing suite, in the close and absorbing work of assembling footage, and for hours at a time
the accident recedes. He values this. He describes editing as the one place where he still feels
like himself — where the world is manageable, sequential, and responsive to his decisions.

But the accident has changed something in his relationship to his own imagination. He is currently
in pre-production on a new film, a road thriller, and he has found himself unable to watch the crash
sequences his second unit has been filming. He sits outside the screening room while the footage
runs, telling himself he will go in shortly. He has not gone in yet. He has not told his producer.

Marcus presents as composed, articulate, and somewhat reluctant to assign weight to his own
experience. He mentions, almost in passing, that he has always believed people who make films about
suffering ought to be able to tolerate a certain amount of it themselves.
