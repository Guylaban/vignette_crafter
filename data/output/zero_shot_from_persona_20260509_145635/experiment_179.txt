# The Road That Stays With Her

Aroha is thirty-one years old, a lecturer in Pacific Studies at a mid-sized university, and by most
outward measures, she is thriving. She is married to a man she describes as patient and kind. She
publishes. She teaches. She shows up. But showing up, she will tell you quietly, is the hardest
thing she does every single day.

Eighteen months ago, Aroha was driving home from a late faculty meeting on a wet winter evening when
a truck ran a red light and struck her car on the driver's side at full speed. The car spun, rolled,
and came to rest against a concrete median barrier. She remembers the sound — a compressed, metallic
shriek she says had no beginning and no end — and then she remembers nothing until she was looking
up at a paramedic's face and could not understand why she couldn't move her left arm. She spent four
days in hospital with a fractured clavicle, three broken ribs, and a laceration across her cheek
that required seventeen stitches. The truck driver walked away uninjured. She has never been able to
make peace with that detail.

The physical injuries healed on schedule. What did not heal was harder to name at first. In the
weeks after she came home, Aroha found herself lying awake long after her husband had fallen asleep,
replaying the moment of impact with a precision that felt involuntary and almost mechanical, as
though her mind were a machine stuck in a single loop it could not exit. She would feel the jolt in
her body while lying completely still in a dark room. She began sleeping with the light on, then
with the television on, then barely sleeping at all.

She returned to teaching that semester because she believed she had to, because her identity is
threaded through her work, because her students needed her. But standing at the front of a lecture
hall, she would sometimes lose the thread of a sentence entirely — not from distraction but from a
sudden, sourceless terror that something catastrophic was about to happen. She learned to pause and
breathe and smile through it, and her students noticed nothing. Her husband notices everything.

Driving is now functionally impossible for her. She takes rideshares to campus, and even then she
sits in the back seat with her hand braced against the door, watching every intersection approach
with a certainty that it will be the last one. Rain makes it worse. The sound of brakes — any
brakes, anywhere — sends a cold spike of adrenaline through her chest that takes long minutes to
subside. She has restructured her entire schedule around avoiding situations where she might be a
passenger in a car during peak traffic hours.

At home, she is short-tempered in ways that shame her. She startles violently at ordinary sounds — a
door closing, her husband dropping his keys — and then feels a wave of humiliation at her own
reaction. She has stopped attending the community gatherings she once loved, finding crowds loud and
unpredictable in ways that exhaust her. She tells her husband she is tired, which is true, but it is
not the whole truth. The whole truth is that she feels separated from her own life by a membrane she
cannot name or dissolve, watching herself move through her days as though she is not quite the
person living them.

She speaks about the accident with a flat, careful precision that sounds like control but is
actually the opposite — if she allows any emotion into the telling, she says, she is afraid she will
not be able to stop. She has not cried about it once. She dreams about it several times a week. She
wakes with her heart already running.

What Aroha wants, more than anything, is to drive herself home in the rain and feel nothing more
than the ordinary irritation of traffic. It is a small wish. It feels enormous.
