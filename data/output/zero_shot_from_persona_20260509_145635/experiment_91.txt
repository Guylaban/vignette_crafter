# The Weight of Gravity

Marcus is a 56-year-old combat pilot with over three decades of military aviation behind him — a
career built on precision, composure, and the ability to make split-second decisions at thirty
thousand feet. He has flown through hostile airspace, navigated mechanical failures mid-mission, and
brought damaged aircraft home more times than he cares to count. Danger, in his world, has always
been something he managed. Something he controlled. Until the day it managed him.

Four years ago, during a routine training exercise, Marcus experienced a catastrophic hydraulic
failure that left him with almost no control of his aircraft. For eleven minutes — a duration he can
recite to the second — he fought to keep the jet from entering a terminal spin. He ejected at the
last possible moment, sustaining a fractured vertebra, a shattered right wrist, and a severe
concussion when he struck the ground. He watched the aircraft explode from the hillside where he
landed. He has not spoken about those eleven minutes to anyone, including his wife of twenty-eight
years, Elena.

In the weeks after the accident, Marcus threw himself into rehabilitation with the same discipline
that had defined his career. He told himself — and anyone who asked — that he had been through
worse. He returned to limited flight duties within six months, which felt, at the time, like proof
that he was fine. But something had quietly broken loose inside him, and it has been drifting ever
since.

He now sleeps in fragments. Most nights, he wakes between two and four in the morning, heart
hammering, sheets soaked, certain he is still in the cockpit and that the ground is rising toward
him. He lies rigid in the dark, listening to Elena breathe, unwilling to wake her and unable to
explain what is happening even if he did. During the day, he moves through the world with a
hypervigilance that exhausts him — scanning rooms when he enters them, sitting with his back to
walls, flinching at sounds that no one else seems to notice. A car backfiring in the base parking
lot last spring sent him flat against a concrete wall, hands shaking, before he could register what
he was doing.

In the cockpit, the man who once flew by instinct now second-guesses every instrument reading. He
runs checklists he has memorized for thirty years as though seeing them for the first time,
terrified that he has missed something, that something is about to fail. His commanding officer has
noticed the hesitation. Marcus knows he has noticed. This knowledge sits in his chest like a stone.

At home, he has become someone Elena describes, quietly and carefully, as "far away." He sits at the
dinner table and cannot follow the conversation. He cancels plans with friends, citing fatigue he
cannot fully explain. He has stopped flying the small civilian Cessna he kept at a regional airfield
for pleasure — a ritual he once described as the only place he felt completely himself. He drove to
the airfield twice in the past year and both times sat in the car for an hour before turning around
and going home.

Marcus does not think of himself as someone who suffers. That is not the vocabulary his life has
given him. What he thinks, in the quieter moments, is that he used to understand the sky — its
physics, its risks, its rules — and that understanding was the foundation of everything he knew
about himself. Now, when he looks up, something in him braces. The sky looks the same as it always
did. He is the thing that has changed, and he cannot find the instrument panel that tells him how
far off course he has drifted, or whether there is still enough altitude to recover.
