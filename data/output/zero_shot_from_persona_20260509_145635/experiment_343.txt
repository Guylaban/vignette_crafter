# Vignette: Amara, Age 38

Amara has spent most of her adult life on the water. She grew up watching her father mend nets
before dawn, and by the time she was a teenager, she had learned to read the currents of Lake
Victoria the way other women read the faces of their children. Fishing is not simply what she does —
it is the language through which she understands herself and her place in the world. She and her
husband, Kofi, built their life around the rhythms of the lake, waking before light, returning in
the late afternoon with their catch, raising their three children in a small home close enough to
the shore that they could always hear the water.

Two years ago, Amara's eldest son, fourteen-year-old Daniel, came out to help her haul in a net that
had snagged on something beneath the surface. It was an ordinary afternoon. The sky was clear. She
remembers that detail with a particular cruelty — how ordinary everything looked. Daniel leaned too
far over the side of the boat, lost his balance, and went into the water. Amara could swim, but the
current that day was treacherous and fast, and by the time she reached him, Daniel had been under
for too long. He was pulled to shore and survived, but he sustained a severe hypoxic brain injury.
He now requires full-time care and can no longer speak or walk independently.

In the weeks after the accident, Amara moved through her days as though she were underwater herself.
She did not cry at first. She went back to fishing because the family needed income and because she
did not know what else to do with her body. But the lake, which had once felt like an extension of
herself, became something she could barely look at. She began waking at three in the morning, heart
slamming, convinced she could hear Daniel calling her name from somewhere beneath the surface. She
would lie rigid beside Kofi, afraid to move, replaying the moment he went over the side — the sound
of the splash, the color of his shirt, the way the current took him before she could fully
understand what was happening.

She still goes out on the water every morning, but she is not the same woman who once moved through
her work with easy confidence. She scans the surface constantly, convinced that something terrible
is about to happen again. She snaps at the younger fishermen who work alongside her, and then feels
a hollow shame about it afterward. She avoids the section of the lake where the accident happened,
adding an extra forty minutes to her route each day, and she has never told anyone why.

At home, she cares for Daniel with a devotion that frightens her in its intensity. She cannot leave
him with anyone else, even her mother, without spending the entire time gripped by a certainty that
something will go wrong in her absence. She watches him sleep. She checks his breathing. Kofi has
tried to hold her at night, but she often pulls away, not out of anger toward him, but because she
feels encased in something he cannot reach through. She describes feeling like a person standing
behind glass, watching her own life from a distance.

She has stopped attending church, where she used to sing in the choir. She does not see friends.
When neighbors ask how Daniel is doing, she gives brief answers and changes the subject, because any
extended conversation about him threatens to pull her into a grief so large she is certain it would
swallow her entirely. She carries a guilt that she has never spoken aloud — that she asked him to
come out on the boat that day, that she was the one who needed help with the net, that her hands
were the last thing that could have caught him and did not. She holds this belief the way she holds
the ropes of her nets: tightly, without examining whether they are still sound.
