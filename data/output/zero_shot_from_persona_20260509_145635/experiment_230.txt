# Clinical Vignette: Tariq

Tariq is a 54-year-old software engineer of Lebanese descent who has spent the better part of three
decades building a quiet, orderly life around logic and precision. He lives alone in a modest
apartment, his days structured around code repositories, debugging cycles, and the comforting
predictability of machines that do exactly what they are told. He has never married. Until recently,
he would have described his solitude as a choice.

Two years ago, Tariq was sexually assaulted by a colleague at a work conference — a man he had
considered a trusted friend of nearly eight years. They had gone out for drinks after a long day of
presentations. Tariq remembers the hotel corridor, the way his key card kept failing, and then very
little after that with any coherence. What he does remember arrives in fragments: the weight of
another person, the ceiling of a room that wasn't his, the feeling of being unable to move or speak,
as though his body had simply stopped belonging to him. He drove home the following morning without
telling anyone. He has not told anyone since.

In the weeks that followed, Tariq convinced himself that if he worked harder, the memories would
have less room to exist. He began logging twelve- and fourteen-hour days, sometimes sleeping at his
desk. His team noticed he had become withdrawn and irritable, snapping at junior developers over
minor errors — behavior entirely unlike the patient mentor he had always been. He stopped attending
team lunches. He stopped answering his phone.

The nightmares began within the first month. He wakes most nights between two and four in the
morning, heart hammering, sheets soaked through, the smell of the hotel bar somehow still present in
the air of his own bedroom. He has taken to sleeping with every light in the apartment on. He tells
himself this is because he reads late. He knows this is not why.

Tariq has not returned to a work conference since the assault. He declined two invitations before
his manager stopped asking. When a younger colleague mentions the colleague who assaulted him —
still employed at the same company, still well-regarded — Tariq feels his chest close like a fist.
He has rehearsed what he might say in those moments, but the words never come. He simply nods and
looks back at his screen.

He carries a particular shame that is shaped, in part, by how he was raised. In his family, in the
culture he grew up in, there was no language for what happened to him — certainly not for a man his
age. He finds himself cycling through a familiar and exhausting loop: the belief that he should have
been stronger, that he misread the situation, that what happened was somehow a consequence of a
weakness he has always suspected in himself. He has never once considered that he was wronged.

His concentration, once his greatest professional asset, has become unreliable. He loses threads
mid-task, forgets variable names he has used a hundred times, stares at functions he wrote himself
as though reading a foreign language. He has started keeping detailed handwritten notes of things he
is afraid he will forget, filling notebook after notebook in his careful, cramped script.

He does not drink alcohol anymore. He does not go to restaurants where the lighting is low. He takes
the stairs rather than ride in an elevator with a man he does not know. These are not rules he
consciously made — they are simply what his body insists upon now.

Tariq came to therapy after his physician, during a routine visit, asked him gently whether anything
had happened. He sat in the examination room for a long time before he answered. He is not yet able
to say the word *assault* aloud in session. He refers to the night as *the situation at the
conference*, and he speaks about it the way he might describe a bug in someone else's code —
carefully, at a distance, as though the problem belongs to a system he is only just beginning to
understand.
