# The Weight of the Overpass

Henrik is a 68-year-old man who has spent the better part of four decades in public life. He has
navigated election campaigns, budget crises, and the grinding machinery of coalition politics with a
reputation for composure under pressure. His colleagues know him as unflappable. That reputation
feels, to Henrik, like something that belongs to another man now.

Fourteen months ago, Henrik was traveling home from a regional conference on a wet November evening.
His driver took a familiar route beneath a highway overpass just as a structural section gave way.
The collapse was sudden and total — a concussive roar, the windshield dissolving into white, the car
folding around them. Henrik was conscious throughout. He remembers the weight of the concrete on his
legs, the sound of his driver making a noise that wasn't quite a voice anymore, and the particular
quality of silence that followed. Emergency crews reached them after forty minutes. His driver
survived with permanent injuries. Henrik walked away with a fractured collarbone, three broken ribs,
and something he cannot name that has not healed at all.

In the weeks after the accident, Henrik told himself and his wife, Marta, that he was fine. He
returned to his parliamentary duties within a month, which felt like the right thing to do — the
only thing to do. But fine was not what he was. He stopped sleeping in any meaningful sense. He
would lie beside Marta and watch the ceiling, listening to the house settle, his heart already
moving too fast. When sleep did come, it delivered him back under the overpass. He wakes from these
dreams certain that the ceiling is coming down, and it takes him long minutes to understand where he
is.

During the day, Henrik functions — he chairs committee meetings, gives interviews, moves through the
choreography of his public role — but he does so from behind what he describes as thick glass. He
watches himself speak and cannot feel the words. Constituents shake his hand and he registers the
gesture without receiving it. His chief of staff has noticed that he flinches at unexpected sounds:
a door slamming in a corridor, a microphone dropping at a press conference. Henrik is aware that
people notice, and the awareness shames him in a way he finds difficult to sit with.

He has stopped taking the car on the motorway. He tells Marta it is a preference, a small
adjustment. In truth, approaching any underpass or bridge now produces a physical response he cannot
override — his chest tightens, his vision narrows, and the November rain is always suddenly present
in his memory with a precision that feels less like remembering and more like returning. He has
rerouted his commute by twenty-five minutes to avoid a particular stretch of road. He has not told
anyone this.

What troubles Henrik most, and what he speaks about with a careful, measured quality that suggests
he has been holding it for some time, is the change in how he understands the future. He has spent
his career making arguments about the long term — infrastructure investment, pension reform, climate
policy. He believed in those arguments. Now when he tries to think ahead, even a few years, he
encounters a blankness that frightens him. He cannot picture himself at seventy, at seventy-five. He
cannot picture the grandchildren his daughter speaks of wanting. The future, which was always the
substance of his work, has become inaccessible to him.

Marta knows something is wrong. She is a patient woman and she does not press him, which Henrik says
he is grateful for and which he also, quietly, resents — because her patience means he can continue
not speaking. He came to his first session at her insistence, describing it to colleagues as a
routine check-in. He sat down, and before any question was asked, he said: *I keep hearing that
sound. I don't know how to make it stop.*
