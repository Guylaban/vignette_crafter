# The Weight of Walls

Tariq is a 41-year-old Lebanese-American construction worker who has spent the better part of two
decades building things — framing houses, pouring foundations, raising structures that are meant to
stand. There is something deliberate in that choice of work, though he has never quite put it into
words. He grew up in a home where things fell apart.

He was seven years old the first time he saw his father hit his mother. He remembers the sound more
than anything — the particular crack of a palm against a cheekbone, the way his mother's body folded
against the kitchen counter. He stood in the hallway in his socks, frozen, holding a school
permission slip he had wanted her to sign. He never handed it to her. He went back to his room, slid
under his bed, and pressed his face into the carpet until the shouting stopped. This became a ritual
that lasted eleven years. The hiding. The waiting. The listening for silence to know whether the
silence was safe.

His father was not a monster in the way people imagine monsters. He worked, he prayed, he brought
home groceries and sometimes laughed loudly at the dinner table. The violence arrived without
announcement, and that unpredictability was its own kind of education. Tariq learned to read rooms
before he entered them — the set of his father's jaw, the particular stillness that preceded an
explosion. He became fluent in threat before he understood what threat meant.

He left home at eighteen, came to the United States with a cousin, and threw himself into physical
labor with the kind of dedication that leaves little room for memory. For years, it worked well
enough. He was good at his job, respected on job sites, known for showing up early and staying late.
He didn't drink much, didn't cause trouble. He told himself he had left all of it behind in Beirut.

But the body keeps its own records. In his mid-thirties, the dreams started — vivid, suffocating
replays in which he is always small and always watching. He wakes most mornings before four, heart
hammering, the sheets damp, and he cannot return to sleep. He sits at his kitchen table in the dark
and drinks coffee until it is time to go to work, because lying in bed feels unbearable, like being
trapped.

On job sites, certain sounds reach inside him without warning. A foreman raising his voice at a
subcontractor. The sudden clatter of scaffolding. A door slamming in a neighboring unit. His body
responds before his mind can intervene — a flooding of adrenaline, a tightening in his chest that
takes long minutes to subside. His coworkers notice that he startles easily and have learned not to
approach him from behind. He laughs it off. He is skilled at laughing things off.

He has not been in a serious relationship. He has tried — there have been women he cared about — but
he finds himself hypervigilant in domestic spaces in ways he cannot explain to a partner. The sound
of raised voices in an apartment hallway can ruin an evening entirely. He becomes withdrawn, then
apologetic, then distant until the relationship quietly dissolves. He tells himself he prefers
solitude. He is not entirely sure he believes it.

What Tariq carries most heavily is a specific and corrosive shame — not for what was done to him,
but for what he did not do. He was a child, and he hid. He knows, intellectually, that there was
nothing else to do. But the seven-year-old in him has not accepted this, and at forty-one, Tariq
still sometimes lies awake rehearsing the moment in the hallway, the permission slip in his hand,
wondering what would have happened if he had simply walked into the kitchen and stood between them.
He builds walls for a living. He has been building them inside himself for much longer.
