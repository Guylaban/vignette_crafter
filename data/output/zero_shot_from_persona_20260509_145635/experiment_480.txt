**Vignette: Rajan, Age 34**

Rajan has been driving delivery routes for the same logistics company for six years. He knows the
streets of his city the way most people know their own neighborhood — intuitively, almost without
thinking. That familiarity is part of what makes what happened eighteen months ago so difficult for
him to reconcile. He was doing everything right. He has told himself this hundreds of times, and he
still cannot make it feel true.

It was a Tuesday morning in late autumn, a route he had driven dozens of times. A twelve-year-old
boy ran from between two parked cars directly into the path of Rajan's van. Rajan braked hard, but
not hard enough. The impact was slight by mechanical standards — the boy was struck at low speed —
but the child suffered a fractured pelvis and spent three weeks in hospital. He survived. He
recovered. Everyone has told Rajan this, as if the outcome should close the matter. For Rajan, it
has not closed anything.

In the immediate aftermath, he sat on the pavement beside the van while someone else called for an
ambulance. He remembers the sound of the boy crying and the particular gray of the sky that morning,
and he remembers his own hands, which would not stop shaking. The police investigation found no
fault on his part. His employer kept him on. His wife, Priya, held him that night and told him it
was not his fault. He nodded and said he knew, and he did not sleep.

In the months that followed, Rajan returned to work because the family needed the income and because
he did not know what else to do with himself. Every shift is an act of endurance. He grips the
steering wheel with both hands even on empty roads. When a pedestrian steps off a curb ahead of him,
his foot moves to the brake before his mind has consciously registered the movement. He scans
constantly — children especially, children near parked cars, children on bikes — and the vigilance
exhausts him in a way that sleep does not repair. Several times he has had to pull over and sit for
a few minutes, breathing, before he could continue.

At night the images return without invitation. He sees the boy's body against the front of the van.
He sees his own hands on the wheel. Sometimes he wakes at two or three in the morning with his heart
already racing, the dream dissolving but the feeling staying with him for an hour or more. Priya has
begun sleeping lightly because of his restlessness, and Rajan carries guilt about this too — the
sense that he is spreading damage outward, that the accident is still injuring people through him.

He has become quieter in general. His younger brother has commented on it. At family gatherings he
sits at the edge of conversations, present but not quite reachable. He used to enjoy cooking on
weekends, something he and Priya did together; he has largely stopped initiating it. Small pleasures
have gone flat in a way he struggles to articulate. He does not think of himself as depressed — he
goes to work, he functions, he loves his wife — but he recognizes that something in him is running
on a reduced register.

What troubles Rajan most, in the quiet moments he allows himself to examine it, is the question of
what kind of person he is now. He was raised in a household where responsibility was taken
seriously, where causing harm — even accidentally — carried moral weight. He cannot fully accept the
verdict of the investigation or the reassurances of the people who love him. The boy healed. Rajan
is still, in some interior way, sitting on that pavement in the gray autumn light, waiting for
something he cannot name.
