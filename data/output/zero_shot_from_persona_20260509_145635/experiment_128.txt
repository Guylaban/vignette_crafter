# Farida

Farida is an eighty-year-old Palestinian woman who spent the better part of five decades working as
a social worker in community health clinics, first in Amman and later, after her husband's death, in
a mid-sized American city where two of her adult children had settled. She is small and deliberate
in her movements, and she speaks about her professional life with the quiet authority of someone who
has sat across from a great deal of suffering and learned how to hold it. What she has not learned,
she admits, is how to hold her own.

Thirty-one years ago, when Farida was forty-nine, her youngest son Tariq was diagnosed with
bacterial meningitis. He was seven years old. The illness came on within hours — a fever in the
morning, a stiffened neck by afternoon, and by nightfall he was unconscious and surrounded by
machines in a pediatric intensive care unit. The doctors told Farida and her husband that the next
forty-eight hours would determine everything. She remembers standing in the corridor outside his
room and feeling the floor tilt beneath her, as though the building itself had become unreliable.
She did not sleep for three days. She held his hand through a gap in the bed rail and recited Quran
verses under her breath until the words stopped having meaning and became only sound.

Tariq survived. He recovered fully, with no lasting neurological damage — an outcome the medical
team called remarkable. His parents were told to feel grateful, and they were. The crisis passed,
the family exhaled, and life resumed. Farida went back to work. She told herself that what she had
felt in that hospital corridor was simply what any mother would feel, and that it was over.

But it was not over. In the months after Tariq's discharge, she began waking between two and four in
the morning with her heart slamming in her chest, certain that something was wrong with him. She
would get up and check on him, standing in the doorway of his room watching his small chest rise and
fall until her own breathing steadied. She became hypervigilant about illness in a way that her
husband gently called excessive. A cough, a warm forehead, a child who seemed quieter than usual —
any of these could send her into a state of controlled panic that she hid carefully behind her
professional composure.

Now, at eighty, Farida is retired, and Tariq is a healthy thirty-eight-year-old man with children of
his own. Yet the vigilance has not quieted with time. When her grandchildren are ill, she
experiences a flooding return of the hospital corridor — the smell of antiseptic, the sound of
monitors, the particular quality of fluorescent light at three in the morning. These intrusions
arrive without warning and leave her feeling hollowed out for hours. She finds herself avoiding
conversations about childhood illness and changes the subject when medical dramas appear on
television. She has never told Tariq what those forty-eight hours were like for her, because she
believes that her fear belongs to her alone and that naming it would somehow burden him with
something he didn't ask to carry.

What brings her to therapy now is her granddaughter's recent hospitalization for a respiratory
infection — a minor admission, resolved in two days. For Farida, it reopened something she had
assumed was simply part of her character. She sat in the waiting room of the children's hospital and
felt thirty-one years collapse into a single moment. She could not eat for a week afterward. She has
been sleeping poorly since, and she finds herself moving through her days with a sense of dread she
cannot locate or name, only feel.

She describes herself as someone who has spent her entire career helping other people find language
for their pain. She says, with a small and careful smile, that she finds it harder than she expected
to do it for herself.
