# The Road He Carries

Kwame is twenty-seven years old, and he spends most of his waking hours behind the wheel of his car,
ferrying strangers from one part of the city to another. He chose this work deliberately. There is
something about the arrangement — him in front, passengers behind, everyone facing forward — that
feels manageable. He is in control of where the car goes. Nobody is standing over him.

He did not always feel in control of anything.

Kwame grew up in a household shaped by his uncle's fists. After his parents sent him from Ghana to
live with relatives in Europe at age seven, hoping for a better life, he found himself instead in
the custody of a man who believed that discipline was something you delivered with your hands, your
belt, or whatever was nearest. The beatings were not occasional. They were routine — for coming home
late, for a poor grade, for looking the wrong way, for reasons that shifted so unpredictably that
Kwame eventually stopped trying to understand them. What he learned instead was to read the
atmosphere of a room the moment he entered it: the set of his uncle's jaw, the particular silence
that preceded danger. He became very good at this. He became, without knowing the word for it,
hypervigilant.

He left that house at seventeen and has not returned. He tells himself he is fine. He has a car, an
income, his freedom. He is nobody's child anymore.

But the body remembers what the mind tries to dismiss.

On a night last winter, a passenger became aggressive in the backseat — drunk, shouting, suddenly
grabbing Kwame's headrest and shaking it. The encounter lasted less than two minutes before Kwame
pulled over and ordered the man out. Nothing serious happened. And yet something cracked open inside
him that night that has not closed since.

Now he sleeps badly, waking at two or three in the morning with his heart hammering, the sheets
damp, a formless dread sitting on his chest. The dreams are not always clear, but they leave behind
a residue — a feeling of being small, of being cornered, of there being no door to walk through. He
lies in the dark and runs through his memories like a man checking his pockets for something he
knows he has lost.

During the day he is easily startled. A car backfiring, a dispatcher's voice crackling too loudly
through his phone — his body responds before his mind can intervene, flooding with adrenaline, hands
tightening on the wheel. He has declined rides from passengers who remind him, in posture or manner,
of his uncle. He knows this is irrational. He does it anyway.

He finds himself irritable in ways that embarrass him. Small frustrations — traffic, a slow app, a
wrong address — can produce a flash of rage that frightens him with its intensity. He does not act
on it, but he feels it move through him like something animal, and afterward he is ashamed. He grew
up swearing he would never become the man who hurt him. The anger feels like evidence against that
promise.

He has told none of this to anyone. His friends know him as easygoing, reliable, the one who shows
up. He maintains this carefully. Vulnerability, in his experience, has never been safe to display.
He learned that lesson young, in a house where showing fear only invited more of it.

What Kwame notices most, on the long night shifts especially, is a pervasive flatness — a sense of
moving through his life without quite inhabiting it. He watches the city lights slide past his
windshield and feels strangely absent from the scene, as though he is observing himself from a
distance. He is twenty-seven years old. He thinks, sometimes, that he has been tired for a very long
time. He cannot remember exactly when the tiredness started, only that it started early, and that he
has been driving away from it ever since.
