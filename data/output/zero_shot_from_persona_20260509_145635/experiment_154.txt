# Vignette: Meena

Meena is a 79-year-old South Asian woman who has spent the better part of five decades in the
cockpit. She flew commercial routes across three continents, retiring only four years ago after a
career that earned her a quiet, hard-won respect in an industry that rarely made room for women who
looked like her. She is married, sharp-minded, and carries herself with the kind of measured
composure that decades of command responsibility tend to produce. And yet, lately, that composure
has begun to cost her something.

The story begins in a small flat in Lahore, where Meena grew up the eldest of four children. Her
father was a man of volatile moods and heavy hands. What she witnessed most nights was not simply
argument — it was her mother pressed against a kitchen wall, her father's voice filling every corner
of the apartment, followed by sounds she learned to identify before she could name what they meant.
Meena would gather her younger siblings into the back bedroom, tell them stories in a whisper, and
listen. She became very good at listening — at reading the atmosphere of a room, at knowing when
something was about to go wrong. She did not understand at the time that she was developing a kind
of hypervigilance that would follow her out of that flat and into the rest of her life.

In many ways, the cockpit suited her perfectly. The checklists, the protocols, the absolute
requirement that she remain calm and present regardless of what was happening — these were familiar
disciplines. She excelled. She found that the altitude helped, literally and otherwise. Up there,
nothing from the flat in Lahore could reach her. She married a gentle man, a structural engineer
named Rajan, and built a life that looked, from the outside, like evidence that the past had been
left behind.

Retirement changed everything. Without the structure of flight schedules and crew briefings, without
the forward momentum that had organized her inner life for so long, the past found its way back in.
It began with sounds. A door slammed in a neighboring house and Meena found herself standing rigid
in her kitchen, heart hammering, unable to explain to Rajan why she had gone pale. She started
sleeping poorly, waking in the early hours with a physical sense of dread that had no clear object.
Some mornings she woke already exhausted, as though she had been working through the night.

She does not talk about her childhood readily. When she does, she describes it in the careful,
slightly detached language of someone who has spent decades organizing experience into manageable
compartments. But the feelings leak through in other ways. She finds herself scanning rooms when she
enters them. She sits with her back to walls in restaurants — a habit Rajan has noticed and gently
teased her about for years, though neither of them has ever named the reason. She avoids certain
television programs, certain tones of voice, certain combinations of sound and stress that she
cannot always predict in advance.

What troubles her most, she says, is the intrusive quality of what she experiences — not quite
memories, more like sudden relocations. She will be washing dishes and find herself briefly,
viscerally back in that bedroom with her siblings, listening. The feeling is not nostalgic. It is
present-tense and physical and leaves her shaken in a way that embarrasses her. A woman who has
landed aircraft in crosswinds and navigated instrument failures at altitude should not, she feels,
be undone by the sound of raised voices through a wall.

She has not spoken about any of this to her children. She describes her mother, now long dead, with
enormous tenderness and some guilt — a guilt she cannot quite articulate but that seems connected to
the question of what she could have done, what a child could ever have done, and whether leaving for
the sky was a kind of abandonment.

She sits across the room with her hands folded in her lap, composed and watchful, choosing her words
with the precision of someone who has always understood that the instruments must be read correctly.
