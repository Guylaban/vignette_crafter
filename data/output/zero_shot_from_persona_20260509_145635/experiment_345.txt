# Clinical Vignette: Marama, Age 43

Marama grew up in Samoa, the eldest of six children, raised in a household where competence and
caregiving were not simply valued — they were expected. She carried that inheritance with her when
she emigrated to New Zealand in her twenties, earned her physiotherapy degree with distinction, and
built a reputation over fifteen years as someone patients trusted completely. She was known for her
precision, her warmth, and an almost instinctive ability to read the body's signals before a patient
could articulate their pain. That identity — healer, steady hand, safe presence — was the
architecture of her entire adult life.

Fourteen months ago, that architecture collapsed in the space of about four seconds.

Marama was conducting a manual spinal mobilisation on a 58-year-old man recovering from cervical
surgery. She had performed the technique hundreds of times. The assessment had been thorough; the
consent was clear. Something went wrong in a way she still cannot fully explain — a subtle
miscalculation of force, a movement he made that she didn't anticipate, a confluence of factors the
subsequent review would describe as a "rare adverse event." The man cried out, then went quiet in a
way that frightened her immediately. He was taken by ambulance. He sustained a spinal cord injury
that left him with permanent weakness in his left arm and hand.

He will never play guitar again. Marama knows this because she read it in his victim impact
statement.

In the weeks after the incident, she returned to work on the advice of her supervisor, believing
that routine would restore her. Instead, she found herself freezing at the treatment table, her
hands hovering over patients' shoulders or necks as though they had become foreign objects she no
longer trusted. She began avoiding any technique that involved the cervical spine, fabricating
clinical justifications to her colleagues. At home, she replays the moment obsessively — the sound
he made, the particular weight of the silence afterward — and she cannot make the memory stop moving
through her. It arrives at night most reliably, jolting her awake at two or three in the morning
with her heart hammering and her nightdress soaked through.

Her partner, Daniel, a gentle and patient man, reaches for her during these nights. She lets him
hold her but feels unreachable, as though she is watching the two of them from somewhere slightly
outside herself. She has stopped telling him the details of what she sees when she closes her eyes,
partly to protect him and partly because she has begun to believe that what she did places her
outside the ordinary consolations available to people who have not caused serious harm.

That belief — that she is fundamentally different now, contaminated by what happened — organises
much of her inner life. She no longer attends her church community, where she was once a regular and
beloved presence. She declined an invitation to her nephew's birthday. She tells herself she is
tired, but the truth she cannot quite say aloud is that she does not feel she deserves the ordinary
texture of belonging.

At work, she functions. Colleagues describe her as quieter than before, more careful — some have
said admiringly so. They do not see that the carefulness is terror wearing a professional face. She
checks and rechecks patient notes. She asks patients to repeat their histories twice, sometimes
three times. After sessions, she sits in her car in the clinic car park and breathes deliberately
until her hands stop trembling.

She has not filed a complaint against herself, and the formal review concluded without disciplinary
action. In some ways, the absence of external punishment makes it worse. The case has been closed by
everyone except Marama, who reopens it every single day, presiding over it as both judge and
defendant, returning always to the same verdict: that she should have known, that she should have
felt it coming, that a person who truly understood the body the way she believed she did would never
have let this happen.
