# The Weight of What He Witnessed

Martin is a 73-year-old retired journalist who spent four decades covering stories across North
America, building a reputation for being the man who showed up when no one else wanted to. He is
divorced, lives alone in a small apartment in Chicago, and still keeps a battered press badge pinned
to the corkboard above his desk — a habit he cannot bring himself to break. He came to therapy
reluctantly, referred by his physician after a series of what he called "bad patches" that had begun
interfering with his sleep, his eating, and what little social life he had maintained since his
marriage ended twelve years ago.

The event that changed Martin occurred eleven years ago, when he was assigned to cover the aftermath
of a large industrial chemical explosion at a manufacturing plant outside Cleveland. He arrived
within hours of the blast, credentialed and experienced, certain that this was simply another story.
What he found was not simply another story. Several workers had been trapped inside a secondary
structure that collapsed after the initial explosion, and Martin was standing close enough to the
perimeter fence to hear men screaming inside the smoke. Emergency responders were delayed by a
second, smaller explosion. Martin watched through a chain-link fence for nearly forty minutes as
rescue workers were held back and three men died within what felt like arm's reach of help. He
photographed it. He filed the story. He won a regional award for his coverage. He told himself, as
he had always told himself, that bearing witness was the job.

He did not fall apart immediately. For several years he continued working, filed more stories,
covered more difficult scenes. But something had shifted in the architecture of how he moved through
the world. He began avoiding assignments that involved industrial sites or large crowds. He told his
editor it was a question of interest, not capacity. His marriage, already strained by decades of
emotional distance, ended the year after the explosion, though Martin connects the two events only
reluctantly.

Now, in retirement, the distance he once maintained through constant motion has collapsed. He wakes
most nights between two and four in the morning with his heart hammering, having dreamed some
version of that fence, those sounds, that smoke. During the day, certain triggers — the chemical
smell of cleaning products, the wail of a distant siren, footage of industrial accidents on the
television news — can drop him back into the scene with a vividness that startles him. He describes
standing in his kitchen one morning, smelling a neighbor's gas stove through the ventilation, and
finding himself gripping the counter, unable to move, certain for a long moment that he was back at
that fence.

Martin describes a persistent, bone-deep sense that he did something wrong that day, though he is
intellectually unable to articulate what he could have done differently. He was not a rescue worker.
He had no equipment. He knows this. The knowing does not help. He carries a quiet conviction that
his presence — his watching, his photographing — was a form of participation in something shameful,
and this belief has calcified into a general sense that he is not a person who deserves ease or
comfort.

He has become largely isolated. He declines invitations from former colleagues, keeps phone calls
short, and fills his days with solitary routines: coffee, newspapers, long walks along the lakefront
that he describes as the only time he feels something close to neutral. He has stopped writing
entirely, which he describes as the most disorienting loss of all — writing was always the way he
processed the world, and now the blank page feels like an accusation.

He sits across from his therapist with the careful posture of a man who has spent a lifetime
presenting composure as competence, and says, with a dry, practiced humor that does not quite reach
his eyes, that he supposes he has been filing the wrong kind of stories for a very long time.
