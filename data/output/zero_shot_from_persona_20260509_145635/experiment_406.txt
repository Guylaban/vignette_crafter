**Vignette: Nadia, Age 46**

Nadia grew up in Lebanon and immigrated to the United States in her late twenties, eventually
building a quiet, ordered life around her work as a pharmacist in a suburban compounding pharmacy.
She is precise by nature — someone who finds comfort in exactness, in the measured weight of powders
and the logic of molecular structures. For most of her adult life, that precision served her well.
Then, four years ago, her eleven-year-old son Kareem collapsed at a soccer practice with what turned
out to be bacterial meningitis. By the time the ambulance reached the hospital, he had already begun
seizing.

Nadia arrived at the emergency department to find her son surrounded by people she recognized
professionally but could not reach through the glass of her own terror. She knew exactly what
bacterial meningitis could do. That knowledge, which had always felt like armor, became something
closer to a weapon turned inward. She stood in a hallway for six hours understanding, with clinical
precision, every worst-case scenario unfolding in real time. Kareem survived, and after three weeks
in the pediatric ICU and months of rehabilitation, he recovered fully. He is fifteen now, healthy
and loud and indifferent to his mother's worry in the way that teenagers are.

But Nadia has not recovered in the same way.

She still drives past the hospital on her way to work each morning and feels her chest tighten the
moment the building comes into view. She takes the longer route now, adding twelve minutes to her
commute, and tells herself it is because of traffic. At the pharmacy, she is meticulous as ever —
her colleagues describe her as the most careful person they have ever worked with — but there are
moments, usually when a parent comes in filling a pediatric antibiotic prescription, when she has to
excuse herself to the back room. She stands at the sink, runs cold water over her wrists, and waits
for the image to pass: Kareem's face, gray and slack, the sound of the ventilator.

Sleep is something she negotiates with rather than experiences. She wakes most nights between two
and three in the morning, heart already pounding, sometimes certain she has heard a phone ringing —
the hospital calling, the way they called four years ago. She checks on Kareem. She checks again. He
has asked her, gently and then not so gently, to stop coming into his room at night. She agrees each
time, and then the darkness comes and the agreement dissolves.

She does not talk about what happened, not really. Her mother in Beirut knows Kareem was sick and
recovered. Her coworkers know there was "a medical emergency" years ago. Nadia has constructed a
version of the story that is factually accurate and emotionally evacuated, and she offers it when
pressed. What she does not offer is the part where she still believes, on some cellular level, that
the worst is simply waiting to happen again. She monitors Kareem's temperature with a thermometer
she keeps in her purse. She has memorized the fastest route to three different emergency rooms. She
has not been on a date in four years because the thought of being unreachable, even for two hours,
feels unconscionable.

There is a particular kind of loneliness in being a person who understands the science of what
happened and still cannot convince her nervous system that it is over. Nadia knows this about
herself. She is not without insight. She can name what her body is doing, can trace the pharmacology
of her own stress response with reasonable accuracy. And yet, when Kareem coughs in the next room,
she is eleven again — not eleven herself, but back in that hallway, watching her child be eleven,
possibly for the last time.

She came to therapy, she says, because she is tired. Not of Kareem. Never of Kareem. Tired of being
afraid of a future that already happened.
