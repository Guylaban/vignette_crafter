# The Weight of Borders

Maria is a 34-year-old Colombian woman who has been married for six years and works as a corrections
officer at a medium-security facility. She carries herself with a practiced stillness — the kind
that looks like composure from the outside but feels, she says, like holding her breath underwater.

When Maria was nineteen, she fled Colombia with her younger brother after paramilitaries killed her
father and threatened what remained of her family. The journey north took nearly four months. She
and her brother moved through jungle, across rivers, and into the hands of coyotes who were
indifferent at best and predatory at worst. At one point, the group she traveled with was detained
in a makeshift holding camp for eleven days — no reliable food, no information, no certainty about
what came next. A woman in the group died during the third night. Maria held her brother's face
against her shoulder so he wouldn't see them carry the body out in the dark. She was nineteen years
old and she made decisions that kept them both alive, and she has never quite forgiven herself for
some of those decisions.

They eventually reached the United States and were granted asylum. Maria built a life — she learned
English with ferocious discipline, earned her GED, completed criminal justice coursework, and joined
the corrections department at twenty-six. She married Andrés, a quiet, steady man who works in
construction. From the outside, her life looks like a success story, and she knows this, and the gap
between how things look and how they feel is something she rarely talks about.

Inside the facility, Maria is known as reliable and unflappable. She walks the cellblocks with
authority. But certain things reach through the professional surface without warning. The sound of
heavy metal doors locking — a sound she hears dozens of times a day — sometimes lands in her chest
like a physical blow. When she processes new intakes and watches people stripped of their belongings
and their names, replaced by numbers and jumpsuits, something in her goes very quiet and very cold.
She does her job. She does it well. And then she drives home and sits in the parking lot of her
apartment complex for twenty or thirty minutes before she can go inside.

Sleep is where the past lives most openly. She wakes two or three times a week from dreams she can't
always remember but that leave her heart hammering and her nightgown damp. Andrés has learned not to
touch her when she startles awake — early in their marriage he reached for her in the dark and she
screamed in a way that frightened them both. They don't talk about it much. She doesn't want him to
see how far back she still lives sometimes.

She avoids the news when it covers immigration. She changes the channel, leaves the room, scrolls
past. When a colleague made a joke last year about people "sneaking across the border," Maria smiled
tightly and said nothing and then spent the rest of her shift cycling through a rage so clean and
cold it scared her. She didn't say anything to HR. She didn't say anything to Andrés. She went home
and cleaned the kitchen until midnight.

What brings her in now is not a crisis exactly. It is more like exhaustion — the exhaustion of
managing everything so carefully for so long. She mentions, almost as an afterthought, that she has
started having trouble concentrating during briefings, that she sometimes feels like she is watching
herself work from somewhere slightly outside her body. She wonders aloud whether she is just tired,
or whether something is actually wrong with her. She asks the question the way someone asks who
already suspects the answer but needs to hear it said out loud by someone else, in a room where it
is safe enough to let it be true.
