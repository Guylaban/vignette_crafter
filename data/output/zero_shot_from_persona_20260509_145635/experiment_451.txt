# The Sound He Can't Unhear

Marco is a 35-year-old Colombian-born guitarist who has spent the better part of his adult life
playing in restaurants, wedding halls, and small concert venues across the city. Music has always
been the language he trusts most — more reliable, he says, than words. He met his wife, Daniela, at
one of his shows eight years ago, and they married two years later. Until eighteen months ago, his
life moved more or less the way he had always imagined it would.

The accident happened on a Tuesday evening in November. Marco was driving home after a late
rehearsal, his guitar in the back seat, when a commercial truck ran a red light and struck the
driver's side of his car at full speed. The car spun, rolled, and came to rest against a concrete
barrier. Marco remembers the sound of the impact — a grinding, tearing roar — and then a strange,
suspended silence before the pain arrived. He was conscious throughout. He lay pinned in the
wreckage for nearly forty minutes, certain, in the way that feels absolute and animal, that he was
going to die there. Rescue workers had to cut him out. He sustained a fractured collarbone, three
broken ribs, and a severe laceration along his left forearm that required surgery. His left hand —
his fretting hand — was spared, though it took weeks before he believed that fully.

Physically, Marco recovered. The bones healed. The scar on his forearm faded to a pale ridge. But
something else did not heal on the same schedule, and this is what brings him in now.

He does not sleep well. Most nights he lies awake past two or three in the morning, his mind
returning, unbidden, to the inside of that car — the smell of the airbag, the way the windshield
looked after it shattered, the sound. It is always the sound that comes back hardest. He describes
it as though it has been recorded somewhere inside his chest, playing on a loop he cannot switch
off. When he does sleep, the dreams are vivid and violent, and he wakes with his heart slamming,
reaching for Daniela, who has learned to simply put her hand on his back without asking questions.

Driving has become almost impossible. Marco sold his car four months after the accident and now
takes the subway or asks Daniela to drive him. When he is in any vehicle, he sits rigid, watching
every intersection, every merging lane, convinced that the next impact is already in motion. He has
turned down gigs that require travel across town at night. He has turned down gigs for smaller
reasons than that.

The music itself has changed. He still plays, because stopping entirely feels like a surrender he is
not willing to make, but he notices that something has gone quiet in him when he picks up the
guitar. He used to lose himself in it. Now he stays on the surface, technically competent but
emotionally elsewhere. His bandmates have not said anything directly, but he has caught the looks
they exchange. Daniela tells him his playing still sounds beautiful. He does not believe her, and
the not-believing makes him feel guilty.

He finds himself irritable in ways that embarrass him — snapping at Daniela over small things, then
feeling a wash of shame so intense it is almost physical. He has pulled back from his family,
declining Sunday dinners at his in-laws' home, avoiding the noise and closeness that used to comfort
him. He tells himself he is protecting them from a version of himself he does not fully recognize.

What Marco says, sitting in the chair across from his therapist, is that he feels like the accident
broke something in him that the doctors could not see on any scan. He is not wrong that something
broke. He is also not wrong, though he does not yet know it, that broken things can sometimes be
repaired.
