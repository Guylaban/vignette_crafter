# Clinical Vignette: Daniel

Daniel is a 53-year-old married social worker who has spent the better part of his career helping
others navigate the aftermath of trauma. There is a particular irony in this that he does not find
amusing — and one he has never, until recently, spoken aloud to anyone.

Three years ago, Daniel was sexually assaulted by a colleague at a professional conference. The two
men had known each other through their regional network for years, and after a dinner with a larger
group, his colleague invited him back to his hotel room under the pretense of continuing a
conversation about a shared case. What happened next left Daniel sitting on the edge of a stranger's
bed in a city far from home, staring at the carpet and trying to understand what had just occurred.
He did not call the police. He did not tell his wife. He showered, checked out early the next
morning, and drove the four hours home in silence.

In the weeks that followed, Daniel told himself he was fine. He is, after all, a man who understands
trauma — who has sat across from survivors for twenty years and helped them name what was done to
them. But understanding something professionally and living it personally turned out to be entirely
different territories. He began sleeping poorly, waking between two and four in the morning with his
heart already pounding, the room feeling too small. His wife, Karen, noticed he had become quieter,
more withdrawn, but when she asked, he told her it was work stress.

He returned to his caseload and, for a time, threw himself into it with a kind of desperate focus.
But sitting with clients who disclosed sexual trauma became increasingly difficult. He began to feel
a tightening in his chest during those sessions — a sensation he recognized clinically as a trauma
response and could not stop. He started rescheduling appointments, finding administrative reasons to
reduce his direct client hours. His supervisor praised his thoroughness. Daniel said nothing.

At home, he and Karen have grown careful around each other in a way that neither of them names.
Physical intimacy, once easy between them, now feels to Daniel like a door he cannot open without
something dark rushing through it. He finds reasons to stay up late, to come to bed after she is
already asleep. He tells himself he is protecting her from something he cannot fully explain.

He is hypervigilant in ways that exhaust him. He scans rooms when he enters them. He no longer
attends professional conferences. When he encounters the colleague's name in a group email — the man
still works in the same regional network — Daniel's hands go cold and he closes his laptop and does
not open it again for the rest of the day. He has rehearsed, hundreds of times, what he might say if
he ever saw him in person. He has not yet had to find out whether he could say any of it.

What troubles Daniel most, perhaps, is the shame — a shame he would immediately recognize as
misplaced in any of his clients, and which he cannot dislodge from himself. He knows,
intellectually, that what happened was not his fault. He has said those exact words to other people.
But knowing and feeling remain, for him, stubbornly separate continents. He wonders, in his worst
moments, whether the fact that he understands the psychology of it all makes him somehow more
culpable for not having stopped it, for not having spoken sooner, for continuing to sit across from
survivors while carrying this unacknowledged wound.

He finally mentioned, in passing, to his own physician that he had been having trouble sleeping.
That conversation led, eventually, to a referral. He arrived at his first appointment carrying a
legal pad, as though he intended to take notes. He sat down, looked at the floor for a long moment,
and said, quietly, that he was not sure he was the kind of person this was supposed to happen to.
