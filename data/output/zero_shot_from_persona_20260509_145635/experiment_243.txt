**Vignette: Amara, Age 42**

Amara came to this country eleven years ago, carrying very little except the memory of what had been
done to her. In the months before she fled, she had been detained by government forces in her home
country following a protest she had attended with members of her community organization. What
followed over the next several days — the beatings, the electric shocks, the threats against her
children who were still at home — she describes in fragments, and only reluctantly, as though the
words themselves carry the capacity to hurt her again. She was eventually released without
explanation, and within weeks she had arranged to leave with her two youngest children. Her husband,
Kofi, followed six months later.

She built a life here. She learned the routes, passed her licensing exams, and has worked as a train
conductor for nearly eight years. Her colleagues know her as steady and dependable — someone who
runs a tight schedule and doesn't waste words. What they don't know is that she arrives at the depot
forty minutes early each morning so she can walk the platform alone before anyone else arrives,
checking that everything is exactly as it should be, reassuring herself in ways she can't fully
articulate that she is safe.

The cab of the train is both a refuge and a source of difficulty. She appreciates the structure of
it — the fixed route, the timetable, the clear set of rules — but certain things undo her. When the
train passes through a long tunnel and the lights flicker, her hands tighten on the controls and her
breath goes shallow. The darkness and the sudden loss of visibility pull her back, not in a way she
can narrate in the moment, but in a way her body recognizes before her mind does. She has learned to
count slowly until the light returns.

At home, Amara is quieter than she used to be. Kofi has said this gently, more than once, and she
knows he is right. She loves her children — now teenagers — but finds herself watching them with an
alertness that exhausts her, cataloguing exits when they are in public spaces, rehearsing what she
would do if something happened. She sleeps lightly and wakes often, sometimes because of a dream she
can't fully reconstruct, other times for no reason she can name, only a certainty that something is
wrong. By morning she is already tired.

She does not speak of the detention to her children. She has told them she left because of political
instability, which is true, but incomplete. She carries the fuller truth alone, or mostly alone.
Kofi knows some of it. She has never told him everything, partly because she does not want to see
his face change when she does, and partly because she is not sure she has the language for it — not
in any language she speaks.

What brought her to therapy was not a crisis but an accumulation. A younger colleague made a joke
about torture in a news story — a careless, thoughtless joke — and Amara excused herself to the
bathroom and could not stop shaking for twenty minutes. That evening she sat in her car in the
driveway for an hour before going inside. She told Kofi she was tired. She told herself the same
thing. But she recognized, somewhere beneath the exhaustion, that she had been managing something
for a very long time, and that managing it was no longer quite enough.

She presents now as composed and articulate, choosing her words with care. She makes eye contact
steadily, though she sits slightly forward in her chair, as if prepared to stand. She describes her
experiences with a measured quality that reads less like acceptance than like long practice at
containment. Underneath that composure, she is a woman who has not felt entirely safe in eleven
years, and who has built an entire life — a good life, she insists, and she means it — around that
unspoken fact.
