# Karim

Karim is nineteen years old, already married, already working — already, in many ways, trying to
build a life that looks nothing like the one he came from. He sits in session with a careful
stillness about him, the kind that takes years to learn, the kind that children develop when they
understand that drawing attention to yourself is dangerous.

He grew up in a household where his father's moods were the weather that everyone lived inside. On
ordinary days, there was simply tension — a held breath, a careful arrangement of behavior around
one man's unpredictability. On bad days, the tension broke into something physical. His father hit
him with an open hand, a closed fist, a belt, whatever was available and whatever Karim had
apparently earned. This began as early as Karim can remember — certainly by age five — and continued
through most of his adolescence. His mother was present but not protective. She managed her own fear
by looking away. Karim learned to do the same, in his own fashion. He became quiet. He became good
at reading rooms. He became, as he describes it with a small, humorless smile, "very good at
disappearing."

What he could not disappear from was the inside of his own mind.

He left home at seventeen, married young — partly for love, he says, and partly because having his
own household meant having walls his father could not walk through. He threw himself into his
studies with the kind of focus that is less about passion than about necessity, and he now works as
an environmental scientist for a consulting firm, spending long hours in the field collecting soil
and water samples. He finds the work genuinely absorbing. Being outside, measuring things,
documenting what is real and quantifiable — it suits him. The natural world does not change its
rules without warning.

But the past follows him into the present in ways he is only beginning to name. He startles badly at
raised voices — a colleague snapping at someone across the office, a stranger arguing on the street
— and the startle does not resolve quickly. His heart keeps hammering long after the sound has
stopped, and he finds himself scanning the environment with an alertness that exhausts him. At home,
he describes a persistent low-level vigilance, a habit of listening for footsteps, of tracking where
his wife is in the apartment without quite meaning to. She has noticed. She worries about him. He
does not know how to explain it to her without also explaining everything else.

Sleep is difficult. He wakes in the early hours from dreams he cannot always recall clearly —
impressions of confinement, of being small, of waiting for something bad to arrive. Some nights he
lies awake for hours, and the thoughts that come are not memories exactly but something more like a
feeling-state: a dull, pervasive sense that he is in danger, that he has done something wrong, that
punishment is pending. He knows, intellectually, that this is not true. Knowing does not dissolve
the feeling.

He has noticed that he goes long stretches without feeling much at all. He describes sitting through
moments that should matter to him — a good meal with his wife, a quiet evening, a result at work he
is proud of — and feeling strangely distant from them, as though he is watching himself from across
the room. He does not like this. He wants to be present for his life. He chose this life
deliberately.

What Karim carries is the particular weight of a child who was hurt by someone who was supposed to
protect him, and who spent years making himself smaller and quieter in response. He is nineteen now,
technically an adult, technically safe. But some part of him is still standing in the hallway of his
childhood home, listening, waiting, trying to calculate whether it is safe to move.
