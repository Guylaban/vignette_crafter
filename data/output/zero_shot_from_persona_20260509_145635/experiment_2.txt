# The Weight of Silence

Roberto is a 67-year-old widowed Colombian-born prosecutor who has spent four decades standing
before courtrooms, commanding the room with the kind of measured authority that comes from years of
practice. He is a man who has built his identity around being unshakable — the one who asks the hard

decade. He had not seen it coming. He had not fought back the way he believed he would have — the
way he imagined any man would. That failure of response, as he understands it, has become one of the
most corrosive parts of what followed. He froze. He dissociated. He walked back to his room and sat
on the edge of the bed until morning, not calling anyone, not moving, trying to find a version of
what had happened that made more sense than the one he was living inside.

He told no one for six months. His wife, Marisol, had died three years earlier from pancreatic
cancer, and the absence of her is a silence he carries everywhere. There is no one he trusts in the
way he trusted her. His adult children call on weekends, and he answers their questions about work
and health with the careful economy of a man who has spent his life constructing arguments. He gives
them nothing that worries them.

sexual assault cases. He sits across from survivors and prepares them to testify, and something in
him has shifted in ways he cannot fully articulate. He finds himself unable to maintain the
professional distance he once wore like a second skin. He cancels meetings without explanation. He

Sleep comes rarely and badly. He wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, heart
hammering, the dream always some variation of the same corridor, the same helplessness. He has
