# Elena

Elena is a 44-year-old Colombian-born diplomat who has spent the better part of two decades
navigating the careful, measured world of international relations. She is precise, composed, and
accustomed to holding herself together in rooms where the stakes are extraordinarily high. It is
this same composure that made it so difficult for her to recognize, let alone name, what has been
happening to her since she returned from her posting in a conflict region eighteen months ago.

She had volunteered for the assignment. That is something she returns to often — the fact that she
chose to go. Her posting placed her in a mid-sized city that, within three months of her arrival,
became the site of sustained urban combat. What began as sporadic shelling in outlying neighborhoods
moved inward with terrifying speed. Elena spent eleven days sheltering in the embassy compound with
local staff and a handful of other diplomats, listening to artillery strikes that rattled the
reinforced windows and turned the sky outside a permanent, chalky gray. On the eighth day, a rocket
struck a building two blocks away. She was standing near a window when it happened. She remembers
the pressure wave before she remembers the sound — a physical blow to the chest, a sensation she
describes as the air being stolen from the world. Two local embassy employees she had worked closely
with did not survive the evacuation that followed.

She was extracted safely. She filed her reports. She attended the debriefings. She told everyone,
including herself, that she was fine.

Back in her home country, reassigned to a quieter post, Elena began to notice things she could not
explain through exhaustion or jet lag. She stopped sleeping through the night. She would wake at two
or three in the morning with her heart hammering, certain — absolutely certain — that something was
wrong, though she could never say what. During the day, she found herself scanning rooms when she
entered them, positioning herself near exits, tracking the movement of strangers in ways that felt
involuntary and embarrassing. She is a diplomat; she understands how to read a room. But this was
different. This was not strategy. This was fear.

Loud, sudden noises undo her in ways she finds deeply shameful. A car backfiring in traffic sent her
ducking behind a parked vehicle last spring. A colleague slammed a door during a meeting and she
felt her vision narrow, her hands go cold, her mind suddenly and completely elsewhere — back in that
compound, back near that window. She excused herself and sat in a bathroom stall for twenty minutes,
breathing, waiting to return to herself.

She has become careful about what she allows herself to think about. She does not watch news footage
from conflict zones, though her work often requires her to. She has developed elaborate habits
around avoiding it — delegating briefings, skimming rather than reading, leaving the room when
certain images appear on screens. She tells herself this is self-care. She knows, somewhere, that it
is avoidance.

Socially, Elena has pulled back. She was never someone who made friends easily, but she had a small,
close circle — colleagues, a few old university friends, a sister in Medellín she used to call every
Sunday. The calls have become irregular. She finds it hard to explain why she is not quite herself,
and she finds it harder still to pretend that she is. It feels dishonest in a way that exhausts her.

What troubles her most, she says quietly, is the guilt — not survivor's guilt exactly, though that
is part of it. It is something more specific: the belief that she should have been able to hold
herself together better, that the two colleagues who died deserved a response from her that was more
than paperwork and professional composure. She carries them with her in a way she does not yet have
words for, and she is only beginning to understand that carrying them this way is costing her
something she cannot afford to keep losing.
