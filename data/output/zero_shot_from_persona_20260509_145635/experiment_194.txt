**Vignette: Anna, Age 35**

Anna is a 35-year-old married woman of European descent who works as a counselor at a community
mental health center. She is thoughtful and measured in the way she speaks, choosing her words
carefully — a habit, she says, that comes with years of sitting across from people in pain. What she
finds harder to explain is why, lately, she cannot extend to herself even a fraction of the patience
she offers her clients every day.

Eighteen months ago, Anna was driving home from a long shift on a wet November evening. She was not
speeding. She had not been drinking. She was simply tired, and for a moment — she estimates it was
no more than two seconds — her eyes drifted to her phone on the passenger seat. When she looked up,
a cyclist was directly in front of her. She braked, but not in time. The impact was a sound she
cannot unhear: a dull, heavy thud followed by silence. The cyclist, a man in his forties, survived,
but he sustained a severe spinal injury that left him with permanent damage to his left arm and
chronic pain that has altered his life entirely.

In the days after the accident, Anna moved through the world as though wrapped in gauze. She gave
her statement to police, cooperated fully, and was not charged with any criminal offense. Colleagues
told her it was an accident. Her husband told her it was an accident. She repeated the word to
herself at night like a mantra that refused to take hold. She began having nightmares in which she
replays the moment of impact, except in these versions she had looked up a second earlier, or a
second later, and the man rides past unharmed. She wakes from these dreams with her heart hammering
and her hands gripping the sheets, already bargaining with a past that will not change.

What surprises her most, she says, is how the trauma has followed her into the one place she always
felt competent — her work. She sits with clients who describe accidents, medical crises, sudden
losses, and she finds herself going somewhere else entirely. A client mentions a car and Anna's
chest tightens. She notices she has been holding her breath. She has become skilled at concealing
this, but the effort exhausts her, and by the end of each workday she feels hollowed out in a way
she never did before.

She has stopped driving at night almost entirely. Her husband now handles evening trips, and she has
constructed an elaborate set of justifications — she is tired, the roads are bad, it is more
efficient — that she knows are not the real reason. When she does drive, she grips the wheel with
both hands and keeps the radio off, scanning the road with an intensity that her husband has gently
described as frightening to witness. She cannot pass a cyclist without her stomach dropping.

Anna describes a persistent, low-grade sense of guilt that she cannot metabolize. She knows,
intellectually, the difference between negligence and malice. She has sat with clients who carry
irrational guilt and helped them find their way through it. But knowing the map, she has discovered,
is not the same as being able to read it when you are lost inside it. She thinks about the man often
— his name, his arm, the life he had before. She has looked him up online more than once, reading
whatever she can find, as though information might eventually add up to absolution.

Her sleep is fragmented. She is irritable with her husband in ways that shame her afterward. She has
noticed herself withdrawing from friendships, finding social gatherings effortful and hollow. There
is a version of herself she remembers from before that November evening — someone who felt, if not
carefree, then at least unencumbered by the specific weight of having hurt someone. She is not sure
how to find her way back to that person, or whether, after everything, she is even allowed to try.
