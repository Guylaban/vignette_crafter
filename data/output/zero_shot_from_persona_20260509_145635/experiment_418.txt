# The Weight of the Sky

Mei Lin is twenty-nine years old, a fighter pilot stationed at a forward operating base, someone who
has spent years learning to trust her instruments, her training, and her own hands in the cockpit.
She is the kind of person her squadron describes as unshakeable — calm under pressure, precise,
methodical. That reputation feels like a costume she borrowed from someone else now.

Eighteen months ago, Mei Lin's younger brother, Dani, was struck by a vehicle while crossing an
intersection near their family home in the Philippines during one of her rare visits. She was there.
She saw it happen. She held him on the asphalt while his breathing became labored and thin, speaking
to him in Tagalog, pressing her jacket against his side, watching his face for signs that he was
still present with her. He survived, but only after three weeks in intensive care, two surgeries,
and a period during which the family was told, more than once, to prepare themselves. Dani now walks
with a permanent limp and lives with chronic pain. He is alive. Mei Lin reminds herself of this
constantly, as though repetition might eventually make the fact feel like enough.

It does not feel like enough.

In the months that followed, Mei Lin returned to duty and threw herself back into flying. The
cockpit, she thought, would be the one place that still made sense. And for a while, it was. But the
intrusions started quietly — a flash of Dani's face when she looked at her instruments, the sound of
impact replaying during pre-flight checks, a sudden and overwhelming certainty during one training
sortie that something catastrophic was about to happen, not to her aircraft, but to someone she
loved on the ground, somewhere she could not reach. She broke off the exercise and requested to
return to base. She told her commanding officer it was a systems concern. It was not a systems
concern.

She sleeps poorly, waking between two and four in the morning with her heart already racing, the
sheets damp, the dream dissolving before she can name it but leaving its residue — dread,
helplessness, the specific texture of wet asphalt under her knees. She has begun checking her phone
compulsively before flights, needing to see a message from Dani, needing confirmation that he is
still there. If he does not respond within the hour, her mind constructs elaborate and convincing
narratives of disaster.

She has stopped video calling her family as often as she used to. It is easier, she tells herself,
to send short messages. The truth is that seeing Dani's face — the way he moves now, the careful way
he lowers himself into a chair — pulls her back to the intersection with a force that leaves her
breathless and ashamed. She is a combat pilot. She has flown missions in contested airspace without
flinching. She cannot watch her brother sit down without her chest collapsing inward.

Mei Lin describes herself as irritable in ways she does not recognize. She snaps at junior officers
and then spends hours replaying it, cataloguing her own failures. She has declined two social
invitations from close friends in her squadron this month, preferring the controlled quiet of her
quarters, where at least the variables are manageable. She has not told anyone at work what is
happening. In her culture, in her profession, in the particular intersection of both, she
understands that asking for help requires a vocabulary she has never been taught to use.

What she does say, quietly, in a session she almost did not attend, is this: she did everything
right that day. She knew first aid. She stayed calm. She kept him conscious. And none of it felt
like enough, and she cannot stop returning to the moment before — the half-second when she saw the
car and understood what was about to happen and could not move fast enough. She lives in that half-
second more than she lives anywhere else.
