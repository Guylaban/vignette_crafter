**Vignette: Nadia, Age 24**

Nadia grew up learning to sew at her grandmother's knee, and by the time she was nineteen she had
turned that inheritance into a small tailoring business, working out of a rented space in a strip
mall where the hum of her Singer machine filled the hours. She was on her way home from a fabric
supplier one evening fourteen months ago when a pickup truck ran a red light and struck the driver's
side of her sedan at full speed. The car spun, the airbag detonated against her face, and she
remembers the smell of something burning and the strange silence that followed before anyone came.
She was not seriously injured — a fractured wrist, bruised ribs, a deep laceration along her left
forearm — but she lay pinned by the crumpled door for nearly forty minutes before the fire
department could extract her. She was certain, in those forty minutes, that she was going to die.

The wrist healed. The laceration faded to a pale seam she can trace with her fingertip. What has not
healed is harder to describe to the people around her, and so mostly she does not try.

Nadia no longer drives. She tells customers her car is in the shop, a fiction she has maintained for
over a year. She takes rideshares to work and sits in the back seat with her hand braced against the
door, watching every intersection with the focused vigilance of someone who has learned that
ordinary moments can end without warning. When a driver accelerates sharply or another car drifts
too close, her heart slams into her sternum and her breath goes shallow and thin, and for a few
seconds she is back inside the crushed sedan, smelling that burning smell, unable to move her left
hand.

At the shop, she is most herself. The work requires her full attention — measuring a hem, matching a
pattern repeat, coaxing a difficult seam into alignment — and those hours are the quietest her mind
gets. But even there, intrusions find her. The backfire of a delivery truck in the parking lot once
sent her to the floor behind her cutting table, shaking, before she understood what the sound was.
She told the customer waiting in the fitting room that she had dropped something.

She sleeps poorly and lightly, waking at two or three in the morning with her heart already racing,
the details of the collision vivid and immediate, as though no time has passed at all. She has
stopped watching television in the evenings because she cannot predict when a crash scene will
appear, and the images lodge in her chest for days. She has pulled away from her two closest
friends, partly because she is exhausted by the effort of seeming fine, and partly because she
cannot fully explain why she no longer wants to go anywhere that requires a car.

Her family knows something is wrong. Her mother calls every evening and asks if she is eating, if
she is sleeping, if she has spoken to anyone. Nadia says yes to all three questions. She was raised
to move forward, to be grateful for what she still has — her hands, her work, her life — and she
holds that instruction close, though it sits uncomfortably alongside the knowledge that she flinches
at the sound of brakes and cannot look at photographs from before the accident without feeling a
grief she cannot quite name.

What she wants, when she lets herself think about it, is to feel safe inside a moving car. She wants
to drive herself to the fabric supplier on a Tuesday morning the way she used to, with the radio on
and her mind already sorting through what she needs. She wants to stop scanning every intersection
for the vehicle that is about to come from nowhere. She is twenty-four years old, and she is tired
of living as though the collision is still happening, somewhere just ahead of her, waiting.
