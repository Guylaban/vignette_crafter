**Vignette: Clara, Age 28**

Clara grew up in a small city in the Netherlands, the kind of place where she knew her neighbors and
felt safe walking home after dark. She trained to become a primary school teacher and, by her mid-
twenties, had found a rhythm she loved — mornings with her students, evenings with her partner,
Joris, and weekends spent cycling along the canals. She describes that period of her life as
ordinary in the best possible way.

The trouble began about two years ago, quietly at first. A man she had briefly dated — someone she
had met through mutual friends and seen for only a few weeks — did not accept that she had ended
things. He began appearing at places she frequented: her gym, the café near her school, the
supermarket two streets from her apartment. She told herself it was coincidence. Then the messages
started. Hundreds of them, arriving at all hours, cycling through tenderness and threat. He sent
letters to her school. He waited outside her classroom building. For nearly eight months, Clara
lived inside an escalating siege that no one around her could quite see, because nothing he did was
ever quite visible enough to feel undeniable until suddenly, one evening, it was — he was standing
in the stairwell of her apartment building, and she was alone.

She has not felt alone in the same way since, and yet she has not stopped feeling alone.

In the months following the police involvement and the restraining order, Clara returned to her
classroom because she believed she had to — her students needed her, and she needed to feel like
herself again. But standing at the whiteboard, she finds her eyes drifting to the window that faces
the street. She counts the adults who pass. She has rearranged the desks so that she can see the
classroom door from wherever she stands. Her colleagues notice that she is quieter than she used to
be, though they do not say so directly.

At night, she wakes at small sounds — a door in the corridor, Joris shifting beside her — and lies
rigid until her heart slows enough for her to convince herself she is safe. She rarely convinces
herself fully. Sleep, when it comes, sometimes brings dreams she cannot describe afterward but wakes
from drenched and ashamed. She has stopped telling Joris about them because she can see the worry
accumulating on his face, and she does not want to become a burden inside the relationship that is
supposed to be her refuge.

She avoids the café she used to love. She has changed her route to school three times. She deleted
her social media accounts and then felt guilty, as though she had let him take something else from
her. There are days in the classroom when a child raises a hand too quickly or a door slams down the
hall and her body floods with something she can only describe as electricity — a full-body alarm
that takes the rest of the lesson to quiet. She smiles through it. She is very good at smiling
through it.

What troubles Clara most, she says, is not the fear itself but what the fear has done to her sense
of the world. She used to believe, in a way she never had to think about, that people were
essentially trustworthy and that ordinary life was essentially safe. She does not believe that
anymore, and she grieves it the way she might grieve a person. She looks at her students — eight and
nine years old, trusting and unselfconscious — and feels something that is partly tenderness and
partly a sorrow she cannot name, because she remembers being someone who moved through the world the
way they do, and she is not sure she will ever find her way back to that.

She is here, she says carefully, because she wants to stop surviving and start living again. She is
not sure yet that she believes it is possible.
