**Vignette: Mae**

Mae is a fifty-four-year-old Thai-Australian woman who has worked as a licensed electrician for
nearly two decades. She is methodical and precise by nature — someone who has always taken comfort
in understanding exactly how systems work, in tracing a current back to its source. Three years ago,
that sense of control was stripped from her entirely.

It began with fatigue she attributed to long shifts and the physical demands of her trade. By the
time she saw a doctor, the cancer had already spread beyond her left breast into several lymph
nodes. She remembers sitting in the oncologist's office alone — her divorce had been finalized the
previous year — and hearing the word *aggressive* used in a way she had never associated with her
own body. She drove herself home afterward and sat in the driveway for two hours, unable to go
inside.

What followed was eight months of chemotherapy and radiation. Mae describes that period not as a
single trauma but as a sustained one — a slow accumulation of days in which her body became
unrecognizable to her. She lost her hair, her appetite, and eventually her ability to work. For a
woman whose identity had been built around physical competence and independence, the helplessness
was its own kind of devastation. She recalls lying on the treatment table during radiation and
consciously telling herself not to think about whether she would be alive at fifty-five. She trained
herself to think only about the next hour. It was the only way she could keep moving.

She finished treatment fourteen months ago and received a clear scan shortly after. By most medical
measures, she is a survivor. She returned to work within a few months, taking on residential jobs
that kept her closer to home. But Mae does not feel like a survivor. She feels, as she puts it, like
she is waiting for the other shoe to drop.

Any physical sensation now carries the potential to unravel her. A headache becomes a brain
metastasis. Fatigue at the end of a long job becomes evidence that the cancer has returned. She
checks her body compulsively — pressing her fingers into her neck, her armpits, her abdomen —
sometimes so often during a workday that she has to stop what she is doing and sit down. She knows,
intellectually, that she is in remission. The knowledge does not help.

Her sleep is fragmented and shallow. She wakes most nights between two and four in the morning with
her heart racing and the vivid, disorienting sense that she is back in the treatment ward — the
antiseptic smell, the cold of the IV line in her arm, the sound of the infusion pump. She cannot
always shake the images before morning. She has stopped watching medical dramas, avoids the hospital
district when she can reroute her drive, and declined an invitation to a fundraiser walk for cancer
research because she could not explain, even to herself, why the idea made her feel physically ill.

Mae lives alone. She has two adult children who live interstate, and she describes her relationship
with them as loving but careful — she does not want to worry them, and so she minimizes. She has a
small circle of friends from work, but she has pulled back from most social contact over the past
year. She describes herself as tired in a way that sleep does not fix, and as someone who no longer
trusts her own body the way she once trusted a circuit diagram.

What troubles her most, she says, is that she used to be the person others called in a crisis. She
was practical, steady, unshakeable. She cannot reconcile that woman with the one who cried in a
hardware store last month because she caught her reflection in a glass display case and, for just a
moment, did not recognize herself.
