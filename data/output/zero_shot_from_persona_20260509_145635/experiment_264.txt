# The Weight of Still Water

Marco is a forty-year-old Colombian-born general surgeon who has spent the better part of his adult
life learning to control outcomes. In the operating room, his hands are steady, his decisions
precise. He has always trusted that preparation and skill can hold chaos at bay. That belief began
to fracture three years ago, during the flooding that consumed his mother's coastal village in the
Chocó region, where he had traveled to visit family during a rare week away from the hospital.

The rains came faster than anyone anticipated. What began as a gray, heavy afternoon became, within
hours, a wall of brown water moving through the streets. Marco was on the second floor of his
mother's home when the ground floor disappeared beneath the surge. He spent eleven hours on that
roof with his mother, his teenage nephew, and two neighbors he barely knew, watching the water rise
and then, agonizingly slowly, recede. A woman from the house across the street did not make it out.
He watched her carried away. He was a surgeon — a man who intervenes, who acts — and he could do
nothing. He stood on that roof and watched her go.

When the water finally fell enough to wade through, Marco helped carry his exhausted mother to
higher ground. He functioned. He organized. He did what needed doing. It was only weeks later, back
in Bogotá and then in the United States where he now practices, that the ground beneath him seemed
to give way again.

The first sign was sleep. Or rather, the absence of it. He would lie beside his wife, Valentina, in
their quiet suburban bedroom and feel the mattress shift beneath him as though it were moving water.
He would startle awake convinced the floor was wet. Some nights he doesn't sleep at all, sitting in
the kitchen until four in the morning, watching the dark yard through the window.

In the operating theater, he remains technically competent — his colleagues have noticed nothing —
but Marco himself knows something has changed. When a case involves significant blood loss, when
fluids pool on the surgical drapes, he sometimes feels a sudden, lurching dissociation, as though he
is briefly somewhere else entirely. He grips the retractor a little harder and breathes through it.
He has not told anyone.

He avoids the news. Any footage of flooding — a hurricane season report, a documentary, even a
streaming drama with a storm scene — sends him out of the room with what he describes as a "pressure
in his chest that has no business being there." He declined a medical mission trip to a coastal
region last spring, offering a scheduling conflict as his excuse. He has not returned to Colombia.

His relationship with Valentina has grown careful and quiet in ways that worry her more than any
argument would. He is present but distant, affectionate in a mechanical way that she struggles to
name. When she asks how he is doing, he says fine and means it in the way that people mean it when
they have stopped checking. He has begun to believe that what happened on that roof is simply part
of him now, a new organ he didn't ask for, one that aches in wet weather.

What troubles Marco most — more than the nightmares, more than the sleeplessness — is the loss of
the conviction that he can keep people safe. He became a surgeon because he believed in the
equation: knowledge plus skill equals survival. He knows now that the equation has exceptions. He
stood on a roof with capable hands and watched someone drown anyway. He carries that knowledge into
every procedure, every consultation, every moment he picks up a blade. The water, in some form, is
always just below the floor.
