# The Weight of Hands

Marcus is a 31-year-old Filipino-American carpenter who has been married to his wife, Leila, for
four years. He builds things for a living — custom cabinetry, hardwood floors, deck frameworks — and
he has always taken quiet pride in the precision of his work, the way a well-fitted joint holds
without apology. That sense of solidity in the physical world is something he has leaned on his
whole life. It is harder to lean on now.

Two years ago, Marcus was stopped while driving home from a job site in the early evening, his truck
bed loaded with lumber and tools. What began as a routine traffic stop escalated quickly. Officers
pulled him from the vehicle, forced him to the ground, and held him there — a knee pressed into his
back, his face against the asphalt — for several minutes while they searched his truck without
explanation. He was not charged with anything. He was told he matched a description. He drove home
afterward with road grit still embedded in his cheek, and he did not tell Leila what had happened
for three days.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus noticed that the drive home from work — a route he had taken
hundreds of times — began to feel like a gauntlet. He started varying his path, adding fifteen or
twenty minutes to avoid the stretch of road where it happened. When he passed a police cruiser, his
hands would tighten on the steering wheel and his chest would fill with something that felt less
like fear and more like a sudden, terrible certainty that something bad was about to happen. He
began leaving job sites before dusk, unwilling to be on the road after dark.

Sleep became unreliable. He would wake at two or three in the morning with the full physical memory
of being pinned — the pressure across his back, the smell of asphalt, the sound of his own breathing
when he understood that struggling would make things worse. Some mornings he would get out of bed
and go to the garage, where he would work with his hands until the sun came up, planing wood or
organizing his tools, needing to feel like he was the one in control of something.

At work, Marcus is still skilled and still respected. His boss has noticed that he has become
quieter, less likely to joke around with the crew, quicker to withdraw when conversations turn to
certain topics — particularly anything involving law enforcement, traffic stops, or news stories his
coworkers discuss over lunch. He sometimes loses focus mid-task, finding himself standing at the
workbench with a chisel in his hand, unsure how long he has been standing there. He has nicked
himself twice in the past year in ways that feel careless to him, and carelessness is something he
does not forgive in himself easily.

At home, Leila describes him as "somewhere else." He knows she is not wrong. He loves her and he can
feel the distance he creates, but crowded spaces — even their own living room when family visits —
make him feel cornered in a way he cannot explain without sounding, to his own ears, unreasonable.
He has declined gatherings with his parents and siblings more than once, and the explanations he
offers feel thin even to him.

What troubles Marcus most is not the fear itself but what the fear implies. He grew up believing
that if he worked hard and kept his head down, he would be safe. He built that belief carefully, the
way he builds everything — joint by joint. The evening on that stretch of road did not just frighten
him. It dismantled something structural. He finds himself looking at the cabinets he makes, the
clean dovetail joints, the things that hold together because he made them hold together, and
wondering whether that kind of certainty is something he will ever fully trust again.
