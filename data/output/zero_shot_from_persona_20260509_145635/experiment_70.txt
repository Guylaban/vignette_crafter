# The Weight of Still Water

Mia is twenty-three years old and commands a small commercial vessel along the coastal routes of the
South China Sea. She is the youngest captain in her company's fleet, a fact her supervisors mention
often and that she receives in silence, neither proud nor dismissive — simply somewhere else behind
her eyes.

She grew up in a fishing village in the Philippines, the eldest of four children. Her father was
present in the way a storm is present: unpredictable, consuming, and capable of leaving damage that
took weeks to fully assess. From the time Mia was six until she left home at sixteen, he beat her
regularly — with his hands, with a belt, once with the wooden handle of a cleaning brush that left a
scar along her left shoulder blade she still cannot bring herself to touch. Her mother did not
intervene. Mia learned early that the sound of her father's footsteps on the porch steps meant she
needed to calculate — how drunk, how fast, where the younger children were, whether she could draw
his attention toward herself and away from them. She became very good at reading the atmosphere of a
room before she entered it.

She found the sea at sixteen when a distant uncle arranged a position for her on a cargo boat. The
water made sense to her in a way that land never had. It was honest about its dangers. She worked
obsessively, earned her certifications ahead of schedule, and by twenty-three held a captain's
license. Her crew respects her. She is calm in difficult conditions, precise, and rarely raises her
voice.

What they do not see is what happens when the vessel is docked and the engine goes quiet.

In stillness, the past finds her. She sleeps poorly in port, lying rigid in her bunk, listening to
sounds that don't belong — a creak of metal that becomes footsteps, a raised voice from the dock
that travels through her chest like a current. She wakes with her heart already running, her body
convinced of a threat her mind cannot locate. Some nights she doesn't sleep at all, sitting in the
wheelhouse in the dark, watching the water, waiting for morning to make the world legible again.

She startles badly. A crew member who dropped a wrench on the deck last month watched her spin
around with her hands raised before she caught herself. She apologized briskly and walked away,
humiliated in a way she couldn't name. She has stopped eating meals with the crew because the noise
and proximity feel like pressure she can't manage.

There are things she will not do. She will not be in a confined space with a man who is angry, even
mildly angry. She will not allow anyone to stand behind her. She keeps her back to walls when she is
in unfamiliar places on shore. These are not rules she made consciously — they are simply the
architecture of how she moves through the world.

She does not think of what her father did as abuse. She thinks of it as something that happened, the
way weather happens. What she notices is that she is always tired, that she feels nothing much when
she should feel something, and that occasionally — without warning — she is six years old again in
the kitchen doorway, doing the math of survival, her body flooded with a terror so complete it has
no edges.

She called her mother once, six months ago, and her mother asked when she was coming home. Mia said
she didn't know. She has not called since.

She is extraordinary at her work. She reads the sea the way she once read her father — watching for
shifts in pressure, for the small signs that precede something larger. It is a skill that was built
in fear and now keeps other people safe. She understands this, dimly, on her clearest days. On other
days she simply keeps the bow pointed forward and waits for the horizon to stay still.
