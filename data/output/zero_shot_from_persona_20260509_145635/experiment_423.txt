**The Weight of Being Watched**

Maya is a 33-year-old graphic designer who works from a home studio in the converted back bedroom of
the house she shares with her husband, Daniel. From the outside, her life looks orderly and creative
— mood boards pinned to the walls, a large monitor glowing with font samples and color palettes, the
smell of coffee always on. But for the past two years, since the stalking ended, Maya has been
quietly rebuilding something that was taken from her in pieces so gradual she almost didn't notice
it happening.

It began when she was 30, shortly after she left a freelance collective and started working
independently. A former colleague — someone she had considered a casual friend — began contacting
her with increasing frequency. At first it was professional inquiries, then personal messages, then
comments that suggested he knew more about her daily movements than he should have. He appeared
outside her gym. He sent letters to her home address, which she had never given him. For fourteen
months, Maya lived in a state of sustained, low-grade terror that she describes as "never being able
to exhale."

The police became involved. There were warnings, eventually a restraining order. The contact
stopped. And then, in the silence that followed, Maya discovered that the ending of the threat did
not mean the ending of what it had done to her.

She finds it almost impossible to work with her back to the door. She has rearranged her studio
three times trying to solve this problem — desk facing the window, then facing the wall, then angled
toward the hallway — but no arrangement feels safe. She keeps the studio door open so she can hear
the house. She has installed two additional locks and a video doorbell, and she checks the feed
compulsively throughout the day, sometimes pausing mid-project to review the last hour of footage
for no reason she can fully articulate.

Her work has suffered in ways that frustrate her deeply. Maya is talented and knows it, but she
struggles to enter the sustained concentration that good design requires. She'll be absorbed in a
layout and then a sound — a car slowing outside, a neighbor's gate — pulls her out completely. She
loses the thread. Some days she misses deadlines for the first time in her career and sits with a
shame she can't quite explain to clients.

Sleep is unreliable. She wakes between two and four in the morning with a racing heart, certain that
something is wrong, though she can't always say what. Daniel has learned to reach for her hand
without speaking. She appreciates this. She has told him most of it, but not all — not the part
where she still sometimes reads the old messages to confirm to herself that what happened was real,
that she wasn't overreacting, that she had a right to be afraid.

She avoids the neighborhood where the collective used to be based. She deleted all her professional
social media accounts, which cost her clients and visibility she had spent years building. She tells
people it was a lifestyle choice. It wasn't.

What surprises Maya most is how small the world has become. She used to love working in coffee shops
— the ambient noise, the anonymity, the feeling of being part of something moving. She hasn't done
that in two years. She tells herself she'll go back when things feel different, but she's beginning
to wonder what "different" would actually feel like, and whether she would recognize it if it
arrived.

She is not someone who thinks of herself as fragile. She runs half-marathons. She has built a
business from nothing. She is the person her friends call in a crisis. And yet she sits in her home
studio most days, listening to the house, checking the door camera, trying to make something
beautiful on a screen while the part of her that is still waiting for footsteps refuses, quietly and
stubbornly, to stand down.
