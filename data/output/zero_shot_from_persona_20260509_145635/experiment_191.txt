# Clinical Vignette: Raymond

Raymond is a 53-year-old married man who has driven city buses for nearly two decades. He knows his
routes the way most people know their own handwriting — automatically, without thought. Passengers
have always been a comfort to him, a kind of moving community he shepherds through the city each
day. That ease, that quiet pride in his work, is something he is only now beginning to understand he
has lost.

Fourteen months ago, Raymond was sexually assaulted. He had stayed late after a union meeting held
in a community center, and a man he recognized vaguely from the neighborhood followed him into the
parking garage and raped him at knifepoint. Raymond did not fight back. He has not forgiven himself
for this. He drove home afterward, sat in his truck in the driveway for two hours, and said nothing
to his wife when he finally came inside. He told no one for three weeks. When he did speak — to a
crisis line, in the middle of the night, while his wife slept — he hung up before the counselor
finished her first sentence.

Raymond eventually told his wife, Denise, in fragments, over the course of several painful
conversations. She responded with love and steadiness, but Raymond finds her tenderness almost
unbearable. Physical closeness between them has become rare. He does not explain this to her in any
way that satisfies either of them. He simply moves away, quietly, and she has learned not to reach
for him.

At work, Raymond functions — barely. He sits behind the wheel for eight hours and moves through the
city on autopilot, which is both a mercy and a source of dread. Certain intersections are dangerous
for him: the street near the community center, the parking garage entrance visible from the Route 14
loop. When he passes these, his hands tighten on the wheel and his vision narrows. Twice in the past
six months he has had to pull over, open the door, and breathe through what he describes as "the
feeling that I'm back there." He does not call these panic attacks. He calls them "episodes," and he
is ashamed of each one.

He sleeps poorly. When he does sleep, he often wakes at two or three in the morning with his heart
hammering, the details of the assault vivid and immediate, the smell of the garage present in the
room. He gets up, sits in the kitchen, and watches television with the volume low until dawn. He has
done this so often that the kitchen chair has become his side of the bed in some private, unspoken
way.

Raymond has stopped attending the union meetings he once looked forward to. He has withdrawn from
the small circle of friends he and Denise socialized with on weekends. He drinks more than he used
to — not dramatically, he would say, but a few beers every night instead of the occasional one. He
does not think of it as a problem. He thinks of it as the only thing that quiets the noise in his
head by ten o'clock.

What troubles Raymond most, and what he articulates with a kind of raw precision, is the belief that
he is no longer himself. He describes the man he was before the assault as someone competent,
steady, good at his job, good to his wife. He does not recognize that man anymore. He feels
contaminated in a way he cannot fully put into words, only that he does not want to be touched, does
not want to be seen, and is quietly convinced that if Denise truly understood what happened — not
just the facts, but what it meant — she would see him the way he sees himself. He has never asked
her whether this is true. He is afraid she would try to reassure him, and he would not believe her,
and then there would be nothing left to hold onto.
