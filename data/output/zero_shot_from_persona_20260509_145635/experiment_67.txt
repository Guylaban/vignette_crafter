# Clinical Vignette: Leilani

Leilani is a 46-year-old Fijian woman who has been married to her husband, Tomas, for nineteen
years. She works as a veterinarian at a mixed-practice clinic in a mid-sized coastal city, a career
she chose because, as she puts it, animals had always made more sense to her than people. She is
precise, competent, and well-regarded by her colleagues. Most of them have no idea that she spends a
portion of every night sitting awake at the kitchen table, listening.

Eight years ago, Leilani volunteered for a six-month placement with an international animal welfare
organization operating in a conflict-affected region in the Middle East. The work was
straightforward in concept — caring for working animals, livestock, and community dogs in areas
disrupted by ongoing fighting. What she encountered was not straightforward at all. Three months
into the placement, her convoy was caught in the middle of a ground assault on a village market. She
watched people die in the street. A young boy, no older than ten, was carried past her by a
screaming woman, and Leilani stood frozen against a crumbling wall, unable to move, certain she was
about to be killed. She was not. She was evacuated two days later with minor physical injuries and
what her supervising organization called "significant distress." She flew home, hugged Tomas at the
airport, and told him she was fine.

She was not fine. Within weeks of returning, she began waking at two or three in the morning with
her heart hammering, convinced she could smell smoke, convinced the house was not safe. She started
checking the locks — doors, windows, the back gate — sometimes three or four times before she could
sit down. She stopped watching the news entirely. She found herself unable to tolerate the sound of
car backfires or the sharp crack of a staple gun at the clinic, sensations that would send her heart
into her throat and leave her hands shaking for an hour afterward.

The harder thing to explain, she says, is what happened to her work. She loves her patients — the
frightened dogs, the stoic horses, the cats that bite — but she now finds herself overwhelmed by a
particular kind of dread when an animal is suffering and she cannot fix it quickly. During a
difficult surgery last year, when a dog coded on the table and her team scrambled, she felt herself
leave the room in some essential way. Her hands kept moving, her voice stayed calm, but inside she
was back against that wall, watching the boy being carried away. The dog survived. Leilani went to
her car afterward and sat there for forty-five minutes.

She has not told Tomas the full extent of what she carries. She describes herself as someone who
"got through it," and she believes this is partially true. She is still working. She still laughs.
She still makes dinner on Sundays and tends the small garden behind their house. But she has become
someone who lives at a slight remove from everything, as though there is a pane of glass between her
and her own life. She avoids any film or conversation involving war. She has declined two subsequent
international placements without fully explaining why. She finds it increasingly difficult to feel
close to Tomas, though she cannot articulate what has changed — only that some part of her is
perpetually braced for something terrible, and that bracing takes up most of the room inside her.

She came to therapy, she says, because she read something about how untreated stress can affect the
immune system, and she is, above all things, a clinician. She wants to understand what is happening
to her body. She has not yet used the word trauma in reference to herself.
