# The Weight of the Water

Rajan is a 58-year-old widowed fisherman of South Asian descent who has worked the coastal waters
off the same stretch of shoreline for nearly four decades. The sea has always been his livelihood,
his identity, and — in the years since his wife passed — his primary companion. He knows its moods
the way other men know the faces of their children.

Fourteen months ago, during a pre-dawn run in late autumn, a sudden squall rolled in without
warning. The swells came fast and enormous. A rogue wave struck the port side of his small trawler
and threw Rajan overboard into water so cold it felt, as he later describes it, "like being stabbed
everywhere at once." He went under twice. He is certain, in the way a man can only be certain of a
thing he has felt in his bones, that he was going to die. He was in the water for nearly twenty
minutes before a neighboring vessel spotted him and pulled him out. He was treated for hypothermia
and a fractured wrist. Physically, he recovered within weeks.

The rest of him has not recovered at all.

In the months that followed, Rajan found that sleep had become a kind of enemy. He wakes most nights
between two and four in the morning, gasping, his undershirt soaked through, his heart hammering as
though the cold water is still closing over his head. In the dreams, he is always going under for
the third time — a third time that never actually happened, but feels more real to him than anything
that did. He lies awake afterward and listens to the sound of the wind against his windows, and if
the wind is strong, he does not sleep again.

During the day, he functions — barely. He still goes out on the water because he has no other income
and no other life, but what was once effortless has become a daily act of endurance. When the boat
rocks unexpectedly, or when the sky darkens along the horizon, something seizes in his chest. He
grips the rail and breathes through his teeth and tells himself he is fine, though he does not feel
fine. He feels like a man standing at the edge of something he cannot name. Some mornings he sits in
his truck at the dock for thirty or forty minutes before he can make himself get out.

He has stopped going out alone when seas are anything above calm. He has stopped taking the longer
overnight runs that used to be his most profitable work. He tells the younger fishermen at the dock
that his wrist is still bothering him. It is easier than the truth.

At home, the silence of the house — which was already heavy after his wife died three years ago —
has taken on a different quality. He finds himself irritable in ways that surprise him, snapping at
his son when the young man calls to check in, then sitting with guilt afterward. He has little
appetite. He has stopped watching the television programs he used to enjoy. He sits at the kitchen
table in the evenings and sometimes realizes an hour has passed and he cannot account for it — he
was somewhere else, back in the water, back in the dark.

What troubles Rajan most, when he allows himself to examine it, is not the fear itself but the
feeling that he has become a stranger to the person he used to be. He was a man who loved the sea.
He was a man who was not afraid. He does not know where that man went, or whether he is still
somewhere beneath the surface, waiting to come back up.

He came to therapy, he says quietly, because his son asked him to. He is not sure it will help. But
he is here, sitting with his hands folded in his lap, and he is willing to try.
