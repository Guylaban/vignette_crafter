**Vignette: Amara**

Amara is an eighty-year-old Congolese woman who has been married to her husband Emmanuel for over
fifty years. She serves as a pastor in a small Pentecostal congregation in the city where she
resettled nearly two decades ago, and on most Sunday mornings she stands at the pulpit with a
steadiness that few in her congregation would question. What they do not see is what happens when
she sits alone in the church after everyone has gone home.

She was in her early sixties when the violence came to her village. She had lived through political
unrest before — her whole life had been shaped by it — but nothing had prepared her for the morning
armed men moved through her community with a purposefulness that left no room for negotiation. She
and Emmanuel fled with two of their grandchildren, walking for days through bush and across borders,
sleeping in fields, eating almost nothing. One of her daughters did not make it out. Amara does not
speak of her daughter by name in sessions, referring to her only as "the one we left." Whether she
means left behind in the village or left behind in death is something she has never clarified, and
her face closes in a particular way whenever the subject approaches, like a door swinging shut.

The years in the refugee camp were long. She was a pastor there too, holding services in a canvas
tent, praying over the sick and the grieving, holding other women's hands through losses that
mirrored her own. She learned to be a container for other people's suffering, which meant she had
little practice acknowledging her own. By the time she was resettled and her congregation grew and
Emmanuel's health began to decline, her own interior life had become a place she rarely visited.

Now, at eighty, she finds herself visited instead. Sleep comes reluctantly and leaves early. In the
hours before dawn she lies in the bed she shares with Emmanuel and watches the ceiling, her mind
returning without her permission to the road out of the village — the sound of her own breathing,
the weight of a grandchild on her back, the smoke behind her. She does not call these memories. She
experiences them as a kind of presence, as though the past has not passed at all but is simply
waiting in the dark room with her.

During the day she manages. She visits congregation members, she prepares her sermons, she counsels
young couples and grieving families. But certain things undo her without warning. A car backfiring
on the street outside the church sends her heart into a kind of frenzy she finds shameful. The smell
of woodsmoke from a neighbor's fireplace in winter can stop her completely in the middle of a
sentence. She has learned to excuse herself quickly in those moments, to step outside or into a
bathroom, to press her hands flat against a wall until the feeling passes. She tells no one.

She came to therapy not for herself, she explained in the first session, but because Emmanuel had
noticed she was not sleeping and had asked her to come. She sat with her handbag in her lap and her
back very straight and said she understood that what happened to her family was God's will, and she
meant it, and she also could not stop seeing the road.

There is a particular grief she carries that she has only recently begun to name: the sense that she
has spent twenty years helping her congregation survive their losses while her own loss has gone
unwitnessed. She built a life of extraordinary purpose in the aftermath of trauma, and she does not
regret a moment of it. But some part of her is still walking out of that village, still carrying the
child, still not yet allowed to stop and sit down and say: *this happened to me, and it was
unbearable, and I am still here.*
