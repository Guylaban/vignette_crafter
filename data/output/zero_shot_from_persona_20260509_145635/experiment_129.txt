**Karim**

Karim is a 38-year-old Lebanese-born man who has worked as a hairdresser for nearly fifteen years,
first in Beirut and then, after immigrating in his mid-twenties, in a busy salon in the city's west
end. He is known among his clients as warm and talkative — the kind of stylist people book not just
for a haircut but for the conversation. He has built his life carefully, chair by chair, client by
client. That life felt solid to him until three years ago, when it came apart without warning.

It began with fatigue he dismissed as overwork. Then came the bruising — strange purple blooms
appearing on his forearms without any injury he could account for. By the time he finally saw a
doctor, his blood counts were alarming enough to send him directly to hospital. Within a week he had
a diagnosis: acute myeloid leukemia. He was 35 years old and, as far as he had known that morning,
perfectly healthy.

What followed was eight months of aggressive chemotherapy, a bone marrow transplant, and a prolonged
hospitalization that he describes as feeling like being buried alive. He remembers the particular
sound of the IV pump at three in the morning, the way the overhead light in his room never fully
dimmed, the afternoon a nurse came in with a look on her face before she had said a word and he
understood, in his body before his mind, that something had gone wrong. There was a period of septic
shock during which he was told, later, that he had been close to dying. He has no memory of those
four days, and the gap frightens him almost more than what he does remember.

He went into remission. People called it a miracle. His mother flew from Beirut and wept at his
bedside. Friends brought food and flowers. Karim smiled and thanked everyone, and felt, beneath all
of it, profoundly alone in something he could not name.

Now, two years out of treatment, he is back at the salon. His hair has grown back — thicker than
before, he jokes, though the joke costs him something. He stands behind the chair for eight hours a
day, running his hands through other people's hair, and he is good at it. But his mind does not stay
in the room with him. Several times a week, without any clear trigger, he is pulled back into the
hospital — the smell of the antiseptic, the cold weight of the IV line in his arm, the specific
terror of not knowing if his body would survive the very treatment meant to save it. These moments
arrive without warning and leave him short of breath, his heart loud in his chest, his hands
continuing their work almost independently while some part of him is somewhere else entirely.

He sleeps poorly. He wakes between two and four in the morning and lies in the dark cataloguing
sensations in his body — a twinge in his side, a headache, a patch of skin that feels different —
convinced, again and again, that the cancer has returned. He has had scans that show nothing. The
reassurance lasts perhaps a day. He has stopped telling people about this because he can see in
their faces that they believe he should be grateful, and he is grateful, and neither of those things
touches the fear.

He avoids the oncology ward where he was treated, rerouting his commute so he does not pass the
hospital. He has declined two invitations to a cancer survivors' group. He tells himself he is busy.

What he finds hardest to explain — to his mother on the phone, to the few friends who ask — is that
surviving did not feel like an ending. It felt like the moment the ground shifted, and he has not
found his footing since. He cuts hair, he laughs with clients, he closes the salon at night. And he
carries, quietly and alone, the knowledge of how quickly a body can become a place you are no longer
safe inside.
