# Marcus

Marcus is a 47-year-old bus driver who has spent nearly two decades navigating the same city routes,
learning the rhythms of his passengers and the pulse of the streets. He describes his work as
something he has always taken quiet pride in — the dependability of it, the way people count on him
to get them somewhere safely. That sense of reliability has been one of the few constants holding
his life together since the night, three years ago, when everything shifted.

It happened on a Friday evening after his shift. Marcus had pulled into a convenience store two
blocks from his depot, a stop he made routinely. What followed unfolded in under four minutes,
though he says it has never felt that short. Police responded to a reported disturbance nearby, and
Marcus — tall, Black, wearing a dark jacket — was ordered to the ground at gunpoint before he had
fully processed what was happening. He was shouted at, physically restrained, and held face-down on
the pavement while officers sorted out the situation. No charges were filed. He was told he could
go. No one apologized.

He drove home that night in a kind of numb disbelief. In the days that followed, he kept waiting to
feel angry, or relieved, or something definitive. Instead, he found himself replaying the moment the
first officer's voice hit him — that particular pitch of command — over and over, involuntarily, the
way a song gets stuck. He started having trouble sleeping, lying awake with his heart beating too
fast, certain something was about to go wrong without knowing what.

The intrusions moved into his working days. Certain sounds on his route — a radio crackling, someone
shouting from the sidewalk, the flash of blue and red lights in his mirrors — could pull him back to
the pavement so completely that he would lose several seconds of the present. He began gripping the
steering wheel harder than he needed to. Passengers noticed he had become quieter, less likely to
exchange the small jokes he used to offer at red lights.

Off the bus, Marcus has reorganized his life around avoidance in ways he rarely names directly. He
no longer stops at that convenience store, or any in that neighborhood. He takes longer routes home.
He has stopped attending the monthly card games he used to host at his apartment, finding the idea
of having people over — the noise, the unpredictability — exhausting rather than pleasurable. A
relationship he had been cautiously building with a woman he met through a friend quietly dissolved;
he found himself unable to explain why he kept pulling back, only that closeness had started to feel
like exposure.

He describes a persistent sense of being watched when he is outside on foot, a low-grade alertness
that never fully powers down. He scans faces at bus stops and intersections with a vigilance he
knows is excessive but cannot talk himself out of. Some mornings he sits in his parked bus before
opening the doors, breathing slowly, preparing himself. He has not told his dispatcher or any of his
coworkers what happened. He worries they would not understand, or worse, that they would minimize it
the way the officers did — efficiently, without cruelty, as if his experience had no particular
weight.

What Marcus returns to most often in conversation is not the fear he felt on the ground, but the
moment after — being told he was free to go, and realizing that no one in that interaction had ever
seen him as a person with somewhere to be, someone who was trusted daily with other people's lives
and safety. He carries that realization the way he carries everything now: quietly, while still
showing up for his route, while still getting people where they need to go, while the world outside
his windshield continues to feel like a place that can turn on him without warning and offer no
accounting for it afterward.
