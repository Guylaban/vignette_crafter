# The Weight of Quiet Rooms

Raymond is a 74-year-old Māori man who spent the better part of four decades shaping other people's
words for a living. He worked his way up from a junior copy editor at a regional newspaper in
Wellington to a senior editorial role at a respected publishing house, a career defined by his
attention to what lies beneath the surface of language — what a writer means but cannot quite say.
It is not lost on him, he admits quietly, that he has spent years helping others find their voice
while remaining almost entirely silent about what happened to him.

The abuse began gradually, the way he says most terrible things do — so slowly that by the time he
understood what was happening, it had already become the shape of his life. His marriage to Diane
lasted eleven years. The physical violence started in the third year, following the loss of a
pregnancy. He remembers the first time she struck him with something — a ceramic mug, across the
shoulder — and how he stood very still afterward, more confused than hurt, telling himself it was
grief, that she was not herself. He did not leave for another eight years. He is still working out
why.

The violence escalated across those years in cycles he learned to read the way he reads a manuscript
— looking for the signs, the shifts in tone, the pressure building beneath the surface. He developed
a kind of hypervigilance he mistook for competence. He thought he was managing things. What he was
actually doing, he now understands, was surviving.

The divorce was finalised when Raymond was 58. He moved into a small flat in Auckland and threw
himself back into his work, editing longer hours than ever, grateful for the noise of other people's
stories filling his head. For a time, it seemed to work. He functioned. He socialised carefully, at
a distance. He did not speak of Diane to anyone.

The intrusions began in earnest after he retired at 70. Without the manuscripts to absorb him, the
memories moved in like weather. He wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, his heart
hammering, the sheets damp. Sometimes he is back in the kitchen of the house in Karori, bracing
himself against the counter. Sometimes he does not know where he is for several seconds, and those
seconds are the worst. He has taken to sleeping with a lamp on, something he finds quietly
humiliating.

During the day he avoids the suburb where they lived, will not drive past certain streets. He cannot
watch films in which a man is struck or cornered — he leaves the room without explanation, then sits
with the shame of having left. He keeps his flat meticulously ordered, every object in its place, a
control he understands intellectually but cannot relinquish. Loud voices from neighbouring
apartments send a cold alertness through him that does not settle for hours.

He describes feeling hollowed out in a way that is difficult to articulate — a persistent numbness
that lifts occasionally but never fully departs. He has grown estranged from his adult children, not
through any rupture but through a slow withdrawal he initiated and now regrets but does not know how
to reverse. He tells himself they are busy. He does not tell himself the truth, which is that
closeness frightens him now in ways it did not before.

What troubles Raymond most, he says, is not the nightmares or the hypervigilance or even the
loneliness. It is the belief, persistent and corrosive, that he should have known better. That a man
his age, an educated man, a man who reads people for a living, should have seen it clearly and left
sooner. He edits this belief occasionally, crosses it out, writes something kinder in the margin.
But it keeps returning to the page.
