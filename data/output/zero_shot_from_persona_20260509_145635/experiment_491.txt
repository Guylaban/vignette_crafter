# Eleanor

Eleanor is a 76-year-old woman who has spent the better part of four decades in public service,
currently holding a seat on the city council where she is known for her composed, authoritative
presence in chambers. She speaks precisely, chooses her words with care, and is the kind of person
other councillors defer to when a meeting threatens to unravel. Very few of them know that she
drives home some evenings gripping the steering wheel so hard her knuckles whiten, trying to outrun
something she has never quite been able to name.

She grew up in a small terraced house in the north of England, the eldest of three children. Her
father was a drinker — not always, and not predictably, which was in many ways worse. Eleanor
learned early to read the particular weight of his footsteps on the stairs, the specific pitch of
her mother's silence. On the nights he came home transformed, Eleanor would press herself against
the wall of the hallway and watch. She watched her mother absorb blows with a kind of terrible
stillness. She watched her own small hands do nothing. She was seven, then eight, then ten, and the
watching continued until she was old enough to leave.

She left at seventeen and did not look back — or so she told herself for the next fifty years. She
married, had children, built a career, divorced. The marriage ended, she says matter-of-factly,
because she was never fully present in it. She has wondered, in quieter moments, whether she ever
learned how to be.

It was a council meeting eighteen months ago that undid her. A planning dispute had grown heated; a
male colleague raised his voice and brought his hand down flat on the table. The sound — sharp,
percussive, entirely ordinary — moved through Eleanor like electricity. She did not flinch visibly.
She is too practiced for that. But something closed off inside her, and she sat through the
remaining forty minutes of the meeting in a state she can only describe as watching herself from the
ceiling.

Since then, the distance between Eleanor and the present moment has widened. She finds herself
scanning rooms when she enters them, positioning herself near exits, cataloguing the emotional
temperature of every person nearby. At home, alone in the flat she has lived in for eleven years,
she sleeps poorly and wakes at three or four in the morning with her heart already racing, the
bedroom unfamiliar for a few disorienting seconds. She does not always remember the dreams, but she
wakes knowing something has been happening in the dark.

There are images that come without invitation — her mother's face, turned away; the particular
pattern of the hallway wallpaper; the sound of something breaking in another room. These arrive most
reliably when she is still: reading, or sitting in traffic, or waiting for a meeting to begin. She
has become quietly avoidant of anything that might invite stillness.

She is irritable in ways she dislikes in herself. She snaps at her assistant over small things and
then feels a wash of shame. She has pulled back from her adult children, cancelling dinners,
offering thin excuses. She tells herself she is protecting them from a version of herself she cannot
currently manage. She is not sure that is entirely untrue.

What troubles Eleanor most, she says, is not the sleeplessness or the images or even the
hypervigilance she now recognises in herself with a certain clinical clarity. What troubles her is
the feeling that she has been carrying this particular weight for sixty-eight years without knowing
it had a shape. She spent decades believing she had simply moved on — that competence and forward
motion were the same thing as healing. She is not angry about this, exactly. She is something
quieter and harder to name. She sits with her hands folded in her lap and looks out the window, and
the expression on her face is one of a woman doing very careful arithmetic.
