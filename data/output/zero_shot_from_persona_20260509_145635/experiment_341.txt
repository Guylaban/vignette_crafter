# The Weight of the Waiting Room

Marcus is a 51-year-old school principal who, by most external measures, appears to be exactly the
kind of man who holds things together. He is the first car in the parking lot each morning, the one
who knows every student's name, the one teachers call when a situation feels impossible. He has
built his professional identity around steadiness. Which is why, he says quietly, what happened
three years ago feels like a particular kind of betrayal — not just of his body, but of everything
he thought he was.

At 48, Marcus was diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia. He had gone to his physician for what he
assumed was persistent fatigue from overwork — he had recently buried his wife of nineteen years,
who died of a stroke, and had thrown himself into his job to survive the grief. The diagnosis
arrived instead. He remembers the oncologist's face before she spoke, the particular stillness of
it, and says that image surfaces without warning still — at faculty meetings, in the cereal aisle,
in the dark before sleep arrives.

The treatment was aggressive. Four rounds of induction chemotherapy, a bone marrow transplant,
months in an isolation room where the window faced a concrete wall. He describes lying in that
hospital bed not knowing whether the nausea or the terror was worse, listening to the sounds of the
ward at night, calculating odds in his head the way a person counts sheep. He had no wife to sit
beside him. His adult daughter flew in when she could, but she had young children, and Marcus told
her — repeatedly, insistently — that he was fine, that she should go home. He spent a great deal of
time alone with the hum of IV machines and the certainty that he was going to die.

He did not die. His oncologist calls him a success story. Marcus uses different language.

Now, three years into remission, he functions — impressively, from the outside. He runs a school of
600 students. He attends every basketball game. He mentors new teachers with genuine care. But he
lives, he says, in a body he no longer fully trusts, in a future he cannot bring himself to picture.
Every three months he sits in the oncology waiting room for bloodwork, and in the forty-eight hours
before each appointment, he stops sleeping. He checks his skin for bruises. He avoids his daughter's
calls because he doesn't want her to hear something in his voice that he cannot name.

At school, he has developed what his assistant principal calls an "intensity" — a hypervigilance
about safety drills, about sick children being sent home, about anything that feels medically
adjacent. A student with a nosebleed in the hallway last fall sent him into his office, door closed,
heart hammering, for twenty minutes before he could compose himself enough to return. He told no
one.

He rarely speaks about the illness to colleagues. When a teacher recently mentioned a family
member's cancer diagnosis, Marcus excused himself from the conversation and stood in the men's room
gripping the sink until the feeling passed. He describes this feeling as a wave — not of sadness
exactly, but of something colder, a sense of the floor dissolving beneath him.

He sleeps four or five hours most nights, wakes from dreams he cannot always recall but that leave
him damp and disoriented. He has started running at five in the morning, partly for his health, he
says, and partly because the movement keeps the thoughts from assembling. He has not been on a date
since his wife died. He tells himself this is loyalty, but in quieter moments he admits it may be
something else — a reluctance to let anyone close to something so fragile, so recently almost gone.

He came to therapy, he says, because his daughter asked him to. He is not sure, he adds, that he has
anything left worth saving. Then he corrects himself, embarrassed. He didn't mean it that way.
