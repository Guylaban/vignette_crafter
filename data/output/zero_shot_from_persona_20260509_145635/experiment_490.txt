# The Weight of Knowing

Martin is a 68-year-old psychologist who has spent four decades helping others make sense of their
inner lives. He sits in the consulting room now on the other side of the desk — a position that
feels both familiar and profoundly disorienting to him. He chose a colleague in a different city
deliberately, someone who would not know him from conferences or shared professional circles. Even
so, he straightens his jacket before speaking, as though the habit of composure might protect him
from what he is about to say.

The relationship with Elise began when Martin was 44. He had been recently divorced, his children
grown, and he describes meeting her as feeling like "a second life opening up." She was charming,
intellectually sharp, and intensely attentive. Within months they were living together. The first
time she struck him, she had been drinking, and she wept afterward with what seemed like genuine
devastation. Martin, who understood trauma responses, who understood cycles of abuse with clinical
precision, told himself it was an isolated event. He did not leave for three years.

During those years, Elise hit him on at least a dozen occasions — open-handed across the face, once
with a ceramic mug that left a gash above his eyebrow he told colleagues he got walking into a
cabinet door. The violence was unpredictable. It could erupt over a misplaced set of keys or a
dinner he had praised too enthusiastically. What Martin found most difficult to articulate, even
now, is not the physical pain but the way he learned to read the atmosphere of a room the moment he
entered it — the particular quality of her silence, the angle of her shoulders — scanning for danger
with a vigilance that has never fully left him.

He ended the relationship after she broke two of his ribs during an argument. He told no one why
they separated. He married his current wife, Ingrid, eight years later, and describes their marriage
as genuinely loving and safe. And yet.

Martin still startles violently when Ingrid raises her voice, even in laughter. He finds himself
frozen in doorways sometimes, taking inventory of the room before stepping in, a habit so automatic
he rarely notices it until Ingrid asks why he is standing there. He has not been able to tolerate
the smell of the particular wine Elise preferred; he once left a colleague's dinner party without
explanation when a bottle appeared on the table. At night, the dreams come with a regularity that
exhausts him — not always the same scene, but always the same quality of dread, the same sensation
of being small and cornered in a body that will not move.

What brings him in now, after more than twenty years, is a moment in his own consulting room three
months ago. A patient — a young man describing his abusive mother — used a phrase Elise had said to
him often: *You made me do this.* Martin felt the floor tilt. He completed the session, he believes,
professionally. Then he sat in his car in the parking structure for forty minutes, unable to
identify what had just happened to him, which is perhaps the most frightening part — that he, of all
people, could not name it.

He carries a particular shame that he identifies as uniquely his: he knows the literature. He has
treated survivors. He understood, intellectually, why people stay, why they minimize, why they
reconstruct memory to preserve dignity. He believed all of it, taught all of it, and still could not
apply it to himself. This knowledge has not softened the shame so much as given it a sharper edge.
He describes feeling like a fraud, though he is careful to say he does not believe his patients are
fraudulent. The logic that protects others does not seem to reach him.

He looks out the window for a moment before adding, quietly, that he thinks he has been waiting
twenty-four years for someone to simply believe him.
