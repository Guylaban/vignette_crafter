# The Weight of the Diagnosis

Marcus is a 35-year-old Filipino-American UX designer who works remotely from the home he shares
with his wife, Diane, in a quiet suburb outside Seattle. He is thoughtful and precise by nature —
someone who has spent his career thinking carefully about how people experience the world around
them. These days, however, the world he is trying to make sense of is almost entirely his own.

Two years ago, Marcus was diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia. He was 33, training for a half-
marathon, and had gone to his doctor only because Diane had noticed some bruising on his arms that
wouldn't fade. He remembers sitting in the hematologist's office, watching the doctor's mouth move,
and feeling as though the sound in the room had been turned down to almost nothing. He drove home
alone. He sat in the driveway for forty minutes before he could go inside.

The months that followed were brutal in ways he still struggles to describe. There were three rounds
of induction chemotherapy, a bone marrow transplant, and a six-week hospitalization during which he
developed a serious infection that put him in the ICU for eleven days. He recalls lying in that bed,
watching the ceiling, genuinely unsure whether he would survive the night. He did not tell Diane how
frightened he was. He did not want her to lose hope.

Marcus achieved remission fourteen months ago. His oncologist calls his recovery remarkable. On
paper, he is well. In practice, he is not.

He wakes most mornings between three and four o'clock, heart hammering, convinced something is wrong
with his body. Sometimes it is a dream — the ICU, the sound of monitors, a nurse he doesn't
recognize telling him something he cannot hear. Other times there is no dream at all, only the
certainty of dread. He lies still and takes inventory of his body the way another person might check
the locks on their doors: his lymph nodes, the skin on his forearms, the faint ache in his lower
back that could mean anything or nothing.

At work, Marcus sits in front of wireframes and user flow diagrams and finds his concentration
dissolving within minutes. He used to love the problem-solving dimension of his work — the way a
well-designed interface could reduce friction and create clarity. Now, when a project requires him
to map out pathways and decision trees, he sometimes finds himself staring at the branching
structures and thinking about all the points at which his own life could have gone differently. He
misses deadlines. He apologizes. He does not explain.

He has become hypervigilant in ways that exhaust both him and Diane. He monitors his body
obsessively — checking his temperature twice a day, cataloguing fatigue, Googling symptoms in the
middle of the night with a specificity that frightens him even as he cannot stop. He avoids
conversations about illness and declines invitations to visit friends who are going through health
challenges, not out of indifference but because proximity to sickness makes him feel as though he is
standing again at the edge of something he barely survived.

Diane has been patient. She is also tired. She tells him gently that he is here now, that he made
it, that the doctors say he is well. He knows she is right. He also knows that knowing something and
feeling it are not the same thing. The body, he has learned, keeps its own records.

What troubles Marcus most is the guilt he carries about his fear. He survived. He is among the
fortunate. He has read about others who were not. And yet he cannot seem to locate himself fully in
the present — cannot quite believe that the crisis has passed, that the ground beneath him is solid,
that he is allowed to stop bracing for what comes next.

He came to therapy, he says, because he is tired of living inside a body he no longer trusts.
