# The Weight of Numbers

Fatima is a 45-year-old auditor who spent three years embedded with a humanitarian accounting team
in a conflict zone, tracking financial flows through active war zones in the Middle East. She was
meticulous, precise, and proud of her work — the kind of person who believed that accountability and
transparency could, in some small way, reduce suffering. That belief ended on a Tuesday morning
seven years ago when a convoy she was traveling in was ambushed on a road outside a provincial city.
Two colleagues were killed in the vehicle directly ahead of hers. Fatima survived by lying beneath
the body of a wounded colleague for nearly two hours, pressing her hands against his side wound
while gunfire continued around them. He lived. She has never fully forgiven herself for the relief
she felt when she finally heard friendly forces arrive.

She returned home to her family in Toronto within weeks, telling almost no one what had happened.
Her marriage, already strained by her long absences, collapsed within a year. Her ex-husband
described her as "gone" even when she was in the room. She did not disagree with him.

Fatima now works for a mid-sized accounting firm, auditing corporate financial statements from a
quiet office where nothing, on the surface, should be dangerous. And yet danger finds her
constantly, in forms that make no rational sense to her. The sound of a staple gun across the office
floor sends her out of her chair. A car backfiring in the parking garage below her building means
she cannot re-enter the elevator for the rest of the afternoon. She takes the stairs. She has
learned the location of every exit in every building she enters, and she sits with her back to the
wall in every meeting room, a habit her colleagues have quietly stopped remarking on.

Sleep is a negotiation she mostly loses. She falls asleep without difficulty — exhaustion sees to
that — but she wakes between two and four in the morning with her heart hammering and the specific
smell of that road in her nose, dust and diesel and something she cannot name and will not try to.
She does not always remember the dream, but her body remembers everything. She showers at three in
the morning more nights than not.

Her work, paradoxically, is where she functions best and worst simultaneously. The precision of
auditing suits her hypervigilance — she catches discrepancies that junior staff miss entirely, and
her supervisors regard her as exceptional. But she cannot delegate. She reviews everything herself
because she cannot tolerate the possibility that something will be missed, that a small error will
compound into catastrophe. She works sixty-hour weeks and calls it professionalism. Some days she
recognizes it for what it actually is: the only way she knows how to feel in control.

She has largely withdrawn from her social world. She declined her sister's wedding invitation last
year because the venue was in a hotel with a single entrance and no clear sightlines. She told her
sister she was traveling for work. She was, in fact, at home, sitting in the dark in her kitchen,
aware of exactly how irrational she was being and completely unable to stop. The shame of that
evening sits in her chest like a stone she cannot put down.

She does not speak about the convoy. She has never spoken about the convoy, not to her ex-husband,
not to her sister, not to her closest friend, who knows only that Fatima "had a hard time overseas."
When the subject of the war in her home region appears on the news, she turns the television off
immediately and goes to do something with her hands — washing dishes, reorganizing files, anything
that requires counting. Numbers, she has discovered, are one of the few things that reliably bring
her back to the present. She counts her steps. She counts her breaths. She counts the lines on a
spreadsheet and feels, briefly, like someone who is still entirely here.
