# Vignette: Margaret

Margaret is a seventy-year-old woman who has managed the same four-star hotel in the city centre for
nearly three decades. She is the kind of person who notices when a flower arrangement is wilting
before any guest does, who remembers the names of regulars and the particular way they take their
coffee. Order and attentiveness have always been the pillars of her professional life, and for a
long time, of her personal life too. She was widowed six years ago, and although the grief was
considerable, she managed it the way she manages most things — with quiet discipline and the comfort
of routine.

That routine shattered fourteen months ago during a public demonstration that spilled into the
street outside her hotel. Margaret had stepped outside to ask a small group of protestors to move
away from the entrance, concerned about her guests. What followed happened quickly and without
warning. A line of riot police advancing along the street did not distinguish her from the crowd.
She was struck across the shoulders with a baton, knocked to the ground, and pinned there by an
officer's knee pressed into her back while she called out that she worked there, that she had
nothing to do with the protest. She lay face-down on the pavement for several minutes before a
colleague rushed out and the officers moved on. She was not arrested. No one checked on her. She
walked back inside, went to her office, and sat behind her desk for an hour before she was able to
speak.

In the weeks that followed, Margaret told herself it was an unfortunate incident, a
misunderstanding, the kind of thing one simply puts behind oneself. She returned to work the next
morning. But her body seemed unwilling to cooperate with this reasoning. She began waking at three
or four in the morning, heart hammering, sheets damp, the weight of the officer's knee as present
and physical as if it were still happening. During the day, any sudden sound — a trolley clattering
in the corridor, a door slamming — would send a jolt of terror through her that she struggled to
conceal from her staff.

The hotel's main entrance, with its glass doors opening onto the street where it happened, became
almost unbearable. She rerouted her morning walk-through to avoid it, delegating the front-of-house
checks she had performed herself every day for twenty-eight years. Her deputy has noticed, though
neither of them has named what is happening. Margaret tells him she is delegating more as she thinks
about retirement, which is not entirely untrue, but not the reason either.

She finds herself unable to trust her own judgment about safety in the way she once did. The hotel
is full of uniforms — security staff, concierge, the occasional police officer accompanying a
dignitary — and she feels a low, persistent dread around all of them now that shames her. She knows,
intellectually, that these are not the same people. The knowledge does not help. She avoids the
evening news. She has stopped attending the industry association dinners she used to enjoy, finding
that crowded public spaces make her feel trapped and scrutinised.

At home, the silence of the flat she shared with her husband feels different now — less like grief
and more like exposure. She sleeps with the television on. She has found herself going over the
incident repeatedly, not to make sense of it, but as though her mind is trying to locate the moment
she could have done something differently, could have been less visible, less present on her own
pavement. The question she returns to most often, lying awake in the small hours, is not what the
officer was thinking, but why, after a lifetime of doing everything correctly, she ended up face-
down on the ground outside her own hotel, and why no one came back to ask if she was alright.
