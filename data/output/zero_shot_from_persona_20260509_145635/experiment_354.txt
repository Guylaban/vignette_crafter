# Vignette: Miriam, 58

Miriam has spent the better part of three decades standing at the front of her congregation, her
voice steady and her presence a source of comfort to others navigating grief, uncertainty, and fear.
She has buried members of her community, counseled couples through crisis, and sat with the dying.
She has always understood herself as someone who could hold difficult things. That understanding
shattered on a wet Thursday morning eighteen months ago, when a logging truck ran a red light and
struck her car broadside at sixty kilometers an hour.

She does not remember the impact itself. What she remembers is waking to the sound of her own voice
— a low, animal sound she did not recognize — and the smell of engine fluid and something burning.
She remembers a stranger's hands reaching through the broken window and a voice telling her not to
move, and she remembers thinking, with extraordinary clarity, *I am going to die here on the side of
this road and no one will know where I am.* She did not die. She sustained a fractured pelvis, three
broken ribs, a collapsed lung, and a laceration across her left forearm that required forty-two
stitches. She was in hospital for eleven days.

The physical injuries healed on the schedule her doctors predicted. The rest has not followed any
schedule she can make sense of.

In the weeks after she returned home to her small apartment in Auckland, Miriam found herself unable
to drive. She told herself this was practical — her car was destroyed, she was still in pain — but
even as a passenger she would grip the door handle until her knuckles whitened, certain that every
intersection was about to become the last thing she saw. She began walking everywhere, adding hours
to her day, exhausted before she arrived anywhere. She stopped taking the motorway entirely.

Sleep has become a kind of combat. She lies down and her body refuses to settle, her heart already
quickening before she closes her eyes. When she does sleep, she returns to the car — not always to
the accident itself, but to the moments just before, the ordinary Tuesday errand quality of it, the
radio playing, and then the terrible wrongness of what came next. She wakes at two or three in the
morning with her nightgown soaked through and a certainty of dread that takes an hour to slowly
dissolve.

What troubles her most, and what she speaks about with the most difficulty, is what has happened to
her relationship with her work. She stands at the bimah on Shabbat and delivers her d'var Torah, and
she can hear herself speaking, can feel the congregation listening, but she has begun to feel like
someone watching herself from a great distance. When congregants come to her with their fears —
illness, loss, the ordinary terror of being alive — she finds herself going through the motions of
presence while something in her has quietly retreated. She has always believed, genuinely and
without performance, that meaning can be found even in suffering. She is no longer sure she believes
this about her own suffering, and the gap between what she says and what she feels has become a
source of private shame she has not yet spoken aloud to anyone.

She is irritable in ways that surprise her. She snapped at her cantor last month over something
trivial and spent the rest of the day flooded with remorse. She has declined three social
invitations from friends she loves. Her ex-husband called to check on her and she kept the
conversation to four minutes, not because she was angry with him but because she could not locate
the energy to be known by anyone.

She sits in the consulting room with her hands folded in her lap, composed and articulate, and says
she is not sure why it is so hard to ask for help. *I tell people all the time that reaching out is
a form of courage,* she says, almost to herself. *I apparently did not believe I was included in
that.*
