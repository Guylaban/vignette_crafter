# The Weight of Hands

Marcus is twenty-four years old, a painter who works out of a small studio apartment in the city,
the walls perpetually smelling of turpentine and linseed oil. He came into therapy quietly, choosing
a seat as far from the door as possible, and spent the first session mostly looking at his hands —
the same hands he uses to build color onto canvas, now often trembling slightly when the
conversation moves toward certain places.

Two years ago, Marcus was in a relationship with a man he describes as magnetic, someone who filled
every room he walked into. They had been together for nearly eighteen months when the violence
began. It did not start with a fist. It started with a shove into a doorframe, followed by an
apology so thorough and tender that Marcus convinced himself it was an aberration. Over the
following six months, the incidents escalated — grabs that left bruises along his forearms, a thrown
ceramic mug that caught him above the ear, and finally, one evening after a disagreement about
money, a sustained beating that left him with a fractured cheekbone and a split lip. A neighbor
called the police. His partner was removed from the apartment. Marcus packed a bag and never went
back.

What Marcus describes from the months immediately following is a kind of internal collapse. He
stopped painting almost entirely. He would stand in front of a blank canvas and feel nothing except
a low, nauseating dread he could not name or locate. He began sleeping with the lights on, not
because he was afraid of the dark exactly, but because waking up in darkness made his heart race in
a way that felt unbearable. He started taking longer routes through the city to avoid the
neighborhood where they had lived together, rerouting his entire day sometimes by forty minutes.

Now, two years later, Marcus is painting again, and he is in a new relationship with a woman he
describes as patient and kind. He wants badly to believe in this. But there are nights when she
reaches for him in the dark and his body goes rigid before his mind catches up. He apologizes
afterward, sometimes crying in a way that embarrasses him, and he finds it nearly impossible to
explain what happened inside him in that moment — only that something old and automatic took over.

At work, the triggers arrive unexpectedly. The sound of something dropped heavily in the studio
above his can send him to the floor of his own space, heart hammering, certain for a split second
that someone is coming through the door. Certain it is him. He has started avoiding paintings that
require deep reds and dark browns — the colors remind him of something he cannot fully articulate
but feels viscerally in his chest.

Marcus carries a particular shame that sits alongside everything else. He is a young Southeast Asian
man, raised in a household where difficulty was absorbed quietly, where masculinity meant endurance.
The idea that he was hurt by a romantic partner — and that it still hurts him, daily — feels to him
like a private indictment. He has told almost no one. His family knows only that the relationship
ended. His new partner knows something happened but not the shape of it.

What he does talk about, carefully and with long pauses, is what he misses most: the version of
himself who painted without thinking, who moved through the world with a kind of loose confidence he
can now barely remember inhabiting. He keeps a photograph of one of his early paintings on his phone
— bright, almost reckless with color — and sometimes he looks at it like a person looking at a
photograph of someone they used to know.

He is here, he says, because he wants to find his way back to that. He is not sure yet whether he
believes it is possible.
