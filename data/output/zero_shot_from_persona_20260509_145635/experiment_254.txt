# The Weight of Pages

Kofi is a 78-year-old Ghanaian man who has worked for nearly four decades as a librarian at a
university in the city where he settled after emigrating in his forties. He is soft-spoken and
precise in the way he uses language, a man who has always found comfort in the orderly world of
catalogued knowledge. His wife of fifty-one years, Abena, describes him as someone who once moved
through life with a quiet steadiness. That steadiness shattered three years ago, and it has not
returned.

Kofi was present during a bombing attack at a crowded open-air market on a visit back to Accra to
attend a family funeral. He had stepped away from the group to buy plantains when the device
detonated near the market's central entrance. He remembers the sound before anything else — not an
explosion the way he had imagined explosions, but something that seemed to swallow all other sound
first, a terrible silence and then everything at once. He was thrown to the ground. When he raised
his head, he saw things he will not yet name aloud in session. Several people he had spoken with
moments before were dead. A young woman selling fabric had been standing no more than four meters
from him. He crawled toward her before bystanders pulled him away. He sustained lacerations to his
hands and a perforated eardrum. He flew home to his wife eight days later, and he has not spoken of
what he saw at the market to anyone in his family.

Back in the library, colleagues noticed the change almost immediately. Kofi had always been the
person who remembered where everything belonged. Now he loses his place mid-sentence. He stands in
the stacks sometimes and cannot recall why he walked there. Loud sounds in the building — a door
slamming, a cart of books tipping — send him pressing his back against the nearest shelf, heart
hammering, certain for several seconds that something catastrophic is happening again. He has begun
arriving before the library opens so he can identify every exit before patrons arrive. He counts the
exits each morning. He does this without fully realizing he is doing it.

At home, Kofi sleeps in fragments. He wakes most nights between two and four in the morning with his
heart already racing, the images from the market assembling themselves behind his eyes before he is
fully conscious. He does not wake Abena. He goes to the sitting room and reads until dawn, though he
often finds that he has read the same page four or five times without absorbing a word. Reading,
which was once his deepest pleasure, now feels like trying to hold water in cupped hands.

He has become careful about where he sits in restaurants — always with his back to the wall, always
near a door. He stopped attending his church congregation after a visiting choir performed with
drums, and he found himself on his feet and moving toward the exit before he understood what his
body was doing. He told Abena he had a headache. He has told her many things that are not quite
true, because the true things feel unspeakable.

What troubles Kofi most, and what he admits only haltingly, is a feeling he describes as being
hollowed out. He says he used to feel things — joy at a grandchild's visit, satisfaction at helping
a student find a source, tenderness toward Abena in small moments. Now he watches those moments from
a distance, as though he is observing someone else's life through glass. He loves his wife, he says
carefully, but he cannot seem to reach her the way he once could. He sits across from her at dinner
and feels, to his shame and bewilderment, profoundly alone.

He came to therapy, he explains, because Abena asked him to. He is not certain, he says, that
anything can be put back the way it was. But he came.
