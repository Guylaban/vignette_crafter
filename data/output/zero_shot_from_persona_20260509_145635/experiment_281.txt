# Marta

Marta is a 74-year-old woman who has spent the better part of five decades helping other people move
through pain. As a physical therapist, she has always understood the body's capacity to carry what
the mind cannot easily name. She knows about muscle memory, about the way tension lodges itself in
tissue long after the original injury has healed. What she did not fully reckon with, until
recently, is how thoroughly that principle applies to her own life.

She was eleven years old when her family left Hungary in the winter of 1956, in the weeks following
the Soviet crackdown on the uprising. She remembers the cold most of all — a particular, merciless
cold that came up through the ground as the family moved on foot through fields at the edge of the
Austrian border. Her father carried her younger brother. Her mother held her hand so tightly that
Marta's fingers went numb, and she was not sure for a long time whether it was the frost or the
grip. There were other people moving alongside them in the dark, strangers who had become temporary
family by necessity. At one point, there were shouts somewhere behind them, and everyone dropped to
the ground without a word, faces pressed into the frozen earth, and Marta understood without being
told that making a sound might mean they would not survive the night.

They did survive. They made it to Vienna, and eventually to England, and Marta built a life that by
most measures has been full and fortunate. She married a kind man named Geoffrey, raised two
children, and found genuine meaning in her work. For decades, the memories of that night existed as
a kind of sealed room inside her — present, but not entered.

Then, two winters ago, her granddaughter showed her a news broadcast. Families at a European border,
night footage, people moving through cold and darkness with children in their arms. Marta watched
for perhaps forty seconds before she had to leave the room. She told Geoffrey she felt unwell, which
was true, though not in any way she could immediately explain. That night she did not sleep. She lay
in the dark and felt the cold from 1956 in her hands, felt the grip of her mother's fingers, heard
sounds that were not in the room.

Since then, the sealed room has been open. She finds herself startled by things that should not
startle her — a door closing firmly, a car backfiring in the street outside the clinic. She notices
that she scans exits when she enters unfamiliar spaces, something she has apparently always done but
only recently begun to observe in herself. She dreams of the border crossing several times a week,
and in the dreams she is always eleven, always cold, and the shouts behind her are always getting
closer.

At work, she remains competent and present with her patients — that part of her, she says, has
always felt safe. But she finds it harder to concentrate on documentation afterward, and she
sometimes sits in her car in the car park for longer than she intends, not quite ready to re-enter
the world. She has told Geoffrey some of this, and he listens with the patience she has always loved
in him, but she can see that he does not entirely know what to do with it, and she does not entirely
blame him.

What troubles her most, she says quietly, is the shame attached to it. She is seventy-four years
old. She has a profession built on understanding how the body heals. She has had, she insists, a
good life. She cannot quite account for why a television broadcast should undo something she
believed she had long since resolved. She speaks about the eleven-year-old girl in the field with a
kind of careful distance, as though she is describing someone she once knew but lost track of — and
perhaps that is, in some sense, exactly what happened.
