# The Weight of the Altar

Sister Margaret is forty-four years old and has spent the better part of two decades walking
alongside people in their darkest moments — hospital bedsides, prison corridors, hospice rooms. As a
chaplain, she has always understood suffering as something she could hold with steady hands. That
understanding shattered three years ago on a Tuesday morning in October, when a coordinated bombing
tore through the downtown civic center where she had been leading an interfaith memorial service.

She remembers the smell first — something chemical and wrong — and then the sound, which she says
was not loud so much as *total*, a pressure that erased everything else. She was standing at the
lectern when the first device detonated in the lobby. She was thrown sideways. When she came to, she
was lying across a pew, and the room was filled with a gray-white dust and the sounds of people she
had been praying with moments before. She crawled toward a woman whose name she later learned was
Dolores. Dolores did not survive. Margaret held her hand until the paramedics arrived, and she has
not been able to let go of that hand since, not really.

In the months that followed, Margaret told herself she was fine. She was a chaplain — she knew
grief, she knew trauma, she knew the language of healing. She returned to work within three weeks.
She sat with families in crisis. She administered last rites. She held it together, until she
didn't.

The collapse came gradually and then all at once. She began waking at three in the morning with her
heart hammering, certain she could smell smoke. She started avoiding the downtown corridor entirely,
rerouting her commute by forty minutes rather than pass within two blocks of the civic center.
Crowds became intolerable. She resigned from a hospital chaplaincy position because the PA system —
the particular flat tone of it — sent her into a cold sweat she could not explain to her supervisor
without falling apart. She stopped attending Mass, which she describes as the deepest loss, because
the incense now triggers something in her body that her mind cannot override.

What troubles Margaret most is what happens when she tries to pray with patients. She will be mid-
sentence, holding someone's hand in a dim hospital room, and without warning she is back on the
floor of the civic center, the dust in her throat, Dolores's fingers going slack. She has learned to
disguise these moments — a slow breath, a pause framed as contemplation — but inside she is
somewhere else entirely, and she carries a profound shame about that. *I'm supposed to be the one
who stays present*, she says.

She has become hypervigilant in ways that exhaust her. She scans every room she enters. She sits
with her back to the wall. She has memorized the exit routes of every building where she regularly
works. She sleeps with the light on. She has stopped inviting people to her apartment because she
cannot tolerate the unpredictability of another person's movements in her space.

Socially, she has retreated. She was never someone with a wide circle, but she had close friends —
fellow chaplains, women from her parish. She answers their texts with brief reassurances and
declines most invitations. She tells herself she is protecting them from something she cannot name.
The truth, she admits quietly, is that she no longer trusts the world to hold still.

What keeps her going, she says, is the work itself — the moments when she manages to be genuinely
present with someone dying, when the training and the vocation break through the noise. But she is
aware, with the particular clarity of someone who counsels others through denial every day, that she
is running on something that is slowly being depleted. She came to therapy, she explains, not
because she believes she deserves help, but because she is afraid of becoming useless to the people
who need her. That, she says, would be the real devastation.
