# The Weight of Crossing

Kofi is a 77-year-old Ghanaian man who has lived in the United States for nearly three decades. He
sits in the therapy room with a deliberate stillness, his hands folded in his lap, his speech
measured and precise — habits, he says, that served him well through forty years of writing code. He
retired from software engineering five years ago, and it is in that retirement, in the quiet that
replaced the structured rhythms of work, that the past has found its way back to him.

In 1994, Kofi fled his home country during a period of severe political violence. He was in his late
forties then — already a man with a career, a wife, two teenage children, and a small house with a
garden his wife tended carefully. Within the span of a single week, that life collapsed. Armed men
came to their neighborhood. Kofi witnessed the killing of his neighbor and close friend, a man who
had shared meals at his table for twenty years. He watched it happen from behind a door he did not
open. That detail — the door he did not open — is something he returns to again and again, though he
does not always say it aloud. He and his family fled that same night with what they could carry,
eventually spending fourteen months in a refugee camp in Togo before resettlement brought them to
the United States.

For years, Kofi managed. He learned new systems, new languages of both code and culture. He found
work at a technology firm, threw himself into the logic and problem-solving that software demanded,
and built a second life with his wife, Abena, who remained his steadying presence. Work gave him
structure. Deadlines, debugging, the clean satisfaction of a program running correctly — these
things occupied the parts of his mind that might otherwise have wandered back.

Retirement removed that occupation. Now the mornings are long. He wakes before dawn, often from
dreams he cannot fully recall but that leave him with a heaviness he carries through the day. He
does not tell Abena about the dreams. He does not want her to worry, he says, and then pauses,
correcting himself quietly — he does not want to speak the things aloud and make them more real than
they already are.

He startles at sounds that do not bother other people: a car backfiring, a neighbor's raised voice,
the particular quality of silence that falls before a storm. He has begun avoiding the news
entirely, not because he does not care, but because images of displacement and violence — anywhere
in the world — send him somewhere he struggles to return from. He sat with his grandchildren last
winter watching a film that contained a crowd scene and found himself gripping the armrest, his
heart loud in his chest, certain for a moment that he was back in the camp, that the years between
then and now had been a dream.

He describes a persistent sense of being watched, even in his own home. He checks the locks twice
before bed. He has rearranged the furniture in his study so that his desk faces the door. These are
small adjustments, he says, practical ones — though he acknowledges, with the precision of a man
trained to identify errors in complex systems, that they may not be entirely rational.

What troubles him most is not the fear itself but the shame that accompanies it. He is a
grandfather, a husband of fifty years, a man who built something from nothing in a country not his
own. He believes he should be past this. He thinks of his neighbor's family, of what they lost, and
feels that his own suffering is a kind of theft — an indulgence he has not earned.

honesty he once brought to his work. He is trying, he says, to debug something he cannot quite see.
