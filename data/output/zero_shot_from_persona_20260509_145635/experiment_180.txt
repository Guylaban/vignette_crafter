# Case Vignette: Marcus

Marcus is a 33-year-old graphic designer of Dutch descent who lives alone in a one-bedroom apartment
on the edge of the city. He is lean, quietly articulate, and chooses his words carefully — a habit,
he explains, that developed after learning how badly things can go when a situation is misread. He
has been divorced for just over a year. His ex-wife, Lena, left shortly after what he refers to
simply as "the night everything changed." He does not blame her. He says this without bitterness,
which somehow makes it sadder.

Three years ago, Marcus was walking home from a late client meeting, carrying his laptop bag and
talking on his phone. It was past midnight in a neighborhood he had lived in for six years. Two
plainclothes officers approached him rapidly from behind, identified themselves only after grabbing
him, and forced him to the ground. There had been a series of break-ins in the area, and Marcus —
tall, dark-coated, carrying a bag — apparently matched a vague description. He was handcuffed,
pressed face-down against the pavement, and held there for nearly twenty minutes while officers
searched his bag and ran his details. He was not charged with anything. He was told he was free to
go. No one apologized. He walked the remaining six blocks home, let himself in, sat on the bathroom
floor, and did not move for two hours.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus told himself he was fine. He went to work. He met deadlines. He
is good at his job — his studio specializes in brand identity and editorial design, and he has
always found a kind of safety in the precision of visual structure, the way a well-composed layout
holds everything in its right place. But the work began to feel hollow. He started missing details
he would never have missed before. His concentration, once his sharpest professional tool, had
become unreliable and porous.

Sleep is where things unravel most completely. He wakes most nights between two and four in the
morning, heart hammering, the full weight of a man's knee pressed into the back of his neck as vivid
as the moment it happened. He has learned not to fight the waking, but he has not learned what to do
with the hours that follow. He lies in the dark and listens to the street outside, tracking every
sound.

He stopped walking home at night almost immediately after the incident. Then he stopped walking home
in the evening. Now he takes rideshares even for short distances, and he has quietly rearranged his
entire daily geography to avoid the block where it happened. He knows this. He calls it "routing
around the problem," the way a designer works around a constraint. He says it with a small, self-
aware smile that does not reach his eyes.

His relationship with authority has fundamentally reorganized itself. He becomes acutely watchful in
the presence of police officers — hypervigilant in a way that exhausts him and that he recognizes as
disproportionate and yet cannot modulate. At a friend's wedding last spring, an officer was
directing traffic outside the venue. Marcus stood on the steps for several minutes, unable to make
himself walk past. He eventually went back inside and told Lena — they were still together then —
that he had forgotten something. She knew. She had been watching him shrink for months.

He describes his emotional life now as "running at low resolution." He feels present enough to
function but rarely enough to connect. He has stopped seeing most of his friends. He finds it
difficult to explain what happened without feeling the particular shame of having done nothing wrong
and still feeling responsible. He sketches constantly in the margins of his notebooks — tight
geometric patterns, interlocking grids — and when asked what they mean, he pauses for a long time
before saying he thinks they are about trying to make something that cannot be broken into.
