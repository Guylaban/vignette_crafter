# Vignette: Amara, 25-Year-Old Surgical Resident

Amara grew up in Nairobi, the eldest daughter of a physician father who spoke often about service
and sacrifice. When she was accepted into a military-sponsored medical training program at twenty-
two, she understood the terms — two years of service in an active conflict zone in exchange for her
surgical residency. She arrived at the field hospital in the northeastern region with her textbooks
still dog-eared, her hands steady, her ambition intact.

The event that changed everything happened fourteen months into her deployment. A convoy attack
brought in seventeen casualties within forty minutes. Amara had been the most senior medical officer
present when the helicopters landed. She worked for eleven hours straight, making triage decisions
that she understood, even in the moment, meant choosing who would live and who would not. A soldier
she had treated twice before — a young man named Daud, who had shown her photographs of his daughter
— was placed in the expectant category. She remembers walking past him twice. She remembers that he
was still conscious. He died before the second surgical team arrived.

Amara returned to civilian life eight months ago, completing the final leg of her residency at a
large urban hospital. On paper, she is exceptional. Her attending surgeons describe her focus in the
operating theatre as almost preternatural. What they do not see is that her focus is not calm — it
is controlled terror. Before every procedure, she runs through scenarios in which she makes the
wrong call. She arrives forty minutes early and checks equipment that has already been checked. When
a case involves blast or penetrating trauma, her chest tightens in a way she has learned to breathe
through quietly, because she cannot afford to be pulled from a case.

Outside the hospital, she manages less well. She lives alone in a small flat and keeps the
television on through the night because silence has a particular quality she cannot tolerate — it
sounds, she says, like the moment after the helicopters shut down. She sleeps in fragments of two or
three hours. When she does sleep deeply, she wakes with her pulse already racing, the images from
the field hospital vivid and immediate, Daud's face among them. She does not always know, in the
first seconds of waking, that she is in her flat and not in the tent.

She has stopped returning to the gym she used to love because the locker room — the smell of it,
something industrial and close — triggers a sudden, nauseating sense that she is back there. She
cancelled plans with her closest friend three times in the past two months, finding that social
gatherings require a kind of emotional availability she cannot locate. She describes herself as
"behind glass" around people she cares about, watching them enjoy themselves and feeling genuinely
puzzled by her own distance.

Amara does not think of herself as someone who is struggling. She thinks of herself as someone who
is managing. She makes this distinction carefully and often. She has told no one at the hospital
what she saw during deployment, partly because she fears it will alter how her competence is
perceived, and partly because she is not sure she has the language for it yet. What she knows is
that she chose surgery because she believed in the possibility of intervening — of arriving in time.
The memory of Daud has become, for her, a kind of proof against that belief, and she carries it the
way she carries everything now: quietly, efficiently, and entirely alone.
