# The Weight of the Crossing

Rabbi Daniel Stern is sixty-three years old, a man whose voice still carries the particular warmth
of someone who has spent decades offering comfort to others. He sits in the consulting room with his
hands folded in his lap, dressed carefully, and speaks with measured precision — until he doesn't.
Until something slips through.

Daniel was born in the Soviet Union and came of age in a Jewish community that practiced its faith
in whispers and locked rooms. When he was thirty-one, the political situation deteriorated sharply
for Jewish families in his region. There were threats, then violence — a neighbor's home burned, a
community leader beaten in the street. Daniel made the decision quickly, the way he describes all
the decisions from that period: *there was no time to think, only to move.* He took his then-wife
and their two young children and fled, joining a larger group attempting to cross into Western
Europe through a route that was neither safe nor legal. The crossing took eleven days. There was a
period — three days — when the group was detained by border guards in a country he declines to name.
The guards were not kind. Daniel watched a man in their group beaten badly enough that he could not
walk afterward. He was separated from his family for thirty-six hours and was certain, with a
completeness he has never been able to fully describe, that he would never see his children again.
He eventually reached safety, eventually rebuilt. He became a rabbi in a mid-sized American city. He
remarried — his current partner, Miriam, is patient and perceptive. His children are grown. By any
external measure, he has constructed a life of profound meaning.

But the thirty-six hours never left him.

It surfaces most reliably at night. Daniel sleeps poorly, waking between two and four in the morning
with his heart already racing, the residue of dreams he can sometimes recall and sometimes cannot.
When he does remember them, they are not always literal replays — sometimes it is simply the
sensation of walls, of smallness, of his children's faces receding. He gets up rather than lie
there. Miriam has learned not to ask immediately; she waits until morning.

During the day, he functions — more than functions. His congregants find him deeply present, and he
is. But there are specific textures to the world that reach back and grab him without warning. The
sound of boots on a hard floor. A certain quality of fluorescent light. Men in uniforms of any kind,
even airport security, even parking enforcement. His body responds before his mind has time to
intervene — a cold flooding sensation in his chest, a sudden and irrational certainty that something
terrible is about to happen to someone he loves.

He has never spoken about this from the bimah. He has spoken around it — about exile, about the
obligation to welcome the stranger, about the theology of displacement — but the personal center of
it remains sealed. He carries a quiet shame about this, which he articulates carefully: *I counsel
people through grief and fear every week. I should be able to manage this.*

What troubles him most presently is what he calls his *vigilance*. He cannot sit in a restaurant
with his back to the door. He monitors exits in every room he enters. When his adult son travels
internationally, Daniel checks his phone compulsively until he receives confirmation of safe
arrival. Miriam has gently noted that he seems more tightly wound than he used to be, not less —
that the years have not softened it. He agrees, and the agreement costs him something.

He came to therapy, he says, because he is aware that he has been carrying something for thirty-two
years that he has never set down, and he is beginning to wonder, at sixty-three, whether he is
allowed to.
