**Vignette: Meena**

Meena is a 66-year-old South Asian woman who has worked as a security guard at a large shopping
centre for nearly twelve years. She is married, the mother of three adult children, and by her own
description, has always been the steady one in her family — the person others call when something
goes wrong. That self-image began to fracture three years ago, on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon,
when her youngest son was struck by a car while crossing the street near his workplace. He survived,
but the injuries were severe: a fractured spine, months of rehabilitation, and a long, uncertain
road back to partial mobility. Meena was the first family member to arrive at the hospital. She
remembers the exact colour of the waiting room chairs, the way a nurse looked at her before saying
anything, and the sound her own voice made when she asked if her son was going to die.

In the weeks that followed, she functioned. She had to — there were hospital visits to coordinate,
her husband's grief to manage, her son's children to help care for. But something had shifted
beneath the surface in ways she did not yet have words for. She began replaying the phone call she
had received that day, the drive to the hospital, the image she had constructed in her mind of the
accident itself — a scene she had not witnessed but which felt, somehow, as vivid and intrusive as a
memory. She would be standing at her post near the shopping centre entrance, watching cars move
through the car park, and suddenly her chest would tighten and her hands would go cold. She started
positioning herself away from the automatic doors that faced the main road, telling her supervisor
she preferred the interior posts. She did not explain why.

Sleep became difficult almost immediately. She lies awake most nights, her mind returning unbidden
to the hospital, to the image of her son's face when she first saw him in the ICU, to the thought
that she could have lost him. When she does sleep, she often wakes abruptly, heart pounding, from
dreams she cannot always recall but that leave her feeling as though something terrible is happening
right now, in this moment. Her husband notices that she startles easily — a door closing too hard, a
car horn from the street — and has learned not to approach her from behind.

Meena describes a persistent low-level dread that she cannot quite locate or name. She finds herself
monitoring her phone constantly during her shifts, needing to know where her children are. She has
asked her son, now living more independently again, to text her when he arrives anywhere safely.
When he forgets, she does not call him — she does not want to seem overbearing — but the anxiety
builds quietly until she hears from him. She has stopped attending the community events at her local
temple that she used to enjoy, finding crowds and noise harder to tolerate than they once were. She
tells herself she is simply tired, that this is what getting older feels like.

What troubles her most, she says in quieter moments, is that she no longer feels like herself. She
used to take pride in being unshakeable. Now she notices that she is irritable with her husband over
small things, that she feels distant from the grandchildren she adores, that there is a kind of
flatness to days that used to feel full. She describes watching her son walk across a room during a
recent family dinner and feeling, unexpectedly, a grief so sharp it took her breath away — not
because anything was wrong in that moment, but because she understands now, in her body, how quickly
everything can change. She has not told anyone in her family how she is feeling. She does not want
to make the trauma about herself.
