# The Weight of Helplessness

Rajan is a 58-year-old South Asian man who has spent the better part of three decades rendering safe
devices that could kill people. He is methodical, calm under pressure, and trained to function in
conditions that would paralyze most human beings. None of that prepared him for what happened to his
daughter.

Seven years ago, Rajan's youngest child, Priya, then nine years old, was diagnosed with acute
lymphoblastic leukemia. The diagnosis came quickly, but the illness moved faster. Within weeks of
the first symptoms — a bruise that wouldn't fade, a fatigue that sleep couldn't touch — Priya was in
a pediatric intensive care unit, septic, her small body failing in ways that no one could fully
predict or control. Rajan stood at the glass of her room in full PPE, watching monitors he didn't
understand, unable to touch her, unable to do anything at all. He describes that period,
particularly one night when the medical team called a rapid response and asked him and his wife to
step back, as the moment something inside him "broke open and never closed again."

Priya survived. She is now sixteen, healthy, and doing well in school. Rajan knows this. He reminds
himself of it often, sometimes aloud, as though repetition might finally make it feel true.

It doesn't.

He still wakes most nights between two and four in the morning. The content of the dreams varies,
but the feeling is always the same: he is watching something terrible happen and he cannot stop it.
Sometimes it is Priya. Sometimes it is a device he cannot identify in time. The boundary between the
two fears has, over the years, grown thin. During the day, if his phone rings and he doesn't
recognize the number, his chest tightens immediately. He has learned to answer quickly, before his
mind can finish the thought it always starts.

At work, colleagues describe Rajan as one of the steadiest people they have ever encountered. He is
known for his precision, his patience, and his almost eerie composure in the field. What they don't
see is that when a job involves a school or a hospital — any building where children might be — he
spends the drive home sitting in the car park outside his house for twenty minutes before he can go
inside. He tells his wife, Meena, that he is finishing paperwork.

Meena knows he is not finishing paperwork. She has learned not to ask.

Rajan finds it difficult to talk about Priya's illness with anyone, including Meena, who was there
for all of it and carries her own grief from that time. When the family gathers and someone mentions
how far Priya has come, Rajan smiles and excuses himself. He describes feeling like a man watching
his own life through a window — present in form, absent in some way he cannot name. He takes
pleasure in very little. He still follows cricket, but more out of habit than feeling.

What troubles him most, and what he mentions almost reluctantly, is the shame. He is a man who has
walked toward live explosives without flinching. He has held his nerve in situations that have
broken other people. And yet he could not protect his child from illness. He stood in a corridor and
did nothing, because there was nothing to do, and that helplessness — total, unfamiliar, and
humiliating — is the thing that will not leave him. He understands, on some level, that he was not
supposed to be able to fix it. He understands it the way he understands many things: intellectually,
at a distance, without it touching him where it needs to.

He came to therapy, he says, because Priya asked him to. He would not have come otherwise. He is not
sure he deserves to still be struggling, when she is the one who was sick.
