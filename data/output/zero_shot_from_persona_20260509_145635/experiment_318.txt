**Vignette: Margaret**

Margaret is a sixty-six-year-old widowed woman who has worked as a model for most of her adult life
— primarily in commercial and print work, the kind that kept her busy well into her sixties through
catalogue shoots and lifestyle campaigns targeting older demographics. She carries herself with a
practiced composure that, she admits, has become harder to maintain over the past two years.

Two years ago, Margaret was driving home from an evening shoot in the city. It was late autumn, the
roads were wet, and she was tired — not impaired, she is careful to say, just tired in the way that
accumulates after a long day under studio lights. At an intersection she knew well, she misjudged
the speed of an oncoming cyclist and turned left. The impact was immediate and terrible. The
cyclist, a man in his thirties, sustained a traumatic brain injury and spent weeks in intensive
care. He survived, but Margaret learned through the legal proceedings that he has not fully
recovered. He walks with difficulty now. He left behind the job he had before the accident.

Margaret was not charged criminally. Everyone — her attorney, the insurance representatives, even
the cyclist's family in their measured, devastating way — acknowledged it was an accident. This, she
says quietly, is almost the worst part. There is no verdict that accounts for what she carries.

In the weeks after the accident, she found herself unable to sleep past three or four in the
morning. She would lie in the dark in the house she has shared with no one since her husband died
five years ago, and the sound of the impact would return to her — not as a memory she was choosing
to revisit, but as something that arrived without permission. She stopped driving entirely within a
month. She tells herself she takes taxis and rideshares for convenience, but she knows better.

Her work has suffered in ways that are difficult to explain to people outside the industry. Modeling
requires a certain presence — an ability to inhabit the moment in front of a camera, to project ease
and aliveness. Margaret finds herself dissociated during shoots now, going through the motions with
a professionalism that her body remembers even when her mind is elsewhere. Photographers
occasionally ask if she is all right. She always says yes.

She avoids the intersection where the accident occurred, which means she has reorganized her entire
geography of the city. She takes longer routes, plans trips in advance so she is never a passenger
in a car that might pass that block. When she occasionally catches herself near it — when a driver
takes an unexpected turn — she feels her chest constrict and her hands go cold.

What troubles her most, she says, is not the fear but the guilt, which has settled into her like
weather. She finds herself scanning news articles about the cyclist, searching his name occasionally
despite knowing it does no good. She has written letters to him that she has not sent. She wonders,
with a regularity that exhausts her, what kind of person she must be to have done this — even
accidentally, even in the rain, even tired after a long day of work that felt, in retrospect, so
inconsequential.

She has withdrawn from most of her social world. The friends she and her husband cultivated together
have drifted, and she has not worked to close the distance. She attends a grief support group she
initially joined after her husband's death, but she does not speak about the accident there. She is
not sure those losses belong in the same room.

Margaret presents as articulate and self-aware, with a tendency to intellectualize her experience —
to describe what happened with precision while keeping her affect carefully managed. Beneath that
management, however, there is something raw and unresolved: a woman who has not yet found a way to
live alongside what she did, even knowing she did not mean to.
