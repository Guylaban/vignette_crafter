# The Weight of Evidence

Roberto is a 58-year-old Colombian-born forensic analyst who has spent more than three decades
making sense of chaos — cataloguing, measuring, and documenting the aftermath of violence so that
courts and investigators can find meaning in what remains. He is methodical by nature, a man who has
always found comfort in procedure. His colleagues describe him as unshakeable. His wife, Marisol,
uses the same word, though lately she says it with less certainty.

Fourteen months ago, Roberto was part of a specialized response team deployed to a industrial
chemical plant explosion in the outskirts of the city — a disaster later determined to have been
caused by deliberate negligence on the part of plant management, who had falsified safety records
for years. The explosion killed thirty-one workers. Roberto arrived in the second wave, when the
fires had been partially contained but the site was still unstable, the air thick with particulate
and the smell of burning synthetic material. His job was to document and collect physical evidence
before the scene degraded further. He had worked disaster scenes before. He told himself this was no
different.

It was different. Midway through his documentation work, a secondary structural collapse occurred
without warning. Roberto was thrown to the ground by the concussive force. When he looked up, two
colleagues were buried under debris less than twenty feet away. He was uninjured. They were not. He
spent the next forty minutes helping extract them before emergency crews arrived, his hands working
mechanically while his mind registered details he could not stop registering — details that, as a
forensic analyst, he was trained never to look away from. Both colleagues survived, though one lost
a hand. Roberto finished his shift.

He did not tell Marisol what had happened until three weeks later, when she found him sitting in the
kitchen at 2 a.m., unable to explain why.

Sleep has become unreliable territory. Roberto lies down each night beside his wife and waits,
knowing that at some point the images will come — not as dreams exactly, but as a kind of
involuntary replay that feels more like reliving than remembering. The smell is always part of it.
At work, certain chemical compounds used in evidence processing can trigger it without warning. He
has learned to identify which ones and to leave the room before the sensation fully takes hold,
offering his younger colleagues technical explanations for why he is stepping out. He has become
skilled at this kind of quiet management.

What troubles him more than the nights is what has happened to his concentration. Roberto built his
professional identity on precision, on the ability to hold complex information in careful order. Now
he finds himself re-reading the same paragraph of a lab report, losing the thread of his own
analysis mid-sentence. He has begun double-checking his work in ways he never needed to before,
arriving early to review documentation he completed the day before, afraid of errors he cannot
afford to make. He has not told his supervisor.

At home, Roberto is present in the way that a man can be present without quite being there. He and
Marisol have dinner together most evenings. He asks about her day. He is not unkind. But there is a
distance she has named and he has not disputed — a kind of glass wall that went up after the
explosion and has not come down. On weekends he tends the small garden behind their house with a
focus that Marisol recognizes as something other than enjoyment. He is managing himself, she says.
He agrees, though he would not have used that word six months ago.

What Roberto returns to most often, in the quiet moments he cannot fill with work or routine, is a
question that has no forensic answer: he was the one standing in the right place. His colleagues
were in the wrong place. There was no variable he could have measured that would have predicted it.
For a man whose life's work depends on evidence leading somewhere, this remains the most
destabilizing fact of all.
