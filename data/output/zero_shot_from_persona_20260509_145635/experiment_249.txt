**Vignette: Mae**

Mae is a 52-year-old woman of Thai descent who has lived in the United States for nearly two
decades. She is married, the mother of three grown children, and works the morning shift at a fast
food restaurant not far from the apartment she shares with her husband. To her coworkers, she
appears quiet and dependable — the woman who always arrives early, who rarely takes a full lunch
break, who keeps her eyes down and her hands moving. What they do not know is that Mae has not slept
through the night in almost three years.

In the winter of 2022, Mae traveled back to her home province in Thailand to visit her elderly
mother. She had saved for the trip for months. On her third day there, a flash flood struck the
rural valley where her mother's house sat. The water came without adequate warning — a wall of
brown, churning force that swallowed the road, the neighbor's fence, the low wall of her mother's
garden in what felt like seconds. Mae managed to pull her mother onto a table and then onto the roof
of the house, where they clung together for nearly eleven hours in the dark and the rain, listening
to the sound of the flood taking apart the world below them. A neighbor's child did not survive. Mae
watched the recovery of the small body the following morning from across what had been the street.

She returned to the United States two weeks later, telling herself and her husband that she was fine
— that they were safe, that her mother was safe, and that was what mattered. She went back to work.
She smiled at customers. She restocked the napkin dispensers and called out order numbers and told
herself she was grateful.

But gratitude did not stop the dreams. Almost every night, Mae finds herself back on that roof — the
cold, the dark, the sound of the water. She wakes drenched in sweat, her heart slamming, sometimes
crying out in a way that frightens her husband. She lies awake after that, listening to the silence
of the apartment and reassuring herself, over and over, that the floor beneath her is solid. Some
mornings she gets up at three or four o'clock and sits in the kitchen with the lights on, unable to
tolerate the dark.

At work, certain things undo her without warning. The sound of the industrial sink running at full
pressure. Rain against the restaurant's large front windows. Once, a coworker accidentally knocked
over a mop bucket and the water spreading across the tile floor sent Mae into the back room, where
she stood shaking against the wall for several minutes, unable to explain herself when her manager
came to check on her. She told him she felt dizzy. She has become skilled at small lies.

Mae finds herself unable to watch the news, particularly anything involving storms or flooding. She
has quietly stopped calling her mother as often as she used to, because her mother lives in that
valley still, and thinking about the valley brings the roof back — the cold, the dark, the sound of
the child's name being called by the child's mother in the hours before dawn. She carries that sound
with her constantly, as though it has been threaded into the fabric of her ordinary days.

Her husband has noticed that she flinches at sudden sounds, that she has grown withdrawn, that she
sometimes stares at nothing during dinner. He has asked her gently what is wrong. She tells him she
is tired. She is not wrong about that. She is tired in a way that sleep — when it comes — does not
touch. She feels older than 52, and she feels, in some way she cannot fully articulate even to
herself, that the woman who climbed onto that roof in the rain is not entirely the same woman who
came back down.
