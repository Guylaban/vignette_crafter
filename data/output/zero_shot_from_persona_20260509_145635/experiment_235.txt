# Clinical Vignette: Kofi, Age 50

Kofi has been a plumber for nearly twenty-five years. He knows how to read a building — the way
pipes run hidden behind walls, the pressure that builds unseen until something gives. These days, he
thinks about pressure a lot differently than he used to.

Eighteen months ago, Kofi was working late on a commercial job in an unfamiliar part of the city. He
had stayed behind to finish a stubborn fitting while his crew left for the evening. Walking to his
van in the parking structure, he was approached by two men. What followed happened fast — a demand
for his tools and his keys, a refusal born of twenty-five years of hard work, and then a beating so
severe that he was found an hour later by a security guard, his face unrecognizable, two ribs
cracked, and his right hand — his working hand — fractured in three places.

He spent four days in hospital. His wife, Adwoa, sat beside him every night. He told her he was
fine. He told himself the same thing.

He was back on the job in six weeks, earlier than his doctor advised. He needed to work. He needed
to feel like himself again. But the man who returned to work was not quite the same man who had
left.

The first thing Kofi notices, every single morning, is whether it is still dark outside. He will not
leave the house before sunrise. He tells Adwoa it is about traffic, about timing, and she does not
push him, though he can see in her face that she knows. At job sites, he positions himself so that
his back is always to a wall. He has developed a habit of scanning every room he enters — doors,
windows, exits — a ritual so automatic now that he barely registers doing it until a colleague
catches him staring at nothing and asks if he is all right.

He is not all right. At night, he lies rigid beside Adwoa, listening to the building settle. When he
does sleep, he is back in the parking structure. He can smell the concrete, the exhaust. He wakes
soaked through, heart hammering, and sits on the edge of the bed in the dark for an hour, sometimes
two, before his body believes it is over. Adwoa reaches for him during these episodes and he moves
away — not from coldness, but because he cannot bear to be touched unexpectedly, even by her. This
has put a distance between them that neither knows how to name.

At work, the hand healed well enough, but Kofi finds himself hesitating in ways he never did before.
A colleague dropping a wrench on a concrete floor will send a spike of adrenaline through him so
acute that he has to set down whatever he is holding and breathe through it. He has snapped at
apprentices twice over small things and felt ashamed both times. He used to be known as the calm
one, the one who could troubleshoot under pressure without raising his voice. That version of
himself feels remote to him now, like someone he once knew.

He has stopped attending the Saturday morning football matches he played in for fifteen years. He
has stopped going to the church he and Adwoa have attended since they arrived in this country. The
thought of crowds, of being hemmed in, of having his back exposed, makes his chest tighten until he
cannot think clearly. He tells people he has been busy. He is not busy. He is managing a radius of
safety that keeps shrinking.

What troubles Kofi most, sitting in the quiet of his van before a job — the engine running, hands on
the wheel — is not the fear itself but the arithmetic of it. He survived. He knows he survived. And
yet some part of him, the part that still wakes at 3 a.m. in a cold sweat, does not seem to have
received that information.
