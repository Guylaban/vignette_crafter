eight months since the phone rang. Eight months since the world ended. She is here now because her
husband, Sam, made the appointment. He had found her, once again, awake at 3 a.m., the blue light of
the television flickering across her gaunt face, an untouched plate of food from dinner still on the
coffee table. "I can't lose you too," he had whispered, and the quiet desperation in his voice was
the one thing that had managed to penetrate the thick fog she lived in.

The event itself doesn't play like a film in her mind. It is a series of frozen images, stark and
brutal in their clarity. The first is the digital clock on the microwave reading 11:17 PM. The
second is the jarring, metallic shriek of their landline, a sound she now associates with pure
dread. The third, a snapshot of her hand trembling as she picked up the receiver. And the last, the
sight of Sam’s face, crumpling like paper as the disembodied voice on the other end of the line
calmly explained that their seventeen-year-old son, Manu, had been in an accident. When the memory
comes, it is not just the pictures; sometimes, she is ambushed by the sound of that officer’s voice,
a flat, procedural tone delivering the words that cleaved her life in two. The wave of emotion that
follows is so immense, so physically crushing, that she has to brace herself against a wall or sit
down, gasping for air until it recedes, leaving her hollowed out and shaking.

In the first few weeks, people spoke of healing and time. Leilani nodded, but she had already come
to believe that her life had not been altered, but utterly destroyed. The person she was—a
meticulous forensic analyst, a proud mother, a wife who laughed easily—had died with Manu. What
remained felt like a ghost haunting the shell of a life. Her work, once a source of deep
satisfaction and meaning, became a landscape of unbearable triggers. A case file involving a traffic
fatality sat on her desk for a week, unopened. She would stare at the folder, her heart hammering,
until she had to flee to the restroom. Eventually, she stopped going in altogether, using up her
sick leave and vacation days, inventing migraines and family emergencies. The formal warning from
her supervisor felt distant, like a problem happening to someone else.

Her home, once a sanctuary, became a different kind of prison. The evenings were the worst. The
profound quiet of the house after Sam went to bed was a roaring emptiness, a direct echo of the
silence that followed the phone call. To escape it, she began staying up later and later, until dawn
was a pale suggestion in the sky. She would watch infomercials, old movies, anything to fill the
void. Sleep was not a refuge but a place where the snapshots lay in wait. Food lost its taste, its
purpose. The act of eating felt like a betrayal, a pointless continuation of a body she no longer
cared to inhabit. She found a strange, grim comfort in the gnawing hunger; it was a physical
sensation she could control, a pain that felt earned.

Her belief system crumbled into dust. She used to find solace in her community, in shared rituals

rage so bitter it chokes her. Faith in a higher power, in the fundamental fairness of the world,
feels like a childish fantasy she was foolish to ever entertain. Nothing holds meaning anymore. The
promotion she had worked towards for years, the garden she and Sam had tended, the future they had
planned—it has all turned to ash. Leilani is not here for hope. She is certain there is none left.
She is here because she is tired, and because the man she loves is watching her disappear. Her
greatest fear is not that therapy will fail, but that it will succeed only in making her functional
enough to endure a meaningless existence for the rest of her days.
