quiet. At forty-five, he still finds himself scanning the edges of the vast, open fields, his body
tensing at the snap of a branch or the distant rumble of a truck on the gravel road. He tells

rests, a deep and abiding conviction that danger is always just out of sight. This feeling, a low
hum of dread beneath the surface of his days, has been his companion for as long as he can remember.
It is a relic of a childhood home where safety was a myth, a place where the creak of a floorboard
after dark meant his father was awake and the night was about to be filled with shouting and the
sound of his mother’s weeping.

The memories come back not as a coherent story, but in fragments of sensory detail. The sour smell
of beer on his father's breath, the precise pattern of the floral wallpaper he would stare at from
his bed, the sickening thud of a body hitting the wall in the next room. He was seven years old,
small and powerless, hidden under his thin blanket. He remembers the overwhelming urge to run, to
scream, to do *something*, but his limbs felt like lead. He did nothing. He just lay there,
listening, praying for it to stop. That inaction has haunted him for nearly four decades. It has
calcified into a bitter self-recrimination that no amount of adult logic can dislodge. He is certain
he will never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened, for being the boy who hid
instead of the son who protected.

This profound sense of failure has hollowed out his life. He looks at photos from his twenties and
barely recognizes the smiling young man. He used to be a happy person, he thinks, but now he feels
he is always miserable, the capacity for joy having been extinguished long ago. His faith, too, has
evaporated—not just in a higher power, but in the basic goodness of people. He sees the potential
for violence everywhere, a darkness lurking behind every friendly face. This is why he cannot
believe his partner, Amara, when she tells him he is safe. Her words are kind, but they are just
sounds. They cannot penetrate the certainty, born in that small bedroom, that safety is an illusion
and that those who are supposed to protect you are often the most dangerous.

Over the years, Kofi has developed a rigid set of rules to manage the constant threat. He tries hard
not to think about the past, building a mental wall that he reinforces daily. But the thoughts are
persistent. When they break through, they are often consumed with fantasies of revenge. He spends
hours while driving the tractor imagining a confrontation with his father, now a frail old man. In
these daydreams, Kofi is strong and fearless, saying all the things he never could, physically
stopping the man who terrorized his family. The scenarios provide a fleeting sense of power, but
they always dissolve into the same familiar shame. His most entrenched rule is to never, ever be
alone with another person. At work, he orchestrates his tasks to ensure he is always part of a team,

line at the far end of the property by himself; Kofi claimed the parts were too heavy and waited two
hours until a co-worker could join him, earning a frustrated sigh from his boss.

room, unable to settle if she is not in his line of sight. The nights are the worst. As darkness

from outside a potential intruder. He cannot relax, cannot sleep until he is sure she is asleep
beside him, a human shield against the terrors the night holds. It was after one such night, when

thought of losing the one person who makes him feel even a sliver of safety is infinitely worse. He
has come to believe he has lost himself to the past, and he is desperate, for the first time, to
find a way back.
