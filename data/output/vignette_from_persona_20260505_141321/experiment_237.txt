Mai had always found a quiet sanctuary in her studio, a converted garage behind her house where the
scent of clay and turpentine was a comfort. For forty years, the space had been her world, a place
of control and creation. But for the last six months, it had felt more like a cage. The change began
on a crisp October evening after a gallery opening downtown. She was walking to her car when she
heard a cry from a side alley. Peeking around the brick corner, she saw a young woman being thrown
against a wall by a man, his hand over her mouth. The scene unfolded in a series of searingly vivid,
disconnected flashes: the terrified white of the woman’s eyes, the dull glint of a purse strap being
yanked, the sudden, cloying scent of a musky floral cologne that hung in the air. Then, the man was
gone, the sound of his running footsteps echoing down the alley, leaving only a stunned silence and
the woman sliding to the pavement.

In the weeks that followed, the world outside her home began to feel fundamentally unsafe. Mai had
come to believe it was a predatory place, and that people, at their core, could not be trusted. She
stopped taking evening walks with her husband and declined invitations to social events. The memory
of the attack wouldn't settle into the past like other memories. When she tried to think it through,
to create a story she could understand, the narrative would shatter. She’d see the attacker’s
jacket, or the woman’s shoe lying on its side, and the images felt more real, more present, than the
ceramic cup in her own hands. When her husband, concerned, would ask her to talk about it, she’d
start, “I was just walking, and I heard…” but then the thread would vanish, her throat tightening,
leaving her unable to finish the sentence. The story had no beginning or end for her; it was just a
loop of sensory horror.

Her studio, once a refuge, became a place of high alert. The unpredictable arrival of a courier,
their footsteps crunching on the gravel path outside, would send a jolt of ice through her veins,
her heart hammering against her ribs as if she were back in that alley. She’d flinch at the sudden
clang of a dropped chisel, her whole body tensing for a fight that wasn’t there. One afternoon, a
potential client came to view a new bust. As the woman leaned in to inspect the detail, Mai was
overwhelmed by the scent of her perfume—a heavy floral, so similar to the attacker’s cologne. A wave
of nausea and dizziness washed over her, and she had to mutter an excuse about feeling unwell,
retreating into the house and leaving the client bewildered in the studio. The sale was lost. This
incident solidified a painful new belief: there was something deeply and permanently wrong with her.
She was defective, broken in a way that made her incapable of navigating even the simplest of
interactions.

Night was the worst. As darkness fell, the familiar shadows in her home twisted into threats. Every
creak of the floorboards, every rustle of leaves outside the window, was a potential intruder. She
found it impossible to feel safe, lying awake for hours, her body rigid, listening, waiting. Her
work, which required a deep well of calm and concentration, began to suffer. Her hands, once so
steady, now trembled. Her focus was gone, replaced by a constant, humming vigilance. It was after
she found herself unable to even begin a new piece for three straight weeks, staring at a block of
untouched clay, that she finally made the call. She came to therapy not knowing what she wanted,
only that she was tired of being so afraid and so certain that the person she used to be was gone
forever.
