Kalani has not left his apartment in three months. At seventy-one, he had always taken pride in his
resilience, a quality he felt was etched into the deep lines of his face. His studio, once a sun-
drenched space of vibrant creation, has become a fortress. The easel stands in the middle of the
room, but the canvas on it has been untouched for weeks. He spends most of his days by the window,
not looking out at the city he once painted with such love, but listening. Every distant siren,
every sharp bang from a construction site, sends a jolt through his body. He has come to believe,
with a certainty that feels as solid as the floor beneath him, that the world is a profoundly
dangerous place. It is a lesson he learned two years ago, on a Tuesday afternoon at the farmers'
market.

He had been sketching the fruit vendors, capturing the brilliant chaos of colour and commerce, when
the world tore itself apart. The blast threw him sideways, the sound a physical blow that slammed
him against the leg of a wooden stall. He remembers the screech of metal, the smell of dust and
something acrid, and the sudden, shocking silence that followed before the screaming began. He’d
curled into a ball on the pavement, hands clamped over his head, cheek pressed into the gritty
concrete. He saw a blur of running feet, faces contorted in terror, but no one stopped. No one
looked down. In that moment, he felt an absolute solitude, a chilling awareness that in the face of
true danger, you are utterly on your own. This memory is not a hazy recollection; it is a library of
pristine, detailed images that his mind can access at any moment.

Now, those moments happen without his permission. Last week, a car backfiring on the street below
was all it took. The memory didn't just flash in his mind; it seized his body. He was no longer
Kalani, the painter, standing in his studio. He was on the floor, his limbs involuntarily pulling
into the same tight crouch from that day, his arms locking over his head as if to shield him from
falling debris. He could feel the phantom grit of the pavement against his cheek. The sensation is
so real, so consuming, that it has convinced him he can no longer rely on himself. His own body is a
traitor, ready to betray him at any unexpected sound.

In the wake of these episodes, a cold dread settles in. He fights to maintain a rigid calm,
believing that any strong emotion—fear, anger, even joy—is a crack in his defenses through which the
terror might pour. He breathes shallowly, keeps his jaw tight, and tries to think of nothing at all.
This constant, exhausting effort to control his emotions has left him feeling hollowed out. His
connection to the world has frayed. The rich textures of life that once fuelled his art are now just
potential threats. He stopped going out to paint, then he stopped going to the art supply store, and
finally, he stopped going out at all. His daughter, Leilani, now brings him groceries, her worried
face a mirror of his own internal decay.

His isolation is punctuated by frantic phone calls. He’ll ring Leilani three, sometimes four times a
day. “Is that news report about the fire near you?” he’ll ask, his voice tight. “Everything is okay,
right?” He isn’t seeking conversation; he is seeking a temporary anchor, a confirmation from an
outside source that he is, for the moment, safe. He can no longer trust his own judgment. The memory
of the crowd running past him, each person an island of self-preservation, has cemented a deep
conviction that he cannot rely on other people for his safety, yet paradoxically, he now needs their
constant reassurance just to get through the day. It was Leilani who finally convinced him to seek
help, after she arrived with his groceries to find him on the floor, shaking and disoriented, lost
in a memory from two years ago. Kalani agreed, not out of hope, but out of fear. He is terrified of
losing what little is left of himself to the past, yet the thought of opening his front door feels
like an impossible act of faith in a world he knows is waiting to hurt him again.
