six months ago, on the day his son Daniel had been driving him home from the hospital. It was a
Tuesday, the rain so heavy it blurred the world into a watercolour of grey and red tail-lights. They
were talking about Daniel’s upcoming wedding when the screech of tires tore through the
conversation. Mateo remembers the impact not as a single event, but as a series of brutal, sensory
fragments: the sickening lurch of the car spinning, the explosive crack of the airbag deploying, and
the rough, synthetic burn of its fabric scraping against his cheek.

In the weeks that followed, that moment refused to stay in the past. The memory would ambush him,
not as a hazy recollection, but with a terrifying vividness that felt more real than the present
moment. He would be scrubbing in for a procedure, the slightly coarse texture of the disposable gown
under his fingers suddenly becoming the airbag against his skin, and the memory would slam into his
mind. He’d see the spiderweb of shattered glass, smell the acrid chemical propellant, feel the
crushing weight on his chest. It would force its way in, uninvited and overwhelming, and he would
have to shove it back down before it could reach the worst part—the sight of Daniel, slumped and
still against the steering wheel. He never let the memory get that far; he was certain that if he
did, the horror would swallow him whole.

to go was driving with his son. When Daniel would call, Mateo’s heart would hammer against his ribs,
and he’d let the phone ring out, the sound of his son’s voice now a direct line to the accident. He
told his wife it was better if they just met at restaurants, avoiding the intimacy of a shared car.

much like the body-wracking panic of the crash. The joy drained from his days. He stopped reading,

was an open invitation for the memory to intrude. He spent his evenings in a state of deliberate
distraction, flipping through television channels, trying to outrun his own thoughts.

frozen over the patient. The memory had surged—the airbag, the smell, the sudden, violent stop—and

seem to leave behind.
