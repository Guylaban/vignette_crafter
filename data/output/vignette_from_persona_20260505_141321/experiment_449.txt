Kai, a 27-year-old special education teacher, described the last year as living inside a bell jar.
The threat was gone, legally and physically, but the sound of it still echoed. The stalking had
lasted for six months, an escalating campaign of dread orchestrated by a former acquaintance. It
wasn't one single event but a slow, suffocating accumulation of moments: the same grey car parked
across from their school at dismissal, the shadow that seemed to detach from a doorway and follow
them home, the unnervingly specific social media comments. The worst part was the footsteps. For
weeks, they heard them on the pavement behind them during their walk from the bus stop, always
maintaining the same distance, a rhythmic, patient sound that promised proximity without ever
arriving. The memory of that time feels to Kai like a shattered mirror; they can recall the exact,
sickly gleam of a streetlight on wet asphalt one evening with photographic clarity, but the hours
before and after are a complete blank. They know they got home, but they have no memory of the
journey. This sense of an incomplete picture, of missing pieces, leaves them with a persistent,
gnawing feeling that they are forgetting a crucial detail that could have prevented it all.

Now, months after the restraining order was issued, the world remains a landscape of potential
threats. At school, where Kai teaches children with significant support needs, the environment is
inherently unpredictable, and they find themself in a constant state of high alert. A student’s
sudden movement to grab a fallen crayon causes Kai’s entire body to tense, their heart hammering as
if bracing for an attack. They have come to believe that danger is hidden in any abrupt action.
While their students are a source of joy, Kai feels a profound guilt for the vigilance that pulls
their focus away. They find their eyes scanning the hallway through the small window in their
classroom door, mistaking a parent waiting for pickup for an observer with intent. The feeling of
being watched is a physical sensation, a prickling on the back of their neck that is as real as a
touch.

At home, the quiet moments are the hardest. Kai’s partner tries to create a space of safety and
calm, but Kai has come to see relaxation as a form of surrender. To truly let go would be to drop
their guard, and that feels like an invitation for disaster. So, they keep busy, cleaning,
organizing, anything to avoid stillness. Lying in bed at night is a torment of rumination. They sift
through the fragmented memories, turning them over and over, searching for the mistake. If only they
hadn't walked home that one night, if they had blocked the person’s number sooner, if they had
recognized the signs earlier. This looping analysis feels like a protective strategy, a way to map
out past errors to prevent future ones, but it offers no peace, only exhaustion.

The habits of survival have become ingrained. Before getting into their car, Kai checks the back
seat. Before going to sleep, they check the locks on the doors and windows three times. Walking
anywhere feels fraught; every person behind them is a potential follower, every footfall a prelude
to a threat. The hypervigilance has taken a toll on their relationship. Their partner has grown
worried, noting how Kai can no longer sit through a movie without scanning the room, how they flinch
at the sound of a neighbor in the hallway. It was this observation, spoken with gentle concern, that
finally broke through Kai’s belief that they could manage this on their own. They came to therapy
not with a clear goal, but with a desperate, quiet hope: to one day feel safe in their own skin, to
be able to hear footsteps behind them and believe they are simply someone else on their own journey
home.
