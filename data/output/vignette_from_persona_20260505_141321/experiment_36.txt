together. Yet, for the past seven months, she had been unable to mend herself. The accident had

to the radio, when a blue sedan hurtled through the intersection. She remembered the screech of
tires, a sound that seemed to stretch for an eternity, followed by the violent, deafening crunch of
metal. In the moments that followed, she was aware only of fragments: the spiderweb of cracks
spreading across her windshield, the acrid smell of deployed airbags, and a deep, crushing pressure
in her chest that made each breath a ragged, painful effort. She had felt profoundly, terrifyingly
uncomfortable, pinned in the wreckage of her own car.

street—any sudden, loud noise would cause her to flinch, her body instantly braced for an impact
that never came. Driving became impossible. Every blue car felt like a threat, a ghost of the one

altogether. She began distracting herself with old movies, turning the volume up loud enough to
drown out the silence where the intrusive thoughts lived. She tried with all her might to push away
the memory, to wall it off, but it was a flimsy defense.
