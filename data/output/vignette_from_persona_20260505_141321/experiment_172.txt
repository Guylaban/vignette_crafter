unyielding torment. The event itself replayed not as a continuous story, but in searing flashes of

In the months that followed his return to the United States, Eli’s body remained on that floor. He
found himself flinching violently whenever a well-meaning congregant would place a hand on his arm

through his veins, and he could feel his heart hammering against his ribs, a frantic prisoner inside
his own chest. He began to believe his body was an enemy combatant in his own life, reacting with

deeper, more corrosive conviction: he could no longer rely on himself. If his body could not
distinguish between a threat and a kindness, what good was he as a leader, a counselor, a man? He
would watch the footage of the event in his mind’s eye, a silent movie of his own paralysis, and was
certain that somebody else—a younger man, a braver man—would have done something, anything, to stop
it from happening.

deserved this suffering, that it was a just penance for his failure on that day.

desperate fear that the man he once was, the Rabbi his community trusted, was already gone for good.
