Boun sits stiffly on the edge of the armchair, his hands clasped so tightly the knuckles are white.
At sixty-nine, he is a man accustomed to perpetual motion, and the stillness of the therapy office
feels unnatural, almost threatening. It was his employer, a kind young woman whose children he cares
for, who finally insisted he come. For months, a dry, rattling cough has settled deep in his chest,
a cough he refuses to take to a doctor. The very idea of a clinic waiting room, the smell of
antiseptic, the detached authority of a physician—it is a door he cannot bring himself to open.

It all goes back to the winter his daughter, Dara, was seven. The memory isn’t a story he can tell,
not even to himself. It exists in him as a series of blindingly clear, disconnected fragments. There
is the memory of Dara’s forehead, impossibly hot under his palm. There is the memory of the
fluorescent lights of the emergency room humming over his head, a sound that felt like a physical
weight. He can recall, with a clarity that eclipses the memory of his own wedding day, the exact
shade of blue on the blanket they wrapped her in, and the rhythmic, terrifying beep of the heart
monitor that became the soundtrack to his life for weeks. When he tries to piece the days together,
to form a coherent narrative of what happened, his mind goes blank. There are gaps, whole days
missing, replaced only by the phantom sensation of the cold vinyl of a waiting room chair on the
backs of his legs. When a memory does break through, it feels less like a recollection and more like
a sudden, violent teleportation. The memory is not in the past; it is happening right now. He is
back in that sterile room, watching his daughter’s small chest struggle for air, the feeling of
absolute helplessness washing over him as if for the first time.

In the years that followed Dara’s eventual, miraculous recovery, Boun learned to outrun his own
mind. He and his late wife never spoke of that time. It was an unspoken agreement to build a wall of
silence around the experience. After she passed, the silence became a cavern, and he took the job as
a nanny to fill it with the noise of children’s laughter and demands. His days are now a masterclass
in mental distraction. He throws himself into his work with a ferocious energy that his employers
mistake for simple dedication. He builds elaborate Lego castles, reads picture books with
exaggerated voices, and organizes the playroom with meticulous precision, all in a desperate effort
to keep his mind occupied. Any moment of quiet is a danger zone. “Don’t think about it,” he tells
himself a hundred times a day, scrubbing a dish or folding a tiny t-shirt. “Just focus on this.” The
effort leaves him utterly exhausted by evening.

This constant vigilance has changed him. He looks in the mirror and sees a stranger. The man he used
to be—calm, patient, trusting—is gone. He has come to believe that the experience permanently
damaged something essential inside him. He sees this new, broken self most clearly at work. When
four-year-old Leo scraped his knee at the park last week, Boun’s reaction was so swift and panicked
that it frightened the boy more than the fall itself. He feels a deep, gnawing distrust of the world
now. The doctors at the hospital had offered shifting diagnoses and reassurances that felt hollow;
he came to see them not as helpers, but as gatekeepers of a truth they were unwilling to share. This
suspicion has since bled into everything. He is certain that people are not what they seem, that his
employers are secretly judging his every move, that the other nannies at the playground are
whispering about him.

He is here now because that distrust has finally turned inward, paralyzing him. His own body is
sending warning signs, but he cannot bring himself to seek medical care. To him, a doctor’s office
is not a place of healing, but a place where your life is taken out of your hands, where you are
made small and powerless. He has chosen the familiar pain of his memories over the terrifying
unknown of a diagnosis. But his employer’s ultimatum has left him with no choice. He doesn’t believe
he can ever go back to the man he was before the hospital, but he is terrified of losing his job, of
being left alone in the quiet with nothing but the beeping of the machine in his memory.
