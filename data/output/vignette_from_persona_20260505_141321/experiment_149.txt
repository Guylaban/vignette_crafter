Javier has been a parole officer for thirty years, a career built on a belief in order, fairness,
and the possibility of reform. At fifty-four, he carried himself with a quiet authority that most of
his clients respected. But for the last eight months, that foundation has felt like sand. The story
of how it all fell apart begins on a humid summer evening, not on the job, but on his own
residential street. A dispute between two neighbors had escalated into shouting. Javier, off-duty
and in a t-shirt and jeans, had gone out to mediate, his voice calm and steady as he’d done a
thousand times before. He was making progress when the cruisers arrived, lights flashing but sirens
off. The sudden, unnatural quiet that fell over the block was the last moment of peace he would
know.

What happened next plays back in his mind not as a linear story, but as a series of brutal,
disjointed snapshots. He remembers turning, hands slightly raised, to explain the situation to the
responding officers. He remembers the blinding beam of a flashlight, a shove from behind, and the
shocking, grinding pain of his face hitting the pavement. He can recall the weight of a knee in his
back and the frantic, useless thumping of his own heart against his ribs, a sound so loud it seemed
to drown out the confusing shouts. He remembers seeing the glint of another officer’s badge and the
thought, sharp as glass, that these were his people, his colleagues in the same system of justice.
The rest is a blur of fragments he can’t assemble. When he tries to force the memory into a coherent
sequence, to see the faces or recall the exact words, a thick, protective wall rises in his mind,
and he finds himself pushing it all away before the picture can become complete. The pieces he does
hold feel so extreme, so utterly unbelievable, that he sometimes wonders if he imagined the sheer
senselessness of it all.

This fractured memory now dictates the rhythm of his life. At the parole office, a place he once
navigated with confidence, he is constantly on edge. He scans the faces in the waiting room, not for
recognition, but for threats. He feels jumpy, startling violently when a clerk drops a heavy file on
a desk. The most unnerving moments are the quiet ones. A lull in the usual office clamor, a moment
of stillness before a parolee enters his office, fills him with a dreadful certainty that something
dangerous is about to happen. This feeling is most potent when he is confronted with what he now
sees everywhere: people misbehaving. A client showing up late with a defiant attitude, an argument
breaking out in the hallway—these minor infractions trigger an immediate and overwhelming physical
response. His heart begins to pound in his chest, that same frantic, trapped rhythm from the night
on the pavement, and the room seems to shrink around him.

He has come to believe that the world is fundamentally unsafe and that the institutions designed to
protect people are a lie. He dedicated his life to a system that, in his moment of need, had not
only failed him but had actively harmed him. This sense of institutional failure is compounded by a
deep, personal feeling of betrayal. These were men who wore a similar uniform, who should have been
his brothers. The fact that the internal investigation went nowhere, that no one was held
accountable, has become a constant, grinding source of bitterness. What happened to him was
profoundly unfair, and the lack of any justice has left a permanent stain on his view of the world.

Over the months, he began to withdraw. He started passing off more volatile cases to junior
officers, citing paperwork as an excuse. His wife, Elena, noticed he no longer sat with his back to
the door in restaurants and that he would flinch at the sound of a passing siren. The final straw
came last week. During a routine check-in with a low-risk parolee, the young man became agitated
about a failed drug test. As his voice rose, Javier felt the familiar thunder in his chest, so
intense this time that he was certain he was having a heart attack. He abruptly ended the meeting
and spent the next hour in his car, waiting for his heart to slow down, drenched in sweat. It was
Elena who made the appointment. Javier is here now because he is losing the career that defined him
and because he feels like a stranger in his own life. He is terrified that speaking the story aloud
will make the broken pieces whole and unbearable, but he is more terrified of what will happen if he
doesn't.
