Mateo describes the last six months as living in someone else’s body. The person he was
before—confident, skilled, proud of his hands and the complex machinery he could command—seems like
a character from a story he once read. The memory of the day that changed everything feels similarly
detached, like a film clip that plays on a loop in his mind but belongs to a different person. He
can see the brilliant summer sun glinting off the rooftop unit, hear the low hum of the condenser,
and see his senior partner, Javier, laughing at a joke he’d just made. Then there’s the flash, the
acrid smell of ozone and burnt wiring that sometimes ambushes him when he opens his own toolbox, and
the terrible silence that followed the electrical arc. When the memory forces its way into his mind,
uninvited, it’s this sudden, unnatural quiet that he finds most terrifying. It’s a silence he has
come to believe is the sound of disaster unfolding.

In the weeks following the accident, Mateo began a relentless process of self-interrogation. Night
after night, he would lie awake, going over every single detail, every turn of a screw, every word
exchanged with Javier. He was certain that if he just replayed it enough times, he could pinpoint
the one moment, the one fatal oversight, that led to Javier’s severe burns. This mental ritual
offered no clarity, only a deepening conviction of his own guilt. He felt he would never be able to
forgive himself for not doing more, for not being better, for not somehow preventing the inevitable.
This belief has become the central truth of his new life. The person he is now, this hesitant and
frightened young man, is unrecognisable to him, a stranger who has taken up residence in his skin.

His work as an HVAC technician, once a source of pride, is now a landscape of potential threats. The
world has shrunk to a few known locations where he feels a semblance of safety: his small apartment,
his truck, and the supply warehouse. Any job site feels perilous. An unexpected quiet, like a
compressor cycling off without warning, can make the hair on his arms stand up and his heart hammer
against his ribs. He feels his own body betraying him in these moments, the sweat on his palms and
the tremor in his hands confirming his deepest fear: that he can no longer trust himself to do his
job safely. As a result, he has started avoiding anything that might be stressful. He turns down
complex commercial jobs, making excuses to his boss, and sticks to simple residential filter
changes. The consequence was a formal warning last month; his career is now as precarious as his
sense of self.

His social life has evaporated along with his confidence. He avoids his friends, certain they can
see the failure written all over him. The calendar has also become a source of dread. The twenty-
first of each month, the date of the accident, is a day he spends in his apartment, curtains drawn,
avoiding calls and the possibility of any reminder. He has come to believe that safety is only
possible through constant vigilance and radical avoidance. This shrinking life, however, has become
its own kind of prison. It was after he found himself unable to even enter a client’s basement,
frozen on the top step by the unfamiliar quiet and the enclosed space, that he finally made the
call. He came to therapy not out of hope, but out of a dawning terror that if something doesn't
change, the person he used to be will be gone for good, leaving only this ghost who is too afraid to
live.
