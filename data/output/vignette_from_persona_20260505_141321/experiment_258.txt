into something else entirely. The satisfaction he once found in restoring order to a home has been
replaced by a constant, humming dread. The turning point was an afternoon eight months ago, at the
home of a long-time client, an elderly woman named Mrs. Gable. He had just finished mopping the
polished oak staircase, a feature of the house he always took extra care with. He was in the kitchen
rinsing the mop when he heard the slip, a soft scuff of a slipper on wet wood, followed by a
sickening, final thud.

The memory doesn't play like a film for Dev. When it comes, which it does without warning, it is a
series of searing photographs burned into his mind. The first is the glint of the overhead light on
the single wet spot he must have missed on the top step. The second is Mrs. Gable’s floral
housecoat, a blur of pink and green as she fell. The third, the most vivid and recurring, is her
body at the bottom of the stairs, twisted at an angle that seemed impossible. He doesn't remember
the sound of his own shouting, only the silent, static images. With these pictures comes not fear or
sadness, but a white-hot surge of rage that leaves him trembling. The rage is aimed squarely at
himself, a furious, looping monologue about his own stupidity and carelessness.

This internal condemnation has become the central truth of his life. Dev had always seen himself as
a careful, considerate man, someone who made spaces safer and more pleasant. He had come to believe
that in that single moment of inattention, he had not just made a mistake, but revealed a
fundamental, monstrous flaw in his character. The event happened, he is certain, because of the way
he acted; it was a failure of his very being. This conviction that he violated his own deepest
values has hollowed him out. Nothing in his life feels meaningful anymore. The satisfaction of a
perfectly clean room is gone, tainted by the knowledge that his work can cause such profound harm.

and found wanting, and the life he knew was his punishment.
