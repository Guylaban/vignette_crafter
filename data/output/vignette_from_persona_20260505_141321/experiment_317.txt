he felt had cleaved his life in two. Before that Saturday, he described his life as ordered and
meaningful; now, he felt adrift in a constant state of alert. The event itself was a blur of chaos.

with the crowd. Fights broke out, and police moved in. David was trying to leave the area when he
saw an officer strike a young man with a baton, opening a deep, bleeding gash above the man’s eye.
The image was seared into his mind. Moments later, mistaken for a rioter, David was thrown to the
ground by two officers. He remembers the rough texture of the pavement against his cheek and a
profound sense of powerlessness.

In the weeks that followed, the memory refused to settle into a coherent story. When he tried to
explain what happened to his wife, he reported that the words would get stuck; he would lose the
thread, overwhelmed by a surge of panic that forced him to stop talking. The memory was not a
narrative but a series of sensory fragments that could be triggered without warning. A siren in the
distance, the sight of a police car parked outside a coffee shop—these would instantly bring back
the feeling of the pavement on his skin. He began altering his driving routes to avoid the precinct
near his home. The most potent trigger, however, was seeing injuries. Watching an action movie where
a character was cut, or even seeing a child with a scraped knee at the park, would plunge him back
into the moment he saw the gash on the young man’s face.

To manage these intrusions, David began to systematically fill every moment of silence. Working from
home provided the perfect environment for this. He kept podcasts playing constantly, from the moment
he woke up until he went to bed, a wall of sound to keep his own unwanted thoughts at bay. When the
noise wasn't enough, he would lose himself for hours scrolling through design forums or playing
mindless games on his phone, long after his work for the day was done. He started declining
invitations from friends, avoiding the unpredictability of other people and their conversations. His
world shrank to his home office, his screen a shield against the intrusive memories.

home, a place he knew was safe, he found he was unable to relax. If his wife suggested they go out

intolerable.

minutes on the pavement. Nothing felt meaningful anymore—not his work, which he once loved, nor his
