Mark’s face contorted with rage, the pattern of the wallpaper behind his head, a raised fist.

aggression. David was held down, his autonomy stripped away in a matter of moments. It is this
feeling of absolute powerlessness that now haunts his daily life. At his job in a large municipal
building, a surprise project reassignment or a last-minute change in deadlines can trigger a
disproportionate sense of panic. The lack of control feels viscerally dangerous, echoing that night.

shallow, certain that he is trapped and something terrible is about to happen. He has started making
excuses to keep the office door open or to have a third person present, a strategy that is becoming
increasingly difficult to explain to his team.

When the photographic memories do break through, they bring with them a phantom taste. He can
suddenly, vividly, taste the metallic tang of blood mixed with the stale whiskey on Mark’s breath
from that night. In those moments, he feels a profound sense of unreality, as if he is watching a
scene that happened to someone else. He has learned to detach himself, to float away from the images
and the taste, observing them from a great distance until they recede. This detachment is a shield,
but it leaves him feeling hollowed out and disconnected from his own life.

After leaving Mark, David found a small apartment, but the silence and solitude he thought he craved

frantic patchwork of social engagements and overtime at work. He keeps himself constantly surrounded
by people, ensuring he is never alone with his thoughts. He hasn't spent a single night by himself
since moving. He either stays on friends' couches or insists his new partner, Alan, stays with him.

unfamiliar place is equally impossible, making even a weekend trip to visit family a source of
intense anxiety.

It is this exhaustion that has finally propelled him to seek help. The relentless effort of managing
his world—of avoiding solitude, of navigating professional interactions that feel fraught with
peril, of bracing against the next sensory snapshot—has depleted him. He came to therapy not with a
dramatic story of a breakdown, but with a quiet admission of defeat. He is tired of being afraid in
his own home. What frightens him most is the thought that this is permanent, that he will never

door behind him and feel not terror, but peace.
