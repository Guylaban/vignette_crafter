physical injuries, the man who walked off that set was not the same one who had walked on.

narrative. Instead, it lives in him as a series of silent, searing Polaroids: the web of sparks
cascading from the ceiling, the twisted metal frame just feet from where he stood, the chalky dust
hanging in the air when the emergency lights finally flickered on. These images are perfectly,
horribly clear, intruding without warning when he’s driving, or trying to fall asleep. To keep them
at bay, he throws himself into anything else. He spends hours in his home editing suite, not working
on a new project, but endlessly re-cutting old footage, the familiar hum of the machinery a shield

confirmation of a belief that had been hardening inside him: his reactions since the event showed he
was a lousy coper.
