Bao had always taken pride in his understanding of the human body—its resilience, its mechanics, its
capacity for healing. As a physical therapist, this was his trade and his passion. But for the last
eight months, since the rainy Tuesday when a panel van ran a red light, his own body had felt like a
foreign and treacherous country. He remembered the moment with a sickening, fragmented clarity: not
as a continuous story, but as a series of sensory snapshots. The squeal of tires on wet asphalt, the
sudden, blinding glare of headlights through his passenger-side window, and the deafening, metallic
roar as the world compressed around him. He’d woken up in the hospital with a shattered femur and
three fractured ribs, his professional knowledge a source of cold comfort against the searing pain.

The physical recovery was arduous but, by all objective measures, successful. His colleagues praised
his diligence with his own rehabilitation. His wife, Linh, celebrated each milestone. Yet, as his
bones mended, his mind seemed to unravel. The world, once a predictable space, now felt riddled with
hidden threats. Walking through the clinic where he worked, a place he once navigated with ease,
became an exercise in threat assessment. He would find his eyes drawn to the heavy kettlebells lined
up against the wall, no longer seeing them as tools for recovery but as blunt instruments. A dropped
metal chart in the hallway would cause him to flinch violently, his heart hammering against his ribs
as the sound of the impact echoed the crash. He began staying late after his last patient, not to
finish notes, but to meticulously wipe down and rearrange every piece of equipment in his treatment
room. The perfect alignment of weights, the sterile gleam of the parallel bars—this imposed order
was the only thing that quieted the chaos in his head, if only for a little while.

At home, Linh noticed he was no longer present. He would be in the middle of a sentence and his
focus would drift, his eyes scanning the room as if searching for an unseen danger. He couldn’t
concentrate on a movie, couldn’t relax. He’d wake from nightmares drenched in sweat, the phantom
sensation of being crushed still lingering in his limbs. He developed a ritual before they drove
anywhere, mentally rehearsing a dozen different worst-case scenarios—a tire blowout on the freeway,
a brake failure on a hill, another car appearing from nowhere. He believed this prepared him, but
Linh just saw the tension coiling in his shoulders, his knuckles white on the steering wheel.

His work, his calling, suffered most. He had come to believe that his body was permanently damaged,
a fragile thing that could betray him at any moment. This deep-seated conviction made it nearly
impossible to trust himself with his patients. While demonstrating a simple lumbar stretch, he’d
feel a twinge in his own back and be seized by the thought that his spine was about to give out. He
could no longer trust his own physical intuition, the very sense that had made him a gifted
therapist. Unwanted thoughts—the image of shattering glass, the memory of the paramedic’s strained
voice—would intrude while he was trying to count a patient’s reps, leaving him disoriented and
struggling to find his place. The confidence he once had in the body’s ability to heal had been
replaced by a profound fear of its vulnerability. He was certain he could no longer be trusted, that
you could never truly know what might cause harm.

The breaking point came last week. He was helping an elderly woman regain her balance when a car
backfired on the street outside the clinic’s large window. Bao didn’t just startle; he physically
recoiled, letting go of the patient’s support belt. She stumbled, catching herself on a nearby
table, her face a mixture of fright and confusion. The shame was immediate and overwhelming. In that
moment, he saw himself through her eyes: not a healer, but a source of instability and fear. It was
the final piece of evidence that he was failing, that the accident had broken something inside him
that physiotherapy couldn't fix. He came to therapy a week later, his voice low as he explained that
he just wanted to feel solid in his own skin again, to be able to trust his own body enough to help
others trust theirs.
