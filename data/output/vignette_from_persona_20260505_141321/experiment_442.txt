Amir, a 64-year-old state congressman, presents as a man of immense public composure, his tailored
suits and measured speech a testament to a long career in politics. Yet, beneath this carefully
constructed exterior is a profound and unceasing state of alert that has governed his life for
decades. The origin point is his time as a young relief worker in the Middle East, an experience he
rarely speaks of but which replays in his mind not as a narrative, but as a series of sensory
assaults. He doesn't remember the day of the bombing as a sequence of events; instead, his memory
offers only disconnected pieces that refuse to form a whole. There is the memory fragment of the
unusual, deadening silence just before the marketplace was hit, a silence so complete it felt like
the world holding its breath. Then comes the smell of burnt plastic and scorched earth, a scent that
can ambush him now from a backyard barbecue or a city fireplace. He remembers the dust, thick and
choking, and the disembodied sounds of screaming, but not the faces of the people he was trying to
help. When these fragments surface, they bring with them not just sadness, but the same raw, animal
terror he felt then, an emotional wave so powerful he feels it might drown him, forcing him to
immediately stop what he's doing and find a distraction.

For years, his political career served as that distraction. Amir threw himself into his work with a
relentless energy that colleagues mistook for ambition. In reality, it was a desperate flight from
the quiet moments. He learned that as long as he was drafting legislation, attending committee
meetings, or preparing for a debate, his mind was occupied. Overworking became his primary strategy
for survival. This devotion to his job, however, came at a cost. His ex-wife, he recalls, used to
say he was a ghost in their home, present in body but absent in spirit. He couldn't explain to her
that the silence of their evenings was not peaceful for him; it was menacing, a vacuum that his mind
would rush to fill with the whine of incoming mortars. The marriage eventually dissolved under the
weight of his emotional unavailability, a consequence he accepted with a sense of grim
inevitability.

Now, living alone, his routines are rigid and defensive. Before he can even think of sleeping, he
must perform a meticulous ritual of checking every window and door in his apartment, sometimes two
or three times, convinced he has missed something. In public, his political role places him in the
path of his most potent triggers. During town hall meetings, he finds himself unable to focus on the
person speaking. Instead, he constantly scans the room, cataloging exits, assessing the posture of
strangers in the audience, his body tensing at any sudden movement. He has come to believe that
danger is always imminent and that only his vigilance keeps it at bay. Talking to constituents, a
core function of his job, has become an exercise in extreme self-control. He forces a smile and a
firm handshake while his mind races, interpreting every unfamiliar face as a potential threat. Any
mention of military affairs, a frequent topic in his line of work, sends a jolt of ice through his
veins, threatening to shatter his composure. He grips the podium tighter, focusing on the grain of
the wood, trying with all his might to keep his emotions from showing on his face.

What has finally brought Amir to seek help is a growing sense of exhaustion. The constant vigilance,
the endless work, the decades of holding his breath have worn him down. Last month, during a
televised debate, an opponent brought up foreign military aid, and for a terrifying moment, Amir
felt the memory fragments rise with such force that he completely lost his train of thought. The
silence stretched on, and in that void, he was back in the dust and the noise. He managed to
recover, but the incident terrified him. He is afraid of losing control, of the carefully built

hopes, he stated quietly in his first session, to one day be able to sit in a quiet room and feel
