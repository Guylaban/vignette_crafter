Priya, a 23-year-old auditor, presents with significant distress following a trip to her family’s
ancestral village six months prior. The visit, intended to be a joyful reunion, was shattered by a
marketplace bombing. Her memory of the event is not a continuous film but a series of searingly
clear, disjointed images. When she tries to recount what happened, the words fail her; she can only
describe these snapshots, feeling as if the connecting narrative is a void she cannot fill. The
first picture is of a young boy on a rusty blue bicycle, a bell ringing faintly. The next is a
silent, blinding flash of white light. The final, and most persistent, image is of a dismembered
hand lying in the dust, its fingers still curled around a piece of fruit. She knows more must have
happened in those moments—the sounds, the smells, the chaos—but her mind offers only these three
silent photographs, leaving her with a profound sense of an incomplete, broken story.

Since returning to the United States, these fragments have intruded upon her daily life with
devastating effect. The auditory world has become a minefield; a car backfiring on the street sends
a jolt of terror through her, her body tensing for an impact that never comes. The sharp clap of a
colleague dropping a heavy ledger in their quiet office can leave her breathless and trembling at
her desk. The sight of a bicycle, once an unremarkable part of the urban landscape, is now a potent
threat. She has altered her route to work to avoid a popular bike lane, adding twenty minutes to her
commute, because the sight of a cyclist gliding past can trigger the boy’s image and a wave of
nausea. Even looking at a colleague’s hand resting on a keyboard can instantly superimpose the image
from the marketplace, forcing her to look away, her stomach churning.

These experiences have corroded her sense of self. Priya was once defined by her sharp, analytical
mind and meticulous attention to detail—qualities that made her a promising auditor. Now, she feels
she is no longer capable of the focus her job demands. She rereads the same line of a financial
statement a dozen times, her concentration shattered by an intrusive thought or a sudden noise. She
has come to believe that the competent, intelligent person she used to be was destroyed in the
blast. This sense of loss is compounded by a profound feeling of detachment. She describes moving

feeling "dead inside." Her parents’ worried phone calls feel like they are happening to someone
else, their concern unable to penetrate the thick wall of numbness that surrounds her.

gentle, pointed to a series of errors in her recent audits—mistakes she would have never made
before. Seeing her professional identity, the last pillar of the old Priya, begin to crumble was the
final catalyst. She came to the first session stating that she doesn't want to feel like a ghost in
her own life anymore. Her greatest fear is that this hollowed-out version of herself is permanent,
and her hope is to find a way to feel like a person again, not just the remnant of one.
