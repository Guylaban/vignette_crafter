Sofia sits in the waiting room, a space she knows well from her own work in social ministry, though
she has never been on this side of the desk. At forty-seven, she feels a profound disconnect from
the woman she was just eight months ago. The assault happened on a Tuesday evening in late autumn.
She had stayed late at the community center, waiting for a parishioner who was delayed, and decided
to walk the few blocks home as dusk settled. The walk was usually quiet, a time for her to
decompress. That quietness was the first thing he took from her. The silence of the empty side
street was absolute for a moment before he grabbed her, a void of sound that felt like the world
holding its breath. Now, any unusual silence—a lull in conversation at work, the moment after the
furnace clicks off at home—sends a jolt of pure terror through her. She is certain that something
violent is about to fill the void.

Her memory of the event is not a continuous story. It exists in her mind as a series of stark, still
images, like horrific photographs she cannot discard. There is the snapshot of the cracked pavement
beneath her cheek, the glint of his silver watch, the specific floral pattern on the discarded
mattress in the alley. When she tries to speak about it, to her husband or now to the intake
coordinator, the words jam in her throat. She knows the sequence, but language fails her; all she
has are the pictures. And with the pictures comes a wave of guilt so powerful it makes her nauseous.
She feels it as a physical weight, a certainty that she is somehow to blame for the delay that put
her on that street at that exact time.

This sense of pervasive threat has rewired her daily life. Her husband, Miguel, will text that he is
running fifteen minutes late, and for Sofia, that delay is not an inconvenience but a confirmation
of catastrophe. In those fifteen minutes, she has already pictured a car crash, a heart attack, a
mugging. She cannot relax, not even in her own living room with the doors locked. The state of being
at ease feels like a dangerous indulgence, an invitation for disaster to strike when her guard is
down. She finds herself constantly scanning, listening, waiting for the other shoe to drop. The
woman who once led prayer groups with a calm, open heart now feels like a soldier on enemy
territory, twenty-four hours a day.

In the months since the attack, she has come to believe that the joyful, faithful person she once
was is gone forever, replaced by this miserable, fearful stranger. Her faith, the bedrock of her
life and career, has crumbled. She looks at the crucifix in her office and feels nothing but a
hollow ache. How could a benevolent God allow such a thing? This loss has made her work unbearable.
She counsels people on finding hope while she herself feels that all faith in humanity is a fool’s
errand.

To cope with the chaos inside, she has seized on the few things she can still command. Control has
become her obsession. She restricts her eating with rigid precision, weighing every portion,
counting every calorie. The feeling of hunger is a sharp, clean distraction; the control over her
own plate is a small island of order in a world of violent unpredictability. She feels deeply
ashamed of her body, seeing it not as her own but as a scene of a crime. She tells herself, in the
quiet moments of self-loathing, that she deserves to suffer, that this constant misery is a fitting
punishment. The avoidance of relaxation, the strict diets, the self-recrimination—they have become a
form of penance. It was a recent moment at work, when a grieving mother reached for her hand and
Sofia flinched away, unable to bear the quiet intimacy of offering comfort, that she knew she could
not go on like this. She is here today not with hope, which feels too extravagant, but with a
desperate need to stop the freefall, to find a way to exist in the world without being in a constant
state of terror.
