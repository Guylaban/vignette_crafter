Kelepi traces the grain of the wood on his drafting table, a motion that once brought him comfort
but now feels hollow. At sixty-seven, he had envisioned a gentle winding down of his interior design
practice, a celebrated career culminating in passion projects and time with his grandchildren.
Instead, his world has shrunk to the dimensions of this one room. The change began three years ago,
on what was supposed to be a routine sourcing trip to North Africa. The details of his abduction
remain a chaotic blur, a series of disjointed sensory snapshots. What he remembers with sickening
clarity is the suffocating darkness of the storeroom where he was held for nineteen days. The coarse
texture of the burlap sack over his head, the cold concrete floor, and above all, the sounds. The
guttural, angry shouts in Arabic from the hallway were a constant torment, a language he didn't
understand but whose menacing tone needed no translation.

In the first year after his release, Kelepi tried to reclaim his life. He threw himself back into
his work, but the familiar spaces of his profession had become hostile territory. While measuring a
walk-in closet for a client, the closing of the door behind him plunged the small space into shadow,
and suddenly he was back on the concrete floor, the smell of dust and fear filling his lungs. He
fled the appointment, leaving his equipment behind. This became a pattern. Elevators, windowless
bathrooms, even client meetings in small conference rooms became impossible. He began turning down
lucrative jobs, making excuses that grew increasingly flimsy. His business, once his pride, is now
nearly defunct.

His body remains on high alert, a sentinel that never stands down. A car backfiring on the street
sends him diving for cover before his mind can even label the sound; his heart hammers against his
ribs, a frantic drumbeat of pure terror, long after he recognizes the source. He feels profoundly
unsafe unless his back is to a wall, his eyes scanning every entrance. When his daughter invites him
for dinner, he spends the hour before she arrives rehearsing the conversation, planning his answers
to simple questions like "How are you?" because the thought of being caught off guard, of feeling
emotionally exposed, is intolerable. He has come to believe that any strong feeling is a sign of
weakness, a vulnerability that could lead to disaster. The fear and shame are a constant, bitter
taste in his mouth.

The memories of those nineteen days are a cruel paradox. Sometimes, when a news report mentions the
Middle East, a memory will flash into his mind, unbidden and complete with the auditory echo of his
captors' voices. He can hear the shouting as if it were happening right now, in his sunlit studio.
Yet, when he tries to intentionally explain what happened to his concerned daughter, the narrative
collapses. He loses the thread, the sequence of events becomes muddled, and he trails off,
frustrated by his inability to articulate his own story. At other times, he can recount the facts of
his captivity with a chilling detachment, feeling absolutely nothing, as if he is describing a
character in a film he once saw. It is this fragmentation that frightens him most—the sense that the
experience is both intensely present and completely inaccessible, a part of him that he can neither
integrate nor escape. It was after one such attempt to explain, seeing the helpless worry in his
daughter's eyes as he fell into confused silence, that Kelepi finally agreed to seek help. He holds
little hope of feeling like his old self again, but he is desperate for an end to the siege within
his own mind.
