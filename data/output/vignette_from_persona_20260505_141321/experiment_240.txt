Imani, a 23-year-old environmental scientist, measures her life in two parts: before the phone call,
and after. Six months have passed since her younger brother, Daniel, died, but the memory of that
evening operates in the present tense. It isn’t something she recalls; it’s something that happens
to her, again and again. The flash of his last text message—"Leaving now, see u soon!"—followed by
the hours of silence. She remembers the growing knot of irritation that slowly curdled into dread.
The memory often arrives this way, as a sensory fragment that feels utterly real: the cold weight of
her phone in her hand, the screen’s glare in the dark room. It feels like it is happening right now,
a loop of dread that she is powerless to stop. But just as she feels the vibrations of her mother’s
incoming call, her mind slams a door. She finds herself pushing the memory away, a reflexive flinch
before the image of her mother’s face or the sound of her voice can fully form.

This internal battle leaves her exhausted. At her lab, where she analyzes soil and water samples,
the quiet precision she once loved has become a minefield. Last week, a colleague was thirty minutes
late returning from a solo field collection. For Imani, the delay was not an inconvenience; it was a
catastrophe in progress. Her heart began to hammer against her ribs, her palms grew slick with
sweat, and her body braced for an impact she couldn't name. This often happens now—her body reacts
to a perceived danger before her mind has even had a chance to process the situation. When her
colleague finally walked in, apologizing for the traffic, the wave of relief was immediately
replaced by a surge of raw, unfocused anger. She instigated a sharp argument with him over a minor
data-logging protocol, the conflict a strange and temporary relief from the terror that had just
consumed her. It was a way to feel something other than helpless.

In the quiet of her apartment, the self-recrimination is a constant, humming engine. She replays

stopped the event from happening. "If only I'd called him sooner," she tells herself, tracing
patterns on her blanket, unable to sleep. "If only I'd told him to wait until morning." This thought
spirals into a deeper, more painful belief: that the event happened because of the sort of person
she is—careless, distracted, fundamentally flawed. It feels like a judgment delivered by the
universe, a profound and terrible injustice for which there is no appeal. This conviction has
hollowed out her relationships, leaving her feeling isolated and uniquely broken.

Her world has shrunk. She avoids the news and turns off any television show that might feature a car
crash or a hospital scene. The thought of seeing a fictionalised version of her own horror is
unbearable. When her parents try to talk about Daniel, to share a memory, she finds the words simply
won't come. She knows what happened, the sequence of events is seared into her brain, but she is
unable to find the language to describe the chasm it left behind. The silence frustrates them, and

conversation, where she sat mute while her father cried, that she finally made an appointment. She
came to therapy not with a clear goal, but with a desperate fear that this fractured, hyper-alert,

to say her brother’s name without feeling the world collapse around her.
