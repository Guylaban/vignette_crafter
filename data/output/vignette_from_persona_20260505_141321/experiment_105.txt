Imani had lived in the same small house for forty-five years, thirty of them alone since her husband
passed. At seventy-eight, her life was measured by the familiar rhythms of her potter’s wheel and
the warmth of the kiln in her backyard studio. That rhythm was shattered six months ago on a Tuesday
night. She had been cleaning her tools, her back to the studio door, when the sound of splintering
wood and shattering glass made her drop a throwing rib. In the large mirror she used to check the
symmetry of her pots, she saw him: a figure in the doorway, his face a blur of shadow and rage. The
memory doesn't come back to her as a story, but as a series of stark, silent images that force their
way into her mind: the glint of the broken glass on the floor, the reflection of his shape behind
her, the feeling of her own breath catching in her throat. When these pictures ambush her, which
they do without warning, she feels the cold studio air on her skin again, the terror so immediate it
is as if the event is happening right now, in the present moment.

In the weeks that followed, Imani’s world, once circumscribed by the comforting loop between her
house and her studio, began to shrink. She had come to believe the world is a dangerous place, and
her home was no longer a sanctuary but the epicentre of that danger. Sleep became a fraught and
fragmented affair. She would jolt awake multiple times a night, heart hammering against her ribs,
convinced she had heard a floorboard creak or a window rattle. She would lie perfectly still,
straining to listen for sounds that might mean someone was trying to get in, her body rigid with an
alertness that never subsided. Even in the brightest daylight, sitting in her favourite armchair
with a cup of tea, she found she was unable to relax. Her body, she felt, had betrayed her; it kept
reacting as if she were still in danger, a constant, humming alarm that left her exhausted. Her
hands, once so steady they could pull the most delicate walls from a lump of spinning clay, now
trembled at the slightest unexpected noise, making her work nearly impossible.

To manage the terror, Imani developed a series of quiet rituals. Before bed, she would begin a
circuit of the house, checking the lock on the front door, then the back, then the sliding door to
the patio, then every window. She would push and pull on each one, her knuckles white, and then, ten
minutes later, feel a surge of doubt and have to start the entire circuit over again. The large
mirror in her studio was now a source of profound dread. She would find herself compulsively
checking it and every other reflective surface in the house, not to look at herself, but to scan the
space behind her. The fear was always the same: that she would see that shape in a doorway again.
She tried desperately to suppress the intrusive images, to push them from her mind. If she let
herself truly think about what happened, she was certain she would not be able to handle it; the
memory felt like a force that could shatter what was left of her. She would hum loudly or count the
cracks in the ceiling, anything to build a wall against the memory.

The consequences of this constant vigilance have been profound. She has stopped taking commissions,
terrified of the tremors in her hands and the inability to focus. The joy she once found in her
craft has been replaced by a gnawing anxiety. Her daughter, visiting last week, found her staring
into the middle distance, her teacup rattling in its saucer, and commented on how tired and
frightened she looked. It was that moment of seeing her fear reflected in her daughter’s eyes that
finally prompted her to seek help. Imani wants her hands to be steady again, to feel the familiar
peace of her studio, but she is deeply afraid that the quiet, safe world she once knew is gone for
good.
