protect them. When her son collapsed a week later, the truth was revealed in a sterile hospital
room: a rare and aggressive form of leukemia.

The months that followed became a blur she now struggled to consciously access. If she tried to
deliberately recall the sequence of events—the consultations, the first round of chemotherapy—the
memory would slip away, leaving only a frustrating haze. She knew what happened, of course, but the
words to describe the profound terror of watching her child waste away felt lodged in her throat,
impossible to form. What remained were sensory fragments and raw emotion. Sometimes, while stocking

her constant companion in the pediatric oncology ward.

perpetually exhausted. Even at home, sitting in her favorite armchair with her husband, she could

scanning for what might go wrong next.
