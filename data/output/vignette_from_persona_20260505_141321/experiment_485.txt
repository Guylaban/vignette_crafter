Elena has worked from her small apartment for the past six years, the headset of her call center job
a near-permanent fixture. At seventy-eight, the work provides a structure to days that would
otherwise feel formless and vast. The calls are usually about billing errors or service outages, a
predictable rhythm she finds tolerable. But sometimes, a customer will mention a recent death, a
spouse lost, a parent gone, and the carefully constructed wall around her past crumbles. The polite
script vanishes from her mind, and all she can hear is a ringing silence, the same that followed the
blast. Her throat tightens, a familiar ache radiates from the base of her skull down her neck, and
the words to apologize or offer condolences simply will not form.

It has been two years since the day she and her husband, Mateo, were at the downtown market. They
were picking out vegetables for a soup he was planning to make, his hand resting on the small of her
back. She remembers the bright orange of the carrots and the sound of his laughter. Then, there is a
gap. The next thing she knows is pressure and dust, the sensation of falling without seeing where
she was going. When she tries to grasp the memory, it’s like trying to hold smoke. There are no
clear images, no sequence of events she can narrate. There is only the phantom smell of concrete
dust, the searing pain in her neck as her head was thrown back, and an overwhelming sense of Mateo’s
absence. She knows what happened, of course. She has read the news reports. But when she tries to
describe it, to give her own account, the words dissolve. It lives in her body, not in her language.
There are pieces she cannot locate at all, entire minutes that are simply gone, leaving a terrifying
void in her recollection.

In the months that followed, Elena’s world began to shrink. The city map in her mind was redrawn,
with a large, forbidden zone around the market square. She takes a bus route that adds forty minutes
to any necessary trip just to avoid getting within a dozen blocks of the location. The anniversary
of the attack, and their wedding anniversary which falls in the same month, are days she spends in
bed with the curtains drawn, refusing to answer the phone. She had come to believe that she had
failed in her most important role in life: to grow old with Mateo, to protect their shared future.
On that day, she had been helpless, a passive object thrown by a force she couldn't see or
comprehend, and that feeling has never left her. She is certain that she is fundamentally incapable
of stopping bad things from happening.

This certainty has built a wall between her and her children. When they visit, she feels a painful
distance. She cannot bring herself to share her grief, because to speak of it is to invite the
wordless horror back in. When they hug her, she remains stiff, unable to reciprocate the warmth.
Being emotionally close to them feels like a risk she cannot afford; the thought of enduring another
loss is unbearable, so she keeps them at arm's length to preempt the pain. Her daughter once found
her staring blankly at the wall, rubbing her neck, and asked what was wrong. Elena simply shook her
head, the explanation trapped behind a wall of silence. The consequence was a slow, painful drifting
apart, her children’s visits becoming less frequent, their calls more cautious.

It was a call at work last week that finally brought her to seek help. A man, his voice heavy with
grief, was cancelling his wife’s phone line. He spoke of his loneliness, of the empty space in his
home. Elena felt the familiar tightening in her chest, the pain flaring in her neck. She couldn’t
speak. The man, misinterpreting her silence for impatience, grew angry. After she finally managed to
end the call, she sat in the ringing quiet of her apartment and was overcome by a profound sense of
hopelessness. She saw her future as an endless series of these moments: isolated, trapped with a
memory she couldn't articulate, and failing even at the simple task of speaking to a stranger. She
is terrified of therapy, of being asked to find words for the unspeakable, but the thought of living
out the rest of her days this way has finally become more frightening.
