Amir’s world had shrunk to the size of his studio apartment and the path to the bus stop. At forty-

chaotic symphony of the fast-food restaurant. A dropped tray of fries would send a jolt of ice

overlapping buzzers and beeps of the fryers and drink machines kept a knot of tension permanently
lodged in his shoulders. He was on high alert from the moment he clocked in, interpreting every
unexpected sound as a prelude to violence. This vigilance was exhausting, but the alternative,
letting his guard down, felt like an impossibility.

It had all started eight months ago, in the late afternoon. He’d been working at his cousin’s small
grocery, stacking oranges as the 5 p.m. sunlight streamed through the front window. A man had burst
in, his voice a raw, angry gash in the quiet store. He screamed at Amir’s cousin to empty the
register, his words laced with a venomous contempt. “Faster, you idiot! Are you stupid?” When his
cousin fumbled with the key, the man’s rage escalated. He turned on Amir, who stood frozen by the

blow, pinning him in place. Then came the sound—a sickening, explosive crash as the man swept an
entire shelf of glass olive oil bottles onto the tile floor. The noise was absolute, a final,
shattering punctuation mark on the terror of the moment.

Amir never went back to the grocery. He found the job at "Burger Barn" a few weeks later, desperate
for an income. But the triggers were everywhere. Last week, his shift manager, a woman half his age,
had corrected him for putting too much ice in a soda. "No, Amir, it's a half-scoop, remember?" Her
voice was patient, but all he heard was the robber's derisive shout of "Useless." His heart began to
pound against his ribs, his palms grew slick with sweat, and he had to mumble an excuse about
needing the restroom, where he stood gripping the sink until the shaking subsided. He had come to
believe that any form of criticism was a direct precursor to attack, a sign that he had failed and
punishment was imminent.

The end of his shift was a daily ordeal. As 5 p.m. approached, the same time of day the robbery
occurred, a familiar dread would begin to creep in. He would watch the clock, the final fifteen
minutes stretching into an eternity of suspense. Once he was out the door, he would walk, almost
run, to the bus stop. If the bus was not immediately visible down the street, panic would set in. A
delay could not simply be traffic; in Amir’s mind, it meant something terrible had happened. A
crash, a hijacking, a disaster unfolding just out of sight. He would stand at the curb, scanning the
street, his body rigid with anticipation.

The only place this feeling receded was inside his apartment. It was his fortress, the one location

the sink, and rarely ventured out after dark. His life had become a simple, rigid loop: work, bus,
apartment. He had stopped returning calls from his cousins, unable to explain why the thought of a
family dinner felt as threatening as walking into a war zone. His world was now divided into two

was terrifying. What frightened him most was the possibility that his safe zone could shrink even
further, until it was nothing but the space between his own ears.
