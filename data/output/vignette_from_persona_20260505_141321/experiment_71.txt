Mateo sits stiffly in the chair, his calloused welder’s hands resting on his knees, palms down, as
if to hold himself in place. At seventy, his body is a testament to a life of hard, physical work,
but his eyes are far older, carrying the weight of something that happened nearly fifty years ago in
a country he has not seen since. The story has lived inside him, mostly in silence. It was in a
small, windowless room where his life fractured. He had been a university student, full of political
fervour and loud opinions. He came to believe this was his fatal error; the event, he remains
certain, happened because of the way he acted, because he had been too bold. They took him in the
night. The memory isn’t a neat narrative he can recount. When he has tried, perhaps for his late
wife, Elena, the words would tangle and choke him. He would start to explain the feeling of the
rough concrete floor against his cheek, the smell of stale sweat and fear, and then the thread would
vanish, leaving him adrift in a sea of raw sensation he could not articulate.

What he remembers most clearly are the moments of utter paralysis. He recalls one of his captors
explaining, with chilling calm, all the things that were about to happen to him, and the absolute
certainty that there was no way out. This feeling of being trapped, of having no options, is the
ghost that haunts his waking life. It can find him anywhere. Last month, at the fabrication shop,
his foreman boxed him into a corner on a tight deadline for a complex pipe fitting. As the foreman
stood over him, listing the impossible demands, the workshop air suddenly grew thin and heavy, the
scent of ozone from the welding torch turning metallic and sharp, like blood. Mateo felt the old,
familiar helplessness wash over him. He became overly compliant, nodding, agreeing to everything,
his voice a strained whisper. It was the only way he knew to survive a threat: make himself small,
agreeable, invisible. He saw the foreman’s surprise, but the internal storm of rage and shame was so
consuming he could barely register it. He felt an intense, burning anger at the man, but even more
so at himself. He will never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened all those
years ago, for not fighting back in that room, and every time he fawns and complies now, it feels
like the same failure, repeated.

The memories, when they break through the dam of suppression he has spent a lifetime building, are
not memories at all. They are present-tense ambushes. A car backfiring on his street is not a
similar sound; it *is* the sound of the pistol being cocked beside his ear, the cold click of the
hammer echoing in the silent room. In that instant, he is no longer a 70-year-old widower in his
quiet Chicago neighbourhood; he is twenty years old and on the floor, convinced his life is over.
The past collapses into the present with a terrifying, visceral force. To cope, he learned to float
away. When the images start to flicker—a face contorted in a sneer, the glint of a tool—he detaches,
watching the scene as if it’s a poorly made film happening to a stranger. It’s a desperate attempt
to create distance, but the rage always finds him. The rage is pure and clean, a searing fury at his
torturers, but it quickly curdles into self-blame.

For decades, Elena was his anchor. Her presence was a buffer, a reason to keep the past locked away.
Since her death last year, the silence in their small house has become cavernous, and the memories
fill the void. He feels exposed, believing you can never know who will harm you, scanning the faces
of neighbours with a suspicion that feels both exhausting and necessary. The old strategies are
failing. Trying hard not to think about it is like trying to hold back the tide. He is afraid of his
own anger, afraid of the moments he loses the thread of his own life story, afraid that one day, the
memory will ambush him and he won’t come back. It was after a particularly bad week, where he found
himself apologising to a dismissive clerk at the grocery store with the same desperate energy he
once used to plead for his life, that he finally made the call. He came to therapy not with a clear
goal, but with the exhausted plea of a man who has been fighting the same war for fifty years and
can no longer bear to fight it alone.
