Adam, a 35-year-old special education teacher, presented for treatment six months after being mugged
while walking home. The incident itself was brutally simple and lasted less than a minute, but its
aftermath had slowly dismantled his life. He had been on his usual route from the train station, a
shortcut he’d taken a hundred times, when a man stepped out from a narrow alley. The man’s shout was
the first thing that registered, a sharp, angry command to hand over his wallet. Before Adam could
fully process it, he was shoved hard against a brick wall, the impact jarring his head and sending a
searing pain through his neck. The memory of that moment doesn't play like a film in his mind;
instead, it exists as a series of distinct, high-definition photographs. He can call up the image of
the frayed cuff on the man’s jacket, the cold fury in his eyes, the specific cracks in the brickwork
inches from his face. When these images surface, unbidden, they are followed by a wave of intense,
burning rage at his own helplessness, which quickly curdles into a profound guilt. Somebody else, he
is certain, would not have gotten into this situation. They would have been more aware, or fought
back, or simply not been so weak.

In the weeks that followed, Adam’s world began to shrink. He came to believe that he was
fundamentally inadequate, unable to keep himself safe, and therefore had to be on guard at all
times. This belief became the organizing principle of his life. At school, a place that once felt
like a sanctuary, he now finds himself constantly scanning for exits as soon as he enters any
room—the classroom, the library, the faculty lounge. He is unable to relax, his body held in a state
of coiled tension even when helping a student with a reading exercise. This perpetual state of alert
has been exhausting, but the alternative feels far more dangerous. The triggers are everywhere. The
sudden, piercing shout of a student having a meltdown can instantly transport him. Just last week, a
child’s cry of frustration sent him stumbling back a step, heart hammering, the classroom walls
momentarily replaced by the grimy brick of the alley. He felt the phantom pressure on his neck and
the overwhelming certainty that he was about to be attacked again.

The somatic pain is a constant reminder. A dull ache in his neck is now his baseline, but when it
sharpens, it acts as a key, unlocking the photographic memories and the suffocating guilt that
accompanies them. Even the presence of authority figures, which should be reassuring, has become a
source of threat. When he sees a police car on his commute, he doesn’t feel safer; he feels the same
sense of exposure and scrutiny he did when the officers took his statement on the curb, their
questions feeling like an indictment of his own carelessness. As a result, his routines have been
entirely re-written. He no longer walks anywhere after dusk and spends a significant portion of his
salary on ride-shares, a financial strain he can ill afford. He has declined social invitations and
isolated himself from friends, unable to explain why the noise of a crowded bar feels like a
physical assault.

The decision to seek therapy came after a meeting with his school’s principal. A parent had
expressed concern that Adam seemed “on edge” and distant. It was a devastating blow. He realized
that his constant threat monitoring was not only failing to keep him safe but was also making him
fail his students, who rely on his calm and steady presence. He felt he was becoming a ghost in his
own life, a collection of reactions rather than a person. Adam is terrified of losing his job, the
last part of his identity that feels intact. More than anything, he wants to quiet the internal
critic that insists on his inadequacy and to once again feel that it is safe to simply exist in the
world without constantly preparing for the worst.
