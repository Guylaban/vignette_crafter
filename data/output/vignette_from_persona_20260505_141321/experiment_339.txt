Robert is a man who has spent his career holding space for the distress of others. At fifty-five, as
a counselor in a busy community practice, he had believed he understood the architecture of trauma.
He knew the theories, the terminology, the pathways to healing. But for the past eleven months,
since the fire, he has felt like a fraud, a cartographer lost in a terrifying landscape he can only
describe to others but cannot navigate himself. The event itself lives in him not as a coherent
story but as a collection of sensory shrapnel. It was a fundraiser at the old town hall, the air
thick with the smell of roasted coffee and old wood. Then, a different smell, sharp and chemical,
the scent of burning plastic from wiring in the walls. The smoke came next, thick and black,
swallowing the exits. He remembers the panic, the shouting, and a sudden, violent crash as a heavy
lighting fixture tore free from the ceiling and exploded on the floor nearby. He remembers the
feeling of being utterly powerless.

Now, the world is a minefield of sensory cues. Last week, a colleague, late for a meeting, tossed a
heavy binder onto her desk; the sudden thud made Robert flinch so hard he spilled hot tea down his
shirt, his heart instantly hammering against his ribs as if the ceiling were again about to fall.
The smell of a neighbor’s barbecue or even burnt toast in his own kitchen can send a jolt of panic
through him, a physical response so immediate and overwhelming that it leaves him dizzy and
breathless. He has come to believe his body is an enemy, a traitor that keeps reacting as if he is
still in the burning hall even when his mind knows he is safe in his quiet office. This conviction
fuels a state of exhausting watchfulness. He feels he has to be especially careful now, because the
fire taught him that catastrophe can erupt from the mundane at any moment.

His memories of the event are slippery and strange. Sometimes, a wave of pure, unadulterated terror
will wash over him while he’s driving or reading a client file. It’s the full emotional force of the
fire, a feeling of imminent death, but it arrives without any clear image or sound, leaving him
gasping for air and profoundly confused. When he does try to intentionally recall what happened, the
fragments that surface feel disturbingly detached, like watching a poorly made film about someone
else. The memory feels like it belongs to another person, and the disconnect is terrifying. The only
part that feels truly his is the smell; when a fragment of memory does break through, it often
brings with it the phantom scent of acrid smoke, so real he can almost taste it.

To manage this internal chaos, Robert’s life has shrunk. He is certain that if he allows himself to
truly think about the event, to let the images and feelings in, he will not be able to handle it. He
believes it would shatter him completely. This fear has become the organizing principle of his
existence. He works late, fills his evenings with mindless television, and does anything to avoid
the quiet moments when the thoughts might intrude. He has become a sentinel guarding his own mind,
constantly monitoring his thoughts for any sign of "going crazy," any hint that he is losing his
grip. This internal vigilance has bled into his relationships. He has systematically started to
avoid his friends, declining invitations and ignoring calls. The thought of sitting with them, of
pretending everything is normal while this battle rages inside him, feels impossible. The risk of
breaking down in front of them is too great.

He finally sought help after a session with a young client who was describing a panic attack. As the
client spoke, Robert felt his own chest tighten, his palms begin to sweat. He was no longer a
therapist in a safe room but a man trapped in a burning building, and he had to use every ounce of
his professional training to remain outwardly calm. The experience left him shaken to his core. He
sat in his office long after the client left, gripped by the fear that he was losing his ability to
function, not just as a person, but as the counselor he had built his life around being. He is here
now, not with a clear hope for recovery, but with the desperate, terrified wish to stop feeling like
he is at war with his own mind.
