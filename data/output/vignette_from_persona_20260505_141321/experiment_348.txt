within days, a terrifying inability to draw a full breath. She remembers the ride to the hospital in
fragments: the wail of the siren, the metallic taste of panic, and most of all, the crushing weight

the sharp, sterile scent of antiseptic that seemed to cling to the back of her throat. The core of
the memory, the part that replays itself without warning, is the feeling of drowning on dry land,
the desperate, silent fight for air while a ventilator did the work her body no longer could.

Now, that fight continues long after her recovery. The world, once a stage, has shrunk to the size
of her small apartment, the only place she feels she can breathe freely. This is her safe zone.
Outside, danger is everywhere. A simple walk up the stairs can leave her gasping, not from exertion,
but from a sudden, phantom pressure on her lungs. Her body reacts to threats before she has time to
think; the whiff of hand sanitizer from a passerby on the street is enough to make her heart hammer

immediately crosses the street, her breath catching in her throat. Keilani has stopped going to her

like the beginning of the illness all over again. She has turned down two orchestra tours and
stopped taking on new students. The career she spent decades building has been dismantled by a fear

coper, that some fundamental strength everyone else possesses is missing in her. This self-doubt has
become a constant companion, a whisper that tells her she can’t trust herself to do the right thing,
whether it’s performing a complex piece or simply managing her own health.
