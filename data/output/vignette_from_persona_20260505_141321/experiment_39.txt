Bao presents as a meticulously dressed 68-year-old public defender, though his posture is stooped,
and his eyes carry a profound weariness that belies the sharp crease in his trousers. He has come to
therapy eight months after the death of his wife, Mai, stating simply that he can no longer live the
way he has been living. The traumatic event occurred on a Tuesday evening in their home. Bao’s
memory of that night does not exist as a coherent story, but rather as a series of sharp,
disconnected sensory fragments that ambush him without warning. He recalls the splintering crack of
the back door frame, an image of a dark shape filling the doorway, and the glint of metal in the dim
light of the television. Most torturously, he remembers the sound of Mai calling his name from the
kitchen, a note of confusion in her voice just before it was cut short. He cannot piece these
moments together in a linear sequence; they exist as isolated islands of horror in his mind. When
these fragments surface, they are often accompanied by the distinct, phantom sound of that door
frame giving way, a sharp auditory echo that can make him physically flinch, even in the quiet of
his own office. With each recollection, he is swamped not just by terror, but by a wave of
overwhelming grief that feels as fresh and immobilizing as it did in the first days after her death.

In the months since the attack, Bao’s world has shrunk dramatically. He had come to believe that the
world was an fundamentally unsafe place and that he had been naive to ever think otherwise. His
home, once a sanctuary, now feels like a fortress under permanent siege. Every night, before
attempting to sleep, he engages in a rigid ritual of checking and re-checking every lock, rattling
each window sash, and pressing his hand against the door three times to confirm it is bolted. This
provides only fleeting relief. He lives in a state of constant, low-grade alarm, where any
unexpected sound can trigger a full-body panic. The backfire of a car on his street or the sudden
clang of a dropped pan in a neighbor’s apartment sends his heart racing, convinced for a terrifying
moment that it is happening again. He feels saturated by a toxic cocktail of emotions: shame at his
inability to protect Mai, a cold, hard anger at the perpetrators who were never caught, and a deep,
abiding fear that permeates every aspect of his existence.

His professional life, once a source of pride, has become another source of torment. As a public
defender, Bao’s days are spent in courthouses and meeting rooms, surrounded by strangers. He now
finds himself unable to focus on his clients’ cases, his attention instead consumed by a relentless
and exhausting hypervigilance. He scans every person who walks past him in the hallway, assessing
their gait, their hands, their eyes, searching for any sign of a threat. He interprets a sudden
movement or a loud voice as a precursor to violence, his body tensing as if preparing for an attack.
The work he once saw as a noble defense of the vulnerable now feels like being locked in a cage with
potential predators. To manage the constant anxiety and the surges of grief, he has developed two
primary coping strategies. At home, he avoids all news and television shows that might depict
violence, a self-imposed media blackout to prevent being ambushed by similar events. He has also
begun overeating, finding a temporary, dulling comfort in large meals consumed alone in the silence
of his kitchen, a way to fill the emptiness and quiet the intrusive thoughts and sounds for a short
while. The isolation this has created is profound. He came to therapy after a junior colleague
gently pointed out that he had jumped and sworn when she’d accidentally dropped a heavy legal binder
behind him. It was the moment of public exposure of his private terror that made him realize he
could not continue to manage this alone. He is afraid he will never feel safe again, but he holds a
desperate hope for a future where the memory of his wife can bring him comfort instead of just
horror.
