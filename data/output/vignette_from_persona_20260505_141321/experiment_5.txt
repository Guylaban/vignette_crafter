Bao does not like to walk past the campus security office. He takes a longer route to the library
now, adding ten minutes to his trip, just to avoid the sight of the navy-blue uniforms. Six months
ago, he had returned from a summer volunteering with a relief agency, a trip he had seen as the
first step toward a life of purpose. Now, he was certain he had failed in his most important role
before his life had even truly begun. The memory that fuels this conviction is not one of grandeur
or widespread disaster, but a small, quiet moment of terror at a dusty checkpoint. He and his team
were trying to deliver medical supplies when their van was stopped by local militia. The soldiers,
young men in mismatched fatigues, swarmed the vehicle. Bao remembers the intense, scrutinising gaze
of their commander, a man with cold eyes who watched him without blinking. As the soldiers pulled a
family from the line behind them, Bao was ordered to stay put. He stood frozen by the van, the
commander’s eyes fixed on him, a silent accusation. He did nothing as the family was dragged away.
He just stood there, watched.

Since his return to university, that feeling of being watched has followed him. In a crowded lecture
hall, he feels the weight of hundreds of eyes on the back of his neck, and is convinced everyone can
see his cowardice. He walks across campus with his gaze fixed on the pavement, certain that making
eye contact with anyone will invite the same intrusive, judgmental scrutiny he felt from the
commander. His body, he has come to believe, is an enemy combatant in his own life. A glimpse of a
police officer on the street corner is enough to make his heart hammer against his ribs and his
palms break out in a cold sweat. He knows he is safe, that this is a quiet university town in
America, but his body keeps reacting as if he is still in danger, still standing helpless at that
checkpoint. This physiological betrayal feels like proof of his own weakness, a constant reminder
that he can't stop bad things from happening to him.

The world that once felt full of connection has shrunk to the size of his small dorm room. In the
first few weeks back, friends would text, asking him to hang out, to come to parties, to join their
study groups. Bao would read the messages and feel a wave of shame. How could he explain the person
he had become? They knew the idealistic, confident young man who had left in May, not the hollowed-
out version who returned. He was certain they would see the failure he saw in himself. So, he
stopped replying. He started avoiding the dining hall during peak hours and skipping events he once
loved. The invitations eventually slowed, then stopped altogether. The silence of his phone is now a
constant, quiet hum of isolation.

This withdrawal has solidified his belief that he is fundamentally broken. He sees his life as a
series of defensive manoeuvres against a world full of potential threats. The sight of a uniform,
the feeling of an uninvited gaze, the presence of any authority figure—all of it confirms his
deepest fear: that he is utterly helpless, a passive observer to his own life unraveling. It was
after a particularly bad panic attack, triggered by two police officers calmly ordering coffee in
front of him at a cafe, that he finally called the university’s counselling service. He fled the
cafe, heart pounding, and spent the rest of the day locked in his room, shaking. What brought him to
therapy was not a hope for a cure, but a desperate, terrifying thought: that if something didn’t
change, the feeling of being watched, judged, and perpetually unsafe would be the only thing he had
left.
