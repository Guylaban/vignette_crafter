Mark has always taken pride in his ability to see a space not for what it is, but for what it could
be. As an architect, his mind is trained to map sightlines, traffic flow, and structural integrity.
But for the last seven months, this skill has felt more like a curse. It began on a Tuesday
afternoon in a downtown cafe. He was reviewing blueprints with a client when the violence erupted
with a shocking suddenness. It was just noise at first—a chair scraping violently, a shout—and then
the sickening thud of a man’s head hitting a marble countertop. Mark looked up to see one patron
brutally attacking another. He remembers the scene in disjointed, searing flashes: the crimson
blooming on a white tile floor, the glint of the overhead lights, the acrid smell of burnt coffee
from a shattered pot mixing with something metallic and sweet. A wave of nausea so intense it
buckled him forward was his only physical response. He remained frozen in his seat, a silent
witness.

In the weeks that followed, the memory refused to settle into a coherent story. It returned not as a
linear narrative, but as a sensory assault. The smell of a strong brew in his own office kitchen
could trigger the lurch in his stomach. A loud noise on a construction site would make him flinch so
hard his foreman asked if he was alright. He would be sitting at his drafting table and suddenly
feel as if he were back in the cafe, the same terror constricting his chest, the entire event
happening right now, in real-time. When his wife, concerned by his new silence, would ask him what
was wrong, he found he couldn't articulate the horror. He knew what he had seen, but the words to
describe the raw brutality of it simply wouldn't form, leaving him feeling profoundly alone in his
experience. He felt set apart from everyone who hadn't seen what he had seen, as if he were living
behind a pane of glass.

His world began to shrink. He started insisting on client meetings only at his firm’s office. When
he had to enter a new building for a site survey, the first thing he did, before even assessing the
structure, was locate every possible exit. He found he couldn’t sit in a restaurant or a conference
room unless his back was against a wall, a solid surface protecting him from the rear, his eyes free
to scan the entrance. He felt a gnawing certainty that he was fundamentally helpless, that the world
was a chaotic place where terrible things could happen at any moment without reason. His
hypervigilance was a desperate attempt to prove that belief wrong, to see the threat before it
materialized.

The deepest wound, however, was the one he inflicted on himself. Over and over, his mind replayed
the moment he froze. There was something wrong with him, he had come to believe, some intrinsic
fault in his character that made him a man who watches instead of acts. He was convinced that his
inaction was not a result of shock, but a reflection of a permanent, shameful weakness. This belief
became the quiet, corrosive engine of his distress. The final impetus for seeking help came during a
family dinner at his sister's house. His young nephew, playing, ran up behind him and grabbed his
shoulders with a shout. Mark leaped from his chair, heart hammering, a strangled cry escaping his
lips, nearly knocking the boy over. The look of fear and confusion on his family’s faces was
unbearable. It was then he finally admitted that he could not architect a solution for this on his
own. He came to therapy not knowing if he could ever feel safe again, but with a desperate hope to
quiet the alarms constantly ringing in his head and to find the words for the memory he could no
longer bear to carry alone.
