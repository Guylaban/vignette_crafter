spreading on the concrete, the way the world seemed to go silent and loud all at once. She remained
frozen in her chair, a spectator to a horror she couldn't comprehend.

In the weeks that followed, the world began to feel like a series of potential ambushes. The open,
collaborative space of the university architecture studio, once a place of inspiration, became a
source of constant, low-grade terror. She found herself unable to sit with her back to the door, her
focus shattered by the need to track every person who entered or left the room. A dropped T-square
would send a jolt of adrenaline through her body, her heart hammering against her ribs as if
responding to a gunshot. She was perpetually on guard, her muscles tense, her senses stretched to

creativity from her work. Her meticulous blueprints were now smudged with shaky lines, her
concentration fractured by the constant, scanning vigilance she felt was necessary for survival.

The nights offered no reprieve. Sleep, she had come to believe, was a dangerous vulnerability. As
soon as she turned off the lights, her mind would supply a slideshow of the event, replaying the man
falling, the sound of his head hitting the pavement. To combat this, she began a ritual of
resistance against her own exhaustion. She would lie in bed, rigid, her ears straining to catch any
unfamiliar sound from the hallway of her dorm or the street below. She was certain that if she could
just stay alert, if she didn't let herself slip into the defenselessness of sleep, she could prevent
something terrible from happening again. Most nights, she would only succumb to a fitful, dream-
plagued sleep in the pale light of early morning, waking up feeling more tired than when she went to
bed.

This constant state of threat was saturated with a toxic brew of emotions. Waves of horror would

most persistent feelings were guilt and shame. She would torment herself with questions: Why did I
