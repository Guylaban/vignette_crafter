eventually tells began not in an office, but in a dusty, sun-baked compound in Kandahar, where she
was a civilian contractor maintaining communications equipment for the military. The day of the
attack was unremarkable until the moment it wasn't. A sudden, deafening explosion shook the
building, and the world dissolved into dust and the high-pitched ringing in her ears. Her first
coherent thought was of the server racks. When she scrambled to the comms center, the air was thick
with the acrid, unforgettable smell of fried circuits and melting plastic. A rocket had hit the
adjacent barracks, and through the haze, she saw the frantic work of medics and the rhythmic *thump-
thump-thump* of a medevac helicopter landing, its blades kicking up more sand and debris.

For years after she returned home, Mai believed she had left that day behind her. She met and
married a man who knew only that she had "worked in Afghanistan," a topic she made clear was not
open for discussion. She found a stable job at a corporate headquarters, a world of climate-
controlled quiet that felt a universe away from the war. The past remained dormant until a Tuesday
afternoon when a server in her own office overheated. A plume of grey smoke, carrying that specific,
sharp smell of burning electronics, billowed from the back of the machine. The scent hit her, and
she was no longer in a suburban office park; she was back in the Kandahar comms center, the floor
vibrating from the helicopter, the air choked with dust. Her heart hammered against her ribs, sweat
slicked her skin, and she gasped for air, collapsing against a wall as her colleagues stared in
confusion.

Since that day, her workplace has become a landscape of potential threats. The low hum of the server
room, the fluorescent lights, the very air she breathes feels charged with menace. Her life has
shrunk in response. She avoids any situation that might provoke a strong emotion, which meant she
stopped going to movies, engaging in political debates, or even having difficult conversations with
her husband. He grew frustrated with her emotional distance, her refusal to talk about what haunted
her, and the marriage eventually fractured under the strain of the silence. Now divorced, her
isolation is more acute. When a memory fragment breaks through—the image of a wounded soldier, the
sound of a distant siren that morphs into the whine of an incoming rocket—she has found a desperate
way to stop it. She will dig her fingernails into the flesh of her forearm, pressing down until the
sharp, immediate sting of pain is all she can feel, a physical anchor that pulls her out of the
past.

Her journey home from work is a gauntlet. The sound of a news helicopter overhead can send her
ducking into a doorway, her body braced for an impact that never comes. She feels a primal fear of
strangers, a certainty that anyone walking behind her is a threat. She finds herself constantly
glancing over her shoulder, pretending to check her phone while she scans the street behind her.
This hypervigilance is depleting, leaving her drained and irritable by the time she reaches her
apartment. The final impetus for seeking help came last week. A coworker burned popcorn in the

pillar of stability in her life, was finally greater than the fear of speaking about the

is losing control and can no longer manage the war inside her on her own.
