Liam is twenty-one, but he feels much older. The six months since his honorable discharge have
stretched into a lifetime of quiet, exhausting vigilance. He drives a city bus now, a job he took
because it felt straightforward, a predictable route with clear stops. He had hoped for routine, for
monotony, but has found only a moving container of potential threats. The traumatic event that
redefined his world replays not as a clear story, but as a series of sensory assaults. He remembers
the deafening bang of the IED, a sound that ripped through the air and the armored vehicle alike. He
remembers the thick, acrid smell of burning fuel and electronics that followed, a scent that clung
to his uniform for days. The rest comes in disconnected pieces: the jarring lurch of the vehicle, a
flash of his friend’s face contorted in a silent scream, the chaotic shouts, and then the percussive
*whump-whump-whump* of the medevac helicopter blades beating the dusty air as he was carried away.
He knows what happened, but when he tries to explain it, the words catch in his throat, inadequate
to describe the sheer sensory violence of it all.

These fragments do not stay in the past. They bleed into his present, ambushing him on his daily
route. A car backfiring in traffic becomes the IED’s initial blast, and for a terrifying second,
Liam is no longer on a city street but back on a dirt road in a forgotten province. His hands grip
the wheel, his heart hammering against his ribs, and the phantom smell of smoke fills his nostrils.
A news helicopter circling a downtown building can send a jolt of pure adrenaline through him, the
sound of its rotors an undeniable signal of emergency and loss. He has come to believe the world is
a fundamentally dangerous place, and that safety is an illusion. This belief has reshaped his
existence into a state of constant alert. On the bus, every passenger is assessed, every unattended
bag a potential threat. He watches people in his mirrors, his body tensed for an attack that never
comes, leaving him drained and hollow by the end of his shift.

To manage this perpetual state of high alert, Liam’s world has shrunk. He used to love going to
baseball games with his friends, but the thought of being lost in a massive crowd, unable to see
every exit and monitor every face, is now unbearable. He makes excuses when they call. His parents,
devout churchgoers, have asked why he no longer joins them for Sunday service; he can’t explain that
the quiet, enclosed space packed with people feels like the most dangerous place on earth. Instead,
he spends his evenings and weekends in the solitary glow of his phone or a video game console. The
endless scrolling and the repetitive missions are a functional anesthetic, a way to occupy his mind
so thoroughly that the film of the explosion cannot play. It is the only time he feels a semblance
of quiet.

The isolation, however, has deepened a sense of despair that frightens him. The constant vigilance
has yielded no peace, only exhaustion. The avoidance has cost him his friends. In the quiet moments,
when the digital distractions fade, a heavy question settles over him: is a life lived this way
worth living at all? It was after a particularly bad panic attack, triggered by the smell of burnt
wiring from a faulty toaster in the depot breakroom, that he finally made a call. He came to therapy
not with a clear goal, but with a profound sense of being broken. What he hopes for is simple, yet
feels impossible: to one day sit in the driver's seat of his bus and see only the road ahead, not
the ghosts of the one behind him.
