Omar, a 34-year-old publisher, presented for treatment six months after his wife, Layla, was
violently mugged on a city bus. He described their life before the incident as stable and
fulfilling, a partnership built on shared ambitions and mutual respect. That evening, however,
shattered the foundation of his perceived safety. They were heading home late after a book launch
party, both bone-weary from the long day. Omar remembers the profound exhaustion, a heavy-limbed
fatigue that made his eyelids feel like lead. It was this feeling of being worn down, he recalled,
that made him feel slow and useless when the man boarded at a poorly lit stop.

The memory does not exist for him as a coherent narrative. Instead, it surfaces in sharp, disjointed
fragments that ambush him throughout the day. He sees the attacker’s hand gripping the overhead
rail, the glint of a small knife, the torn fabric of Layla’s coat as she was thrown against a seat.
These images play on a relentless loop in his mind, a silent film he cannot turn off. Sometimes, the
silence is broken by sounds that seem to manifest in the room with him: the sharp hiss of the bus’s
air brakes, Layla’s short, terrified cry, the dull thud as her head hit the window. He will be in
his office, proofreading a manuscript, and suddenly the quiet hum of the computer is replaced by
that awful sound, and he loses minutes, sometimes longer, staring blankly at the page.

Since the attack, Omar’s world has shrunk. He is certain that danger is everywhere, and that he
simply failed to see it before. He now scans every environment for threats. In editorial meetings,
he finds himself unable to focus on literary merits, instead studying the body language of his
colleagues, assessing the tension in their jaws or the way they hold their hands. His gaze drifts to
objects on the table—a heavy glass paperweight, a sharp letter opener—cataloging them as potential
weapons. He has started declining invitations to author events or industry mixers if they are held
in venues he doesn't know. The thought of entering an unfamiliar space, unable to immediately map
the exits and assess the potential for harm, fills him with a quiet, cold dread.

His professional life has been strained by this, but the most painful changes have occurred at home.
The bus is, of course, out of the question. He and Layla now spend a significant amount on taxis, a
daily, costly reminder of what happened. More insidiously, the attack has invaded their most private
spaces. The image of Layla’s vulnerability, her coat torn and her body exposed in that moment of
violence, has become fused with his own sense of physical exposure. He can no longer tolerate being
naked, even when alone. In the shower, he feels a sickening sense of defenselessness, and he has
begun showering only late at night, after Layla is asleep. Intimacy has become impossible; the
vulnerability it requires now feels like an invitation for disaster.

Omar sought therapy after Layla found him one evening standing motionless in their living room,
staring at the street below. He had been there for nearly an hour, watching every person who passed
their building, his body rigid with tension. She had told him, her voice gentle but firm, that while
her physical wounds had healed, she was watching him disappear. Omar admitted that he feels he is
failing as a husband and a protector. He is haunted by the belief that his exhaustion that night
made him complicit, that a more alert man could have prevented it all. Now, he lives in a state of
constant, exhausting vigilance, terrified of failing her again.
