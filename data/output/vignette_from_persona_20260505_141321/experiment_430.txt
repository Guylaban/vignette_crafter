artificial sigh of a ventilator. He slept, when he could, on a stiff vinyl cot next to her bed, the
constant, low hum of the machines a soundtrack to his helplessness. The sharp, clean scent of
antiseptic seemed to permeate everything, a smell he now associates with the precipice of loss.

Priya made a full recovery, but the world Arun returned to was fundamentally altered. He had come to

guard, causing his heart to pound and his palms to sweat. He would have to flee his desk and walk

impossible. His work, once a source of joy and meticulous focus, now feels like a minefield.

so profound it was physical. The first night, he sat up in bed until dawn, watching the door,
certain that some disaster would befall them in her absence. He mentally rehearsed every possible
catastrophe: a fire, a break-in, Priya waking up with another fever. He mapped out the route to the

floor under his feet. He believes this constant preparation is the only thing keeping his family
safe, but it leaves him perpetually exhausted.

rereading articles about the initial misdiagnosis of meningitis. He ruminates for hours on how he
might have confronted that first doctor, the words he should have used, the scene he should have
made. He fantasises about filing a lawsuit, not for money, but for an admission of fallibility, a
way to punish the carelessness that almost cost him everything. This quest for a target for his rage
consumes the creative energy his job demands.

come to therapy not because he believes he can ever feel truly safe again, but because he is
desperate to stop his own fear from poisoning the family he fought so hard to keep whole.
