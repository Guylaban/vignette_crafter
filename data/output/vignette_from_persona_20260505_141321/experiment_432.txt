twenty-six, his work as a fisherman has weathered his hands and face, but a different kind of
tension now pulls at the corners of his eyes and mouth. The story of the past eight months unfolds

terrifying speed. He was mistaken for someone else, dragged from a bar, and held for two days in a
windowless basement. His captors wanted information he didn't have, and their methods were designed
to break him. They bound him to a metal chair, his arms wrenched behind his back, and for hours on
end, they played a looping recording of babies wailing at a deafening volume, the high-pitched,
frantic sounds drilling into his skull until he couldn't form a coherent thought. Between the noise,
they would beat his back and ribs, the sharp, radiating pain a punctuation mark in the auditory
assault.

When he was finally released and made it home, he believed the worst was over. But the basement came
back with him. At first, it was the rage, a white-hot fury that would surge whenever he remembered
the feeling of utter helplessness in that chair. He would be mending a net on the docks, his hands
going through the familiar motions, when the memory would surface. His breath would catch, the
phantom ache would blossom across his ribs, and his shoulders would instinctively tense and pull
back, his body reenacting its bound position. The fury was so intense it felt like a physical force,

the original injury that had long since healed.

His world began to shrink, reconfigured around a new and constant sense of threat. The bus he used

the closing doors sent a wave of panic through him so strong he had to force his way off at the next
stop, he gave up, adding an hour-long walk to his pre-dawn commute. He finds no peace at home,
either. He spends his evenings listening, certain that the groan of the house settling or the scrape

Sarah, doesn't understand why he can't relax, why he startles at every noise.

the dark, the wailing coming from the speakers. He dropped the basket and fled the store, leaving a
bewildered Sarah behind. Since then, he has avoided any place where there might be children. This
has made him a ghost at family gatherings and a stranger in his own neighborhood. His work, once a

afraid it will one day consume him entirely, leaving nothing of the man he used to be.
