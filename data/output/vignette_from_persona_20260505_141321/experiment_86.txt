Elena had always moved through the world with a sense of earned authority. At seventy-nine, her
posture was still ramrod straight, a habit forged over decades, first as a pioneering combat pilot
in the Air Force and later as a respected instructor. She was a woman who understood calculated
risk, who could read a sky full of threats and navigate a path through them. But for the past six
months, the world on the ground had become an indecipherable map of danger. Her own city, once a
familiar grid, now felt like enemy territory. The shift began on a sunny Saturday at a downtown
rally for veterans’ healthcare reform. Elena, a widow for ten years, felt a civic duty to attend.
The crowd was dense and vocal but peaceful, a river of people flowing through the plaza. She
remembered the smell of roasting corn from a street vendor’s cart mingling with the scent of hot
asphalt. Then, the sirens started, a piercing shriek that seemed to tear the fabric of the
afternoon.

The memory of what happened next doesn’t play like a film for Elena. It exists as a series of stark,
silent photographs that flash into her mind without warning. The first is a snapshot of the crowd
surging backward, faces contorted in confusion and fear. Another is the glint of the sun on a riot
helmet. The most vivid is the image of a young police officer’s face, his mouth a tight line of
anger, as he shoved her backward. She remembers the sensation of falling, the rough scrape of the
pavement against her hands, and then the final, searing snapshot: a pair of polished black boots
inches from her face, the dark serge of a uniform pant leg filling her vision. In those moments, the
smell of corn was abruptly overpowered by an acrid, chemical odor that burned her nostrils. That
smell sometimes returns to her now, phantom-like, while she’s washing dishes or reading the
newspaper.

In the weeks that followed, Elena’s meticulously ordered life began to unravel. Her pilot’s instinct
for scanning, once used to identify distant aircraft, was now turned on her immediate surroundings.
She found she was unable to relax, even in the perceived safety of her own apartment. A walk to the
grocery store became a tactical mission. She would constantly scan the street, her body tensing at
the sight of any uniformed official, even a mail carrier. Inside the store, her eyes would
automatically catalogue potential weapons: a stack of heavy canned goods, a wine bottle, the box
cutter by the register. She had come to believe that threat was everywhere, and only she was
vigilant enough to see it.

Avoidance became her new protocol. The crowded VFW hall where she’d met friends for lunch every
Tuesday for years felt suffocating, the press of bodies a prelude to chaos. After two attempts where
she had to flee, her heart hammering against her ribs like a piston, she stopped going altogether.
The red wool coat her late husband had bought her, the one she was wearing that day, was pushed to
the very back of her closet. She couldn’t look at it, let alone wear it or anything of a similar
color. It felt like painting a target on her back. Sirens, once a mundane feature of city life, now
sent a jolt of pure ice through her veins, a powerful physical reaction that left her breathless and
trembling. She began charting her walks to avoid the local police precinct, even if it added twenty
minutes to her errand.

Sleep offered no escape. As soon as she lay down in the quiet of her bedroom, the snapshot memories
would begin their assault. To fend them off, she started delaying sleep, staying up until two or
three in the morning with the television on, waiting for a profound exhaustion to finally claim her.
This new life felt like a betrayal of the woman she had been—the woman who had faced down enemy fire
at altitude but was now defeated by a trip to the farmers’ market. The final impetus to seek help
came when her grandson, a high school senior, invited her to his graduation. The thought of the
packed auditorium, the inevitable presence of security or police, and the lack of a clear exit
strategy triggered such a wave of panic that she had to feign illness. The shame of that lie, of
missing such a milestone, was heavier than the fear itself. Elena came to therapy not with a clear
objective, but with a desperate hope to once again feel like the pilot of her own life, rather than
a passenger bracing for a crash.
