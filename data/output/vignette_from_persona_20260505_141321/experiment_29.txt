Caleb sits rigidly in the chair, his hands clasped so tightly his knuckles are white. At nineteen,
he carries a tension that seems at odds with his youthful appearance. He explains that it has been
six months since the flash flood that tore through his neighbourhood, and in that time, he has
become a stranger to himself. The person he was before, he explains, is gone. He used to be happy,
easygoing; now, he feels he is always miserable. He is certain that some fundamental part of him was
broken in the floodwaters and can never be repaired.

The event itself returns to him not as a story, but in jarring, disjointed snapshots. He doesn't
remember a clear sequence, but rather a series of stark images: the brown water seeping with
impossible speed under the garage door, the sight of his neighbour's sedan lifting and turning
sideways in the current, the glint of a floating propane tank. When these pictures flash into his
mind, it isn't just a memory; he feels the same raw terror he felt when he was trapped in the attic,
listening to the house groan under the pressure of the water. With the terror sometimes comes a
smell—a phantom odour of wet earth, mildew, and sewage that is so vivid he finds himself checking
his clothes or looking around the room for the source. He has learned to brace for these moments,
trying with all his might to push the thoughts and smells away, a constant, exhausting battle to
keep the past from flooding the present.

In the weeks that followed the flood, Caleb’s world began to shrink. He started by avoiding the
news, especially the weather reports. Then he found he couldn't walk near the creek at the edge of
town. Soon, he was avoiding anything he thought might be stressful. This has had a profound impact
on his job at a fast-food restaurant. The gurgle of the large drain in the back parking lot sounds
too much like the sounds he heard that night, so he makes excuses to avoid taking out the trash. The
smell of the dirty mop water in the utility closet can sometimes trigger the phantom smell of the
flood, sending a jolt of panic through him. He has become intensely fearful of making a mistake, of
having a customer get angry, of any interaction that isn't perfectly smooth. Before every shift, and
often before speaking to a customer or his manager, he mentally rehearses what he needs to say,
trying to script every possible conversation to prevent any unexpected stress.

This constant state of high alert has hollowed out his social life and his sense of self. He feels
unrecognisable. He looks in the mirror and sees someone who is perpetually anxious and withdrawn.
His friends still call sometimes, but he usually ignores them, the effort of pretending to be okay
feeling monumental. He is convinced that this new version of himself is permanent, that he has been
damaged in some deep and irreversible way. The happy, confident teenager who worked at the burger
joint to save up for a car is a ghost. In his place is this nervous, miserable person who has to
practice saying, "Would you like fries with that?" in his head three times before he can get the
words out. It was after his manager took him aside last week, gently warning him that his avoidance
of certain duties and his visible anxiety were becoming a problem, that Caleb finally decided to
seek help. He doesn't know if it's possible, but what he wants more than anything is to feel like
himself again, to believe that the person he was before the water rose isn't gone for good.
