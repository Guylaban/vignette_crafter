meaningful. The moment it all changed began not with a bang, but with the squeal of tires from a

registered the men in the back, the glint of metal, and then chaos erupted. The memory doesn't play
like a film for him; it’s a series of frozen, blindingly bright snapshots. The overturned sacks of
grain, the screams of the mothers he had been laughing with moments before, the feeling of being
thrown against a wall. When he talks about it, which is rare, he describes it from a distance, as if
narrating a scene he watched on a screen. This detachment is a skill he has honed; it feels like the
only way to keep the images from swallowing him whole.

moving. His wife, Amara, found him crouched behind the sofa, heart hammering against his ribs. Since
then, a constant, low-level hum of alertness has become his baseline. He is always scanning, always
watchful. In his open-plan office, where he works as a social media manager, he can only sit at a
desk that faces the entrance, his back firmly against a wall. He tracks everyone who comes and goes,
his focus splintering with every new arrival. This state of being perpetually on guard has shredded
his ability to concentrate. The vibrant, fast-paced digital world he once navigated with ease now
feels like a minefield of overstimulation. He will stare at a screen for an hour, tasked with
writing a cheerful caption for a client, but his mind is a blank slate, occupied only by the quiet,
persistent task of monitoring his surroundings for any sign of threat.

The hardest part for Kofi is the gnawing sense of unfairness that churns in his gut. He follows the
news from the region obsessively, searching for any mention of the attack, any report of arrests or
accountability. There is never anything. The men in the truck simply vanished back into the chaos
they created, and the world moved on. This lack of consequence has planted a deep and bitter root of
injustice inside him. It feels personal, a confirmation that what happened to him, to the people he
was trying to help, simply didn't matter. This feeling is tangled up with a profound loss of faith
in himself. He replays the moment of the attack, convinced that he froze, that a better man would
have done something—anything. This belief that he cannot rely on his own instincts has become a core
truth. He questions every decision, from which route to take to the grocery store to how to respond
to an email. He feels fundamentally broken, as if a critical component of himself was shattered in
the dust that day and can never be recovered.

avoided conversations about his work abroad and changed the channel if a news report felt too close
to home. But the strategies are failing. Last month, his manager put him on a performance

with a quiet desperation, terrified of losing his job and the life he has worked so hard to build,
but even more afraid that the watchful, untrusting man he has become is all that is left of him.
