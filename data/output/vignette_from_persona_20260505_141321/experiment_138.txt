of being connected to the city's pulse. Now, eight months after her husband Bao’s death, that same

feels like a human seismograph, registering every minor tremor as a potential earthquake. Her body
is perpetually coiled, ready for a blow that never comes but always feels imminent. Even here,
surrounded by colleagues in the middle of a Tuesday afternoon, she cannot relax. She has come to
believe that safety is an illusion, a story people tell themselves between disasters.

The disaster that shattered her story began with a missed call. She was on a deadline, chasing a
quote for a piece on city council rezoning, and had let Bao’s call go to voicemail, texting a quick
"Almost done, see you soon." He was meant to pick her up. An hour later, when she finally packed her
bag and stepped out into the evening air, his car wasn't there. The calls to his phone went
unanswered, each ring tightening a knot of dread in her stomach. The call that finally came was not
from him, but from a calm, detached voice at the county hospital. There had been an accident. He’d
had a massive, sudden heart attack while driving home. By the time she arrived, breathless and
frantic, he was already gone.

Since that night, Mai’s world has been governed by a state of high alert. As a journalist, she was
trained to be watchful, but this is different. This is a corrosive, exhausting vigilance that finds
threat in the mundane. A ringing phone, particularly an unknown number, sends a spike of ice through
her veins. During interviews, she finds herself scanning the subject’s face not for emotional cues
relevant to her story, but for any flicker of distress, any sign that they are about to deliver
terrible news. This constant watchfulness has bled into every corner of her life. At home in the
apartment they once shared, the quiet is unbearable. She leaves the television on constantly, not
for entertainment, but for the noise, a flimsy buffer against the memory of the ringing silence of
his unanswered phone. Sleep offers little respite; she often lies awake for hours, muscles clenched,
cataloging every creak of the building and siren in the distance, certain that each sound is a
harbinger of the next catastrophe.

Beneath the hypervigilance is a roiling sea of emotion. Waves of guilt crash over her, relentless
and punishing. She replays their last day, their last conversation, her decision to ignore his call,
convinced she missed a sign, a clue, a chance to save him. This guilt sours into a hot,
directionless anger—at the universe for its cruelty, at the doctors for their powerlessness, and, in
her most private moments of shame, at Bao for leaving her so abruptly. These feelings have built a
wall around her, isolating her from friends who try to offer comfort she feels she doesn't deserve.

had felt like a second chance, a promise of a stable, shared future. Now, that promise is just

sit in a quiet room without waiting for the worst to happen.
