groan of the house as the water began its inexorable rise. For six hours, he and his wife, Adama,

The memory isn't a simple recollection; it arrives like a film that plays without his consent, a
full sensory replay. He can still feel the damp chill in the air and the sickening lurch in his
stomach when the front door, their only way out, swelled in its frame and refused to budge. The
feeling of being trapped, of the world itself turning against them, has never left. When the memory
comes, which is often, it feels like it is happening right now, not as something that is over and
done with. The past is not the past; it is a room he is perpetually locked inside.

Eight months later, the physical floodwaters have long receded, but Kofi lives in a state of
constant alert. He returned to his job at the large home improvement store, a place he once enjoyed,
but it has become a landscape of potential threats. The first thing he does at the start of every
shift is scan the entire building, mapping every single emergency exit, his mind cataloguing the
fastest route out from any point in the store. He has come to believe that the world is a
fundamentally dangerous place, and that safety is an illusion one can only maintain through
relentless vigilance. He often positions himself near the main automatic doors, ostensibly to greet
customers, but in truth, it’s so he has a clear line of escape. His manager has commented on it, a
good-natured ribbing about being the store’s "official doorman," but Kofi feels a cold knot of panic
if he has to work deep in the aisles, far from an exit. He has to be on guard all the time; it feels
less like a choice and more like a basic requirement for survival.

This constant scanning extends beyond work. At home, he can no longer relax. He distracts himself
with the television, turning the volume up to drown out the sound of the wind or rain, but his
attention is never fully there. His eyes are always moving, checking the windows, the locks. Sleep
offers no respite. Most nights, he finds it impossible to let go, certain that if he falls asleep,
something terrible will happen. He lies awake for hours, listening to the house settle, his body
rigid with the need to stay alert and protect Adama. She tells him he’s safe, that the storm is
over, but her words feel meaningless. He is certain he has permanently changed for the worse, that
the easygoing, trusting man he was before the storm was washed away with his furniture. That man is
gone, replaced by this tense, watchful stranger.

The consequences of this new reality have been profound. His sleeplessness has left him perpetually
exhausted, making his physical job a daily struggle. Adama has grown quiet, her worry for him a
palpable presence in their small, repaired home. She tries to coax him on walks or to visit friends,
but the thought of being in an unfamiliar place, of not knowing the exits or potential dangers, is
overwhelming. He has started making excuses, isolating them both. The final impetus for seeking help

filling his lungs. He didn't realise he had cried out until a coworker was shaking his shoulder, his
face a mask of concern. Kofi is here now because Adama begged him to be, but he is terrified. What
frightens him most is the idea of letting his guard down, because in his mind, that vigilance is the
only thing that has kept them alive.
