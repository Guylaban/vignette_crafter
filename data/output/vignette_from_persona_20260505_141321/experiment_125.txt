forty-six, his work as a park ranger in the vast, open spaces of the American West should be a
source of peace, but for the last decade, it has been anything but. The trouble, he explains, began
fifteen years ago in a dusty valley thousands of miles from the pine-scented air he now breathes. He
was part of a convoy escort when the lead vehicle was hit. The memory is not a distant story he
tells; it is a place he still visits. It feels more real to him, he admits, than his own wedding
day. He can recall with crystalline clarity the specific shade of beige of the dust, the high, thin
whine of shrapnel, and the frantic shouts over the radio.

The worst part came during the medevac. As the helicopter descended, its rotor wash creating a
blinding storm of sand and debris, his squad was pinned down, taking fire from the ridge. He
remembers the overwhelming noise, the percussive thud of the blades mixing with the crack of rifles.
While laying down suppressing fire, he saw his friend, Corporal Evans, get hit. Adebayo was frozen,
unable to move from his position of cover. He could do nothing but watch as the medics scrambled.
That feeling of being trapped, of profound physical discomfort under the weight of his gear and the
oppressive sun, is a sensation that now haunts him. When the memory rushes back, it brings with it
the phantom smell of hot jet fuel and scorched earth, so potent he sometimes finds himself looking
for the source of the fire. He is certain he will never forgive himself for not doing more, for not
finding a way to reach Evans.

This certainty has shaped the man he has become. Adebayo believes he has been permanently altered
for the worse, that the capable, easy-going man his wife married was left behind in that valley. He
now lives as if perpetually on duty. Sounds are the most immediate threat; a car backfiring or a
sudden shout will cause him to flinch, his body tensing for an impact that never comes. He finds
himself constantly scanning his environment, a habit that feels less like a choice and more like a
basic survival instinct. At the park’s visitor center, he can’t focus on a family asking for
directions because his attention is locked on a man with a large backpack, his mind automatically
assessing exit routes and potential threats. He watches other people closely, their hands, their
eyes, searching for any sign that they might mean harm. He knows it’s irrational, but the feeling of
danger is absolute. He has to be on guard, he reasons, because the one time he wasn't, the worst
happened.

His work as a ranger has become a minefield. The very sounds of rescue can undo him. Last month, a
search-and-rescue helicopter was called in for a lost hiker. As the familiar thwump-thwump-thwump
echoed through the canyon, Adebayo felt the world tilt. A sense of unreality washed over him, the
vibrant green of the forest canopy turning flat and distant, as if he were watching it on a screen.
He had to duck behind a granite boulder, fumbling with his radio to feign a connection issue, his
heart hammering against his ribs until the sound faded. His colleagues have noticed his jumpiness,
his tendency to avoid open meadows where aircraft are more audible. The avoidance has become a quiet
habit, subtly rerouting his patrols and his life.

He decided to seek help after a conversation with his wife a few weeks ago. She didn't yell or issue
an ultimatum. She just looked at him with a deep sadness and said, "I miss you, Ade. The real you."
Her words landed with the force of a physical blow, confirming his deepest fear: that he was a
damaged, lesser version of himself. He came to therapy not with any real hope of fixing what he
believes is irrevocably broken, but because of the small, desperate chance that he could stop being
a ghost in his own home and find a way to live in the quiet country he once fought for.
