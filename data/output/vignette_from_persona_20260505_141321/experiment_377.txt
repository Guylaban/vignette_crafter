Robert was a journalist who had built a career on bearing witness. For two decades, he had believed
that if you just kept your eyes open, you could make sense of anything. That belief had shattered
nine months ago in a dusty, windowless room. He had been on assignment, reporting on a regional
conflict, when his car was stopped. The next three weeks were a blur of darkness, punctuated by
moments of stark, unbearable clarity. Now, those moments were all that remained. He explained that
the memory of it wasn't a story he could tell, but a film that played over and over in his mind,
often without warning. It would force its way in when he was trying to write a deadline story or
sitting at the dinner table with his wife, Sarah. Suddenly, he would be back on the cold concrete
floor, the smell of cheap, sour liquor thick in the air from his captors' breath, the rough grip of
a hand on the back of his neck forcing his head down. When the memory arrived, it wasn't a
recollection; it was a recurrence. The same terror that had seized his lungs then would return with
full force, leaving him breathless and trembling in his quiet suburban home.

The world outside that room had become a minefield. At 45, Robert had always been a social man,
comfortable in crowds, quick with a story at a party. Now, he was constantly on guard, superalert to
a threat he couldn't name but felt in his bones. The open-plan office of the newspaper where he
worked, once a hub of collaborative energy, now felt like an arena of unpredictable dangers. An
intern dropping a stack of papers would send a jolt of pure adrenaline through him; for a split
second, he was certain it was the sound of a rifle bolt sliding home. He would react before he could
think, ducking his head, his body tensing for an impact that never came. He knew, intellectually,
that he was safe, but his body was convinced otherwise. This constant state of high alert made
concentration impossible. He started missing deadlines, his once-sharp prose becoming muddled and
hesitant. He began working from home, but the false alarms followed him there, triggered by a car
backfiring on the street or a neighbour slamming a door.

His life at home had shrunk. The triggers were everywhere, insidious and specific. The sharp,
persistent ache in his neck, a physical remnant of being held in stress positions, was a constant
somatic reminder. On days when the pain flared, the intrusive "film" was more vivid, the terror more
acute. He stopped going to friends' houses for dinner after an evening when a host opened a bottle
of whiskey. The smell of the alcohol hit him like a physical blow, and he had to stumble outside,
gasping for air, leaving Sarah to make his excuses. The most painful change, however, was in his
marriage. Sarah’s attempts to comfort him, a simple hand on his shoulder or an arm around his waist,
felt like attacks. Any physical touch he didn’t initiate sent a shockwave of panic through him. He
would flinch away violently, sometimes pushing her hand away, the reflex faster than his love for
her. He saw the hurt in her eyes, a confusion that was hardening into resentment. He had come to
believe he was fundamentally broken, a danger even to those he loved. It was after a night when he
woke up shouting from a nightmare and found Sarah sleeping on the sofa, her face etched with fear
and exhaustion, that he finally made the call. He came to therapy not with a clear goal, but with a
desperate, fragmented plea: to stop the film, to be able to hold his wife's hand, and to feel safe
in his own home again.
