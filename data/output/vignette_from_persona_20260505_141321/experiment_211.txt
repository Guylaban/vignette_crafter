Sister Agnes traced the rim of her teacup, her gaze fixed on the steam rising in the quiet convent
refectory. It had been eighteen months since the accident, but the sound of a car braking too
quickly on the road outside could still seize her breath. This morning, a delivery truck had locked
its wheels, and in an instant, she was no longer in the refectory but back on the pavement by the
front gate. The memory arrived without warning, a complete sensory assault. She could see the
impossible blue of the sedan, the late afternoon sun glinting off the hood just before the impact.
She could feel the rough texture of the wool of her own sleeve where she had tried, a fraction of a
second too late, to pull Sister Maria back. The memory was always this vivid, this specific, yet the
moments immediately after were a silent, gaping hole. The other sisters told her she had knelt and
prayed, but she had no recollection of it, only the searing image of the car and the sound that
followed.

The event had reshaped the geography of her life. The convent’s grand stone gate, once a symbol of
sanctuary, was now a place of profound threat. She had not used it since that day. Every morning,
she left for matins through the kitchen’s rear service door, taking a long, winding path through the
vegetable garden, even in the rain and winter snow. The inconvenience was a small price to pay for
avoiding the sudden, sickening lurch in her stomach that the sight of the gate now caused. She knew
the other sisters noticed her circuitous routes, her quiet refusal to join them for walks into town,
but no one said anything. The silence felt like a confirmation of her own failing.

Her inner life, once a place of contemplative peace, had become a landscape of negotiation and fear.
She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt as solid as scripture, that the accident was her
fault. She was the senior sister; she had been talking about the upcoming vespers service,
distracting them both. If she had just been more attentive, if she had insisted they wait for a
larger gap in the traffic, Sister Maria would still be in the empty seat across from her. This
conviction, that she had failed in her most basic duty of care, had corroded her confidence. She no
longer trusted her own judgment. When leading the novices in their studies, she would reread simple
passages, certain she had misinterpreted them. She had relinquished her role in managing the
convent’s finances, convinced she would make a catastrophic error. I can’t rely on myself, she would
think, the phrase a constant, punishing refrain in her mind.

To ward off further disaster, a new, rigid piety had crept into her spiritual practice. Before any
sister left the grounds, Agnes felt a frantic compulsion to recite the Ave Maria seven times, her
fingers digging into her wooden rosary beads. She had to complete the cycle without interruption. If
her concentration broke, she would be filled with a terrifying certainty that her failure to perform
the ritual perfectly would result in harm to the departing sister. It was a secret, frantic ministry
of prevention born from the conviction that she had failed to protect someone once and could not
bear for it to happen again. She also avoided the small things that brought Sister Maria to mind:
the row of rose bushes they had planted together, now untended, and the second pew on the left in
the chapel, where Maria used to sit. This avoidance had carved out hollow spaces in her life,
isolating her within the very community that was her home.

It was a conversation with the Mother Superior that finally prompted her to seek help. Her superior,
in her gentle way, had noted not Agnes’s grief, but her fear. “You walk as if you are bracing for a
blow, Agnes,” she had said. “God does not ask us to live in terror.” The words landed with a quiet
force, cutting through the fog of her rituals and avoidance. Sister Agnes realized her life had
become a closed loop of anxiety, a penance for a sin she could not absolve. She was now sitting with
a therapist, terrified of dredging up the details but more terrified of the alternative: a life
spent forever circling the perimeter of her own home, a prisoner of a single, terrible moment.
