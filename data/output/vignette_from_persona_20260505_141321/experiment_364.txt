the rhythmic *whump-whump-whump* of the Huey’s rotors as it descended to evacuate his platoon. They

of the canvas body bag under his palms as he helped lift it onto the chopper. It was coarse and
unforgiving, and the memory of that tactile sensation has never left him.

For decades, Keanu managed. He built a family, a career, a life that looked successful from the
outside. But the war came home with him in fragments. Any helicopter passing over his suburban home,
whether for news or medical transport, can instantly dissolve the present. He will be standing at
his kitchen window and suddenly, without warning, he is not a 63-year-old man looking at his garden
but a terrified teenager in a jungle, the air tasting of cordite. When the memory hits, it brings
with it the same raw terror he felt then, a physical wave of cold dread that makes his heart pound
in his chest. He often describes this experience with a strange detachment, as if watching a film

know anymore." This sense of alienation from his own past has calcified into a core belief: there is
something fundamentally wrong with him as a person.

before he can even process the room’s occupants. He feels a constant, low-level hum of dread, a
conviction that the world is an inherently dangerous place. This is most acute when others move
unexpectedly. A flight attendant rushing down the aisle or a baggage handler making a sudden turn on
the tarmac can cause him to flinch, his body tensing for an impact that never comes. The vigilance
peaks as the sun begins to set. The specific quality of light at dusk, the same light that filled
the sky during the evacuation, signals a shift in him. Night feels porous and unsafe, and he often
finds himself awake for hours, listening to the house settle, certain that some threat lies just
beyond the locked doors.
