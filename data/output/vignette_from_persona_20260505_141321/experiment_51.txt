stitchwork that has been her life’s passion. Her attention is fixed on the large plate-glass window
of her tailor shop, her eyes tracking the movement of every car that passes on the street outside.
Six months ago, she loved this window; it filled her workspace with natural light. Now, it feels
like a vulnerability, a screen playing a constant loop of potential threats. The accident had
happened on a day just like this one, bright and unremarkable. She had been in the passenger seat of
her friend’s car, laughing about something, when a blue sedan shot through a red light. Layla
remembers the brief, nonsensical moment of seeing the driver’s face—a young man, looking at his
phone—before the world dissolved into a deafening roar of metal and shattering glass. There was no
time to brace, no time to scream. There was only the violent, wrenching impact and the sudden,
certain knowledge that she was going to die.

That certainty has never really left her. The memory is not something she recalls; it is something
she re-experiences. Sometimes a car horn from the street is enough to trigger it. Her workshop
vanishes, and she is back inside the mangled car, the smell of gasoline thick in her throat, the
unique, high-pitched ringing in her ears drowning out everything else. In those moments, the terror
is as fresh and absolute as it was then, a physical force that steals her breath and leaves her
trembling. The memory plays like a film on repeat, the same frames, the same impact, the same silent
scream. She has come to believe that the world is a fundamentally dangerous place, and that the
placid surface of everyday life is a lie. Her safety, she is now certain, depends entirely on her
own watchfulness.

This belief dictates the shape of her days. She arrives at her shop an hour early, not to work, but

so that her sewing station is in the back corner, her chair pressed firmly against the wall. She
cannot tolerate having her back to an open space; the feeling of exposure is unbearable. She
constantly scans her surroundings, her senses overtuned to any sign of danger—a person walking too
quickly, a car slowing unexpectedly. This constant vigilance has frayed her nerves and destroyed her
concentration, turning the intricate work she once loved into a source of profound anxiety. She has
to be careful, she tells herself, because you never know what can happen next. The man in the blue
sedan had looked so normal, so harmless, a second before he nearly killed her. This has planted a
deep seed of distrust; people, she now feels, are not what they seem, their capacity for causing
devastation hidden just beneath the surface.

Her world has shrunk dramatically. The thought of getting into a car, even as a passenger, sends a

to reach on foot. This avoidance has begun to cripple her business, which relies on her ability to

accident, but a small part of her hopes that she might one day be able to sit by a window again and
see only the light.
