eight months since the fire, and concentration was a luxury he could no longer afford. The event

mind not as a coherent story but as a series of brutally clear, silent snapshots. One image, in
particular, would force its way into his mind without warning: the sight of a woman’s silk dress,
soaked in spilled champagne, catching the reflection of the first flames as they erupted from behind
the bar. The memory was always a still picture, vivid and sharp, arriving uninvited while he was
driving, reading, or trying to fall asleep.

smell, a sickening blend of burning alcohol and smoke, had fused itself to the terror of that night.

wine at dinner. The sharp, fruity aroma hit him, and instantly, he was no longer in his quiet dining
room but back in the chaos, his heart hammering against his ribs. He had recoiled from the table,
unable to explain the wave of dread that washed over him. He hasn't been able to tolerate the smell
since.

circumscribed. He made excuses to avoid seeing the friends he and Mele had shared for decades, the
effort of pretending to be fine too exhausting to contemplate. At work, he insisted on a new chair
in his office, one tucked into a corner so he could sit with his back firmly against the wall, the
entire room and the doorway within his field of vision. During editorial meetings, his colleagues
noticed his gaze was never still; he was constantly scanning the room, the windows, the faces of his
team, as if searching for a threat they couldn't see. He knew it made them uneasy, but the feeling
of being exposed from behind was intolerable.

The sense of being on guard did not end when he left the office. At home, the familiar comfort of

make him jump, a sharp, involuntary flinch that left him breathless and trembling. Mele had learned
to announce her presence when entering a room, her soft footsteps no longer a comfort but another
potential alarm.

The nights were the worst. He would often wake in the dark, the residue of a nightmare clinging to
him like smoke. The dream was always the same—the crushing weight of the crowd, the inability to
find an exit—and he would lie awake for hours, his body rigid with tension, listening to the house
settle around him. Talking about the event was impossible. Whenever Mele tried to gently broach the
subject, asking what he was thinking, the act of speaking the words would summon the images, making
them sharper, more immediate. He would fall silent, the memory a suffocating presence in the room
between them.

The decision to seek help came after he turned down an invitation to an awards ceremony honoring his
oldest and most successful author. The thought of the crowded ballroom, the clinking glasses, the

attend the very events that were the lifeblood of his industry. He saw the path ahead narrowing to
nothing—a career built over forty years crumbling, his friendships withering from neglect. He was
afraid of losing everything, but what frightened him most was the dawning realization that he had
already lost himself. He wanted, more than anything, to feel safe in his own home again, to sit in a
room without constantly searching for a way out.
