around stability: a long, loving marriage to his wife, Layla; a successful career as a social media
manager for a non-profit; and a deep pride in his own resilience. That was before the night, six
months ago, when his world fractured. Driving home from a community meeting, he was pulled over. The
flashing lights in his rearview mirror were the first spark of an unease that quickly became a blaze
of terror. It was a case of mistaken identity, they said later, but not before two officers had
dragged him from his car, shouting commands he couldn't process fast enough. He remembered the rough
scrape of asphalt against his cheek and the coppery taste of blood and dirt filling his mouth as his
face was pushed into the pavement. What haunted him most was the sound of his own voice, pleading in
a way he had never imagined he would. It felt like a betrayal of everything he thought he was.

In the months that followed, the man Layla knew began to disappear. The trauma didn't live in the
past; it was a constant, predatory presence. A police siren wailing down the street would send a
jolt through him, and for a split second, he could taste the grit of the pavement on his tongue
again. The memory would start to unspool—the blinding headlights, the rough hands—but he would slam
a mental door on it before it could take full shape, a desperate act of self-preservation that left
him breathless and shaking. When a fragment did break through, it wasn't fear that seized him, but a
white-hot rage that felt so vast it could swallow him whole. He began to believe the world was
simply a dangerous place, and that any sense of safety was an illusion. People, he decided, were not
what they seemed; a uniform or a friendly smile could hide anything.

His work, once a source of purpose, became a minefield. As a social media manager, he was required
to be online, to engage with the world’s unfolding story. But he found himself unable to look at
trending topics, terrified of stumbling upon a video of another traffic stop, another act of
violence. He would spend hours meticulously curating his feeds to create a sterile, emotionless
space, deleting any post that might evoke a strong feeling, good or bad. He just wanted to feel
nothing. At the office, he couldn't sit in the open-plan space without his back to a solid wall.
Before any meeting, he would instinctively locate the nearest two exits, his mind mapping escape
routes before he could even register the agenda. He was in a state of constant, humming vigilance,
scanning every room, every face, for threats he couldn't name. He knew, logically, that he was safe
in his office or his own home, but his body refused to believe it. He could not relax.

This new reality bled into his home life. He stopped watching the evening news with Layla, then
action movies, then eventually any drama at all. The risk of seeing something that would echo his
experience was too great. He felt a deep, abiding shame about how he had acted that night, certain
he had failed some fundamental test of character. This conviction made him withdraw, believing he
was no longer the man his wife had married. He avoided family dinners and celebrations, the

good, but willing to try anything to find him again.
