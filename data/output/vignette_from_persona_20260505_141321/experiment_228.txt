Bao presents as a quiet, meticulously dressed man of 75, whose precision as a cybersecurity analyst
seems at odds with the disarray he describes in his own mind. For nearly fifty years, he had managed
to contain the memories of his firstborn son’s brief, harrowing life. Now, following the recent
death of his wife, the containment has failed. The story emerges in fragments, the narrative thread
often breaking mid-sentence as he loses his place, staring at a point on the wall as if watching a
scene only he can see.

It was 1974. His son, Minh, was born seemingly healthy, but within hours, a terrifying blueness
crept into his skin. What followed was a blur of frantic activity in the neonatal intensive care
unit. The memory that haunts Bao is not a cohesive story but a series of sensory snapshots that play
on a relentless loop. He can still feel the cold vinyl of the waiting room chair, smell the sharp,
chemical clean of the floor polish that seemed to permeate everything, and see the flickering
fluorescent lights of the hallway. The most vivid part of this internal film is the image of Minh in
the incubator, a tiny, fragile body lost in a tangle of wires and tubes, his chest rising and
falling with the rhythmic sigh of the ventilator. Bao remembers the hushed, grave tones of the
doctors, the medical jargon he couldn't grasp, and a profound, suffocating sense of helplessness.

For decades, these memories were ghosts, present but manageable. Now, they are solid, immediate
threats. At his office, a younger colleague recently announced his wife’s pregnancy with joyful
photos of an ultrasound. As the team gathered to congratulate him, the cheerful office chatter faded
for Bao. A phantom scent of antiseptic filled his nostrils, sharp and cloying. His heart began to
hammer against his ribs, a frantic, painful rhythm. He felt a sudden, dizzying sense of dislocation,
the modern glass-and-steel office dissolving around him, replaced by the pale green walls of the
hospital. For a terrifying moment, he was certain he was back there, standing uselessly beside the
incubator. He mumbled an excuse and fled to the restroom, splashing cold water on his face until the
present day reasserted itself. Since then, he has avoided the colleague, taking his lunch at his
desk and finding reasons to work from home, a decision that has created a noticeable distance
between him and his team.

Bao has come to believe that his own emotional reactions are dangerous. The wave of guilt that
washes over him—the unanswerable question of whether he should have done something different,
something more—feels like a physical force that could overwhelm him entirely. This fear of his own
feelings has become a prison. He recently received a notice for a routine cardiac check-up, a
necessity at his age. The thought of the examination—the sterile room, the medical equipment, the
simple act of being a patient—is paralyzing. He is convinced that if he goes, the experience will
trigger a complete collapse, that the film in his head will start and this time, it will not stop.

When his daughter, now a mother herself, tries to talk to him about his grief for his late wife, Bao
finds he cannot articulate what is truly happening. He will start to explain the fear, the intrusive
images, but the words get tangled. He loses the thread, trailing off as the memory pulls him back,
feeling not like a recollection from the distant past but like an event happening in the present
moment. It was his daughter’s insistence, her gentle but firm observation that he was disappearing
into himself, that finally prompted him to seek help. He sits in the therapy room, his hands clasped
tightly in his lap, admitting that his greatest hope is simply to be able to attend his grandson’s
upcoming birthday party without the fear that the sight of a child’s joy will plunge him back into
the silent, sterile horror of the hospital ward.
