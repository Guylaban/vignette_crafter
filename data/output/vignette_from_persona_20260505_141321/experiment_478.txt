Mateo describes his life before the collapse as something that belonged to another person. Eight
months ago, he was a 24-year-old QA Engineer, proud of his logical mind and the ordered world of
code he inhabited. He was driving his younger sister, Sofia, home from her community college class,
the afternoon sun glinting off the cars ahead of them on the interstate bridge. They were arguing
playfully about what to have for dinner when a sound like a thunderclap directly beneath them was
followed by a sickening lurch. What he remembered most was not the fall itself, but the split second
of weightlessness and the sight of his sister’s wide, terrified eyes. In the chaos of twisted metal
and shattering glass that followed, he lost his grip on her hand. While they both survived with
fractures and lacerations, the memory of that moment—the feeling of his failure to hold on—became
the true epicentre of his trauma.

In the weeks that followed, Mateo’s carefully constructed world began to unravel. The constant news
coverage of the disaster, with its engineering analyses and survivor interviews, felt like a
physical assault. He stopped watching television altogether and learned to scan headlines with a
practiced aversion, a skill he honed to avoid any mention of accidents, structural failures, or
rescue efforts. This avoidance bled into his daily life. His commute to the tech campus became a
labyrinthine route through side streets, meticulously planned to avoid any overpasses, major
intersections, or the sight of a police car. The presence of law enforcement, which he once saw as a
sign of order, now just reminded him of the helpless, frantic moments after the collapse, a scene of
uniformed chaos he couldn't escape. He was certain that the world was a fundamentally unsafe place,
that danger was random and unavoidable. You can never know who will harm you, he would think, or
what will simply fall out from under you.

At work, he walled himself off. The open-plan office, once a collaborative space, now felt fraught
with potential threats. During a team lunch, a colleague began recounting a story about a minor car
pile-up on the freeway. As the words "crash" and "impact" were spoken, Mateo felt a sudden, strange
distance from his own body. The voices of his coworkers grew muffled, and a hazy, dreamlike quality
settled over the room, as if he were watching a film of his life rather than living it. He excused
himself abruptly, leaving his food untouched, and spent the rest of the afternoon locked in a
bathroom stall, waiting for the feeling of reality to return. After that, he ate at his desk,
headphones on, creating a bubble against the casual conversations of the world.

The belief that he had failed Sofia became a cornerstone of his new identity. He would spend hours
lying awake at night, his mind endlessly replaying the event, editing the details. If only they had
left ten minutes later. If only he had gripped her hand tighter. If only he had been stronger. This
counterfactual thinking became a constant, tormenting loop, a vision of a parallel life where he was
still whole, where he wasn't the brother who had let go. He came to believe that the collapse had
irrevocably tainted his future; nothing good can happen to me anymore, was a thought that felt as
true and solid as the ground he no longer trusted. The idea of dating, of forming new connections,
seemed impossible. Who would want to be with someone so broken?

It was a performance review that finally prompted him to seek help. His manager, a kind woman, noted
his decline in focus and his increasing isolation from the team. "You're here, Mateo," she had said
gently, "but it feels like you're a million miles away." The observation landed with devastating
accuracy. He realized his strategies for survival had merely shrunk his world down to his apartment
and his cubicle, a life devoid of joy or possibility. He came to therapy not with a clear hope for
recovery, but with a desperate fear that this detached, hypervigilant state was now his permanent
reality, and a quiet, terrifying question of whether the person he was before the bridge fell was
gone for good.
