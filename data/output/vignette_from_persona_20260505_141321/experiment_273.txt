Amir, a 37-year-old firefighter, sits stiffly in the chair, his broad shoulders held in a tight,
protective posture. For six months, his world has been shrinking, a process that began with a man on
a bicycle. The man had been a neighbor in his old apartment complex, and the stalking started
subtly: a figure always at the edge of his vision when he went for a run in the park, the same
bicycle leaning against a wall near his favorite coffee shop. It escalated over two months, from
unsettling coincidences to the undeniable feeling of being tracked. The man would appear on his
bicycle at the end of Amir’s street, just watching. The ordeal culminated one evening when Amir
returned from a long shift to find the man waiting by his front door, a confrontation that ended
with a restraining order but left an indelible mark on Amir’s sense of safety in the world.

The initial relief that followed the legal intervention was short-lived. Now, any bicycle can undo
him. A few weeks ago, while driving to the station, a cyclist in his peripheral vision sent a jolt
of pure ice through his veins. The world seemed to tilt, and for a moment, he wasn't on the highway
but back on his old street, the memory returning not as a thought but as a full-body certainty of
imminent harm. The wave of emotion was so powerful, so overwhelming, that he had to pull over onto
the shoulder, gasping for air until the feeling subsided. He can no longer drive past his old
neighborhood, and the park he once loved is now a no-go zone. The feeling of being followed is a
constant companion; the sound of footsteps behind him on a grocery store aisle is enough to make his
heart hammer against his ribs.

At the fire station, a place once synonymous with control and competence, Amir feels like an
imposter. The sudden, jarring clang of the station alarm, a sound he has heard thousands of times,
now makes him flinch violently. He finds himself constantly on high alert, his eyes darting to the
bay doors, watching anyone who approaches the building with a suspicion that feels alien to him.
Even in the familiar breakroom, he reflexively scans for exits the moment he enters, a habit he
cannot seem to break. He watches his crewmates, men he trusts with his life, searching their faces
for any sign of a threat, knowing on some level it’s irrational but unable to stop the internal
surveillance. This constant vigilance is exhausting, and it has not gone unnoticed. His captain
recently commented on his jumpiness, a remark that felt like a confirmation of his deepest fear:
that he is fundamentally broken.

He has come to believe that the man he was before the stalking is gone forever, replaced by this
fragile, terrified stranger. He is convinced he has been permanently changed for the worse. The
intensity of his reactions—the panic attacks in the car, the constant state of dread—has planted a
terrifying seed of doubt in his mind: he fears he is going crazy. As a firefighter, his identity is
built on strength and the ability to run toward danger. Now, he feels like the weakest person he
knows, and this chasm between who he is supposed to be and who he feels he is has become a source of
profound shame. When his wife, Layla, asks him what’s wrong after she finds him staring blankly at a
wall, he is paralyzed. He knows precisely what happened, the images are seared into his mind, but he
is unable to find the words to describe the experience. The memory is a locked box of pure feeling,
and language is a key he cannot find.

This inability to articulate his experience has built a wall between him and Layla. He has stopped
trying to explain, certain she would think less of him, or worse, confirm his fear that he is losing
his mind. It was after a particularly bad night, when the sight of a pizza delivery cyclist on their
street sent him into a silent, shaking panic, that Layla finally gave him an ultimatum filled with
more fear than anger. She told him she felt like she was living with a ghost and that she couldn't
watch him disappear anymore. That was what brought him to therapy. He is here not with hope, but
with a quiet dread, terrified that putting words to his experience will only prove that the damage
is permanent and that the strong man he once was is truly gone for good.
