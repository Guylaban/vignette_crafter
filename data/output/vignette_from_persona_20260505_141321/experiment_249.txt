Anong has not had a full night’s sleep in seven months, not since the night the creek overflowed its
banks. The storm had been relentless, but it was the sound that came first—a low, deep roar that
wasn’t thunder. In her memory, what followed is not a story but a series of sharp, disconnected
images that refuse to fit together. There is the sight of dark, muddy water bubbling up through the
floorboards, the splintering crack of her neighbour’s porch being torn away, the feeling of icy
water climbing her legs in the sudden, terrifying dark after the power went out. She remembers her
husband’s hand slipping from hers for a moment that felt like an eternity. When the memory forces
its way into her mind, which it does without warning, it is these fragments that assault her. The
feeling of that cold water is so real that she gasps, her heart hammering against her ribs, and she
has to brace herself against a wall or a countertop until the wave of sheer panic subsides.

This sense of imminent danger now follows her everywhere, especially into her job at the fast-food
restaurant. The kitchen, once a place of familiar, bustling routine, has become a minefield of
auditory threats. The sudden, high-pitched beep of a fryer timer, the sharp sizzle of frozen patties
hitting hot oil, the metallic crash of a dropped tray—each unexpected sound sends a jolt of pure
adrenaline through her. Her coworkers see her flinch, her shoulders tensing as she scans the room
for a threat she can’t name. She is on constant high alert, her body convinced that any loud noise
is the prelude to disaster. The work she once did with absent-minded efficiency now requires
immense, draining concentration just to get through a shift without her panic becoming visible.

At home, the nights are the worst. When the sun goes down, a different kind of vigilance takes over,
a quiet, gnawing dread that settles deep in her chest. Anong finds it impossible to feel safe in the
dark. She lies in bed for hours, listening. Every creak of the house settling is the groan of water-
logged wood about to give way. The whistle of wind through the eaves is the rising roar of the
floodwaters. She is certain that if she allows herself to fall asleep, she will miss the warning
signs and fail to protect her husband. This terrible responsibility keeps her awake until exhaustion
finally claims her for a few fitful hours before dawn.

In the months since the flood, a profound sense of alienation has taken root. Anong looks at her
husband, her friends from the temple, and her sister on video calls, and feels as if she is watching
them from behind a thick pane of glass. She has come to believe that the flood washed away her
capacity for normal emotions, leaving only fear and a hollow sort of numbness. She no longer joins
her husband for walks in the park, especially not near the creek, and has stopped cooking the
elaborate meals that used to bring her joy. She feels like a ghost in her own life, a stranger to
the people she loves most, convinced that she no longer belongs in their world. This belief has
become a self-fulfilling prophecy; her withdrawal has created a real distance, leaving her husband
feeling helpless and shut out.

What finally brought her to seek help was a summer thunderstorm that rolled in during the dinner
rush. At the first clap of thunder, Anong froze, then fled to the dry-storage closet, where she
huddled between sacks of flour, shaking uncontrollably. Her manager found her there ten minutes
later. The gentle but firm suggestion that she take some time off, combined with the profound shame
of her reaction, broke through her paralysis. Anong is here now because she is exhausted by the
constant watchfulness and the weight of her conviction that she is irrevocably broken. She carries a
small, fragile hope that she might one day feel safe in her own home again, but underneath it is the
cold, heavy certainty that she can’t stop bad things from happening, and that the world is no longer
a place she can trust.
