angle of Omar’s leg, the sight of the wound stark against his small frame. He remembers the smell, a
nauseating mix of rain, gasoline, and the sterile, antiseptic odor from the first-aid kit he always
kept in his car. But there are parts he cannot access, a black hole of several minutes between
seeing the wreck and working on his own son in the back of the responding ambulance. No matter how
hard he tries to retrieve that time, it remains blank, and trying to fill it brings on a wave of the
same raw terror he felt then, a feeling of absolute, helpless freefall.

In the weeks after Omar was discharged from the hospital, a new, suffocating reality began to set in

by this hollowed-out, hyper-alert stranger. He saw himself as permanently damaged goods. At home, he
found himself unable to sit still, throwing himself into tasks with a frantic energy—reorganizing

memories threatened to bloom. When a memory did break through, it wasn't a simple recollection; it
was a full-body hijacking. The faint smell of bleach his wife, Layla, used to clean the bathroom

as if it were happening all over again.
