Anjali had always believed in the power of showing up. It was the principle that guided her work as
a counselor and what led her, six months ago, to a peaceful community rally in the city park. The
protest was loud but joyful, a mosaic of signs and voices. She remembers the smell of the damp earth
after a brief morning shower, a scent she used to love. The trouble started near the park’s edge, by
the bus depot. The line of police officers advanced, and the crowd compressed. In the ensuing chaos,
trying to get away, she was shoved from behind. The impact sent her sprawling onto a muddy patch of
grass. Before she could process what was happening, a boot was on her back, and a gloved hand
pressed her face into the soil. For a moment that stretched into an eternity, all she knew was the
pressure, the shouting, and the raw, earthy smell filling her nostrils.

In the weeks that followed, the memory began to behave in strange ways. Sometimes, Anjali could
recount the event to a concerned friend with a chilling detachment, feeling as though she were
describing a scene from a movie that had happened to a stranger. Then, without warning, a trigger
would collapse time and space. Walking past a construction site, the smell of turned soil would hit
her, and she would be back on the ground, the phantom pressure on her spine so real she would
physically stumble, her body instinctively trying to curl into a protective ball. When this
happened, the memory wasn't a thought but a full-sensory assault; she could feel the grit against
her cheek and hear the distorted shouts through the filter of the mud.

Her life began to shrink. The bus, once her primary mode of transport to the clinic where she
worked, became an impossible space. She couldn't shake the feeling of being trapped, a sitting
target. She started taking expensive ride-shares, a financial strain that added a quiet hum of
stress to her days. She also stopped answering calls from the friends she had been with at the
rally. Seeing them meant remembering, and remembering felt dangerous. She had come to believe she
had lost her connection to her community; the shared cause that once united them now felt like a
shared contamination, a stain she carried from that day. This feeling of being dirty was persistent.
She found herself checking her reflection in mirrors, in shop windows, in the darkened screen of her
phone, searching for a trace of the mud she felt was still on her, a visible sign of her defilement.

At work, her professional poise began to fray. Her job required empathy and an open presence, but
Anjali found herself consumed by a new, exhausting vigilance. She would study the faces of her
clients, not for emotional cues, but for micro-expressions of threat. She was certain that people
were not what they seemed, that danger could hide behind any smile. A sudden noise in the hallway
would cause her heart to hammer in her chest, her body reacting with a jolt of adrenaline before her
mind could even register the sound as benign. Sleep offered no respite. She would lie in bed for
hours, convinced that she needed to stay alert, that to fall asleep was to lower her guard against
an unseen threat.

Anjali sought therapy after a particularly difficult session with a client. As the young woman spoke

professional role was mortifying. She realized she could no longer separate the then from the now.
She came to her first appointment terrified that the world truly was as menacing as it felt, but
holding onto a desperate hope that she could one day feel clean again and trust another person’s
face without searching for the danger she was now sure was hidden there.
