Liam presents as a well-spoken 28-year-old social media manager, though his posture is tense and his
gaze frequently drifts towards the window. He came to therapy at the urging of his girlfriend, four
years after his honorable discharge from the Army. The event that haunts him occurred during his
second tour in Afghanistan. He was in the passenger seat of a Humvee, part of a routine patrol on a
dusty, sun-bleached road. He remembers the jarring lurch of the vehicle, a soundless flash of white
light, and then a chaotic sequence of disconnected sensations: the metallic taste of blood, the
high-pitched ringing in his ears, the sight of his sergeant’s grievously wounded leg. The memories
don't form a coherent story; they exist as fragmented pieces that refuse to fit together. Sometimes,
when he tries to recall the sequence of events, the whole memory feels so extreme and cinematic that
he finds it hard to believe it actually happened to him.

In the months after returning home, Liam tried to slot back into his old life. He moved in with his
girlfriend, landed a good job, and reconnected with friends. But the war had followed him home in
ways he couldn't articulate. The sound of a news helicopter hovering over his downtown apartment
building would send a jolt of ice through his veins, his heart hammering against his ribs as he
fought the urge to seek cover. He began insisting on being the one to drive whenever he and his
girlfriend went anywhere; the feeling of being a passenger, of not being in control of the vehicle's
movement, was unbearable. Even a minor pothole could make him flinch. He was once on the subway and
saw a man with a complex series of surgical scars on his arm, and Liam had to get off at the next
stop, overwhelmed by a sudden, suffocating wave of dread. He couldn't explain to his girlfriend why;
often, he experiences the full emotional intensity of the event without any clear image attached, a
phantom terror that descends without warning.

To manage this internal chaos, Liam’s world has gradually shrunk. He quit the gym he used to attend
with his girlfriend, unable to tolerate the feeling of a racing heart and shortness of breath that a
hard workout induced, as it felt too similar to the panic of the attack. At work, his role requires
him to be agile and responsive, yet he finds himself spending hours rehearsing what he’s going to
say before any client call, scripting his side of the conversation to avoid being caught off guard.
He will cross the street to avoid walking past a police officer, the sight of the uniform a stark
and unwelcome reminder. These strategies provide a temporary sense of safety but have deepened his
isolation. He looks at his colleagues and friends and feels like he’s watching them from behind a
thick wall of glass. He has come to believe he is permanently an outsider now, marked by an
experience they could never comprehend.

Night offers no respite. Liam finds it nearly impossible to fall asleep, convinced on some primal
level that he must remain alert for danger. He lies awake for hours, cataloging every creak of the
floorboards, every distant siren. His girlfriend’s slightest movement in her sleep can make him
jump. Last week, she came up behind him while he was working and gently placed a hand on his
shoulder; he leapt from his chair with a strangled cry, his body reacting before his mind could
identify the source of the touch. The fear in her eyes was the final catalyst for him seeking help.
He carries a profound and private shame, a looping thought that somebody else, someone better
trained or more aware, would not have gotten into this situation. He is terrified that he has been
permanently changed for the worse, that the easygoing man he was before his deployment is gone for
good, and that he will eventually drive away the last person who is trying to hold on to him.
