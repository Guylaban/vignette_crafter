Layla has worked as an HVAC technician for fifteen years, a trade she learned with a fierce
determination after arriving in the United States. At fifty-five, she is known for her meticulous
work, but her colleagues and clients see only the result, not the process. The story of how she came
to be here is one she has never told in full, not even to the man she later divorced. It lives
inside her not as a coherent narrative, but as a collection of sensory fragments and unbearable
emotions. The event itself, the crossing, feels so extreme that when pieces of it surface, she
struggles to believe it actually happened to her. It’s like a film she once saw, terrifying and
distant, until a trigger makes it horrifyingly real.

The journey was on a small, overloaded fishing boat, low in the black water. For days, the world was
reduced to the constant, oppressive uncertainty of survival. She remembers the coarse, splintered
wood of the gunwale digging into her palms, the one solid thing she could hold onto as the boat
pitched. That texture is something she now avoids at all costs. Last month, while servicing a
furnace in a dusty crawlspace, her hand brushed against an unfinished support beam. The rough wood
under her fingers didn't just remind her of the boat; for a moment, she was on it. The smell of
diesel and salt filled her nostrils, the floor beneath her seemed to lurch, and she felt the
overwhelming paralysis of that time. When the memory returns with such force, a wave of shame so
potent it makes her physically recoil follows close behind. She remembers her child, small and
terrified, and her own inability to offer anything more than a desperate, silent grip. The shame is
not for what she did, but for what she couldn't do: be a mother, a protector, anything other than
another piece of frightened cargo. The feeling is so consuming that she has to stop whatever she is
doing, bracing herself until it passes.

This constant threat has reshaped her life and her work. Her job requires her to enter unfamiliar
basements, boiler rooms, and attics—enclosed spaces that her mind immediately registers as traps.
Before she even unloads her tools, she conducts a silent, methodical sweep. She identifies every
exit, planning her escape route should the need arise. She has turned down lucrative emergency calls
for flooded basements, the sight of murky water rising up concrete steps being too potent a reminder
of the sea that nearly swallowed her. This has caused friction with her dispatcher, who sees it as
unreliability. In the evenings, alone in her apartment, her mind often drifts not to her own fear,
but to the men who put her on that boat. She dwells on their faces, their casual cruelty, replaying
their broken promises. This rumination feels like a form of justice, a way of holding them
accountable in the only court she has access to, but it offers no peace, only a well-tended fire of
blame.

What frightens Layla most is silence. The constant hum of a fan or a compressor is a shield. When a
unit cycles off and a basement is plunged into an abrupt quiet, a primal alarm goes off inside her.
She becomes certain that the silence is a prelude to danger, that she has been forgotten, locked in.
In those moments, she finds herself scanning the shadows, her body tensed, listening for a footstep,
a shout, a sign that the trap is about to be sprung. This hypervigilance is exhausting, a constant
low-level hum of anxiety that never fully dissipates. She feels as though she is always on watch,
scanning her environment for dangers no one else can see.

It was a severe flashback at a client’s home two weeks ago that finally brought her to seek help.
Startled by a sudden silence when a circuit breaker tripped, she had dropped her tools and flattened
herself against a wall, a choked cry escaping her lips. The homeowner found her moments later, pale
and shaking. The ensuing complaint to her company was not angry, but concerned, yet it felt to Layla
like a final warning. She had built a life of competence and independence from the wreckage of her
past, and she could now feel it slipping through her fingers. She came to therapy not to erase the
memory—she knows that is impossible—but in the desperate hope that she might one day be able to
stand in a quiet room without believing the walls are closing in.
