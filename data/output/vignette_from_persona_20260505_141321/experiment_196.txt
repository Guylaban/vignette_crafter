Bao, at twenty-four, had come to believe that safety was an illusion, a story people told themselves
between disasters. He spends most of his days in his small painting studio, the scent of turpentine
a constant, sharp comfort. Six months have passed since he left his ex-partner, but the event that
ended their relationship does not feel like it is in the past. It lives in the present, replaying
itself in disjointed, searingly bright fragments. He can recall with perfect, photographic detail
the way his ex-partner's knuckles whitened as he gripped a heavy glass frame—one of Bao's own
pieces—but he cannot remember what they were arguing about. The memory jumps from that image to the
sound of shattering glass, and then to the cold shock of the kitchen tile against his cheek. He
knows there are missing pieces, entire minutes that are just a void, and the inability to string the
moments into a coherent sequence makes him feel like he is losing his mind. When these images surge
up, unbidden, it is not like watching a film; he is there again, feeling the floorboards vibrate
with heavy footsteps, his body tensing for an impact he can’t place in time.

In the months since, Bao has constructed a life of careful routines designed to keep the world at a
manageable distance. He rarely leaves his studio apartment before noon and always ensures he is home
before dark. When he does go out for supplies, he feels a prickling sensation on his neck, a
certainty that he is being observed, his movements tracked. He finds himself automatically mapping
every new space he enters, his eyes immediately flicking to find all possible exits before he can
even register what is on the walls or who is in the room. This vigilance extends to his phone; if
his current partner, Leo, takes more than a few minutes to reply to a text, a cold dread washes over
him. Uncertainty of any kind has become a potent threat, a sign that the fragile order he has built
is about to collapse. Back in his apartment, the building’s normal groans and settling sounds become
sinister. He listens constantly for the specific scrape of a key in the lock, the soft tread of
footsteps in the hall, monitoring for any sign of intrusion.

These behaviours have reshaped his life. He once loved the vibrant, chaotic energy of gallery
openings, but now the thought of a crowded room is unbearable. He has come to believe that what
happened was a direct result of who he is—that some deep-seated inadequacy or flaw in his character
invited the violence. This conviction has hollowed out his sense of self, leaving him feeling like
an outsider in his own life, a person who no longer belongs anywhere, not with his old friends, and
certainly not in the art world he once cherished. His work, once his greatest source of pride, now
feels like a testament to this failure. He paints through the night, not from a place of
inspiration, but in a frantic effort to outrun the nightmares that ambush him the moment he drifts
off to sleep.

The decision to seek help came after an incident with Leo. His new partner is gentle and patient,
but Bao’s world has shrunk to a size that barely includes himself, let alone another person. A few
nights ago, Leo came up behind him while he was washing brushes and wrapped his arms around his
waist in a quiet hug. Bao did not scream or pull away. He simply froze, his body becoming rigid and
unresponsive, his breath caught in his chest. In that moment, he wasn't in his studio with the man
he was learning to love; he was back in the old apartment, cornered and silent. The look of
confusion and hurt on Leo’s face when he finally turned around was what broke through his paralysis.
Bao is terrified of losing this new, tentative peace, but even more terrifying is the thought that
he is fundamentally broken, that the person he was before the event is gone for good. He has come to
therapy hoping to find a way to exist in the present moment without the past constantly crashing in.
