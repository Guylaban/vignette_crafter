Bao presents as a 35-year-old UX designer, married, who has been struggling since a significant
health crisis eight months prior. The event occurred on what felt like a perfectly ordinary Tuesday.
He was at his office, leading a design sprint for a new mobile app, when a crushing pressure bloomed
in his chest. At first, he dismissed it as indigestion or stress, trying to push through the
meeting. He remembers the minutes stretching into an eternity as he tried to focus on the wireframes
while a cold sweat slicked his skin. The delay in acknowledging the severity of his symptoms became
a central, terrifying feature of the memory. By the time he finally collapsed and a colleague called
911, he was certain he was dying. The chaotic ambulance ride and the frantic efficiency of the
emergency room are now a blur of disconnected sounds and flashes of light, a memory fragment he
actively works to suppress.

In the months since his recovery from what doctors termed a 'major cardiac event,' Bao’s world has
progressively shrunk. His body feels constantly tense, a coiled spring perpetually ready to react to
a danger only he can perceive. This hypervigilance is most acute at his job. When a webpage takes an
extra second to load or a colleague is two minutes late for a virtual meeting, his mind immediately
leaps to catastrophic conclusions—a system-wide crash is imminent, his colleague has been in a
terrible accident. He understands, on a rational level, that these are improbable, but the physical
sensation of panic is overwhelming. He cannot trust assurances of safety, even from his own wife,
Lien, or his cardiologist. When they tell him he is healthy and the danger has passed, the words
feel hollow. The only truth he can access is the relentless tension in his shoulders and the
shallow, watchful quality of his own breath.

To manage this pervasive sense of threat, Bao has constructed a life defined by avoidance. He has
mapped out a new, longer route to work to circumvent the hospital district, the sight of the
building alone enough to make his heart hammer in his chest. He deleted the health app from his
phone and has cancelled two consecutive follow-up physicals, unable to bear the thought of a doctor
listening to his chest. Any situation that puts his body under stress is now unthinkable. He was
once an avid cyclist, but now he avoids even a brisk walk, terrified that a rising heart rate will
trigger another event. When thoughts of that day in the office surface, he immediately immerses
himself in the most complex coding problem he can find, forcing the memories out with lines of
intricate logic.

These strategies have come at a cost. Bao has come to believe there is something fundamentally wrong
with him as a person, that his body and his mind are both defective. He sees his colleagues moving
through their lives with an ease he can no longer imagine and is convinced that his inability to
"move on" is a sign of profound personal weakness. This belief is compounded by a pervasive
emotional numbness. He describes feeling "dead inside," as if he is watching his own life from
behind a thick pane of glass. He sits beside Lien on the sofa in the evenings, aware that he should
feel love or comfort, but feels only a vast, grey emptiness. It was this hollowness, and Lien’s
tearful confession that she felt she was "living with a ghost," that finally prompted him to seek
help. He is now terrified of revisiting the trauma in therapy, yet more terrified of continuing to
live a life devoid of feeling, forever on alert for a danger that has already passed.
