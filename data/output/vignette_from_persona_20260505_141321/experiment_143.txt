For the past eight months, Sarah has felt like a ghost in her own life, a sentry perpetually on duty
in a world that has become a landscape of potential threats. The stalking began subtly, a man she
didn't know appearing at the coffee shop she frequented, then later, standing across the street from
the elementary school where she teaches. It escalated over three terrifying months. He learned her
schedule, her route home, her friends’ names. The police were involved, reports were filed, but his
presence felt more constant than theirs. The sight of a police car now does little to reassure her;
instead, it serves as a stark, uniformed reminder of a time when she was desperately in need of
protection and felt she received none. The most visceral memories are auditory. He would call her
apartment from a blocked number and say nothing, his presence conveyed only by the sound of his
slow, heavy breathing on the other end of the line.

Now, in the quiet of her own home, silence is no longer peaceful. It is a vacuum, a holding of
breath before something terrible happens. She keeps the television on constantly, a low murmur of
noise to fill the void. This is especially true at night, when the memory of those phone calls is
most potent. Fragments of that time surface without warning. While reading a picture book to her
first-grade class, the image of his face, seen through her peephole, can flash into her mind with
sickening clarity, only to vanish a second later. These moments are islands of terrifying detail in
a fog of what she can’t—or won’t—fully recall. When her partner, Mark, gently asks her what she is
thinking, she finds she knows what happened but is unable to give it form. The words simply are not
there, leaving her feeling isolated in an experience she cannot share.

Her work, once a source of joy, has become a minefield. As a teacher, she is constantly meeting new
people, and she has come to believe that any stranger could harbor a hidden malevolence. A new
father dropping off his child, a delivery person at the school’s front office—each one is a
potential threat to be assessed. She scans their hands, their eyes, their posture, convinced that
she must remain on guard at all times to prevent another catastrophe. This constant vigilance is
exhausting, leaving her drained and irritable. She no longer walks the few blocks from the bus stop
to her apartment, certain that the feeling of footsteps behind her is not paranoia but a reality she
has yet to confirm. The extra cost of taxis is a small price to pay for a few minutes of perceived
safety.

This deep-seated mistrust has poisoned her relationships. She finds herself questioning Mark’s
whereabouts, needing to know his exact location to quiet a frantic part of her brain that insists
people cannot be trusted. She knows this is unfair, that he is her greatest support, but the belief
is as persistent and invasive as the memories themselves. She feels as though her life, the one she
was building, has been irrevocably destroyed. Having strong feelings of fear or even anger now feels
dangerous in itself, a sign that she is losing control and might shatter completely. It was after a
week of barely speaking to Mark, retreating into a silent state of high alert, that he finally told
her, "I feel like I'm living with a stranger." The words landed with the weight of truth, prompting
her to seek therapy. Sarah is here now because she is terrified that this guarded, frightened
version of herself is all that is left, but she holds onto a desperate hope that she might one day
feel safe in a quiet room again.
