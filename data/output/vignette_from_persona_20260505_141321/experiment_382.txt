who has been bracing for impact his entire life. He has worked the same warehouse job for the last
thirty years, a place of concrete floors and echoing sounds, a landscape he finds far more

from his doctor about his blood pressure and his nightly drinking, that finally propelled him to
seek help for a weight he has carried since he was a small boy in rural Georgia.

events, what was said, or even what day of the week it was. What he has are fragments: the specific,
sour smell of whiskey on his father’s work coat, the slam of the flimsy front door that meant he was
home, and the sight of his mother’s hands twisting in her apron. These pieces exist in isolation,
disconnected from a linear narrative. More often, however, the memory comes without any image at
all. He will be stocking shelves at work, the scent of alcohol-based cleaner hanging in the air from
the morning crew, and suddenly a wave of pure, childlike terror will wash over him. It’s a feeling
so potent it makes his legs weak, an absolute certainty that something terrible is about to happen,
yet it is attached to nothing he can see in his mind’s eye. It is just the feeling, raw and
undiluted, visiting him across a chasm of seventy years.

These sensory echoes have shaped the entire architecture of his life. The slam of a heavy loading

was alright, a question that left him speechless. For this reason, he has always preferred the
noisy, open spaces of the city. He finds parks and wooded areas deeply unsettling. His ex-wife,
Maria, never understood his visceral rejection of a simple walk in the woods or a picnic by a lake.

not a place of peace, but a place to hide.

conversation lulls, he will instigate a small, pointless argument—about sports, politics, the right

his mind so he can get through the night without incident. Now, with his doctor’s words ringing in

might, at last, be able to put the little boy down to rest.
