long accustomed to solitude. It has been five years since his wife, Meera, passed away, but the true
hollowing out of his world began decades earlier, on a churning grey sea off the Oregon coast. For
forty years, he served in the Coast Guard, a source of immense pride that now feels like a brand of
shame. He cannot deliberately recall the full sequence of that day; when he tries, his mind meets a
strange, resistant fog. Instead, the memory lives in him as a series of disconnected, violent
snapshots. The shriek of the winch cable under strain, the icy sting of salt spray hitting his face,
the sickening, hollow thud against the hull, and the wail of the medevac siren that seemed to cut
right through the storm’s roar. When these fragments ambush him, they are not memories but a full
sensory assault. He is back on the deck of the cutter, the vessel lurching beneath his feet, the
freezing rain soaking his uniform, the metallic taste of fear in his mouth.

In the immediate aftermath, Arun developed a set of silent rules for managing the intrusion. He
learned to sense the approach of a memory and would immediately try to push it from his mind, a
mental shove that left him breathless. If that failed, he would tell himself a story: it was a
chaotic rescue, a freak wave was to blame, the event was not as bad as the fragments made it seem.
When the guilt still clawed at him, he would force himself to think only of positive things—Meera’s
smile, the birth of his son, the taste of a perfect mango—a desperate and exhausting act of will. He
had come to believe the event was not a tragic accident but a direct result of his own personal
failing. A better man, a more vigilant guardsman, would have anticipated the swell. The incident
happened, he was certain, because of the sort of person he was: fundamentally defective. This belief
became the lens through which he saw the world, a place of hidden dangers he was ill-equipped to
handle.

This pervasive sense of threat dictates the geography of his life. His son has tried to get him to
join a senior group at a new community center, but Arun refuses. He avoids any unfamiliar place
where he cannot immediately assess the risks and identify all the exits. Even in his own home, he
occupies only one chair: a worn armchair in the corner of the living room, which offers a clear view
of both the front door and the hallway. From this post, he remains on constant guard. Yet, this
vigilance coexists with a strange recklessness. Some evenings, he takes long walks after dusk,
striding along uneven sidewalks without a flashlight, a part of him almost daring a fall. It is a
quiet form of risk-taking, a way of tempting a fate he feels he probably deserves.

His triggers are woven into the fabric of daily life. An ambulance screaming past his street can
leave him trembling for an hour, the sound yanking a sensory fragment of the failed rescue into the

uncharacteristic anger; the sight of waves crashing against rocks is unbearable, a direct portal to
his greatest failure. Even discussions about the future feel perilous. When his granddaughter
