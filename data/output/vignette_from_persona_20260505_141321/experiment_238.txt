Arthur presents as a man accustomed to control, his posture rigid and his suit immaculate, a stark
contrast to the turmoil he describes. At 49, he is a respected City Council Member, known for his
steady hand in public debates. Privately, however, he feels he is losing a war that ended for him
two decades ago. The event itself is something he struggles to articulate. When he tries, the
narrative fractures. He speaks of a house-clearing operation, the suffocating darkness thick with
dust and the smell of cordite. He remembers the splintering of a door, a sudden weight on his back,
a hand grabbing his shoulder. But the sequence is lost, a collection of terrifying snapshots without
the connecting film. He feels like he is only remembering parts of what happened, and the rest is a
black void. Trying to explain it to his wife, he finds he loses the thread, the words dissolving as
the same raw terror he felt in that darkened room floods his body, leaving him breathless and mute.

This terror has become a constant, unwelcome companion. Arthur has come to believe the world is an
irredeemably dangerous place, and his only rational response is to be on guard all the time. He
finds it impossible to truly rest, his nights fragmented by an inability to fall or stay asleep. He
lies in the dark, cataloging the sounds of the house, certain that any unfamiliar noise is a
precursor to threat. His wife’s assurances that they are safe feel like hollow words; he cannot
trust safety, even when it is all around him. His body, he feels, has become his enemy, an
independent entity that keeps reacting as if he is still in that house. A colleague placing a hand
on his back in congratulations after a successful vote caused him to spin around, arms raised, a
gasp escaping his lips. The alarm in his own reaction is as frightening as the touch itself. He now
navigates his professional life with a calculated distance, ensuring a buffer of space that prevents
any form of physical contact with others.

This avoidance has seeped into his home life, creating a painful chasm between him and his wife. He
flinches from her touch, recoiling from the hand that once brought him comfort. He sees the hurt in
her eyes but feels powerless to stop the reaction. To manage the constant sense of being
overwhelmed, he has developed routines. In his car and his office, the television or radio is always
on, the volume turned up high enough to drown out his own thoughts. This sensory distraction
provides a temporary shield, but it is exhausting. The most draining task, however, is the one he
performs internally. He constantly monitors his own thoughts, scanning them for any sign that he is
losing his grip, that he is “going crazy.” This internal vigilance is a full-time job that leaves no
room for peace.

It was after a recent town hall meeting that Arthur finally sought help. An elderly constituent,
grateful for his work on a local park, had reached out and squeezed his arm. Arthur’s reaction was
instantaneous and severe; he jerked his arm away with such force that the woman stumbled. The public
embarrassment was acute, but it was the look of fear on the woman’s face that shattered his
composure. He saw in her eyes a reflection of his own loss of control. He came to therapy not with a
clear hope for recovery, but with a desperate fear that if he cannot regain command of his own mind
and body, he will lose everything he has spent the last twenty years building.
