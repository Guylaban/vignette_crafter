Elena has come to believe that her life is now divided into two distinct eras: before the storm, and
after. At fifty-seven, the first era feels like a lifetime ago, a memory belonging to a different
woman. It has been two years since Hurricane Alma, and the water has long since receded, but for
Elena, the flood is a constant presence. She works as a physical therapist, a profession built on
helping others regain strength and stability, yet she feels as though her own foundations have been
washed away.

The storm arrived with a ferocity that defied all warnings. Elena remembers the sound most vividly—a
relentless, deafening roar of wind and rain that seemed to be tearing the world apart. Then came the
water, first seeping under the door of the home she had lived in for thirty years, then pouring
through the windows. The smell of mud, sewage, and decay filled the air, and a wave of nausea rose
in her throat that has never quite left. She remembers wading through the chest-high, contaminated
water, the grief hitting her with the force of a physical blow as she looked back at her submerged
life.

In the months that followed, Elena found a small, second-floor apartment, believing the height would
offer a sense of security. But safety remained elusive. Her world, once expansive, began to shrink
until only the four walls of her apartment felt manageable. She is on constant alert, startled by
the gurgle of pipes in the wall or the rumble of a garbage truck on the street below, each sound a
potential harbinger of disaster. Sleep offers little respite; she lies awake for hours, listening,
waiting. Going out requires a monumental effort of will that she can rarely muster. This has had
tangible consequences for her work. She had to ask her supervisor to reassign all of her patients
who used the clinic's hydrotherapy pool. The sight of that large, still body of water was
unbearable, a placid mask for the destructive power she knew it held.

Her experience of the trauma itself has become fragmented and strange. Often, a wave of pure,
unattached emotion will crash over her—the sickening lurch of terror from the flood, the hollow ache
of loss—but with no accompanying image or thought. It is just the feeling, raw and overwhelming.
When she does intentionally recall the event, perhaps after hearing the name "Alma" on the news, the
memory feels oddly distant, like a story she was told about someone else. She can see the woman in
the floodwaters, but it doesn't feel like her. This sense of detachment makes the profound grief
that inevitably follows even more confusing. To escape these feelings, Elena has taught herself to
"check out." When a trigger is unavoidable, like the sight of a puddle after a downpour, she will
fix her gaze on a point on the wall and deliberately try to blank her mind, to leave her body until
the worst of it passes. More and more, she finds herself dwelling on the past, scrolling through old
photos of her garden, of neighborhood parties, of a life where she did not constantly scan the sky
for rain.

It was her daughter’s visit that finally prompted her to seek help. A light drizzle had started, and
her daughter found her sitting in the dark, unable to bring herself to turn on a light. The look of
deep concern on her daughter’s face made Elena realize that her safe zone had become a cage. She is
now terrified that this constant state of threat is her new reality. Her hope for therapy is not
grand; it is a simple, desperate wish to feel safe enough to walk in a park again, to hear the rain
without her heart beginning to race, and to feel, even for a moment, like the woman she was before
the storm.
