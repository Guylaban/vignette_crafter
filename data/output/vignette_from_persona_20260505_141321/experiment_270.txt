becoming a smooth, symmetrical vase, suddenly faltered and collapsed into a shapeless mound. At 75,
he had spent over fifty years at this wheel; it was the one place he had always felt in control.

capable of doing the things that had defined him. The frustration felt immense, a tidal wave of
despair over something that should have been a simple, muscle-memory task. He was certain he
couldn't deal with even the slightest upset anymore. The sound of a truck backfiring on the street
outside his small studio sent a jolt through his body, and instantly, he was somewhere else.

It was not a story he could tell, not with a beginning, middle, or end. The memory of fleeing his
village as a small boy didn't exist in words. It came in fragmented pieces, sensory snapshots that
felt more real and vivid than the sight of his own hands covered in wet clay. There was the frantic
grip of his mother’s hand, the smell of dust and sweat from the crowd pressing in on all sides, a
blur of khaki uniforms that meant danger, not safety. And there was the sound: a low, constant
rumble of engines and shouting that was not about rescue but was part of the chaos itself. When he
hears a car engine rev too loudly or sees a police cruiser pass his home, the memory doesn't feel
like the past; it feels like it is happening right now, just outside his door. He had come to
believe that he had to be especially careful, that you never know what can happen next, a lesson
learned in a single, terrifying week nearly seventy years ago.

In the decades that followed, Raj built a new life. He met his wife, Meera, raised a family, and
opened his pottery studio, a quiet sanctuary of creation. But the vigilance never left. Over the
years, it hardened into a set of unspoken rules. He would find himself at the window multiple times
an hour, scanning the quiet suburban street for any sign of threat. Before locking up his studio for
the night, he would circle the building twice, checking every window and door, even though his was
the only shop on the small, sleepy block. This constant need to check his surroundings was
exhausting, but the alternative—the feeling of being unprepared—was unthinkable.

Slowly, his world began to shrink. He started avoiding any situation that might involve
disagreement. The slightest elevation in Meera’s voice during a discussion about finances would
cause a knot of panic in his stomach, and he would immediately concede, desperate to avoid the
confrontation. He learned to placate, to smooth over any ripple of discord, and in doing so, a
quiet, hollow space grew between them. He also began to retreat from joy. When their grandson’s
fifth birthday party was planned in the local park, Raj developed a sudden, unshakable fatigue. The
idea of being in a crowd, of celebrating, of feeling happy or hopeful, felt dangerous, like tempting
fate. It was easier to stay home, to keep the world, with its potential for both joy and disaster,
at a safe distance.

The breaking point came last month. A dear friend and neighbor of thirty years passed away. The
thought of attending the funeral sent Raj into a state of near-paralysis. It was not about mourning

nonverbal memories of unburied, unmarked, and countless losses from his childhood. His refusal to
go, his inability to even explain why, was a profound hurt to Meera, who had to face their

were now costing him the very life he had worked so hard to build. He came to therapy not with a
clear story to tell, but with a collection of fragments and a desperate hope that he might one day
be able to sit at his wheel, center the clay, and feel his own hands become steady again.
