Eleanor presents as a composed and articulate public figure, a 58-year-old City Council Member known

feels both intensely present and strangely unreal. The trauma dates back fifteen years to her time

shock, and the dark stain spreading across his shirt. These pictures force their way into her mind,
unbidden, often during the quiet hum of a late-night council session or while she’s trying to read
briefing documents at home. When they come, she experiences them with a profound sense of
detachment, as if she is watching a tragic film about a stranger. The memory, she explains, feels
like it belongs to someone else, a story she was told rather than a life she lived.

The initial explosion that collapsed the field clinic is the anchor of this fragmented memory. The
sound of it has echoed through her life ever since, making her hometown feel like a minefield. A car
backfiring on the street below her office window can make her flinch so violently she spills her
coffee. During a particularly contentious budget meeting last year, the chairman banged his gavel
for order, and for a split second, Eleanor was not in the council chambers but back in the dust and
the dark, the sound ringing in her ears. The other primary trigger is less dramatic but more
pervasive: the sound of heavy breathing. In the rubble, trapped beside the dying young man, his
labored, ragged breaths were the only sound for what felt like hours. Now, that same sound can
unravel her. At a recent town hall, a passionate resident leaned close to the microphone, his
breathing amplified through the speakers as he made his point. Eleanor felt a cold wave of panic
wash over her, her heart hammering against her ribs, and had to feign a coughing fit to excuse
herself from the dais.

Over the years, these experiences have solidified into a set of rigid, unspoken rules for living.
She has come to believe that the world is an irredeemably dangerous place, and she moves through it
accordingly, always noting the exits, never sitting with her back to a door, and scanning every
crowd for threats she cannot name. This vigilance is exhausting. More insidious is the belief she
holds about her own mind: if she allows herself to truly think about the event, to try and piece the

a threat, something to be managed and contained. She fills her days with work, scheduling back-to-
back meetings and taking on extra committee assignments, anything to keep her mind occupied and
prevent the unwanted thoughts from finding purchase.

numb, as if a vital part of her was left behind in the rubble. She attends community celebrations
and ribbon-cuttings, smiling for the cameras, but feels nothing. When a constituent weeps in her
office over a personal tragedy, Eleanor offers practiced words of comfort, but the genuine empathy
she was once known for is gone. She is certain she will never be able to feel normal emotions again,
