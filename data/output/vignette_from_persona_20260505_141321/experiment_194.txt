Sarah sits in the waiting room, her gaze fixed on a scuff mark on the floor. At 35, she is a
counselor, a professional trained in the art of listening and holding space for others’ pain. Yet,
for the past six months, her own mind has been an unbearable place to be. It all began on a rainy
Tuesday in November. The memory doesn’t exist as a coherent story she can tell, not even to herself.
Instead, it comes in a series of disconnected, visceral fragments: the rhythmic thump of the
windshield wipers, the smear of streetlights on wet asphalt, the sound of her own voice humming
along to the radio. Then, the sickeningly soft thud that vibrated through the steering wheel and up
her arms, followed by an image of a young man’s bicycle twisted on the curb. She tries hard not to
think about it, pushing the images down whenever they surface, but they are relentless. When a piece
of the memory breaks through, it brings not just fear, but a wave of profound, suffocating grief
that leaves her breathless.

In the weeks that followed the accident, Sarah’s world began to shrink. She was certain the accident
happened because of the way she acted; she had glanced at her phone for a split second to check a
map notification. That single, mundane action had become the focal point of her guilt. She had
failed in the most fundamental way—she should have protected others, and instead, she had caused
grievous harm. This belief settled deep inside her, a cold, heavy stone of shame. Soon, the sound of
a distant siren would send a jolt of panic through her, forcing her to pull over if she was driving
or to quickly change the channel if watching television. She started taking long, convoluted routes
to her office to avoid the street where the hospital was located, the sight of its entrance too
potent a reminder of the ambulance she had followed that night.

Her hypervigilance became a constant companion. At home, she was superalert, startled by the sound
of the mail slot or a car door slamming outside. Sleep offered little escape; she would lie awake
for hours, her mind racing, or jolt awake from shallow, unrestful dreams, her heart pounding. The
world felt menacing, a place where catastrophic failure was always just one second of inattention
away. This constant state of being on guard has been exhausting, leaching the colour from her life.
Her work, once the source of her identity and purpose, now feels like a hollow performance. Nothing
in her life feels meaningful anymore when weighed against the immutable fact of the harm she caused.
How can she guide others toward healing when she feels so fundamentally broken?

This sense of being a fraud has manifested in a way that is crippling her career. In sessions with
her own clients, Sarah finds she can no longer hold their gaze. Making eye contact with people has
become an unbearable act of intimacy. She is convinced that if she looks into their eyes, they will
see the truth of who she is—a failure, a source of harm. So she stares at the wall just behind their
heads, or at the pen in her hand, offering therapeutic reflections to a space in the room. This
avoidance has not gone unnoticed. A long-term client recently asked if she was alright, noting that
she seemed distant. The question felt like an accusation. The shame of it was so intense, she almost
cancelled the rest of her appointments for the day. It was this moment, coupled with her husband
gently asking why she wouldn’t look at him when he spoke to her, that finally propelled her to seek
help. Sarah is here now, terrified of telling the story she cannot piece together, but more
terrified of what will happen if she continues to live with the fragments alone.
