Rohan, a 41-year-old park ranger, sits with a stillness that seems at odds with the tension in his
shoulders. It has been eighteen months since the solo trekking trip that he now refers to only as
"that time." He had been hiking in a remote mountain range, an experienced outdoorsman seeking
solitude, when he was taken by a local militia. They mistook him for a foreign operative. The memory
of it doesn't arrive as a continuous narrative, but as a slideshow of stark, silent images: the
rough texture of the burlap sack pulled over his head, the worn-out insignia on a soldier's uniform,
the specific crack in the ceiling of the room where they held him. Sometimes, when he tries to
recall the sequence of events, he feels nothing at all, a profound numbness that makes him feel like
he’s recounting a story about a stranger.

His captors wore uniforms, and now the sight of any official attire—a police officer at a traffic
stop, a security guard at the mall, even his own crisp National Park Service uniform—sends a jolt of
ice through him. At work, in the vast wilderness he once considered his sanctuary, he feels
constantly exposed. He has come to believe that the park visitors are watching him, judging his
every move, their gazes a silent accusation. This feeling of being seen is a direct echo of the
interrogation room, where he was forced to stand for hours under the unblinking stare of his
captors. He is certain that a stronger, smarter person would never have been so naive as to hike in
that region alone; this self-reproach is a constant, quiet hum beneath his daily thoughts. What
happened feels less like a misfortune and more like a permanent stain of humiliation he can never
wash away.

In the months after his release, Rohan’s life began to shrink. He developed habits he didn't
understand at first. To regain a sense of control that was stripped from him, he began to restrict
his eating with rigid precision, weighing every portion and counting every calorie. The gnawing
hunger was a feeling he could command, unlike the terror. At night, when sleep wouldn't come, he
would lie awake and construct elaborate revenge fantasies, imagining himself powerful and his
captors helpless. These thoughts provided a fleeting surge of strength but left him exhausted and
agitated by morning. With his partner and his colleagues, he found himself becoming excessively
agreeable, nodding along to any suggestion and suppressing his own needs. It felt safer to be
compliant, to offer no resistance, to simply go along with whatever others wanted. He just wanted to
feel safe, and he could no longer trust that he was, even when his partner held him and told him he
was home.

His body remembers the trauma in ways his mind sometimes doesn't. A few weeks ago, while on patrol,
a deer suddenly bolted from the undergrowth. Rohan didn't just jump; he dropped to the ground, his
heart hammering against his ribs as if he were under attack. When a memory-image of a guard’s face
flashes into his mind, he often feels a ghost of a sharp, radiating pain in his side, right where a
boot once connected. These moments are disorienting, visceral intrusions that make the past feel
terrifyingly present. He feels utterly set apart from everyone he knows, isolated by an experience
he cannot adequately explain and which they could never truly understand.

It was an incident at the park’s visitor center that finally compelled him to seek help. A tourist,
a veteran wearing a military-style jacket, began asking him detailed questions about patrol routes
and park security. Rohan froze, the man’s authoritative tone transporting him back to the
interrogation room. He felt the floor tilt beneath him, the sounds of the center fading into a dull
roar. He was there, and he was here, and the terror was absolute. He managed to excuse himself, but
the episode left him shaken for days. He is terrified of losing his job, the one piece of his old
life he has clung to. More than that, he is afraid that this broken, watchful version of himself is
all he will ever be.
