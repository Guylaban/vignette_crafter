and damp earth a constant comfort. It was where she and Kofi had met, where they had married, and
where, six months ago, she had watched him die. The memory of that day didn't come as a story, but

first was always the sound—a sharp crack like lightning, followed by a low rumble that vibrated
through the soles of her boots. Then the image: Kofi, twenty feet ahead on the switchback, turning
to her with a look of startled confusion. Another flash: the cascade of rock and dust, a blur of
grey and brown obscuring him entirely. And the last, the one that made her stomach clench with a
grief so profound it felt like a physical blow: the unnatural stillness afterwards, and the sight of
his brightly coloured jacket, partially visible beneath the scree.

In the weeks that followed, the world she had once navigated with expert confidence became a
landscape of imminent threat. At work, her body was a tightly coiled spring, her shoulders bunched
up to her ears. Every rustle in the undergrowth, every shift of gravel underfoot, sent a jolt of
adrenaline through her. She had come to believe she was no longer capable of the things that had
once defined her. The steep, rocky trails she used to patrol with ease now seemed treacherous, each
ledge a potential collapse. She started finding excuses to hand off the more remote trail
maintenance checks to junior rangers, citing paperwork or equipment inventories. Her supervisor had
noticed, and the unspoken disappointment in his eyes felt like another confirmation of her failure.
She knew she had changed, and she was certain the damage was permanent.

At home, the silence was an enemy. Adeola kept the television on from the moment she walked in the
door until she fell into a fitful sleep, the constant noise a flimsy barrier against the intrusive
snapshots of that day. She couldn't bring herself to just sit, to relax; it felt profoundly
dangerous, as if letting her guard down would invite the full force of the horror to consume her. So
she found tasks, organizing cupboards, scrubbing floors, anything to keep her mind and body occupied
until exhaustion finally claimed her for a few hours. But sleep offered little respite. She would
often wake abruptly in the dark, her heart hammering against her ribs, the feeling of being dead
inside replaced by a raw, active terror.

The vibrant emotional life she once had felt like a distant memory. Most days, she existed in a
state of muted gray, a profound emotional numbness that disconnected her from her own life. Then, a
trigger—the sight of a hiking boot ad, the sound of a distant rumble—would tear through the fog, and
the grief would hit her with the force of a physical impact, leaving her breathless and shaking.
With it came the searing guilt. Why had she told Kofi to go on ahead? Why hadn't she seen the
instability of the rock face? She felt responsible, a feeling that festered alongside the fear and
the sorrow. It was the accumulation of these moments—the near-miss with a formal warning at work,
the realization she hadn't had a real conversation with anyone in weeks, the terrifying thought that
she might feel this hollow forever—that finally pushed her to seek help. She walked into the clinic
not with hope, but with a quiet desperation, clinging to the slim possibility that someone could
piece back together the capable, vibrant woman who had been lost on that mountain trail.
