under her thumb. It has been eight months since she was discharged from the hospital, but she feels
further from her old life than ever. At forty-two, she had always been the one in control, a

preternatural calm. Now, control feels like a distant memory from someone else’s life. The illness
had come on with a shocking swiftness—a fever she’d dismissed as the flu, which then exploded into a
system-wide infection. Her last clear memory before the ICU was of her husband’s panicked face, and
then everything fractured.

searingly vivid fragments surrounded by a grey fog. She can perfectly recall the slick, cold feel of
a needle sliding into the back of her hand and the sharp, antiseptic smell of the solution being
wiped on her skin. She remembers the exact shade of green on the heart monitor, a jagged line that
seemed to be the only thing tethering her to the world. But the hours and days around these moments
are completely blank. This fragmentation makes the entire experience feel profoundly unreal, like a
terrifying film she was forced to watch. Yet, at other times, the emotional truth of it ambushes her
without any image at all. She can be standing in her kitchen and suddenly be gripped by a
suffocating, world-ending terror, the pure, distilled feeling of the ICU, even as her eyes see
nothing but the familiar tiles and countertops.

In the months since coming home, Mai has dedicated herself to building a fortress against these
memories. She actively tries to erase them, pushing down any fragment that surfaces with a force
that leaves her exhausted. This began with small things. She threw out all the household cleaners
that had that specific, sharp hospital smell. She started taking a longer route to work, a fifteen-
minute detour, just to avoid driving past the medical center. She has cancelled her last two
physical examinations, the thought of a doctor’s office with its cold rooms and sterile equipment
being simply unbearable. Her husband doesn’t understand why she recoils when he turns on a medical
drama, or why she insists on keeping the house uncomfortably warm, even in the summer. To Mai, a
chill in the air isn’t just a drop in temperature; it’s the ghost of the profound, bone-deep cold
she felt while lying helpless under a thin hospital blanket.

on pressure, she now feels perpetually on edge. The powerful air conditioning in the conference
rooms is a constant trigger, making it difficult to concentrate. She finds herself perpetually
monitoring her own thoughts, scanning for any sign that she is losing her grip, that the chaos of
the illness is seeping into her mind. This internal surveillance makes it impossible to focus on the
intricate details of a project plan. She had come to believe her body was permanently damaged by
what happened, a fragile system just waiting to fail again. Every minor headache or twinge of
fatigue is, in her mind, a harbinger of another catastrophe.

worry in her husband's eyes and feels a profound sense of shame that she can no longer be the
steady, capable partner she once was. She feels like a ghost in her own marriage, present in body
but absent in spirit. This conviction solidified a few weeks ago when she missed a critical deadline
at work for the first time in her career, a direct result of her inability to focus. Looking at the

months: her life, the one she had so carefully built, had been destroyed by the trauma. She finally
sought help after her husband found her standing frozen in a supermarket aisle, tears streaming down

brought her to therapy was not just the fear of her triggers, but the terrifying certainty that the
capable, confident woman she used to be is gone for good, and a desperate, flickering hope that she
might somehow find her way back.
