Keilani sits in the therapy room, her hands clenched so tightly in her lap that her knuckles are
white. At fifty-three, she feels ancient. It has been eight months since her husband, Maleko, died,
but for Keilani, it is always that night. The memory is not a story she can tell; it is a series of
brutal, disconnected snapshots that ambush her without warning. The flash of headlights through her
windscreen, the screech of tires that wasn't hers, the jarring impact. Then, silence, before the
smell hit her—the sharp, sickeningly sweet scent of spilled whiskey from the other car, a smell that
seemed to cling to the broken glass and twisted metal. That same scent had followed her to the
hospital, radiating from the clothes of the drunk driver who had walked away with only scratches.
That smell is the anchor to the worst moment of her life.

In the weeks that followed, the world became a minefield. The memories were not something she
recalled, but something that happened *to* her. They were like a film reel stuck on a loop, playing
the same fragments over and over: the paramedic’s grave voice, the sterile brightness of the
emergency room, the final, devastating stillness of Maleko’s hand in hers. These images would force
their way into her mind while she was trying to run her small grocery store, a place that had once
been her pride. A customer might drop a jar, and the sudden crash would send her staggering back
against a shelf, heart hammering, the scent of whiskey filling her nostrils as if the accident were
happening all over again right there in the aisle. She would feel the cold linoleum of the hospital
floor on her skin, the world dissolving around her until a concerned shopper’s voice pulled her
back.

She began to change. The warmth and easy laughter that had made her shop a neighborhood hub
vanished. She started by asking her young part-time employee to handle the beer and wine deliveries,
unable to bear the clinking of the bottles or the faint smell of alcohol that sometimes lingered on
the cardboard boxes. Eventually, she stopped selling alcohol altogether, a decision that baffled her
regulars and cut deep into her profits. She couldn't explain that the scent made her feel like she
was suffocating, that it brought the flashing lights and the finality of that night rushing back in.
She just knew she had to make it stop.

Her world shrank further. A lifelong friend invited her to her mother’s funeral, and Keilani stared
at the invitation, her body rigid with a terror she couldn't articulate. The thought of being
surrounded by grief, of the hushed whispers and the sight of a casket, felt like an imminent threat.
She sent a vague, apologetic text and hasn't heard from her friend since. Even conversations with
customers became perilous. If someone mentioned a recent loss, a sick relative, or anything touching
on grief, a cold panic would seize her. She would find an excuse to retreat to the stockroom,
leaving them standing mid-sentence. She knew she seemed cold, rude even, but the alternative—letting
their words connect with the raw, gaping wound inside her—was unthinkable.

Even at home, there is no peace. She feels a constant, thrumming anxiety, a certainty that something
terrible is about to happen. She is always on guard, unable to relax, reacting to the sound of a
passing siren or a car backfiring as if danger were right outside her door. She looks in the mirror
and sees a stranger. The competent, loving woman who built a business and a life with Maleko is
gone. In her place is this frightened, isolated person she no longer recognizes. She has come to
believe her life was not just altered by the accident, but completely destroyed. There is no future,
only an endless present where she is trapped in the wreckage of that one night. It was the letter
from her landlord about the shop’s mounting rent arrears that finally propelled her to seek help.
The fear of losing the last tangible piece of her old life was, for a moment, stronger than the fear
of everything else.
