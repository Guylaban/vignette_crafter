Mele, a sixty-six-year-old curriculum designer, describes her life as having been lived behind glass
for the last three decades. The world is out there, vibrant and moving, but she feels disconnected
from it, watching from a soundproof room. The trauma itself is a memory she struggles to hold onto,
yet can never escape. As a young soldier, she was part of a non-combatant administrative unit during
a rapid deployment. The memory that haunts her is not of a firefight, but of a chaotic evacuation.
She recalls the frantic order to board a helicopter, the crush of bodies, the high-pitched whine of
the rotors that felt like it was vibrating her teeth loose. The memory comes in disconnected pieces:
the pilot’s strained face visible for a second through the cockpit door, the smell of jet fuel, the
violent lurch as the aircraft lifted off under fire, and the absolute, terrifying powerlessness of
being a passenger with no control over her fate. When she tries to speak of it, the words catch in
her throat; she knows what happened, but language feels inadequate to capture the sheer sensory
overload and terror of that ascent. The story feels too extreme, too much like a film, making it
hard to believe it was once her reality.

This fragmented history bleeds into her present. The sound of a medevac helicopter flying over her
apartment building can paralyze her for several minutes, her heart pounding a frantic rhythm against
her ribs. An intrusive image of the ground falling away beneath her will flash through her mind,
vivid and unwelcome. At her job, which she has done remotely for years, these moments of internal
crisis are hidden. However, the structure of her work provides its own challenges. During mandatory
video conferences with senior management, particularly with a new, authoritative department head,
Mele feels a familiar surge of panic. Being in a situation where someone has clear power over her,
where her input can be dismissed with a word, brings back the suffocating helplessness of the
helicopter. During these meetings, she finds herself mentally mapping the layout of her own
apartment, reminding herself that the front door is only a few feet away. This need to be near an
exit is a constant; on the rare occasions she goes to a coffee shop, she will only take a table with
a clear line of sight to the entrance.

Over the years, Mele has constructed a life designed to prevent these feelings. She had come to
believe that she has to be on guard all the time, that a moment of inattention could lead to
disaster. This belief has shrunk her world. She avoids going out unless absolutely necessary,
ordering groceries for delivery and declining the few social invitations she still receives from old
friends. She has also become a master of emotional suppression, carefully curating her media
consumption to avoid anything that might provoke a strong reaction, be it joy or sorrow. She sees
this as a necessary defence, a way to keep the chaos at bay. But the consequence has been a profound
sense of isolation. She feels set apart from others, as if her experiences have marked her in a way
no one else can understand. This feeling is compounded by a deep, gnawing self-doubt. In the
helicopter, she was powerless to act; now, she can't trust that she will do the right thing in even
simple situations. She endlessly revises her curriculum modules, certain she has missed a critical
error that will have terrible consequences, a fear that feels disproportionate but is impossible to
shake.

It was the start of a major construction project next to her building that finally propelled her to
seek help. The daily, unpredictable noise—and especially the frequent use of a heavy-lift helicopter
to move materials—has shattered her carefully constructed peace. Her home no longer feels like a
sanctuary. The constant auditory triggers have left her exhausted and perpetually on edge. Mele came
to therapy not with a grand hope of being "cured," but with a simple, desperate plea: to feel safe
in her own home again. What frightens her most is the thought that this state of constant, vigilant
fear is all that is left for her.
