At 29, Arjun Patel came to treatment after years of insisting to himself that what happened was
behind him. He had been a child when his family fled political violence and spent months moving
through temporary shelters and transit camps before reaching the United States. What stays with him
most vividly is not a single image but the bodily atmosphere of that time: being pressed shoulder to
shoulder with strangers in overcrowded rooms, the sense that there was no way out, men moving too
close without warning, and the sour smell of alcohol on adults whose moods felt dangerous and
unreadable. Certain places still carry an immediate charge for him—narrow waiting areas, packed
subway platforms, building lobbies where people bunch together near an elevator. The smell of
alcohol can make his chest seize before he has even located where it is coming from, and when
someone unfamiliar steps toward him in a crowded space, his body surges into alarm before he has had
time to tell himself he is in present-day Boston and not trapped somewhere else.

As he got older, Arjun came to organize these reactions around a set of painful conclusions about
himself and other people. He had come to believe that people were not what they seemed, that danger
hid behind ordinary faces and ordinary gestures. He was also certain that something about him was
visible, as if other people could look at him and immediately know what had happened to him, know he
came from that history, know there was something wrong with him. Those beliefs sharpened the force
of particular triggers. Crowded spaces did not feel neutral and overstimulating in a general way;
they felt full of strangers he could not trust. Physical locations that resembled transit halls or
institutional corridors drew his attention because they seemed to expose him, and the smell of
alcohol did not register as incidental because in his mind it belonged to people who could turn
unpredictable at any moment.

He now works as an architect, a profession he chose in part because structure, space, and control
feel deeply important to him. Even there, the symptoms have woven themselves into his daily life.
Site visits are especially difficult. If a contractor or client he does not know well approaches him
in a tight stairwell or on a noisy construction floor, he feels the threat instantly in his body:
muscles tensing, heart racing, a jolt of readiness before thought catches up. The reaction itself
leaves him humiliated. When he notices himself scanning exits or recoiling from an ordinary
approach, it seems to confirm his private conviction that there is something defective about him,
that he is not like other people and cannot inhabit the world normally. And the more threatened he
feels by strangers in routine settings, the more certain he becomes that safety cannot be trusted
just because someone else says it is there.

Over time he developed ways of managing this that initially felt sensible. Because he believed
others were untrustworthy and that his damage was somehow visible, he began trying to keep absolute
control over whatever he could. He restricts his eating rigidly, describing it less as concern about
weight than as the one area where he can impose order and not feel at the mercy of his body. He
works hard to suppress any thought related to the past, cutting himself off internally the moment an
image or sensation starts to emerge. He keeps his mind occupied almost constantly with drawings,
podcasts, work problems, or phone games, because silence feels too risky. Yet these habits have had
their own effects. Going long hours without eating leaves him more physically keyed up, and in that
depleted state sudden closeness, noise, or the smell of alcohol can feel even more immediately
dangerous. His constant mental effort to push things away has also deepened his sense that there
must be something profoundly wrong with him if his mind has to be managed so tightly all the time.

In recent months the cycle has tightened. The more threatened he feels in ordinary public
encounters, the more he notices the very settings he dreads: train platforms, office receptions,
crowded design reviews, bars near project sites where coworkers gather after meetings. He has
started declining team events if they are held in busy restaurants, partly because of the crowding
and partly because the smell of beer or liquor hits him so fast. Colleagues experience him as
private and hard to read, which fits too easily with his belief that distance is safer. Being alone
has not brought relief. If anything, the endless effort to distract himself and keep control has
left him more convinced that other people would see him as damaged if they got too close, and less
able to believe reassurance when friends tell him he is safe now.

What finally brought him in was an incident at work: during a packed elevator ride to a client
presentation, a stranger shifted suddenly beside him and he felt a wave of terror so immediate that
he had to get off on the wrong floor and sit on the ground until the shaking eased. Afterward he
could not stop thinking that his body had betrayed him in public and that everyone must have seen
it. He is seeking help because he is exhausted by the vigilance and by how small his life has
become. What frightens him most is the possibility that this is permanent—that he will never trust
anyone, never feel safe in his own body, and never enter an ordinary crowded room without bracing
for danger.
