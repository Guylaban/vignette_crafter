Koa, a 53-year-old photographer from New Zealand, had always found solace in the art of capturing
moments. His days were often filled with the beauty of landscapes and the vibrancy of people, but
beneath the surface, he carried a weight that had become increasingly difficult to bear since the
traumatic incident that changed everything. Several years ago, while on assignment in a remote area,
Koa was taken captive during a violent uprising. The experience was harrowing; he was held for days,
surrounded by threats that felt all too real, leading to a profound shift in his perception of
safety. The memory of the chaos, the fear, and the uncertainty was etched into his mind, and it
haunted him even in the quiet moments of his life.

Since that time, Koa's sense of threat had spiraled out of control. When the threat felt this close,
relying on maladaptive strategies such as planning escape routes became necessary for him to
navigate the world. He found himself hypervigilant, constantly scanning his surroundings for
potential dangers. This overwhelming sense of threat made planning escape routes feel like a
necessary strategy, as he believed that if he didn't prepare for the worst, he would be caught off
guard. When the threat felt this close, relying on maladaptive strategies such as planning escape
routes became necessary for him to navigate the world. In social situations, he would position
himself where he felt the most secure, often choosing seats with his back against a wall, watching
others with a keen eye. Sudden movements from people around him would send his body into a state of
tension, reacting instinctively as if he were still in that hostile environment. Koa's thoughts
frequently raced with images of confrontation and escape, leading him to plan exit routes before
entering any new place, whether it was a bustling café or an art gallery. This constant sense of
threat made him feel as though he needed to prepare for danger at every turn, compelling him to rely
on these maladaptive strategies to regain a semblance of control in his life. However, without ever
testing his fears in real situations, Koa never received the evidence he needed to understand that
the danger was not as substantial as his body insisted, reinforcing his sense of threat further.
Each time he avoided a situation or relied on checking and planning, he inadvertently fortified his
perception of danger, convinced that his behaviors were justified responses to an ever-present
threat. The checking and the avoidance had kept him alive, in his nervous system's accounting, which
meant the danger they were responding to must be real and ongoing.

As his sense of danger intensified, so too did his reliance on maladaptive strategies. Koa found
himself seeking reassurance from his wife, asking her repeatedly if he seemed okay or if he was
acting strangely. His need for constant reassurance was a direct result of the overwhelming sense of
threat he felt; when the world felt so dangerous, seeking validation became a way to momentarily
quell the anxiety gnawing at him. However, the more he sought reassurance, the more his fears were
reinforced; each time he leaned on these strategies without confronting the actual threat, he felt
even less safe, as if the danger he anticipated was validated by his own need for comfort. This need
for validation became a crutch, a way to momentarily alleviate the unease that gnawed at him.
However, the more he leaned on these maladaptive strategies, the more his sense of threat
intensified; without ever challenging his fears, he remained convinced that danger was ever-present,
his body locked in a state of alertness that never seemed to subside. However, each time he sought
reassurance, it left him feeling more vulnerable, as if admitting he needed help was a sign of
weakness. The very act of seeking comfort became intertwined with his perception of threat; the more
he felt he needed to ask for reassurance, the more convinced he became that he was not okay, that
danger lurked just out of sight.

Koa's world had narrowed significantly since the trauma. He avoided crowded places, fearing that
being surrounded by too many people would trigger his anxiety. This avoidance led to a growing
isolation; friends would invite him to events, but he often found excuses to decline, choosing
instead to stay home where he felt safer. The photography he once loved became tainted by his fear;
he rarely ventured out to capture the world around him, feeling the weight of his anxiety overshadow
his passion. His wife noticed the change; the vibrant man she married was retreating into a shell,
and her concern deepened with each passing day.

With a sense of urgency, Koa finally reached a breaking point. The constant tension in his body, the
relentless vigilance, and the paralyzing fear had become unbearable. He realized that his coping
mechanisms were not helping him heal; instead, they were weaving a tighter web of anxiety around
him. It was during a quiet moment at home, as he sat with his camera gathering dust, that he decided
he could no longer manage this alone. He told his wife he needed help, a decision that both
terrified and relieved him. Koa hoped that therapy could provide him with tools to reclaim his life,
yet he was haunted by the fear that he might never feel safe again. As he prepared for his first
appointment, he felt a flicker of hope mixed with trepidation, uncertain of what lay ahead but
yearning for a way to break free from the grip of his past.
