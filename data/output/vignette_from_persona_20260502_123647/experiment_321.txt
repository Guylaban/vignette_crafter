In the months that followed, Anjali's life began to change drastically. She found herself avoiding
any area close to where the attack had occurred, unwilling to face the potential reminders of that
day. Each time she chose to avoid these areas, it only reinforced her fear, making her more certain
that those places held dangers that could trigger memories she wasn't ready to confront. The sound
of sirens would echo in her mind, and rather than face the panic it evoked, she’d find herself
taking longer routes to work, each detour a way to sidestep the memories that lurked just around the
corner. The fear of being overwhelmed by memories became so strong that she stopped taking that
route to work entirely — better to add twenty minutes to her commute than to feel that way again.
Each place she avoided became a reminder in itself — the long way round she walked every morning was
its own daily acknowledgement of what had happened. She stopped taking routes that would bring her
near the site of the attack, opting instead for longer commutes that allowed her to feel a semblance
of safety, but each detour only served to remind her of the places she could no longer bear to
visit. Every time she thought about returning to her office, a knot of anxiety tightened in her
chest. Her avoidance had grown in all directions until the city was full of routes she no longer
used and things she could no longer say, each decision reinforcing her belief that staying away was
the only way to keep the memories at bay. The thought of hearing sirens again filled her with dread,
and she began to retreat into the safety of her home, where she could control her environment. But
even there, the memories would sneak in, often triggered by the faintest whiff of smoke from a
neighbor’s barbecue or the sound of an ambulance rushing by. Her safety behaviors had drawn a map of
the event around her: everything she couldn't do, everywhere she couldn't go, every person she kept
at arm's length. This self-imposed isolation created a cycle where the very act of avoiding places
and people began to remind her of the trauma, as she became increasingly aware of what she was
missing in her life. Every time a siren echoed through the streets, Anjali would instinctively scan
her surroundings, her heart racing as she searched for threats that no longer existed, convinced
that danger lurked just around the corner. Her habits of hyper-vigilance turned everyday sounds into
harbingers of dread, as the more she avoided situations that might trigger her anxiety, the more
acutely aware she became of every siren, every unexpected noise, amplifying her sense of impending
danger. This hyper-vigilance became a familiar routine for her; she would position herself near the
exit whenever she left the house, a compulsion born from the belief that being close to an escape
route would somehow shield her from the chaos she once experienced.

Anjali's once-thriving dental practice suffered as a result of her avoidance behaviors. Her patients
noticed her increasing reluctance to schedule appointments, and her colleagues commented on her
growing isolation. She spent more time at home, consumed by worries that someone might have power
over her again, a feeling that had taken root after that fateful day. In her mind, the world outside
had become unpredictable, and she feared being trapped in situations where she could not escape. The
very thought of being in a position of powerlessness haunted her, manifesting in her reluctance to
engage in social gatherings or even visit the grocery store without carefully planning her route and
timing.

Despite the mounting toll on her mental health and professional life, Anjali felt shame in seeking
help. She had always prided herself on her resilience, but now she felt like a shell of the person
she once was. It was only after a close friend expressed concern about her well-being that she
finally decided to reach out for support. As she sat in the waiting room, anxiety coursing through
her veins, she silently hoped for a way to reclaim her life. What frightened her most was the
thought of living in this perpetual state of fear, forever haunted by the past. What she longed for
was the ability to step outside her door without checking her surroundings, to breathe deeply
without the acrid smell of smoke invading her senses, and to feel safe in a world that had once been
so familiar. Yet, with each avoided street, her world became smaller — the very act of evasion
served as a reminder of her trauma, reinforcing her anxiety each time she reflected on the places
she could no longer bear to visit.
