Asma, a 51-year-old social ministry worker, found herself increasingly trapped in a cycle of
distress that stemmed from memories of traumatic childhood experiences. Growing up in a household
overshadowed by physical abuse from her caregiver, she could still vividly recall the moments when
her body would tense, mirroring the posture of fear and vulnerability she had known so intimately.
These memories, visceral and raw, often surged unbidden, flooding her mind with an overwhelming
sense of rage that felt both familiar and foreign. In this turmoil, she began to devise strategies
to shield herself from the emotional onslaught; for instance, she learned to keep her desk near the
exit at work, believing that if she could escape quickly, she might avoid the overwhelming feelings
that the memories triggered. As these memories resurfaced, they not only triggered her anger but
also led her to develop maladaptive strategies, such as staying close to exits at work, in a
misguided attempt to protect herself from the emotional turmoil they unleashed. Each time a painful
memory surged, it reinforced her belief that she needed to stay vigilant, leading her to further
distance herself from others, as if avoiding connections could shield her from reliving the past.
Each time a memory surged, she would instinctively move closer to the exits, convinced that being
able to escape would quell the emotional storm the memories brought with them, reinforcing her need
to shield herself from further pain. The intensity of these recollections had a profound impact on
her daily life; when they surfaced, she would often find herself retreating from work, unable to
cope with the cacophony of emotions that followed.

At her job, Asma’s strategies for managing her trauma had become maladaptive. She felt an urgent
need to stay near exits, a behavior that had developed over time as a way to protect herself from
the perceived threat of danger. The thought of being trapped, much like she had felt in her
childhood home, was unbearable. Whenever that sense of threat loomed, the memories would resurface
with alarming clarity, echoing her childhood fears and making it nearly impossible for her to escape
from the emotional weight they carried. In meetings, she would sit with her back to the door,
scanning the room for potential exit routes, convinced that if she could see any signs of danger,
she might be able to avoid it. This constant scanning for danger not only fueled her anxiety but
also made her more susceptible to vivid memories of her traumatic past, as if the heightened threat
was a magnet for those recollections, pulling them into her consciousness. When the sense of threat
loomed large, meticulously planning her exits felt not just prudent but necessary, as her body
reacted to the pressure of potential danger, demanding that she remain vigilant at all times. This
vigilance had become a necessity, yet it drained her emotionally, leaving her exhausted and
irritable. As her sense of threat escalated, the vivid recollections of her past traumas would
resurface, reminding her of the dangers that once lurked, making it difficult for her to escape the
cycle of anxiety. The more she relied on avoidance strategies, the more those memories remained at
the forefront of her mind, never fully addressed and continually reinforced by her efforts to avoid
situations that might trigger them. Her colleagues noticed her angry outbursts, which seemed to come
out of nowhere, but Asma believed they were justified reactions to the unrelenting sense of threat
that loomed in her mind.

Her heightened sense of danger was not merely a product of the present; it was deeply intertwined
with her past. The persistent sense of threat kept her close to those traumatic memories, as if the
adrenaline coursing through her when she felt endangered pulled the recollections into sharper
focus, making them feel inescapable. On nights when the sense of threat felt particularly intense,
the memories would flood her mind, vividly replaying the moments of her past trauma, as if the
adrenaline demanded she confront those experiences once again. When she walked through her
neighborhood, she often felt the need to look behind her, convinced that someone was tracking her
movements. This incessant checking was a response to the phantom shadows of her childhood, where
safety was a fleeting concept. Even in the absence of immediate danger, her body reacted as if she
were still a child, fleeing from an unseen threat. The memories of her childhood abuse, with their
accompanying feelings of helplessness, would flood her mind, igniting an instinctive fight-or-flight
response that she could not control. In those moments, she felt overwhelmed and unable to cope,
leading her to devise strategies like remaining close to exits at work, believing that staying alert
would somehow shield her from the emotional turmoil those memories unleashed.

The memories of those traumatic experiences also influenced Asma's decision to avoid situations that
would require her to be alone with others. She had come to believe that being alone with another
person could lead to vulnerability, a state she could not afford. Thus, she would arrange her
workday to ensure that she was never in a secluded space. This strategy, while protective, further
entrenched her memories of fear and helplessness, reinforcing the idea that the world was a
dangerous place. Yet, in her efforts to avoid situations that reminded her of her trauma, she had
inadvertently kept those memories fresh and vivid, as the act of avoidance meant she never truly
confronted or processed them, leaving them intact within her. As she navigated her daily life, it
became increasingly difficult to distinguish between past fears and present realities, leaving her
feeling trapped in a continuous loop of anxiety.

Asma’s current state had reached a critical point; she could no longer manage the weight of her
memories or the relentless threat that shadowed her every move. Seeking help felt both daunting and
necessary, and she was terrified of what it might mean to confront the pain locked away within her.
As she sat in the therapist's office, she couldn’t shake the fear that perhaps opening these old
wounds would only deepen her suffering. Yet, beneath that fear lay a flicker of hope — the desire to
reclaim her life, to find a way to transform those memories from a source of torment into a
narrative of resilience.
