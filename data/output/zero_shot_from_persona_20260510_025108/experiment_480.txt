**Patient Vignette: Raj (Age 34, Delivery Driver)**

Raj sits at the edge of the couch, hands resting on his knees, eyes fixed on the floor. He speaks in
a low, steady voice, as if he has rehearsed this many times but still can’t quite believe the words
coming out.

It happened on a Tuesday afternoon in November, about eight months ago. Raj had been driving his
delivery van for six years—mostly residential routes, suburban houses with long driveways. He knew
the streets by heart. That day he was running behind, stopping at a house near the end of a cul-de-
sac. He checked his mirrors, put the van in reverse, and backed out slowly. He felt a dull thump.
Not the kind of bump from a pothole. Something softer.

He got out and saw a boy, maybe six or seven, lying on the pavement. A bicycle twisted beside him.
Raj remembers the silence first—no crying, no shouting, just the sound of the van’s engine idling.
Then the boy’s mother screamed from the front door. Raj’s hands were shaking so badly he couldn’t
dial 911. The boy survived, but with a traumatic brain injury and a broken pelvis. Raj learned later
that the boy was riding his bike on the sidewalk, darted into the driveway chasing a ball, and Raj
never saw him in his side mirror.

Now, every morning before his shift, Raj checks his mirrors five, six times. He pulls out of his
driveway with his neck craned, turning his whole body. He still delivers packages, but he refuses to
back up. Not even into a loading dock. He walks extra blocks to park facing out. If a customer’s
driveway is narrow, he parks on the street and carries boxes by hand, adding fifteen minutes to each
stop.

At night, he dreams of the thump. Sometimes he sees the boy’s face, sometimes just the bicycle wheel
spinning. He wakes up with his heart pounding, his wife Mala asleep beside him. He doesn’t wake
her—she works long hours at a clinic, and he doesn’t want to worry her. Instead, he lies still and
replays the moment, trying to see if he could have done anything different.

He hasn’t driven his own car in six months. Mala drives them everywhere—to the grocery store, to see
his parents. He sits in the passenger seat, gripping the door handle when she reverses. He doesn’t
tell her why. He just says he gets motion sickness.

Raj’s mother calls him every week from Toronto. She asks why he sounds tired, why he doesn’t visit.
He tells her work is busy. He doesn’t mention the accident, because his father would say *you should
have been more careful* and his mother would cry. Instead, he says *it’s fine, Ma, just the cold
weather.*

At work, his supervisor noticed he was taking longer on routes. Raj said it was traffic. But last
month, another driver made a joke about him being slow, and Raj felt a flash of heat in his chest.
He walked away without saying anything and spent his lunch break sitting in the back of the van,
staring at the floor.

He avoids the street where it happened. He takes the long way around, even if it costs him an hour.
Yesterday, his dispatcher assigned him a delivery two blocks from that cul-de-sac. Raj called in
sick. He sat on his couch all day, watching the clock, feeling his breath get shallow every time a
car reversed in the driveway next door.

Mala has started asking if he’s okay. He says yes. She hasn’t pushed, but he sees her watching him
when he flinches at a car backing out in a parking lot.

He doesn’t know why he’s here, he tells me. He doesn’t think a therapist can undo what happened. But
Mala made the appointment, and he came because he’s tired of sleeping in the living room, tired of
pretending the thump isn’t always playing in his head.
