retirement had been a quiet ceremony—students crying, teachers hugging him—and after forty years of

They beat him with a metal pipe. He remembers the sound of his own ribs cracking, the taste of
blood, the way his glasses shattered on the concrete. They left him on the floor, and it took half
an hour before a janitor found him.

In the hospital he told the police he couldn’t identify the men. He couldn’t. The faces are still a
blur. What he sees, instead, is the pipe—the way it swung, the arc of it, the light reflecting off
the metal as it came down. That image comes to him now, suddenly, without warning. He will be
standing at the front of the church on Sunday or sitting at the dinner table with his wife and the
pipe appears in his mind, and he flinches. He drops his fork. His wife touches his arm and asks if
he is all right. He says yes, but his heart is pounding, his palms are wet. He cannot concentrate on
the sermon or the meal.

He thought he could manage. He returned to the school after six weeks, but he could not walk past
the storage room. He started taking different routes through the hallways. Then he stopped
volunteering altogether. He told the principal that his age made it difficult. In truth, he could
not be inside the building without scanning every door, every corner. He began to stay home. His
wife insisted on grocery shopping now; she does all the errands. He tells himself it is because the
winter ice is dangerous for a man his age. But he knows.

deadbolts on the front door of his house. He checks them before bed, then again at two in the
morning, then again at four. His wife says he is wearing out the locks. He knows he is being
