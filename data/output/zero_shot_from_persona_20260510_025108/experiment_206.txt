**Vignette: María**

María is a 74-year-old retired pharmacist who lives with her husband of forty years in a quiet
suburban house. She keeps the curtains drawn at all times, even during the brightest afternoons. She
says it helps her concentrate when she reads—though she rarely finishes a book anymore. Her hands
tremble slightly as she adjusts the stack of pill bottles on the kitchen counter, a habit from
decades of counting medications precisely.

The memory starts the same way every time: the smell of cheap cologne and cigarette smoke. She was
twenty-three, newly arrived from Colombia, working behind the counter of a small pharmacy in Queens.
Carlos was a delivery driver for the same store, charming, attentive. He would bring her coffee,
wait for her shift to end. For six months, she believed she had found safety in a new country. Then
the first slap came—a disagreement about her speaking Spanish on the phone. He apologized, cried,
bought her roses. She believed him.

The abuse escalated over two years. Black eyes she covered with sunglasses, broken ribs she
explained as a fall on the ice. She never called the police. Her family was in Bogotá; she had no
one here. The pharmacy became her sanctuary—the orderly rows of bottles, the precise dosages, the
quiet focus required to fill a prescription. She learned to compartmentalize. At work, she was
competent, thorough. At home, she walked on eggshells.

She left Carlos after he pinned her against the bathroom wall and held a razor to her throat. She
packed a single bag and fled to a shelter. The shame was enormous—she had been a pharmacist, an
educated woman, and she had stayed. She never pressed charges. She told herself it was over.

But it is not over. Fifty years later, María still scans every room for exits. In the grocery store,
she positions her cart so no one can approach from behind. When her husband of forty years reaches
for her unexpectedly, she flinches—then forces a smile. He is a gentle man, a retired teacher, but
she has never told him the full story. He knows only that her first marriage was “difficult.”

The nightmares come three or four times a week. The same scene: Carlos’s hand raised, the razor
cold, her voice silent. She wakes gasping, drenched in sweat, and lies still until her heartbeat
slows. She avoids the subway. She avoids dark-haired men with deep voices. She has not visited a
family doctor in years because the smell of antiseptic reminds her of the emergency room visits she
never admitted.

Now, in retirement, María volunteers twice a week at a free clinic pharmacy. She loves the work—the
precision, the quiet, the sense of control. But when patients raise their voices, even in
frustration, her chest tightens. She excuses herself to the storage room, counts bottles until her
hands stop shaking. She knows what she is doing. She is a pharmacist; she understands the brain, the
chemistry of fear. Yet she cannot stop the cascade.

Her PCL-5 score of 66 is a number she has never seen. She does not need a test to tell her that her
body remembers what her mind tries to bury. She lives with the weight of a past that refuses to stay
in the past—a ghost that walks beside her in the pharmacy aisles, in the grocery store, in the dark
hours before dawn.
