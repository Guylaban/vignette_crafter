He is forty-six, a firefighter for nearly twenty years, but the memories that haunt him come not
from burning buildings but from the dry, dusty air of a war zone. Two years ago, he volunteered with
an international relief organization, spending six weeks in a conflict-affected region. His job was
to help coordinate medical evacuations from a field hospital near the front line. He had seen trauma
before—car wrecks, collapsed houses, the aftermath of violence in his own city—but nothing prepared
him for the constant, low thrum of mortar shells and the sight of civilians with blast injuries,
their clothes shredded, their eyes wide with a shock he recognized from his own mirror.

The moment that splintered his life came on a Tuesday afternoon. He was helping load a wounded child
onto a helicopter when a nearby explosion threw him to the ground. He remembers the ringing in his
ears, the taste of grit, and the child’s mother screaming. He helped finish the evacuation, but
later that night, while eating a cold meal in a tent, his hands began to shake and would not stop.
He told himself it was adrenaline. He told himself he was fine.

Since returning home, he has not spoken about that day. Not to his captain, not to the department
psychologist, not to his ex-wife—who left him six months after he came back. The marriage had been
strained before, but after the deployment he became distant, irritable, unable to sleep beside her
without startling awake. She said he was not the same man. He agreed.

In the firehouse, he is known as reliable, unflappable. Yet now he avoids riding in the front of the
engine because the wide windshield reminds him of the helicopter view of the refugee camp. He
volunteers for the least risky calls, saying nothing, but his heart races whenever a siren starts.
He checks the exits in every room he enters, sometimes twice. His crewmates joke about his new
“nervous habit” of scanning rooftops. He laughs with them, but inside, the rooftops are empty of
snipers, and he knows this, yet he cannot stop looking.

At night, he drinks two beers to fall asleep, then wakes at three in the morning with his fists
clenched, the sound of the explosion reverberating in his skull. He has not visited the department’s
employee assistance program; the thought of describing what he saw to a stranger feels like exposing
a wound to open air. Instead, he tells himself the flashbacks will fade. They do not.

lieutenant placed a hand on his shoulder and said, “You with us?” He nodded. He went back to work.

He does not connect his divorce, his numbing, his avoidance, his nightmares, and his startle
response to a single event. He only knows that some part of him is still in that tent, still
shaking, still waiting for the next explosion. And he is tired—so tired—of waiting for it to stop.
