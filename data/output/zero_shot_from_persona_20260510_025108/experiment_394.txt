The trauma began when Maya was fifteen months old. Leila came home from a late shift to find Amir

remembers the smell of antiseptic, the harsh fluorescent lights, and the sound of machines beeping
in a rhythm she still hears in her sleep. For ten days, Maya’s life hung on the edge of a needle.

except the doctors. She held Maya’s tiny hand and prayed in a language she hadn’t used since
childhood.

Maya survived, but something in Leila did not. Now, seven months later, she is back at work, but her
body is never fully present. At the conveyor belt, she scans a box and suddenly smells that
antiseptic. Her chest tightens. She blinks hard and forces herself to count the boxes in front of
her—one, two, three—until the image of Maya’s limp body fades. She checks her phone constantly
during breaks, texting Amir to ask if Maya is breathing normally. She knows she is being irrational,
but the thought of not checking feels like abandoning her daughter all over again.

At home, Leila avoids the pediatrician’s office even for well-child visits. She makes Amir take
Maya. She cannot step inside without feeling her throat close. She has also stopped watching any
