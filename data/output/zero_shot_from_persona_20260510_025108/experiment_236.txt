imagined.

In the months after, he kept working. It’s what he knew. He would go to the studio—the same small
building behind their house—and stand at the wheel, but his hands wouldn’t center the clay. They’d
push too hard, or not enough. The walls of the pots would collapse. He’d scrape the mess into a
bucket and start again. He didn’t tell Marie how often he saw the bracket falling, how the sound of
cracking wood would hit him without warning. She was grieving too. They didn’t talk much.

Now, at sixty-two, his days have a pattern. He wakes early, before light, as he always did. But he
lies in bed for an hour, sometimes longer, because getting up means entering the day where Caleb is
still dead. He feels the weight of that fact in his chest, a physical pressure, as if the bracket is
pressing on him too. He has stopped going to the studio regularly. When he does go, he works on
small pieces—cups, bowls, nothing too large. He used to make tall vases and sculpted forms. Now he
can’t trust the kiln. He’ll check and recheck the firing temperature, even though nothing has gone
wrong since. He once spent an entire day loading and unloading the same kiln, unable to settle on a
