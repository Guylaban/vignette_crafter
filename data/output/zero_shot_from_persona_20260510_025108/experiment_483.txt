She is eighteen years old, a first-year architecture student at the university, and she still takes
the bus even though the stop is three blocks from the building where it happened.

She was walking to her studio that afternoon in early autumn, carrying a tube of blueprints she had
stayed up all night to finish. The street was busy, the usual noise of the city. A man was crossing
the intersection ahead of her, slightly ahead of the pedestrian signal. She noticed him because he
was carrying a small child on his shoulders, a girl with a bright pink backpack. The child was
laughing, reaching up toward the leaves of the trees that lined the sidewalk. She remembers the
exact shade of pink of that backpack because she had a similar one when she was small.

The truck came from the left, running a red light. She heard the engine first, a sudden roar, then
the impact. It was not a loud sound, she sometimes tells herself. It was a wet, dense thud, like a
heavy book falling onto a concrete floor. The child was thrown forward, away from the man. The man
was not thrown. He was pulled under.

She remembers screaming. She remembers the child’s pink backpack lying in the gutter, still moving a
little as the girl tried to sit up. She remembers the driver getting out of the truck, a man in a
blue uniform, his hands on his head, walking in circles. She remembers someone saying, “Don’t look,
don’t look,” and she did not look at the space under the truck. But she knows what she did not see,
and that is worse. She sees it anyway, every night, in the fifteen minutes between closing her eyes
and the moment she forces them open again.

That was eight months ago.

Now she cannot take the bus past that intersection. She walks an extra twenty minutes in the cold,
even when it rains, even when her boots are soaked. She has mapped three different routes to the
architecture building, none of which cross a major street with traffic lights. She tells her
professors she prefers the exercise.

In studio class, she struggles to draw. Her line work has become jagged, hesitant. When she tries to
sketch a pedestrian plaza, she finds herself erasing the figures, leaving the benches empty. Her
professor asks why her elevations have no people, and she says she wants the buildings to speak for
themselves. She knows this is not true. When she looks at a human figure on a page, even a stick
figure with a circle for a head, she sees the girl with the pink backpack, and her hand stops
moving.

She started carrying a small notebook in her bag, separate from her sketchbooks. In it she writes
the date and time of every intrusive image, every sudden feeling of falling, every moment she hears
the wet sound again. She has filled three notebooks. She keeps them in a shoebox under her bed and
never opens them after she writes. She does not know why she does this. She does not know why she
cannot throw them away.

She has stopped going to the architecture library after dark because the fluorescent lights buzz the
same way the truck’s engine did in the seconds before. The buzz is not the same, she knows that
rationally. But when she hears it, her chest tightens, her hands get cold, and she needs to leave.
She has left three exams early this semester. She tells her classmates she is sick. They believe her
because she looks tired.

She has not told anyone what she saw. Not her mother, who calls every Friday and asks if she is
eating well. Not her roommate, who once asked why she never wants to walk to the coffee shop on the
corner. She does not know how to start. She thinks if she says it out loud, the sound will come
back, and this time she will not be able to stop screaming.

At night, she lies on her back in the dark and traces the outline of windows onto the ceiling with
her finger, imagining the plans of a building with no doors, no stairs, no intersection outside. A
place where no one can enter and no one can leave. She is still trying to design it. She has filled
an entire roll of tracing paper with sketches of that building, and she cannot finish any of them.
