She first came to see me last autumn, though she will not call it autumn. On the islands where she
grew up, there is only the wet and the dry. At fifty-seven, she is a woman who measures things:
angles, load-bearing walls, the exact height of a ceiling. Her hands move when she talks, drawing
invisible lines in the air. She is an architect, and she has been good at that for thirty years. But
the work no longer holds her.

The story begins four years ago, during the dry season. Her daughter, Tia, was twenty-three, a
marine biologist in training. Tia had been volunteering on a small research vessel, mapping coral
bleaching. The boat went out one morning with a crew of five and did not come back. A sudden squall,
an overloaded hull, a failure of communication—the official report was inconclusive. Tia’s body was
never recovered. There was no funeral, no grave. Just a memorial plaque at the harbor, which the
patient has never visited.

In the early months after the loss, she kept working. She believes now that it was a kind of denial,
a way to fill the hours. She designed a community center on a neighboring island, threw herself into
site visits, stayed late in the office. But the symptoms crept in like tide. She began to avoid the
harbor. Then any waterfront property. Then even the sight of boats on television. She stopped going
to the coast, which is difficult on an island nation where the coast is everywhere. She took inland
routes, drove with her eyes fixed on the road ahead.

Intrusive memories hit her at unpredictable moments. The sound of rain on a tin roof brings back the
morning of the squall. The smell of diesel makes her dizzy. Once, in a client meeting, a weather
alert notification sounded on someone’s phone, and she excused herself to the restroom, where she
stood for twenty minutes, pressing her palms against the cool tile, trying to steady her breath.

Her sleep fractured. She wakes at 3:00 a.m. most nights, heart pounding, convinced she has heard
Tia’s voice calling from the dark. She has not had a full night’s rest in over a year. During the
day, she is hypervigilant. She checks her phone obsessively for messages, even though no one would
send a message from a boat that no longer exists. She cannot tolerate uncertainty. In her drawings,
she now specifies every detail to the millimeter, leaving no room for ambiguity. Her colleagues call
her meticulous. She knows it is something else.

Her marriage had already ended by the time Tia died, but the loss drove a wedge deeper. Her ex-
husband lives on the mainland now, remarried. They do not speak. She has no other children. Her
parents are gone. She has friends, but she has withdrawn from them. They mean well, but she cannot
bear their pity or their silence. She stopped accepting dinner invitations. She stopped returning
calls. The office has become her only social container, and even there, she keeps to herself.

The work, once a refuge, has become a battlefield. She was recently assigned to design a new
boathouse for the inter-island ferry service. She took one look at the brief, felt her chest
tighten, and asked for reassignment. She told her boss it was a conflict of interest. She did not
explain that the project was on the same stretch of water where her daughter drowned.

There are moments when she feels almost normal. This morning, while reviewing a structural plan, she
lost herself in the mathematics of a cantilever for half an hour. Then a delivery truck rumbled past

daughter. But she also knows that if she puts the mug away, she will have nothing left to touch.
