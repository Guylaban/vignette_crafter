He hasn’t driven since the accident. Every morning, his wife drops him off at the middle school
where he works, and every evening she picks him up from the same spot by the gymnasium doors. He
sits in the passenger seat with his hands folded in his lap, eyes fixed on the road ahead, never on
the intersection where it happened. She doesn’t ask why he won’t drive; she already knows. They
share the silence like a familiar weight.

The accident happened two years ago, on a Tuesday in late October. He was coming home from a staff
meeting, running late because a seventh grader had needed extra time after crying in his office. He
remembers the boy’s name—Ethan—and remembers thinking about how to talk to his parents as he turned
left onto Oakwood. The light was yellow. He should have stopped. He didn’t see the woman stepping
off the curb until his front bumper struck her hip, then her head hit the windshield. He remembers
the sound more than the sight: a soft, wet thud that he still hears when the school hallway goes
quiet at the end of the day.

He got out of the car. She was lying on the asphalt, a young woman in a gray coat, her legs bent at
an angle that looked wrong. A crowd gathered. Someone called 911. He knelt beside her and told her
to stay still, that help was coming, that everything would be okay. He didn’t know then that her
spine had been fractured at the T12 vertebra. He didn’t know she would never walk again. He only
knew that the blood on his hands was hers, and that he had done this.

Now, in the present, he can still feel the cold of the asphalt on his knees. He can still taste the
metallic panic in his throat. He avoids that intersection entirely, even as a passenger. If they
have to go near it, he closes his eyes and presses his palms flat against his thighs until the car
has passed.

At work, he sits in his small office with the door slightly ajar, waiting for students to knock. But
lately he has trouble focusing on their stories. A girl tells him about her parents fighting, and he
hears the thud of his bumper. A boy describes being bullied, and he sees the gray coat on the
ground. He nods, says the right things, but feels hollow—like he’s watching himself from across the
room. Some days he pretends to be busy just so he doesn’t have to listen. He knows this is wrong. He
chose this job to help kids, and now he feels like a fraud.

At night, he lies awake beside his wife, staring at the ceiling. Sometimes his body jerks awake just
as he’s falling asleep, as if he’s about to hit something. Other times he dreams of driving, of the
woman’s face pressed against the glass, and he wakes up gasping. He has not told his wife about the
dreams. She has stopped asking if he’s okay. She just turns over and goes back to sleep.

He can’t shake the thought that he is dangerous, that he shouldn’t be trusted with anything
important—not a steering wheel, not a student’s pain, not even his own memories. His chest feels
tight most of the day, like a hand gripping his ribs. He drinks coffee to stay alert, but it makes
his hands shake. He finds himself scanning parking lots for pedestrians, checking his rearview

Last week, a sixth grader ran past his office door, laughing, and the sound of footsteps made him
