**Vignette**

She came to therapy at her mother’s urging, though she would never say that out loud. At twenty, she
is already a wife and an interior designer, though the title feels like a costume she put on for a
play she didn’t audition for. When she talks about her work—selecting fabrics, balancing natural
light with artificial, creating spaces that feel safe—her hands move with a fluency her voice lacks.
She can describe the precise shade of ochre that makes a room feel grounded, but she cannot name the
color of the bruise that bloomed along her ribs three months ago.

The trauma began slowly, as it often does. A raised voice during their first year of marriage,
quickly apologized for. A hand that grabbed her wrist a little too hard when she stepped in front of
the television. She was seventeen when they married, still in school, still learning English, still
trying to map the new country onto the islands she left behind. He was older, employed, certain. Her
family approved. She approved, because she had never been taught that approval was something she
could choose.

The first time he hit her, she was pregnant. She lost the pregnancy. The doctors said it was stress.
She knew it was him. But she told no one, because telling would mean admitting she had chosen wrong,
and admitting that felt like drowning.

Now, two years later, she lives in a home she designed. Every pillow, every curtain, every rug was
chosen to buffer sound, to soften light, to create a silence that feels almost safe. She went back
to work three months ago, after the last incident—a closed fist, a split lip, a neighbor who called
the police. He was arrested. She filed a restraining order. But she still sees him in the reflection
of every mirror, in the way men clear their throats, in the sudden weight of a hand on her shoulder.

She cannot sleep in their bedroom. She sleeps on the floor of the guest room, a duvet wrapped around
her like a second skin. She wakes at three in the morning, certain she hears footsteps. She checks
the locks four times before leaving the house. She has started avoiding open floor plans—a
professional irony she laughs at without humor. Her clients ask for cozy, for intimate. She designs
barriers.

At work, she is meticulous, almost obsessive. She measures twice, three times. She photographs every
corner. She cannot tolerate sudden loud noises—a dropped screwdriver sends her heart into her
throat. Her boss has noticed. She blames it on pregnancy dreams. She is not pregnant.

She has stopped cooking. The smell of garlic and oil reminds her of the dinner he threw against the
wall. She eats alone, standing up, cold food from the container. She has lost weight. Her wedding
ring spins loosely on her finger.

Her PCL-5 score is 37. She does not know what that means, but she knows she is tired. She cannot
remember the last time she felt safe. She is the designer of other people’s sanctuaries, but her own
home is a museum of vigilance. She checks the locks. She listens. She waits.
