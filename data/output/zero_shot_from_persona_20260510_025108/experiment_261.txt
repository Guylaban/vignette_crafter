She remembers the rain starting mid-afternoon, a soft drumming on the studio windows that she barely
noticed because she was deep in a layout for a local bakery’s new branding. She was seventy-three
then, still working freelance from the converted garage she and her husband called the “art barn.”
The rain grew heavier, then turned into a roar, and the power flickered. She ignored the first flood
warning on her phone, thinking it was just another alert for low-lying areas miles away. Then water
crept under the door, brown and fast, carrying leaves and mud. By the time she stood up, it was
ankle-deep. She grabbed her laptop, her backup drive, and her grandmother’s small watercolor set—her
most precious possession. Her husband was at the hardware store, and she couldn’t reach him. The
water was at her knees when she finally waded to the house, slipping on the wet tile, her heart
hammering. The creek behind their property had become a river. The art barn flooded to the ceiling.
She lost twenty years of client files, a portfolio of commissioned illustrations, and the hand-bound
sketchbooks she’d kept since art school.

That was five years ago. Now she is seventy-eight, and the world still feels like it might dissolve
at any moment. She does not talk about the flood unless the therapist asks, and even then she keeps
her answers short, her hands folded in her lap. She still works as a graphic designer, but only from
the dining room table, never from the rebuilt garage, even though it is dry and soundproofed. She
designs mostly for a single long-term client, a small organic tea company—their packaging requires
muted greens and earthy browns, colors that do not remind her of water. She cannot look at a design
in blues or grays. When a color palette includes “rainstorm” or “riverbed,” she closes the file and
tells the client those tones are trending out.

Her husband, now eighty-two, tries to be patient. He buys weather radios and keeps sandbags in the
shed. This angers her more than she can say, because it means he thinks it could happen again. She
sleeps poorly, waking at the sound of rain, her chest tight, her throat closed. She checks the
weather app obsessively, sometimes before she even gets out of bed. If the forecast shows more than
a quarter inch of precipitation, she cancels plans and stays inside, her fingers moving through old
sketches on her tablet, not drawing anything new.

She avoids driving near the creek that runs through town. She took a different route to the grocery
store for over a year after the flood, a twenty-minute detour. The therapist has helped her shorten
it to ten minutes, but she still grips the steering wheel too hard when she crosses the bridge. She
has not visited the art supply store since the flood. She buys paper and ink pens online, delivered
to her door, even though she misses the smell of turpentine and the feel of a fresh pad.

There is a heaviness in her chest that she calls “fog.” It comes when she least expects it: while
washing dishes, while sorting her husband’s pills, while staring at a blank canvas on her screen.
She feels like she is underwater, sound muffled, time slow. She used to love designing for
fundraisers and community events, but now she turns down those jobs. She says she is too tired.
Inside, she knows she is afraid that another disaster will interrupt her work, and she cannot bear
the thought of starting over again.

She keeps the grandmother’s watercolor set in a drawer now. She has not opened it since the flood.
Sometimes she takes out the box, but her hands shake, and she puts it back. Her husband found her
crying over it once, and she told him it was allergies. He did not believe her.

The worst moments come at night, in the half-dark. She hears the water again—not memory, but sound:
the gush, the slap against walls. Her pulse races, and she cannot breathe until she turns on the
bedside lamp and sees the carpet is dry. Her husband sleeps through it. She does not wake him. She
sits up until dawn, sketching small circles in black ink on scratch paper, trying to prove to
herself that her hands still belong to her.
