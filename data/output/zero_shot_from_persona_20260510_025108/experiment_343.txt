Adwoa is thirty-eight years old, a wife and mother of two, and a fisherman on Lake Volta in Ghana.
She has been fishing since she was a girl, learning from her father. Now she works alongside her
husband, Kofi, on their small wooden canoe. Every morning before dawn they push out onto the dark
water, the only sound the dip of paddles and the low chatter of other boats. For years this was the
rhythm of her life—hard, steady, familiar.

That rhythm shattered six months ago.

It was a quiet morning like any other. The net was heavy, tangled, and Kofi leaned far over the side
to free it. Adwoa was at the stern, balancing the boat. She remembers the sudden list, the water
rushing over the gunwale, and the way Kofi looked back at her with surprise—not fear, just
surprise—before the canoe tipped. She grabbed the side, went under, came up gasping. The boat
floated upside down. Kofi was not there. She screamed his name, paddled wildly, dove again and again
until her lungs burned. Another fisherman pulled her into his boat. They found Kofi's body an hour
later, tangled in the net that had become his shroud.

Now Adwoa still goes out on the lake. She has no choice. There are school fees to pay, children to
feed. But everything has changed. She cannot stand near the edge of the canoe. She sits in the
center, knees drawn up, hands gripping the wooden seat until her knuckles blanch. If the boat rocks
even slightly—a shift of weight, a wave from a passing motor—her heart slams against her ribs and
she starts to tremble. She cannot look down at the water. The green-black depth seems to pull at
her, a heavy hunger. She focuses on the sky, on the far shore, on anything solid.

At night she does not sleep. She lies next to her mother-in-law's sleeping mat (she cannot bear the
empty room she shared with Kofi) and listens to the crickets. When she finally drifts, she dreams of
the net, of his face underwater, of her own hands reaching for nothing. She wakes drenched in sweat,
the sheet twisted around her legs like rope. Sometimes she thinks she hears him calling her name
across the water, but when she listens there is only the wind.

Her daughter, eight-year-old Ama, tries to help. "Mama, I will paddle," she says. Adwoa snaps at

the crowd for Kofi. She knows he is dead. She buried him. Yet sometimes she sees a man in a blue

cry. The tears feel locked behind a door that she is too tired to open. She only knows that the
lake, once her life, is now a thing she must survive.
