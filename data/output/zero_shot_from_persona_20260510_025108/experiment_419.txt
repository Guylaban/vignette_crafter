the one place she felt safe and in control—the studio, the mirror, the way her body knows exactly

fire. She remembers the sound—not the explosion, but the screaming. She remembers the heat pressing
against her back as she ran. She remembers a man in a red jacket pushing her toward the emergency
exit, and then the crush of bodies, the way the air turned thick and black, and the taste of ash in
her mouth for days afterward. She got out. Sixteen people did not.

Now she wakes up three or four times a night, drenched in sweat, her heart pounding so hard she can
feel it in her teeth. Sometimes she sees the fire. Sometimes she sees the man in the red jacket, but
his face is melting. She does not tell anyone this. She lives alone in a small apartment above a
laundromat, and she has not been back to a club or a crowded bar since that night.

Her therapist—she started seeing someone two months ago, after her boss said she was missing too
many rehearsals—wrote down a score of 76 on some form. Anna does not know what that means. She only
knows that she cannot breathe when the house lights dim. She only knows that she stopped taking the
subway because the tunnels feel too much like that hallway. She walks everywhere now, even in the
rain, even if it takes an hour. Her feet hurt all the time.

At the studio, she gets through the warm-up by staring at a single spot on the floor. She does not
look at the other dancers. She does not look at the fire alarm on the wall. When the music swells
too loud, her hands start to shake, and she has to stop and pretend she is checking her shoelaces.
Her choreographer, a patient man named Lars, keeps asking if she wants to talk. She says she is
fine. She says she just needs more sleep. But she cannot sleep. When she closes her eyes, she smells
the gas again.

She has not told anyone the full story. She told the police once, and then she told her mother, but
her mother cried so much that Anna decided it was easier to say nothing. Now she tells herself that
the fire was not that bad. She tells herself that she is lucky, that she got out, that sixteen

coffee. The gas burners on the stovetop were blue. Anna stared at them, and then she started to cry.

She still dances. It is the only thing she knows how to do. But the joy is gone. When she moves, it
feels mechanical, like a body going through steps that used to mean something. She cannot feel the
