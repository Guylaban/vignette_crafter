She wakes before dawn in her small apartment, the silence still heavy from another night of restless
sleep. At nineteen, she is the youngest conductor on the line, and the first in her family to hold a
steady job. Her uniform feels like armor. Each morning she checks the train’s schedule with the
precision of someone who learned early that order can keep the world from falling apart.

She was seven the first time she truly understood fear. It was a Thursday. Her father had come home
late, and the shouting started before he reached the kitchen. She remembers the sound of her
mother’s body hitting the wall, the crack of a chair breaking, and then the muffled sobbing that
followed. She hid under her bed, counting the floorboards, telling herself that if she didn’t move,
it would stop. It always stopped. But the counting never really ended.

Now she counts the seconds between stations, the number of passengers boarding, the exact distance
between her hand and the emergency brake. On the train, she is in control. The rails are
predictable. She can see everything ahead.

But the past finds her in small ways. A passenger raises his voice to his partner, and her chest
tightens. She feels her jaw lock, her palms go cold. She turns away, busies herself with a door
check, breathes through the memory of her mother’s bruised arm. She avoids the sections of the city
where she grew up. She tells herself it’s just a shorter route, but she knows the truth: she cannot
walk past that house.

She has never told anyone what she saw. Not her mother, who left when she was fourteen and never
looked back. Not the few friends she made in high school, who stopped inviting her because she
always said no. She keeps people at a distance. Relationships feel like a trap. She has never been
on a date—the idea of letting someone close enough to see her flinch at loud noises, to ask why she
wakes up screaming, feels impossible.

At work, she is excellent. Her supervisors praise her focus, her calm under pressure. They don’t
know that this focus is a cage. She scans every platform for potential threats. She rehearses
emergency drills in her mind. She cannot stop. When a man slams his fist on a ticket machine, she
nearly pulls the brake, heart pounding, before she realizes he is just frustrated.

Last week, a passenger argued with another about a seat. The voices rose—accusations, threats—and
for a moment she was seven again, frozen under the bed. The world went gray. She heard herself say,
“Stop,” in a voice that didn’t sound like hers. The passengers looked at her, surprised. She made
the woman move cars. She handled it. But afterward she sat in her break room, hands shaking, unable
to eat.

She has nightmares. Different variations—sometimes she is the one bruised, sometimes she is
screaming, sometimes the train is the house. She wakes gasping, drenched in sweat, and lies still
until the alarm forces her to move. She has learned not to expect peaceful sleep.

Her body remembers things her mind tries to forget. The smell of beer makes her nauseous. Arguments
on TV make her leave the room. She watches people carefully, looking for the first hint of a raised
voice or clenched fist. She has never had a panic attack on the job, but in private she feels the
ground tilt without warning.

She wishes she could explain why she doesn’t go home for holidays, why she works double shifts, why
she can’t sit with her back to a door. But no one asks. Everyone thinks she is strong. She wears
that label like another uniform, heavy and isolating.

This morning, as the train pulls away from the station, she sees a child in a window seat, pressing
her face to the glass. The child’s mother sits beside her, reading. For a moment, the scene is so
ordinary it aches. She looks away. She focuses on the track ahead. There is a schedule to keep, a
world she can hold together if she just keeps moving.
