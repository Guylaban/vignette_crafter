he had done—just for being in the way, for breathing too loud, for looking too much like his mother.

You are not safe.*

still, to swallow the cry before it reached his throat. At fifteen, he left home and swore he would
never be a man like that—never raise a hand, never make a child feel small.

Now he is twenty-two, and he is a school principal.

He got the job because of his vision and his drive—a young man from a rural community, determined to
build a school where children feel seen, not broken. He is good at it. He remembers every child’s
name, knows which ones have empty stomachs, which ones flinch when a door slams. He notices. He does
everything he can to make them feel safe.

But he cannot make himself feel the same.

He lives with his girlfriend, who tells him he is kind, patient, gentle. She does not know that
sometimes, when she reaches for him in the dark, his body goes rigid before his mind catches up. She
does not know that he still sleeps with his back to the wall, that he wakes at three in the morning

bruises, about the nights he spent crouched in a latrine so his father would not find him.

At work, he is composed. He mediates conflicts, counsels troubled students, speaks to parents with
steady respect. But the effort drains him. By mid-afternoon, his shoulders ache from holding

does to men.

close to joy. Instead, there is a constant low hum of vigilance, a loop in his head that replays his
