He still remembers the weight of the badge pressing into his chest, the cold asphalt against his
cheek. Twenty-three years ago, he was a junior prosecutor, thirty-two years old, driving home after

identity, the officers later said. They pulled him from his car, slammed him face-down on the
pavement, and beat him with batons while his own law enforcement credential sat on the passenger
seat. He never filed a complaint. He told himself it was an isolated incident, that the department
would handle it internally. He went to work the next morning with bruises hidden under a starched
shirt and tie.

Now, at sixty-five, he is the senior trial attorney in the district attorney’s office. He still
wears the same starched shirts, but they hang looser on his frame. His wife, a retired
schoolteacher, tells him he has not slept through the night in years. She does not know about the
nightmares—of flashing lights, of the crack of a baton against bone—because he has never told her
the full story. He wakes at three in the morning, heart hammering, and lies still until dawn,
listening to the furnace hum.

In the courtroom, he is controlled, methodical, but his colleagues have noticed a shift. He avoids
cases involving excessive force allegations. When a young officer takes the stand in a routine
assault trial, he feels his palms slick against the podium, a tightness banding across his chest. He
cuts cross-examination short, tells the defense attorney he has no further questions, and retreats
to his office. There, he sits with the blinds drawn, replaying the stop over and over: the way the
officer’s voice dropped, the stench of sweat and gun oil, the boot on his neck. He knows these
memories are not happening now, but his body does not seem to understand. His hands tremble. His jaw
clenches so hard his molars ache.

He has started driving three blocks out of his way to avoid the intersection where it happened. He
tells himself it is simply a faster route. In the grocery store, he scans the aisles for men in
uniform. Once, a security guard at the courthouse put a hand on his shoulder to greet him, and he
flinched, nearly dropped his briefcase. He apologized, said he had not slept well.

His wife notices he has stopped wearing his dress blues to the annual police appreciation lunch. He
says he does not want the attention. She does not push. They have been married forty years, but
there is a distance now—a silence that grows when the news plays footage of protests or police body-
camera videos. He changes the channel before she can ask why his knuckles are white around the
remote.

At work, his case load is shrinking. He volunteers for desk duties, for research memos, anything
that keeps him out of the trial rotation. The younger prosecutors think he is preparing for
retirement. He is not. He is just exhausted—the kind of exhaustion that coffee cannot touch, that
comes from being always on guard, always scanning, always waiting for the boot to fall again. He has
not told anyone about the flashbacks, the constant checking of rearview mirrors, the way his stomach
drops when he hears a siren behind him.

He still goes to the office every day. He still argues motions, reviews evidence, speaks with
victims. But on the inside, something is locked away, a memory he can neither release nor fully
hide. He is not broken, not exactly. He is just holding very still, hoping no one will notice how
hard he is gripping the edge of his desk.
