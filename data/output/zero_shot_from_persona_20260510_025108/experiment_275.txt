The client, a 57-year-old interior designer, came to therapy at her husband’s urging. She has been

The trauma occurred seven years ago, during what she calls “the festival bombing.” She had traveled

sound but a violence that pressed through her entire body. “The air turned into something solid,”
she said. She was thrown against a stone pillar, her left arm fractured in three places. She lay
among debris and bodies, unable to move, while dust and burning cloth filled her lungs. For what
felt like hours, she heard screams, then silence, then more screams.

She was rescued, flown to a hospital, and physically recovered after months of surgery and physical
therapy. But she has never returned to the marketplace—or to any crowded public space. Her husband
notes that she has not been to a supermarket, a concert, or a restaurant since. At first she
believed this was practical caution. Now she knows it is a trap.

Her work as an interior designer once brought her joy through creating light-filled, airy interiors
that invited connection and openness. But after the bombing, her design choices shifted. She refuses
to specify large windows or open floor plans, preferring enclosed, compartmentalized rooms with
minimal natural light. She recently lost a major client when she insisted on removing all glass
partitions from a proposed open-concept kitchen. “Glass shatters,” she explained to the client,
unable to say more. The client found her alternative suggestion—a warren of solid-walled
rooms—depressing.

At home, she insists on sitting with her back to the wall in every room. Her husband has rearranged
the living room furniture four times because she cannot tolerate chairs that face the windows. She

tried to crawl under the bed because she thought she heard explosions. She does not remember these

occurred. The thought of even stepping foot in that country brings on chest pain and a sense of
