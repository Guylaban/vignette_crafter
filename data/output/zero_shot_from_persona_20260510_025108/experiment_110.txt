She is sixty years old, a state senator for nearly two decades, and she has not driven a car in
eighteen months. That is the first thing she tells me, and the way she says it—flat, without
apology—makes it sound like a choice, a matter of principle. It is not a choice. She knows this. I
know this.

The accident happened on a Tuesday night in early November, after a long day of committee hearings
and a late fundraiser in the district. She was tired, she says, but not drunk. Not even close. Just
tired. She remembers the green light turning yellow as she approached the intersection. She
remembers thinking she could make it. And then she remembers the impact—the sound of metal folding,
the scream of tires, the sudden, absolute stillness of her own car. The other driver was a man in
his forties, a high school science teacher on his way home from a faculty meeting. He had the right
of way. She hit him broadside.

She did not break a bone. The other driver did not die, but he will never walk again without
assistance, and he will never return to the classroom. His spine was damaged in ways that surgery
could not fully repair. She knows his name. She knows his wife’s name, his children’s names. She
sent flowers to the hospital. She wrote a letter of apology that was returned unopened. She has not
spoken to him or his family since.

In the months that followed, there was the legal process, the media coverage, the calls for her
resignation. She did not resign. She hired a lawyer, made a statement, paid a fine, completed
community service. She kept her seat. She calls it a small victory, but her voice does not sound
victorious. It sounds hollow.

Now, when she wakes in the night—and she wakes every night, usually between two and three in the
morning—she sees the man’s face in the headlight glare, hears the sound of her own breathing in the
silence before the sirens. She does not scream. She lies still, her hands pressed flat against the
mattress, as if to anchor herself to the present. Then she gets up and walks through her empty
house, checking the locks, the stove, the windows. She does this three or four times before dawn.

During the day, she is a politician. She attends hearings, votes on bills, shakes hands at town
halls. But she no longer drives herself to any of these places. A staffer picks her up and drops her
off. If the staffer is unavailable, she takes a rideshare. She avoids the intersection where it
happened, and if the GPS suggests that route, she finds herself leaning forward in the backseat, her
palms sweating, her throat tight, until they are safely past. She has not told her staff why. They
think she is being environmentally conscious.

Her concentration has frayed. She used to read briefing packets cover to cover; now she scans
headlines, loses the thread of arguments during floor debates. She finds herself staring at the same
paragraph for minutes without absorbing a word. Her speechwriter has started asking if she is
feeling okay. She says she is fine.

She drinks most nights now, just wine, to quiet the noise in her head. It works for a few hours.
Then she wakes up again at two in the morning, and the silence is worse than the noise.

She is divorced, and her children live in other states. She tells me she is lucky—no one else was
depending on her. She says this with a small, self-deprecating smile. But when I ask her what she
thinks about when she thinks about the future, her smile fades, and she looks at the window. She
