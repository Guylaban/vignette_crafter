It begins with the call. She can still feel the press of the radio against her ear, the cold
plastic, the crackle of dispatch saying her husband’s unit had been ambushed on a routine traffic
stop. Niko, her partner of forty-six years, was a senior constable just like her—they had met at the
academy, grown old together in the job. That night, she was two blocks away on a noise complaint,
but by the time she arrived at the scene, the officers had already taped the street.

She did not see him die. That is what she tells herself. But what she did see—his boots, still
polished, sticking out from under the sheet, the way one of the younger officers kept staring at the
ground—those images have no intention of leaving. The hospital later, the chaplain’s hand on her
shoulder, the funeral with the folded flag. All of it is a series of still photographs, each one
heavier than the last.

Now, two years later, she is still a police officer. She reports to the station every morning at
six, puts on the same navy uniform she wore when Niko was alive, and sits behind a desk in the
community relations unit. Her superiors have been kind—they gave her an inside posting, no patrols,
no street contacts. She is supposed to coordinate school safety talks and senior citizen workshops.
But she cannot concentrate. The phone ringing sounds like the radio dispatch. A car backfiring
outside makes her drop the stack of pamphlets onto the floor. Her colleagues look at her, then look
away.

She avoids the locker room. That was where she and Niko would meet for coffee, leaning against the
rows of lockers, talking about the supervisor or the new shift schedule. Now, if she has to go near
it, she walks quickly, eyes down, breath held until she passes. She does not touch his old locker,
though the department left it untouched for a year. They offered to clear it out, but she said no.
She cannot bear the thought of someone else touching his things.

At night, she wakes at 2:17 a.m. Every single night. The time on the clock is burned into her brain.
That was the time the call came. She lies in bed, her husband’s side empty, her own body rigid,
listening for sounds that are not there. Sometimes she hears the crack of a gunshot, but she knows
it is just a memory. Her heart pounds anyway, and she gets up, walks to the kitchen, and makes tea
she does not drink.

Her married daughter visits on Sundays, bringing the grandchildren. The kids climb on her, show her
drawings, ask to go to the park. She smiles, but her hand trembles when she holds their crayons. She
thinks: *I should be grateful. I have them. I have a job. I have the house.* And then she thinks: *I
should have been there. I could have taken that call. I could have been in the car with him.* The
guilt sits in her chest like a stone she cannot cough up.

She has not told her daughter that she keeps Niko’s service weapon in the nightstand drawer, loaded.
She has not told her that she sometimes takes it out at 2:17 a.m., holds it, and feels the weight of
what she did not stop. She has not told her about the dreams—the ones where she is in the police
cruiser, watching his car roll over on a bridge, or the ones where he is alive and asking her why
she locked the bathroom door.

At work, she has started to avoid the memorial wall in the station lobby. Her own husband’s name is
on it, third from the top. She takes the stairs instead of the elevator now, just to stay away from
that hallway. Her desk calendar has the same date circled for the past two years. She does not know
why she does it. She cannot bring herself to take the pen and cross it out.

She told the department doctor she was fine. She passed the yearly psychological check because she
knows what to say—"I have good support," "The memories are fading," "I am glad to be serving." But
she does not believe any of it. When she drives home, she takes the long route, the one that does
not pass the intersection where it happened. And if she has to go near that part of town, she calls
in sick. Her sergeant has noticed. She knows he has noticed. She does not care. The only thing worse
than staying home is going somewhere that reminds her of the sound of the radio, the feel of the
accelerator, and the sight of Niko’s polished boots under the sheet.
