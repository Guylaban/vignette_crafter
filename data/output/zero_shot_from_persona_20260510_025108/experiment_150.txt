**Patient Vignette: Liam, Age 21**

Liam sits at the edge of the chair, his palms resting flat on his thighs as if he’s trying to keep
himself from floating away. He arrived fifteen minutes early for the session, apologizing twice for
being “a bother.” His voice is soft, almost a murmur, and his eyes rarely lift from a fixed point on
the carpet.

He speaks about his wife, Maya, in fragments. “She’s the one who got me here,” he says. “She said
I’m not the same. She’s right.” He married her just after his nineteenth birthday, and for the first
year, they built a small life together in a rented apartment above a bakery. He worked as a line
cook at a busy restaurant downtown—a job he loved for its chaos and rhythm. “I was good at it,” he
says. “I could move fast, I could handle the heat.”

The trauma happened on a Thursday night in November, just over eight months ago. Liam had just
finished a double shift when his phone buzzed with a call from his mother-in-law. Maya had been in a
car accident on the highway. A driver ran a red light. The paramedics called it a “major trauma
activation.”

Liam remembers the drive to the hospital as a blur of streetlights and humming tires. He remembers
the smell of the emergency room—antiseptic and fear—and the sound of a machine breathing for his
wife. For eleven days, Maya lay in the ICU with a traumatic brain injury, a collapsed lung, and
multiple fractures. Liam stayed in a vinyl chair beside her bed, eating vending machine crackers and
drinking bad coffee. He didn’t sleep. He couldn’t.

“I made deals with God,” he says, his fingers interlacing and unclasping. “I said, ‘Please, just let
her wake up. I don’t care what happens after that.’”

Maya woke up. She is home now, walking with a slight limp and a scar that runs along her left
temple. She has some memory gaps, but her doctors say she is expected to recover fully. That should
be the happy ending.

But Liam cannot find his way back.

He lost his job three weeks after the accident. He went back to the kitchen, but the noise of the
fryers and the clatter of plates sent a jolt through his chest every time. One night, he dropped a
tray of hot oil because a customer laughed too loudly behind him. The manager sent him home. He
never went back.

Now he spends his days at the apartment, often sitting on the floor of the bathroom because it is
the smallest room, the only place without windows. “I feel like I’m waiting for something bad to
happen again,” he explains. “It’s not a thought. It’s like a low hum in my bones.”

He has nightmares almost every night, but he doesn’t call them nightmares. “My brain replays the
phone call. The ringing. The way my stomach dropped. I see her face when she was in the bed, like a
photograph stuck in my eyelids.” He wakes up gasping, drenched in sweat, and cannot go back to
sleep. Maya lies beside him, breathing softly, and he watches her chest rise and fall for hours. He
touches her wrist to feel her pulse.

“Sometimes I get angry,” he says, his voice barely audible. “I get angry at the ambulance for being
slow. At the driver who hit her. At the doctor who took too long. And then I get angry at myself for
being angry, because she’s alive. I don’t have the right to feel anything except grateful.”

But gratitude does not come. Instead, there is a hollow feeling, like someone has scooped out
everything inside him. He stopped calling his friends. He doesn’t answer his phone. Maya asked him
to go grocery shopping last week, and he stood in the aisle for twenty minutes, unable to decide
which brand of cereal to buy, his heart hammering against his ribs. He left the cart and walked
home.

“I feel like I’m disappearing,” Liam says. He looks up for the first time during our session, his
eyes red-rimmed but dry. “I’m still here. I don’t know how to be.”
