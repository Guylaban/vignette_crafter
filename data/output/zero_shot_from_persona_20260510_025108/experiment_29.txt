It was late summer when the fire came over the ridge. He was seventeen, living with his mother in a
mobile home park at the edge of town. The air had smelled like wet ash for two days, but no one
thought it would reach them. He remembers the sound—not a roar, but a low, rushing growl, like a
freight train made of light. His mother grabbed his arm and they ran. They made it to the community
center, but the heat on his back stayed with him long after the flames were contained. The mobile
home was gone, along with everything else.

Now, at nineteen, he lives in a small apartment alone. He works the closing shift at a fast-food
restaurant, flipping burgers and wiping down the fry station. He is fast and quiet, and his manager
appreciates that he never complains. But there are things his manager does not see. When the
ventilation hood kicks on with a sudden rattle, he flinches hard, sometimes dropping a spatula or a
basket of fries. He has learned to keep his back to the wall while working the grill, because he
cannot stand the feeling of someone—or something—approaching from behind. The same way he could not
see the fire coming until it was already there.

He does not talk about the fire. When a coworker mentions the weather—hot, dry, windy—he changes the
subject or walks away. He deleted all the photos from his phone that were taken after that day. He
does not watch news reports about fires anywhere, not even in other states. His mother calls every
Sunday, and he lets it go to voicemail most weeks. He loves her, but hearing her voice brings back
the panic in her eyes as they ran.

Sleep is difficult. He stays up late, watching mindless videos on his phone until his eyes burn,
because the moment he closes them he sees the orange glow against the bedroom ceiling of the old
home. When he does sleep, he dreams of running through a field that is on fire, and he wakes with
his heart pounding and his sheets soaked. He has started sleeping on the floor of his closet,
because it feels enclosed and safe, like a bunker. He tells himself it's just for the heat—the
apartment has no air conditioning.

At work, the smell of cooking oil sometimes triggers a phantom smell of smoke. He has to step into
the walk-in cooler for a minute, breathing in the cold, sterile air until his hands stop shaking. He
has not told anyone this. He has not told anyone that he cannot listen to sirens without his chest
tightening, or that he keeps a go-bag under his bed with a change of clothes and a charger. Just in
case.

He wants to feel normal. He wants to laugh at a coworker's joke without the laugh catching in his
throat. He wants to go to the park and not scan the horizon for smoke. But the fire is still there,
inside him, smoldering. Some days it feels like it is still burning.
