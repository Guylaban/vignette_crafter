**Patient Vignette**

Priya is a 31-year-old South Asian woman, married for six years, who worked as a middle school
science teacher until four months ago. She sits at the edge of the chair, hands clasped in her lap,
unable to hold eye contact for more than a few seconds. When she speaks, her voice is low, measured,
as if each word costs her something.

The event happened six months ago, during an after-school science club. Priya was demonstrating a
chemical reaction using a heated solution. A student—twelve-year-old Rohan—leaned in too close. She
turned to grab a beaker and her elbow knocked the hot liquid onto his arm. He screamed. The skin on
his forearm blistered instantly. Priya remembers the smell, the frantic call to 911, the way she
knelt beside him repeating "I'm sorry, I'm so sorry" as the paramedics arrived. Rohan required skin
grafts and months of physical therapy. His parents filed a formal complaint. The school placed Priya
on administrative leave pending an investigation, which ultimately found no negligence, only an
accident.

But for Priya, the investigation is irrelevant. What stays with her—what she cannot escape—is the
sound of his scream and the image of his face contorted in pain. Every night she dreams of it.
Sometimes she sees the liquid moving in slow motion, sometimes she sees Rohan's mother's eyes. She
wakes up gasping, drenched in sweat, her husband's hand on her shoulder. She has not been able to
sleep through the night since.

She avoids the school entirely. She drives an extra twenty minutes to the grocery store so she does
not pass the building. She has deleted all social media, afraid she might see a photo of Rohan or a
post from his family. When her husband suggests they go out to eat near the school, she becomes
short-tempered and cancels. She no longer responds to texts from her former colleagues. The guilt
sits in her chest like a stone—heavy, constant, untouchable. She tells herself she is a bad person,
that she ruined a child's life. No amount of reasoning from her husband or friends shifts that
belief.

Priya quit her job two months ago. She could not imagine standing in front of a classroom again.
Even the thought of holding a beaker makes her hands tremble. She feels unsafe in her own body. At
home, she startles at the sound of the microwave beep, the doorbell, her husband's footsteps. She
has become irritable with him—snapping at small questions, then withdrawing, ashamed. She spends
hours sitting on the couch, staring at the wall, replaying the moment over and over: *What if I had
used a different table? What if I had warned him more clearly? What if I had been paying attention?*
The questions loop, but there is no answer, only more guilt.

She stopped cooking because she cannot stand the sight of steam rising from a pot. She stopped
driving because every pedestrian on the crosswalk reminds her of Rohan. She has started drinking a
glass of wine before bed, sometimes two, to quiet the thoughts. She has not told her husband this.

Her husband brought her to therapy. She came because she is exhausted, because she cannot keep
living this way. But when she talks about the future, she sees nothing. She does not believe she
deserves to be a teacher again, or even to be happy. She says, "I don't know who I am anymore."
Outside the office, the afternoon light falls across the rug. Priya does not look at it. She stares
at her hands, the same hands that held the beaker, and she waits for the hour to end.
