He was twenty-two when the men came. They took him off the boat, just outside the territorial waters

used electricity. They used water. They used his own hands to show him what they could do to his
fingers if he didn’t talk.

Now he is twenty-six. Married for two years. The wedding was small, on the dock where his boat still
moors—the same dock where he used to tie the lines without thinking. Now he cannot step onto it
without his chest tightening. He leaves the house an hour early every morning so he can walk a
longer route, avoiding the harbor until the last possible moment. He tells his wife it’s for the
exercise. She knows better, but she has stopped asking.

On the boat, he works with his cousin, Mikka. They head out before dawn, past the buoys, into the
gray water. The engine hum is a sound he once loved—a kind of heartbeat under the deck. Now it makes
him flinch. Every time the radio crackles, he freezes. Mikka has learned not to talk to him during
those seconds. He just waits until the color returns to his face.

The worst part is the nights. He cannot sleep in the bedroom anymore. The walls are too close. He
sleeps on the couch, with the television on, volume low, so he can hear the door. His wife wakes
sometimes to find him standing at the window, staring at the driveway. He tells her he couldn’t
sleep. He does not tell her about the dream where the concrete room floods and the water tastes like
copper.

He has stopped going to the pub. He used to drink with the other fishermen, telling stories about
the one that got away. Now the noise of laughter makes his skin crawl. He tells himself they
wouldn’t understand anyway. How can you explain that the smell of diesel brings back the taste of
the rag they stuffed in his mouth? How can you say that the feel of a fish line tightening on his
palm reminds him of the rope around his wrists?

At work, he is competent but hollow. He baits the traps, pulls the nets, bleeds the catch. But he no
longer looks out at the horizon. He looks only at what is immediately in front of him—the next knot,
the next hook, the next minute. His hands do the work, but his mind is somewhere else, tracing the
edges of a room he cannot leave.

His wife told him last week that she found him under the bed, curled up, not moving. He doesn’t
remember it. He only remembers a sound—a car backfiring—and then the darkness under the frame, the
dust smell, the way it felt almost safe because nothing could see him there.

He has not told anyone about the scar on his lower back. He hides it from his wife in the shower,
turning away. It is a small, clean circle, like a cigarette burn. But it was not a cigarette. He

The sea is the same as it always was. Flat. Infinite. Indifferent. He goes out on it every day
because it is all he knows how to do. But he no longer trusts the horizon. He no longer trusts the
