He sits in the cockpit of the Boeing 777, the pre-flight checklist nearly complete. The faint hum of
the auxiliary power unit vibrates through his seat, a sound he has heard tens of thousands of times.
As the first officer calls out the final items, a sudden high-pitched whine from the air

is not in a modern airliner at 35,000 feet. He is nineteen years old, crouched in a muddy rice paddy

That was 1970. He was a young conscript from a small island nation in the South Pacific, shipped

spent the next eight hours pinned down, firing blindly into the treeline, watching Leo’s body grow
cold in the mud. When the medevac finally arrived, they lifted him out of the mud, his hands shaking
so badly he could not hold his rifle.

He returned home in 1971, quiet and thin. He married his high school sweetheart, found work as a
bush pilot flying cargo between islands, and slowly built a life. The nightmares started early but
he never spoke of them. He learned to push the memories away by staying busy. By his forties, he was

structure. Checklists, procedures, the constant demands of instrument scanning: these kept his mind
occupied. He could fly for fourteen hours and not think of the war once. But the triggers always
