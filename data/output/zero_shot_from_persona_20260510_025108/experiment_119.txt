Kwame is 33 years old, a social media manager for a midsize nonprofit based in Accra. He sits in his
home office—the same chair he has used for three years—with the blinds drawn even at midday. He
scrolls through the platform’s content queue, approving posts about community cleanups and
fundraising drives. His thumb hovers over a video thumbnail: children playing in a schoolyard. He
clicks away, heart hammering. He does not know why. He knows why.

Two years ago, Kwame was in a different kind of work. Fresh out of graduate school, he joined an
international relief organization as a logistics coordinator. He was sent to a conflict zone in the
Sahel—a region he had only read about in reports. His job was to help route supplies: food,
medicine, tarpaulins. He was good at it. He spoke the local language, had a knack for calming
nervous drivers. The first three months were exhausting but purposeful.

Then came the afternoon of the market strike. He was riding in a white SUV with a local driver, a
translator, and a water engineer. They had just delivered a truckload of clean-water tablets to a
school. On the way back, the road was crowded. A motorcycle swerved. Then a blast. The sound came
later—a pressure that seemed to swallow the air. Kwame remembers the smell of asphalt and diesel,
and then the silence after his ears shut off. He woke up lying on the ground, his right arm pinned
under the overturned SUV. The driver was dead. The translator was screaming. The engineer had a
piece of shrapnel in his thigh. Kwame does not remember pulling anyone out. The translator told him
later that he did.

He spent six weeks in a field hospital, then three months back home in Accra recovering from a
broken arm and a fractured pelvis. But the other injuries—the invisible ones—did not heal. He
stopped talking about the war, told his wife, Adwoa, that he was fine, that he just needed rest. He
quit the relief organization and found a desk job. Social media management seemed safe: no travel,
no danger, just words and images on a screen.

But the images are not safe. Every time Kwame sees a photograph of destruction—a collapsed building
in a news feed, a friend’s vacation photo of a dusty road—his chest tightens. He has been avoiding
news sites for eight months. He cannot watch films with guns or explosions. Last week, Adwoa put on
a nature documentary, and a scene of a lion hunt made him leave the room without explanation. She is
patient, but he feels her worry. He also feels his own guilt—a constant, low hum that tells him he
should have done more, should have saved the driver, should have stayed in relief work.

At night, he does not sleep through. The dreams come: the market, the pressure, the silence.
Sometimes he wakes thrashing, his arm aching where the bone broke. He has learned to sleep on the
sofa so he does not jolt Adwoa awake. He tells himself it is considerate.

At work, he is meticulous. He schedules posts weeks in advance, double-checks every image for any
sign of violence or suffering. If a user comments with a graphic meme, he hides it immediately. His
colleague once asked why he flinched at a car backfiring outside the office window. He laughed it
off and said he was just jumpy from too much coffee.

He has not told anyone the full story. Not his boss, not his friends. When Adwoa asks how he is, he
