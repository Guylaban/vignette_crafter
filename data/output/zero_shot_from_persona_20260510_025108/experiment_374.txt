The mugging happened on a Tuesday evening in early autumn, just after she left the office. She is an
accountant for a mid-sized firm, and that night she had stayed late to reconcile a client’s
quarterly report. The numbers had been stubborn, and she remembers feeling satisfied as she finally
locked her office door. The walk to the train station was a route she had taken hundreds of
times—past the coffee shop, under the old railway bridge, through the narrow alley that cut between
two parking garages. She never thought twice about it.

He came from behind. She didn’t see his face, only felt the shove that sent her shoulder into the
brick wall, then the hard tug on her bag strap. She tried to hold on—her laptop, her notes, her
wallet—but the strap snapped and he was gone, boots slapping the wet pavement. She stood there for a
long moment, breathing in the smell of damp stone and diesel, her hands shaking. A passerby asked if
she was okay, and she said yes, because what else was there to say? She walked the rest of the way
to the station with her arms crossed over her chest, her empty hands cold.

That was seven months ago. She still works at the same firm, still balances spreadsheets and files
tax returns, but something inside her has shifted, like a piece of furniture she can’t push back
into place. She no longer stays late. At four thirty, her stomach tightens and she starts packing
her bag, even if the numbers aren’t finished. She tells herself it’s just efficiency, but her
supervisor has noticed. Three times she has left a meeting early, claiming a headache, because the
conference room has only one door and she couldn’t stop thinking about someone coming through it.

Her desk is in a cubicle near the elevator bank. Every time the elevator chimes, her shoulders jump.
She has learned to keep her back to the wall in the break room, even if it means standing instead of
sitting with her coffee. The smallest noises—a dropped pen, a slammed drawer—make her heart punch
against her ribs. She checks the lock on her office door four or five times before she can settle
into work, and she has started carrying a small can of pepper spray in her purse, even though she
never leaves the building except to go home.

Home is no longer the safe place it used to be. She and her husband, a high school teacher, used to
take evening walks around their neighborhood. Now she refuses. If he suggests a walk, she says she’s
tired. If he pushes, her voice goes sharp and she walks away. She knows he is trying to help, but
the thought of being outside in the dark, of hearing footsteps behind them, makes her feel like she
is drowning. At night she wakes up gasping, her hands clutching the sheets, her body drenched in
sweat. In the dream, the strap never breaks. He pulls her down, and she cannot get up.

She has stopped meeting friends for dinner. The noise of a restaurant—the clatter of plates, the
loud laughter—feels too big, too unpredictable. She finds herself scanning faces instead of
listening to conversations. Last month, at her sister’s birthday party, she spent half the night
standing in the kitchen, watching the back door. When her husband asked what was wrong, she said

At work, her concentration has frayed. Numbers that once lined up neatly now blur. She rechecks each

happened before. She feels distant from her colleagues, as though a glass wall has slid down between

She doesn’t think about the mugging during the day—not deliberately. But the memory surfaces in
flashes: the scrape of the brick on her shoulder, the grunt of the man’s breath, the taste of fear,
metallic and sharp. She pushes it away. She tells herself she is fine, that it was just a bag, that

should have fought harder. She should have screamed. She should have been smarter, stayed in the
office longer, taken a different route.

She has stopped taking the train. She drives to work now, even though parking costs half her lunch
budget. The drive takes longer, but she controls the doors, the windows, the radio. She hasn’t been
back to that alley. She takes the long way around, even if it means adding ten minutes to her
commute. She doesn’t tell anyone why.

Her husband asked her last week if she wanted to talk to someone. She said no. But she is here, in
my office, because she finally couldn’t stand the weight of it anymore—the constant checking, the
waking up afraid, the feeling that the world has become a place where anything can happen, at any
moment, when she least expects it.
