She remembers the smell first—a sharp, sweet chemical smell she’d never encountered before, even
after eighteen years as a plumber. It was a Tuesday afternoon in late August, and she was working
alone in the crawlspace of a condemned apartment building, replacing a corroded section of pipe. The
job site was old, poorly ventilated, and the building manager had assured her the lines were clean.
She had her respirator in the truck, but she’d left it on the passenger seat because the crawlspace
was tight and hot, and she’d thought it would only take a few minutes.

Within seconds of cracking open the pipe, a cloud of pale yellow vapor hissed out. It hit her face
before she could turn or hold her breath. Her eyes stung. Her throat clamped shut. She scrambled
backward on her elbows, coughing, gasping, vision blurring. By the time she dragged herself out into
the stairwell, she was vomiting. A neighbor called an ambulance. At the hospital, they told her it
was a mix of industrial solvent residue and hydrogen sulfide—likely from an illegal dumping in the
drains years ago. They kept her overnight for observation, treated her for chemical pneumonitis, and
sent her home with an inhaler and instructions to “take it easy.”

That was eighteen months ago.

Now she avoids even the mention of crawlspaces. When a dispatcher calls with a job, she asks about
the location first. If it’s an older building, if there’s any chance of hidden chemical residue, she
makes an excuse—backache, too much work, family obligation. Her boss has started giving her
residential kitchen and bath jobs, mostly straightforward sink and toilet repairs, because she can’t
stop checking air quality before entering a basement. She carries a portable respirator in her bag
now, wears it even when it isn’t necessary, and her coworkers have started joking about “the mask
lady.” She doesn’t laugh with them.

At home, her husband notices she doesn’t cook the way she used to. She used to make elaborate South
Asian meals—sambhar, biryani, fish curry from her mother’s recipes. Now she avoids the kitchen when
he simmers mustard oil because the sharp heat reminds her of that yellow cloud. She eats small,
bland meals and often forgets to eat at all. Her husband asks if she’s okay. She says she’s fine.
She says it every time.

She sleeps in a separate room now, claiming she has restless legs. In truth, she wakes up three or
four times a night, drenched in sweat, with the smell in her nostrils and a tightness in her chest
that sends her to the bathroom to stand over the sink, panting. She never tells him about the
dreams. In the dreams she is back in the crawlspace, but the pipe is her own throat, and it’s
leaking yellow gas from the inside out.

She stopped going to family gatherings. Her sister’s Diwali party last year—she lasted twenty
minutes. The incense, the frying pakoras, the cumin in the air—it all blurred into one suffocating
cloud. She excused herself and sat in the car for two hours, crying without knowing why. She has not
told her mother about the panic. Her mother would say it is in her head. She believes that, too,
most days.

At work last week, a young apprentice accidentally spilled a can of floor stripper near her toolbox.
The smell hit her from across the room. She dropped her wrench, backed into a wall, and slid down to
the floor, hands over her face, shaking. The apprentice ran to get the foreman. She told them it was
a dizzy spell, low blood sugar. They didn’t ask more. She went home early and sat on the bathroom
tile, waiting for the smell to leave her sinuses.

She knows she is not the same plumber she used to be. She used to take pride in being one of the few
women in the trade, especially a South Asian woman, and she used to love the puzzle of an old pipe
system, the satisfaction of a clean joint. Now every job is a checklist of hazards. She runs her
hand over every surface before she breathes normally. She avoids talking about work with her husband
because she can hear the pity in his voice when he says, “Maybe you should consider something else.”

But she doesn’t know what else she would be. She doesn’t know how to tell him that the only thing
worse than the memory of that day is the certainty that it will happen again. She doesn’t tell
anyone that she still carries the hospital discharge paper in her wallet, its edges frayed from
folding and unfolding. She reads it sometimes, to remind herself that it was real. The rest of the
time she wishes it had never happened at all.
