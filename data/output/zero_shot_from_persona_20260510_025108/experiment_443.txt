**Vignette: The Flood That Never Left**

He still smells it. Not every day, but often enough—the thick, muddy scent of river water combined
with diesel and rot. It comes when he is standing at the whiteboard in Maria’s living room, pointing
with a dry-erase marker at a quadratic equation. He will pause, nostrils flaring, and the student
will look at him with nervous expectation. He forces a smile and resumes. But his heart has already
begun its gallop.

The trauma happened in July three years ago, when the river along his town in central Austria rose
without warning. He was seventy-one then, still robust, left alone for a week while his partner
visited her sister in Vienna. The rain had been relentless for forty-eight hours. By the third
night, the electricity failed. He remembers standing at the living room window, watching the water
climb the garden wall. The air grew heavy, then the window cracked. He fled upstairs, carrying only
a bag of photographs and a half-eaten loaf of bread. The water reached the second step of the
staircase before the military boat arrived.

He was pulled from his own house by a young soldier who had to break the bedroom window to reach
him. He remembers being cold, impossibly cold, as the boat cut through the current toward higher
ground. The soldier gave him a foil blanket and said, “Sie sind sicher jetzt.” You are safe now. He
was not safe. He has not felt safe since.

Now he lives with his partner, Helga, in a flat on the third floor of a concrete building. He chose
the top floor specifically. She does not understand why, but she accepts it. He cannot drive past
the old neighborhood without his hands trembling on the steering wheel. When the spring rains come,
he does not sleep. He lies awake, listening to the gutters, parsing the difference between a heavy
downpour and the sound of the river rising. He has checked the local flood maps online more times
than he can count. He knows the evacuation routes. He also knows they never came in time.

His tutoring work has become strained. He used to tutor in his own home, but after the flood he told
his students’ parents it was too difficult to host. He now travels to their houses—or, when
possible, they meet at the public library. He insists on staying in rooms with windows that open and
with at least two exits. At the library he sits facing the door, always.

He has not told any of his students about the flood. They see a lean, silver-haired man who stumbles
over numbers sometimes and who flinches when a book is dropped too loudly on a table. One of his
adult students, a woman studying for her certification in accounting, mentioned that her basement
flooded during a storm last month. He cut the session short, claiming a headache. He spent the rest
of the afternoon walking in circles around the block, breathing through his teeth.

Helga has noticed. She asks if he wants to talk. He says no. He feels guilty—she was away during the
flood, and a part of him resents her for that, even though he knows it is irrational. He has stopped
looking at photographs from before the disaster. He has stopped visiting his old neighbors. He tells
himself it is because the town is not the same, but the truth is that he cannot bear the image of
his house, still standing but forever stained with the watermark of that night.

The smell remains. He keeps packets of coffee beans in his coat pockets. When the odor comes—the
river, the diesel, the rot—he brings a packet to his nose and breathes in the bitter, clean aroma.
It helps for a moment, but only for a moment. The river is always there, silent, waiting.
