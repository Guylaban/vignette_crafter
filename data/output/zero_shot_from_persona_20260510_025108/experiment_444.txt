She still dreams in black and white. Not as an artistic choice—it’s simply how memory works for her
now. The frames are grainy, underexposed, edges bleeding into darkness. She wakes at three in the
morning with her right wrist throbbing, though there is no physical pain. There never was, not for
decades. But her body remembers what her mind has tried to forget.

She was eight years old the first time her *chachi*—her aunt by marriage—picked up the leather
slipper. Her family lived in a small flat in Mumbai, three generations under one roof, the smell of
cumin and turmeric always in the air. Her mother worked long hours as a seamstress. Her father was
often away. The care fell to her father’s sister-in-law, a woman whose kindness ran out before the
afternoon sun shifted. The slipper landed on her palms, across her shoulders, sometimes her face.
She learned to press her back against the wall so the blows landed on the same spots, bruise upon
bruise, until the pain turned into a dull hum.

She stopped crying by the time she was ten. That was the year she discovered her father’s old
Rolleiflex, left in a closet. She would hide in the bathroom with it, pressing her eye to the
viewfinder, turning the world into a tiny, controllable rectangle. She learned to frame things just
right, to exclude the parts she didn’t want to see.

Now, at seventy, she lives alone in a one-bedroom apartment in Toronto, where she moved forty years
ago after her divorce. Her studio is the spare room: filing cabinets of negatives, a light table,
prints stacked in archival boxes. She stopped shooting weddings years ago. Too many people, too many
unpredictable sounds. She shoots abandoned buildings now. Warehouses, hospitals, schools with broken
windows. Empty hallways where the light falls in silvery shafts. She waits hours for the perfect
beam, the exact moment when the dust particles catch the light. Her hands tremble during the wait—a
fine tremor that started in her fifties and never left.

She meets clients, but only remotely. A former student handles her sales. The doorbell startles her
so badly she once dropped a camera. She keeps her phone on silent. Her only regular visitor is a
neighbor who leaves groceries at her door on Thursdays. She texts him a thank-you, never opens the
door until she hears his footsteps retreat.

The nightmares have gotten worse since she turned sixty-eight. In the dream, she is still eight, but
the slipper has become a shoe with a stiletto heel. She sees it coming down toward her face in slow
motion, and she cannot move. She wakes gasping, her palms slick with sweat, the sheets twisted
around her legs. She gets up and walks to the darkroom, turns on the red safelight, and looks at the
photographs from yesterday: a derelict classroom, a chalkboard with a single word still
legible—"SORRY." She does not know why she took it. She does not know why she keeps it.

Her daughter in Vancouver calls every Sunday at noon. They talk about the weather, about recipes,
about the grandchild she has never held. The child is two years old. "You’ll see her soon, Ma," her
daughter says. "When you’re ready." She agrees, but she knows the word "ready" has no meaning for
her. She stopped being ready forty years ago, in the courtroom after her divorce, when she said she
would never let anyone touch her again.

She rarely leaves the apartment now except to photograph. The world outside feels too fast, too
sharp. She was once told she has "hypervigilance," a word that sounds like a diagnosis but feels
like being skinned alive. She scan each room for exits, for faces, for anything that might become a
threat. She chooses her subjects because they are still. They do not move. They do not raise a hand.

Last week she found a negative from 1972: a self-portrait taken in a train station, her face half-
covered by the camera, her eyes two dark hollows. She blew it up to 16x20 and pinned it to the wall.
She looks at it every night before she goes to sleep, as if to remind herself that the girl in the
frame survived. As if to say: *You are not eight anymore. You are seventy. You own all the rooms
with locked doors. You hold the camera now.*
