He doesn’t remember the shouting the way you’d expect. The sounds he carries are small: the crack of
a ceramic mug against a linoleum floor, the wet gasp his mother made when she fell, the scrape of a
chair being shoved backward. He was six years old, sitting at the bottom of the stairs in the house
in Lagos, his knees pulled to his chest. His father was a man who could fill a room with silence
just as easily as he could fill it with fury. That night, the silence came last, after the shouts
had died and the front door slammed. His mother got up, wiped blood from her lip with the back of
her hand, and told him to go back to bed. He went. He never told anyone. Not her. Not the neighbors.
Not the teachers. He just learned to watch.

Now he is eighteen, an officer with the Lagos State Police Command. He is the youngest in his unit
by nearly a decade, and the others still call him “Small Boy” even though he stands six feet tall.
He wears his uniform like armor—every button polished, every crease sharp. He volunteered for day
shifts in the busiest precinct because night patrols are worse. At night, the city gets quiet, and
in the quiet he hears things that aren’t there. The voice of his father. The sound of his mother
apologizing. The soft, terrible click of a lock turning.

Today, he responded to a domestic disturbance call in a low-income flat. A woman screaming, a man
shouting, neighbors gathered in the hallway. He knocked. He identified himself. When the door
opened, he saw a toddler sitting on the floor, watching. The child did not cry. He just stared, the
way children stare when they have learned that crying does nothing. The man was yelling at the
officer now, but he doesn’t hear the words. He hears the crack of the mug. He hears the wet gasp.
His hands start to shake. He grips his belt until his knuckles turn white. His partner touches his
arm, asks if he’s okay. He says yes. He is not okay.

At night, he cannot sleep. He lies on his cot in the police barracks and counts breaths. When he
closes his eyes, the toddler’s face becomes his own face, and he is six again, and the fear wraps
around his throat like a collar. He wakes sweating, heart hammering, convinced someone is in the
room. He checks the corners. He checks the window. There is no one. He stays awake until sunrise.

He stopped calling his mother six months ago. Not because he is angry—he has never been angry with
her. He doesn’t know why he stopped. The calls feel like stepping into a room he has locked for a
reason. She asks how work is. He says fine. She asks if he is eating. He says yes. He does not eat.
Food tastes like paper. His uniform is loose on him, but he tightens the belt and tells no one.

At roll call, Sgt. Adebayo jokes that he looks like a ghost. He laughs. The sound is hollow. He
volunteers for extra shifts because being busy means not thinking. But the thinking follows him. He
replays scenes from calls he went on yesterday, and scenes from the hallway when he was six, and the
two become the same memory: a woman crying, a child frozen, a man who is supposed to protect but
does not protect. He wonders if he is like his father. He wonders if every man is.

He does not drink. He does not smoke. He just holds the shaking in his hands, and keeps the uniform
polished, and waits for the next call that will take him back to that flat, that child, that door.
He is eighteen years old. He has been an officer for eleven months. He has not told anyone what he
saw when he was six, and he does not plan to.
