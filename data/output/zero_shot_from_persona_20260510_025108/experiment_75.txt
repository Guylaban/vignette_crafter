not remember who she saved, only that she could not find the girl with the water bottle.

In the months that followed, she returned to her squadron. She flew missions again, but something
had shifted. The cockpit, once her sanctuary of precision and control, began to feel like a trap.
She would catch herself staring at the altimeter, the dials blurring, and suddenly she would smell
the dust and the burnt fuel of that road. Her hands would tremble on the cyclic. She started
checking preflight checklists three, four times, knowing she had already done it, but unable to
trust her memory.

At home, her husband complains that she is not in the room even when she is sitting next to him. She
has lost the capacity for small talk. The sound of a car backfiring outside their house makes her
drop to the kitchen floor without thinking. She avoids the news, avoids any article about
humanitarian work, but she cannot escape the dreams. In them, she is back in the convoy, but this
time she is not a relief worker—she is a child, and she is the one being carried, and she cannot

every night, the exact time the second mortar hit.

just want to be able to fly again without feeling like I’m going to fall apart.” She has not told
anyone about the shoe. She keeps it in a drawer in her nightstand, wrapped in a cloth. She does not
know why. She says she is not sure she deserves to get better.
