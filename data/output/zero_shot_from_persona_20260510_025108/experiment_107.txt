Now, at forty, he works from home as a software engineer, a job that once gave him a quiet sense of
control. He can write code for hours, solving problems that follow predictable rules. But the
control is an illusion. The moment his chair creaks, or a delivery truck rumbles past the window,

wife it helps his posture. He does not tell her that he maps every exit from the room and keeps the
front door unlocked, even when they sleep.

His wife says he has become a ghost in his own life. He used to play chess with their son on
Saturday mornings. Now he sits on the edge of the sofa, flinching at the slam of a cupboard door. He
no longer rides the elevator in their apartment building. He takes the stairs, five flights, every
time, and his knees ache by evening. At work, he turns down promotions that would require regular
office meetings downtown. He tells his manager he prefers the quiet of home. But the quiet is not
quiet. There is a constant hum of worry beneath his thoughts.

The nightmares began six months after the earthquake, and they have never left. In the dream, he is
always at his desk, and the floor splits open like a mouth. He feels himself falling, and he wakes

that follows—the image of his elderly neighbor who could not run, the sound of a child crying under

stood. He cannot explain why. He simply cannot.

middle of the aisle and cannot remember what he came for. He has not told anyone that he keeps a go

checks it every Tuesday morning, as if the earth's plates are watching him. He knows this is not
