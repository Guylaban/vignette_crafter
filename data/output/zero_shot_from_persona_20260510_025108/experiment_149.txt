Carlos sits in his parole officer cubicle, the same one he’s had for nineteen years. Fluorescent
lights hum overhead. On the wall hangs a faded certificate of appreciation from the state. He
doesn’t look at it anymore. He stares instead at the clipboard on his desk, at a parolee’s name he
has to call next—a young man, twenty-three, same age as his own son. Carlos’s hand trembles slightly
when he reaches for the phone. He steadies it with his other hand. This is new, the trembling. It
started six months ago.

Six months ago, on a Thursday evening in late October, Carlos was driving home from a mandatory
training. The training was about de‑escalation techniques—ironic, he thinks now. A taillight on his
sedan was out. He didn’t notice. The patrol car behind him flashed its lights. He pulled over
calmly, as he had done a hundred times. He kept his hands on the steering wheel, waited. He knew the
protocol. He was one of them, after all.

The officer who approached was young, white, with a crew cut and mirrored sunglasses even though the
sun was setting. Carlos said, “Good evening, Officer. I’m a parole officer with the county.” He had
his badge on his belt, visible. The officer didn’t look at the badge. He told Carlos to step out of
the vehicle. Carlos complied. Then the officer said, “You match a description. Put your hands behind
your back.” Carlos tried to explain. The officer grabbed his wrist, twisted it. Carlos felt a sharp
pain, then his face hit the asphalt. A knee pressed into his spine. He heard shouting, a second pair
of boots. He was handcuffed, his own radio—the one he used to check on parolees—digging into his
hip. He stayed very still, because he knew what could happen. He had seen cases. He had processed
the paperwork for men who did not stay still.

They held him for forty minutes before a sergeant arrived and recognized him. Apologies followed. A
small settlement offer arrived in the mail two weeks later. Carlos did not cash the check. He kept
it in his nightstand drawer, under a copy of his birth certificate.

Now, at work, Carlos feels the phantom pressure of that knee when he leans forward to type. He
flinches if a colleague claps him on the shoulder. His supervisor, a white woman in her sixties,
noticed and asked if he was sleeping. He said yes. He is not sleeping. He wakes at 3:47 a.m. every
night, heart pounding, the smell of asphalt in his nostrils. He lies beside his wife, Elena,
listening to her breathe, and he thinks about the officer’s mirrored sunglasses. He cannot make
himself angry. That would be simpler. Instead, he feels ashamed—ashamed that he is afraid of his own
uniform, ashamed that he carries the paperwork for men who were treated worse than he was, ashamed
that he cannot stop the tremor.

His work has changed. In the past, he visited parolees in their homes, sat at their kitchen tables,
drank their coffee. Now he makes them come to his office. He keeps his office door open. He places
his chair so he can see the hallway. One of his parolees, a middle‑aged man convicted of non‑violent
drug offenses, asked him the other day, “You okay, Mr. Olivares? You seem jumpy.” Carlos said he was
fine. He changed the subject to the man’s upcoming drug test.

strong that his chest seized. He imagined his son pulled over, his son’s face on the asphalt. He
said nothing. He went to the garage and sat in his car for half an hour.

Carlos has not told anyone about the event. Not Elena, not his coworkers. He does not want to be
seen as a victim. He is a parole officer. He enforces the rules. He believed in the system. He still
does, he tells himself. But when he drives through his city and sees a patrol car in his rearview
mirror, his foot lifts off the accelerator. His hands grip the wheel exactly as they did that
October evening. And he waits.
