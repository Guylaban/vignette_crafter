**Vignette: Theresa**

Theresa remembers the day precisely, though she wishes she didn’t. It was a Tuesday in late October,
three years ago. She was a senior policy analyst for the state environmental agency, and her team
had been invited to tour a decommissioned industrial site before finalizing a redevelopment grant.
The facility had once manufactured cleaning solvents, and the tour was routine—hard hats, safety
goggles, a briefing on residual contamination. She signed the liability waiver without reading it.

About twenty minutes into the walkthrough, a maintenance worker accidentally opened a valve on a
storage tank that was supposed to have been drained. A cloud of yellowish vapor rose from a grate
near Theresa’s feet. She heard a hissing sound, then felt a sharp, acrid burn in her throat and
eyes. Someone shouted “Gas leak!” and she ran, coughing, toward the exit. Her lungs seized. For what
felt like minutes she could not draw a full breath. Later, the safety report would classify it as a
“minor release of volatile organic compounds,” with no lasting physical harm. But her body never
agreed with that conclusion.

In the weeks that followed, Theresa developed a persistent dry cough and a metallic taste that came
and went. Doctors ran tests—chest X-rays, pulmonary function, blood panels—all normal. Still, she
began avoiding any place that smelled even faintly of chemicals: the cleaning aisle at the grocery
store, the gasoline pump on windy days, her husband’s garage where he kept paint thinner. At work,
she stopped attending site visits and asked to be reassigned to desk-based policy reviews. Her
supervisor agreed, but Theresa sensed a flicker of disappointment in his eyes. She started working
from home two days a week, then three.

Now, at sixty-one, she has built a small world she can control. Her husband, Mark, does all the
errands that might involve exposure—picking up dry cleaning, refilling the propane tank for the
grill. She checks the weather app obsessively for wind direction before opening a window. If a truck
with a “Hazmat” placard passes her on the highway, her heart races and her palms sweat. She pulls
over until it’s gone.

The intrusive memories are not visual; they are sensory. A whiff of bleach from a public restroom
can send her back to that yellowish cloud, the burning in her throat, the metallic taste flooding
her mouth. At night she sometimes wakes gasping, convinced she is breathing poison, even as the
bedroom air is still and clean. Mark has learned not to touch her shoulder when she startles; she
flinches as if he were a threat.

Theresa’s PCL-5 score is 33—moderate, but enough to color her days. She avoids reading news stories
about industrial accidents or chemical spills. She has stopped attending her book club because the
host’s house has a new laminate floor that off-gasses. She feels a low, constant guilt: she is a
policy analyst who cannot face the very policies she helped create. She knows the statistics, the
safety margins, the toxicology reports. And yet her brain will not believe the data. It believes
only the memory of that breathless moment.

Some sessions, she tells me she feels old and foolish, that other people have real trauma. She
apologizes for wasting my time. But then she describes the ritual of checking every seal on every
window before she goes to sleep, and I see the toll it takes. Her marriage is a quiet arrangement of
allowances and accommodations. She loves Mark, but she cannot explain why she needs the car windows
up, the route planned to avoid the industrial park, the kitchen trash taken out before the banana
peel can decompose and release any invisible vapor.

She still works, still writes reports on clean air policy from her home office. But she has not set
foot in her agency’s building in over two years. She tells me she would rather retire early than
face that hallway. And she means it.
