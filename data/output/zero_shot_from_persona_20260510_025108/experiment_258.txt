He still mops the floors at the Regency Inn, but now he does it only after midnight, when the
hallways are empty. Before, he used to work the day shift—he liked the natural light in the lobby,

It happened in Room 214. A Saturday afternoon. He had just finished scrubbing the bathroom tile, and
he was carrying the bucket of soapy water toward the hallway. He remembers the weight of the bucket,
the slight slosh as he turned the corner. Mrs. Delgado was already coming down the stairs—he saw her
out of the corner of his eye, a small woman in pink slippers. She was reaching for the railing when
her foot hit the wet spot he hadn’t seen. The bucket flew. Her head hit the corner of the doorframe.

He dropped to his knees beside her. She wasn’t moving. There was blood spreading across the carpet,
thin and dark, like spilled tea. He screamed for help. He remembers the manager calling 911. He
remembers the paramedics asking him what happened, and he could only say, “I didn’t put the sign. I
forgot the sign.”

Mrs. Delgado survived, but she suffered a traumatic brain injury. She has short-term memory loss
now, and she walks with a cane. Her family filed a claim against the hotel. The insurance settled.
He was not fired—the manager said it was an accident—but every morning he hears the echo of her head
hitting the wood.

Now, at fifty-two, he works the night shift. He rolls his cart slowly, checking every corner for
shadows. He mops one small section at a time, then stands in the middle of it, waiting for the floor
to dry. He carries a folded yellow sign in his back pocket at all times, even when he is not
mopping. He has four of them stored in his locker.

He cannot sleep. When he closes his eyes, he sees her pink slippers. He sees the bucket tipping. He
wakes up gasping, his hands wet with sweat. His daughter stopped calling after the divorce was
finalized two years ago—his ex-wife said he was “different now,” too jumpy, too quiet. He does not
blame her. At family gatherings, he sits in the corner and watches the door. He counts the exits.

and sat on a stack of towels until his hands stopped shaking. The manager asked if he was sick. He
said yes.

forget the way her skull sounded, or the way his own voice sounded calling for help—thin and
useless, like a child’s. He thinks about it every time he picks up the mop. He thinks about it when
he folds the caution signs at the end of his shift. He carries the guilt the way he carries his
keys, always in his pocket, always within reach.
