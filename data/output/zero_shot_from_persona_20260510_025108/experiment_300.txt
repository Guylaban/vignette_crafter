It was a Thursday afternoon, just over fourteen months ago. She had been called to the community
center a few blocks from the hospital—a massive casualty event, the dispatcher said, voice tight.
When she arrived, the air was thick with dust and the sharp copper smell of blood. Bodies lay
scattered on the floor, some moving, some still. She knelt beside a young woman whose leg was pinned
under a beam, and she began to pray aloud, not for survival but for peace. Then the second explosion
came. It was not as large, but it was close—close enough that the pressure wave knocked her
sideways, and for a moment the world went silent except for a high-pitched ringing. She remembers
the woman’s hand slipping from hers.

In the weeks that followed, she returned to her chaplaincy duties. She told herself the work was
needed more than ever. But slowly, the work began to feel impossible. She finds herself scanning
every room she enters—noticing exits, watching faces, cataloguing the closest heavy object that
could serve as a shield. At the grocery store, she cannot stand with her back to the aisle. At
night, she sleeps with one ear toward the door, and wakes three or four times, heart pounding,
certain she heard an explosion. The nightmares are not replays of the bombing itself; they are
variations—crowded places, sudden loud noises, always a moment before impact.

Her faith, once a quiet anchor, has become a source of confusion. She leads prayers with families,
but the words feel hollow. When a grieving mother asks her why God allowed this, she can only nod
and say she does not know. The mother’s question echoes in her head for days afterward. She has
stopped attending her own church. The hymn “Be Still, My Soul” used to comfort her; now the first
notes send a spike of adrenaline through her chest.

She avoids the hospital’s trauma bay entirely. She has asked her supervisor to reassign her to the
hospice unit, where deaths are slower and quieter. But even there, a patient’s sudden cough can make
her flinch. She has taken to sitting alone in her office during lunch, eating a sandwich while
staring at a spot on the wall. Colleagues have noticed. They ask if she is okay. She tells them she
is just tired.

On good days, she can drive past the community center without her heart racing. On bad days, the
sight of the boarded-up windows sends her home, where she lies on her couch with the television off
and the curtains drawn, waiting for the hours to pass. She has not visited her sister’s apartment
because the elevator doors close too slowly. She no longer attends holiday gatherings. She has
stopped returning phone calls.

She is a chaplain who cannot enter a chapel. She is a woman of prayer who cannot ask God for
anything. She tells herself this is temporary, but the certainty she once carried has been replaced
by a low, constant tremor. She does not know how to tell anyone that the explosion is still
happening inside her, over and over, and that she has not yet found a way to stop it.
