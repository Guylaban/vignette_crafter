earth.

Now, sitting in the therapist’s office, he says he rarely thinks about that day. But he also says he
has not slept through the night in forty-three years. His hands, which once taught a non-verbal

he makes a small joke about his arthritis. He does not mention that his knuckles ache from clenching
the steering wheel every time a car backfires on the highway.

In the classroom, he is calm. He uses a quiet voice with students who are easily overstimulated. But
when a student throws a pencil in frustration, his heart begins to pound. The sound of plastic
skittering across the floor triggers something sharp and wordless. He turns his back, takes a
breath, and mentally counts the exits. The student never knows. He has become an expert at hiding
his startle response, at converting a flinch into a deep breath.

His divorce, twelve years ago, was quiet. His ex-wife said he was “there but not there.” On
weekends, he would sit on the porch for hours, watching the street. She once asked if he was waiting
for something. He said no. He was just tired.

He does not have many friends. He keeps in touch with one old Army buddy, but they only text on
Veterans Day. He deleted all his social media accounts three years ago—too many videos, too many
