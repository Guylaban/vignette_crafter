prettiest when you’re focused.* She had been hunched over her laptop at a coffee shop the day

dropped. She looked around the parking lot. No one. The next day, another note: *Your hair smells
like rain.* She had been caught in the rain on Tuesday. That afternoon she told her husband, Mark,
and they went to the police. There was nothing they could do yet, the officer explained—no direct
threat, no physical harm. She should document everything, keep a log.

Over the next few weeks, she stopped going to the coffee shop. She took different routes home,
sometimes driving an extra fifteen minutes just to lose him. She changed her routine at work,
leaving at odd hours, her eyes scanning the sidewalk before she stepped out. Mark started picking
her up when he could, but he couldn’t be there all the time. The notes kept coming. Once, she found
one taped to her studio door: *I know when you’re alone.* That was the night she couldn’t stop
shaking. She barely slept.

Now, months later, he has been arrested and charged. The trial is pending. She knows she is safe—she
moved offices, changed her phone number, installed cameras at home—but her body doesn’t believe it.
She still checks her rearview mirror compulsively, her hands sweating on the wheel. She walks
through the grocery store with her head on a swivel, heart hammering every time a man of average
height with brown hair turns down an aisle. She no longer works in coffee shops; she works from
home, curtains drawn, jumping at every sound. Her design work suffers. She stares at the screen for

telling a neighbor about their new security system. She doesn’t trust anyone anymore, not even her
closest friends. She canceled a coffee date last week because she saw a man in a blue jacket that
looked like the one he always wore.

At night, she dreams of notes. Hundreds of them, piling up on her desk, her bed, her keyboard. She
wakes up gasping, her skin crawling. She tells herself it’s over. But her body remembers.
