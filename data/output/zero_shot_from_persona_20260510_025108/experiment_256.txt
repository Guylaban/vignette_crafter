He is a man who carries his day in the set of his shoulders. At thirty-five, he walks into the
session room with a slightly labored breath, as if the stairs themselves ask too much of him. He
sits on the edge of the chair, hands resting on his knees, fingertips pressing into the fabric as
though checking that the chair is real. He teaches special education at a middle school, and his
students—the ones with autism, with emotional dysregulation, with quiet brains that refuse to
speak—are the center of his world. But lately, when a student drops a book or slams a desk, he finds
himself on his feet, heart hammering, before his mind catches up.

The mugging happened eighteen months ago, late October, after a parent-teacher conference. He had
stayed late to write IEP notes and was walking to his car in the staff parking lot. The streetlights
flickered—they always had—and he remembers thinking he should report that. A man came from behind
the dumpster. He never saw his face, only the silver blade of a box cutter catching the light. The
man took his wallet, his phone, the lanyard with his school keys, and told him to lie on the ground.
He lay there. The man kicked his ribs once, hard, and left.

He lay there for what felt like a long time. When he finally got up, he walked to the hospital
holding his side. The ribs were cracked. The ER doctor told him he was lucky. He didn’t feel lucky.
He felt like the street had swallowed him whole, and then spat him back into a world that looked the
same but felt different.

Now, he avoids that side of the school at all costs. He parks in a different lot, even though it
adds twenty minutes to his commute. He walks with his keys poking between his knuckles, a habit that
feels both foolish and necessary. He hasn’t been able to stay after school since. His principal,
understanding, assigned a para to wrap up the after-hours documentation. He tells himself he’s
grateful. But he feels like a failure—the same way he felt when his students had a meltdown he
couldn’t soothe.

In class, he notices the smallest changes. A student’s chair scraping too loudly makes him freeze. A
tap on the shoulder from a colleague makes him flinch so hard he drops his coffee. He has started
standing with his back to the wall during circle time. He tells himself it’s about classroom

doesn’t come.
