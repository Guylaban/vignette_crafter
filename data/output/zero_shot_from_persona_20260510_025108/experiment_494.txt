On a Wednesday morning in late September, three years ago, he watches the I-beam swing free from the
crane. The rigging had been wrong—he knew it, felt it in his gut the moment he saw the angle—but he
wasn’t the foreman, so he says nothing. Michael is two stories up on the adjacent scaffold, reaching
for a tie-off point, when the load shifts. The snap of the cable is a sound he will hear in his
sleep, in the shower, in the quiet moments between hammer blows. He sees Michael’s body hit the
rebar cage below. The foreman’s scream is still inside his ears, a low buzz that never fully stops.

After that, he goes back to work the next day. Someone has to finish the pour. He tells himself it’s
what any man would do. But his hands shake when he climbs the scaffolding. He checks every shackle,
every sling, three times, four times, until the foreman yells at him to move. He starts staying
late, just to make sure nothing is left undone, because if the site isn’t perfect, something else
will fall. His wife says he’s never home, and when he is, he’s staring at the wall. He doesn’t tell
her about the dreams. He doesn’t tell anyone.

The divorce comes eighteen months later. She says he’s not the man she married, and she’s right. He
can’t feel her touch the way he used to. When she cries, he feels nothing but a vague irritation,
like a stone in his boot. He moves into a studio apartment above a garage, eats takeout, sleeps in
his clothes. The only thing that still makes sense is the job site—the noise, the grit, the endless
physical push. But even there, he finds himself scanning the sky for falling loads. He listens for
the wrong kind of creak in the scaffolding. Once, he walks off a job because a foreman’s voice is
too loud, too much like that morning’s scream.

His crew notices. They say he’s jumpy. He snaps at a laborer for standing too close to a trench
edge. He refuses to work on the fifteenth floor of a high-rise after a storm. He drives forty
minutes out of his way to avoid the exit by the old site. None of this feels strange to him—it feels
necessary. He keeps a small bottle of whiskey in his lunchbox. He drinks it in the truck before he
goes home, so he can sleep three or four hours before the dream finds him again. In the dream, he’s
the one on the beam, and the cable snaps, and he falls, but he never hits the ground. He always
wakes up right before impact, heart slamming, the sheets soaked.

He doesn’t talk about Michael. Not to the counselor his ex-wife made him see, not to the union rep
who asked if he was okay. He says he’s fine. He says it was an accident. But at night, alone in the
studio, he replays the rigging angle over and over, wondering if he could have said something
different. He still works, because what else is there. But every morning he finds his palms sweating
before he even opens the truck door. He checks his harness three times. He stands with his back to
the wall whenever he can. And he waits for the next thing to fall.
