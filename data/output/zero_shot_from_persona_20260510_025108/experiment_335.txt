She is twenty years old and works as a pharmacist in a small community pharmacy in a city that feels
both safe and foreign. Most mornings she arrives before the store opens, unlocks the heavy metal

lights hum overhead, and the antiseptic smell of the pharmacy is familiar enough to anchor her—but
only just.

The torture happened two years ago, in a country far from here. She was finishing her final year of
pharmacy school when the soldiers came. They accused her of distributing medicines to people they
called “enemies of the state.” In reality, she had simply filled a prescription for an elderly woman
whose son was a known activist. The soldiers did not care about the distinction. They took her from
the clinic, blindfolded her, and kept her in a windowless room for three days.

She remembers the sounds most clearly: the scrape of a chair on concrete, the metallic clink of a
bucket being kicked across the floor, the voice of a man who spoke her own language but with a cold,

could no longer stand. They burned the inside of her left forearm with a cigarette, the scar still a
pale, puckered map beneath her white coat. They asked questions she could not answer, and when she
did not answer, they hurt her again. On the third day, the international news covered a protest in
the capital, and the soldiers grew distracted. She crawled out of a broken window as the sun was
setting, her feet swollen, her mind blank.

She made it to a relative’s house, then to a refugee camp, then, through a series of improbable
phone calls and false documents, to this city. Now she lives with her boyfriend, Kwame, a nursing
student who never asks too many questions about her past. He is kind, patient, and sometimes that
makes it worse. When he touches her shoulder unexpectedly, she flinches so hard she drops her coffee
mug. She apologizes, picks up the shards, and says nothing else.

At work, she has learned to read the faces of customers. A man in a uniform—even a postal
worker—makes her pulse race. She grips the counter until her knuckles whiten. Once, a customer
laughed loudly, and she dropped a bottle of cough syrup, the glass shattering across the tile. Her

She always leaves the bathroom door slightly ajar.

him the full story. She does not know how to say that sometimes she still feels the rubber hose,
that she cannot stand to have her feet touched, that she flinches when he takes his shoes off near
her.
