almost abstract—but the crushed flowers that were pressed into the concrete. Chrysanthemums. They

The noise was the first thing she lost. The sound of the engine revving, the screams, the metal
folding chairs scraping against the pavement—all of it collapsed into a single high-pitched ringing
that stayed in her ears for weeks. She remembers being on the ground before she understood she had
fallen. Her knees were scraped. A woman’s shoe lay beside her, still laced. The parade had been
celebrating music and color, and now the street was a ruin of confetti and glass.

She doesn’t talk about that day. Not with her coworkers at the warehouse, not with her mother who
calls every Sunday from El Salvador, not with anyone. When they ask about her life before moving to
this city three years ago, she says it was quiet. That part is true. She grew up in a small town,
helped her grandmother cook, walked to school past fields of sugarcane. The attack happened two
years after she arrived here, on a day off she had taken to feel like a real American. She hasn’t
been to a parade since.

At work, she is reliable, even preferred by the supervisors. She operates the pallet jack, stacks
boxes on the loading dock, and keeps her head down. But the warehouse is not safe. The forklifts

near her, she drops to a crouch before she can stop herself, heart slamming, hands pressed flat
against the concrete. They laugh it off, call her jumpy. She forces a smile, tells them she’s just
tired.

Sleep is a problem. She lies in bed at night replaying the sequence—the truck, the screaming, the
silence after the second wave of sirens. She replays it differently sometimes, imagining she had
