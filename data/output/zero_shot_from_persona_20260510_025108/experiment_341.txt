He remembers the moment exactly. It was a Tuesday in early November, during the weekly staff meeting
in the library at Lincoln Middle School. He was standing at the whiteboard, marker in hand,
explaining the new schedule for parent-teacher conferences. Then the air turned wrong. A pressure
bloomed behind his sternum, spread up into his jaw, down his left arm. He thought it was
indigestion—too much coffee and stress. He sat down, waved off the concerned faces, said he just
needed a minute. But when the room started to tilt, when the school nurse took one look at him and
called 911, he knew this was not heartburn.

He was fifty-one, healthy enough, no history. The heart attack was a surprise. The stenting was a
blur of bright lights, cold sheets, and a woman in blue scrubs telling him to breathe. He survived.
The cardiologist said his ejection fraction was good, that with medication and lifestyle changes he
could expect a full recovery. But something inside him did not recover.

Now, eight months later, he is still the principal of the same school. He walks the same halls,
greets the same students in the morning, sits in the same conference room for staff meetings. But he
is not the same man. He avoids the library. When he has to go there, he stands by the door instead
of walking to the whiteboard. His heart skips at the smell of floor wax—the same smell as the
hospital corridor. He has stopped drinking coffee entirely, but he has also stopped drinking water
during the day because he once read that dehydration can mimic heart symptoms, and that thought
spirals into a panic.

At night, he lies awake in the house that used to belong to his wife and him. She died four years
ago from ovarian cancer. He thought he had grieved enough, but the heart attack reopened something.
Alone in the dark, he hears his own pulse in his ears—thud, thud, thud—and he is certain each one is
the last. He sleeps maybe four hours a night, and when he does sleep, he dreams of the ER: the
beeping monitors, the cold gel on his chest, the doctor’s eyes looking serious behind the mask. He
wakes gasping, clutching his chest, checking his watch for the time.

He has become hypervigilant about his body. Several times a day he stops what he is doing to take
his pulse. He counts the seconds between beats, sometimes using two fingers, sometimes pressing a
stethoscope he bought online to his own chest. If the rate feels fast, he sits down immediately,
even if he is in the middle of a conversation with a teacher or a parent. People have noticed. His
vice principal has asked if he is okay. He says he is fine, just tired.

He no longer eats lunch in the staff lounge because someone might ask about his diet, and he does
not want to explain that he is terrified of another blockage. He eats alone in his office, plain
oatmeal and a bottle of water. He avoids the nurse’s office entirely. He has stopped driving on the
highway because the speed and merging feel uncontrollable, and control is what he craves most. He
has not exercised since the attack—not because he cannot, but because even a light jog produces
chest pressure from anxiety, and he interprets it as a sign of imminent collapse.

At work, he is irritable. A student’s minor infraction—running in the hall—makes him snap. He
apologizes later, but the anger lingers inside him like a knot. He feels disconnected from the joy
he used to take in his job. The laughter of children sounds like noise. The school announcements
feel like obligations. He goes through the motions: morning duty, meetings, emails, dismissal. But
he is always scanning for exits, always aware of where the nearest defibrillator is mounted on the
wall.

He has not told anyone the full story. Not his grown daughter, not his colleagues, not the grief
support group he tried once and left early because the mention of “loss” triggered a wave of heat
and dizziness. He carries the event with him every minute. It is not a memory. It is a constant
presence, a vibration just beneath his skin. He is waiting for the next one.
