She was making her last delivery of the night, a routine drop-off at an apartment complex on the
edge of town. The address was a second-floor unit, and she parked her sedan under a flickering

That was six months ago.

Now, she tells herself it was just a few seconds. A man came out from between two buildings, his
face obscured by a hood. He grabbed her arm when she stepped out of the car with the food bag. He
had a knife—small, dull-looking, but she saw the blade catch the light. “Give me your phone. Your
wallet. Now.” She didn’t scream. She just handed over everything. Her phone, her wallet, the bag of
food. He shoved her back against the car door and ran. She stood there for what felt like ten
minutes, heart hammering, before she got back in the car and drove straight home. She didn’t call
the police until the next morning.

She still delivers food, because she needs the money. But everything is different now. She only
works day shifts, though even daylight feels unsafe. Her mother, a widow who she lives with, tells
her she is being dramatic. “You were not hurt. You still have your life. Move on.” So she does not
talk about it at home. She tries to smile when her mother asks why she’s jumpy.

In the car, she checks every street corner three times before she pulls over. She locks the doors as
soon as she sits down. She keeps a small can of pepper spray in the cup holder, even though it’s not
legal for her to carry it. When she has a delivery to a building with an alley near the entrance,
she circles the block twice, then parks a block away and walks with her keys between her fingers.
She has not told her manager about the mugging. She is afraid they will fire her for being a
liability.

At night, she cannot sleep. She lies awake listening for footsteps outside her bedroom window. Every
car door slamming makes her chest tighten. She has lost eight pounds because eating feels like a
chore—her stomach clenches every time she sits down to a meal. Her mother complains that she is

spinning. She stopped seeing her few friends because she cannot sit still in a cafe for more than
twenty minutes without scanning every face, every exit.
