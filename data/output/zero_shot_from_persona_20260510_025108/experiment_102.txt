She still opens her laptop every morning, but the screen stays dark for long stretches. The cursor
blinks at her from a blank Figma file, and she stares at it until the edges of her vision go fuzzy.
Before the accident, she used to start her day by sketching wireframes—clean, logical user flows.
Now she can’t look at a button without imagining the weight of her hands on a steering wheel.

It happened nine months ago, on a rainy Tuesday evening. She was driving home from a client
workshop, exhausted but alert. The light was yellow when she entered the intersection. She remembers
the way the windshield wipers slapped against the glass, the smell of wet cardboard from a takeout
bag on the passenger seat. Then a blur of movement—a man on a bicycle, crossing against the signal.
She slammed the brakes, but the car kept sliding. The thud came through the floorboards, the
steering wheel, her own bones. She saw his body roll off the hood and land on the wet asphalt. She
got out, her legs shaking, and knelt beside him. There was blood pooling under his helmet, his neck
at an angle that made her stomach turn. A stranger called 911. She held his hand until the ambulance
came. He survived, but the doctors said he would never walk again. A spinal injury. The police said
it wasn’t her fault—the cyclist had no lights, no reflective gear, and he ran a red. But fault and
guilt are different things, she’s learned.

In the weeks that followed, she stopped sleeping. Every time she closed her eyes, she saw his
face—mid-thirties, dark beard, a wedding ring. She looked him up online. His name was Mark. He had a
wife, two young kids. She found a photo of them at a beach, his daughter on his shoulders. She
memorized that photo. She started checking the local news every day, refreshing the page to see if
there was an update on his condition. She didn’t tell her husband about this. She didn’t tell
anyone.

Her work suffered. She began missing deadlines. Her team lead asked if she was okay, and she said
she was just tired. But tired wasn’t the word. It was more like being hollowed out, her mind full of
static. She would sit in design reviews and hear her colleagues talk about color contrast and
accessibility, and she would think about Mark’s hospital room. Was it accessible? Did someone have
to widen the door for his wheelchair? She couldn’t look at a curb cut without seeing the moment his
body hit the ground.

The smallest things trigger it now. A bicycle bell. The sound of rain. The smell of wet asphalt.
Once, in a grocery store, a child dropped a jar of pasta sauce, and the shatter sent her to the
floor, hyperventilating. Her husband had to help her out of the aisle, whispering that it was okay,
it was okay. But it’s not okay. She avoids driving entirely. Her mother-in-law makes comments about
her being lazy, staying home all day, but she can’t explain. She can’t tell anyone the truth without
feeling like she’s confessing to a crime.

She has nightmares three or four times a week. In the dream, she’s driving, but the brakes don’t
work. Or she’s on a bicycle, and a car is coming, but she can’t move her legs. She wakes up gasping,
her chest tight, and then lies still until the sun comes up. She doesn’t reach for her husband
anymore. She’s afraid she’ll flinch if he touches her.

The worst part is the guilt. She knows she didn’t mean to hurt him. But intention doesn’t change the
aftermath. Sometimes she finds herself halfway through a task and realizes she’s been wiping the
kitchen counter for twenty minutes, lost in the loop of that moment. She replays it over and
over—what if she had braked a second sooner? What if she had honked? What if she had stayed home
that day? The loop has no off switch.

Yesterday, she logged into her work dashboard and saw a message from a product manager: “Can you
present the new onboarding flow at tomorrow’s standup?” She closed the laptop. She sat on the sofa,
hands in her lap, and counted the seconds between breaths. She didn’t open it again.
