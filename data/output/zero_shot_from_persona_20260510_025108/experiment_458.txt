**Clinical Vignette: Patient A. (30, Female, African, Married, School Counselor)**

Over the course of several sessions, A. has slowly begun to share the story she has carried alone
for nearly two years. She speaks in fragments, often stopping to stare at the floor, as if she is
watching the events unfold again behind her eyes.

The trauma happened on a Tuesday evening in late spring. A. was driving home from a parent-teacher
conference, still thinking about a struggling student she had urged to try harder. She was alone,
and it was dark. She noticed police lights in her rearview mirror—she had no idea why. She pulled
over, heart already pounding, hands gripping the wheel. The officer took too long to approach. When
he did, he shouted. He asked where she was going, why she was in that neighborhood, whether she
lived there. She tried to explain: *I’m a school counselor. I was at work. I’m going home.* He told
her to step out of the car. She felt the cold metal of the car door against her back. Another
officer arrived. They searched her car without her permission. They kept her on the side of the road
for forty-five minutes. She did not get a ticket, only a verbal warning and a look that made her
feel less than human.

In the weeks following, A. could not stop replaying the moment the first officer put his hand on his
holster. She started avoiding driving after dark. Then she stopped driving past that intersection
entirely, taking a fifteen-minute detour every day. Her husband, Kofi, noticed she was quieter, more
irritable. She told him it was work stress. She did not mention the police.

As a school counselor, A. spends her days helping children navigate anxiety, grief, and fear. She
runs small groups on emotional regulation, and she is the one teachers call when a student has a
meltdown. She is good at her job. But now, when she hears a knock on her office door, her heart
jumps. One morning, a police officer came to the school to give a presentation on safety. A. hid in
the bathroom for the entire hour, hands shaking, breathing shallow. Her supervisor later asked if
she was sick. She said she had a migraine.

At home, A. has become hypervigilant. She checks the locks three times before bed. She cannot sit
with her back to a window in a restaurant. She startles when Kofi walks into a room too quietly. He
has asked, gently, why she flinches when he touches her shoulder from behind. She says she is just
tired. But she is not tired—she is exhausted from the constant scanning, the bracing for something
bad to happen.

Her sleep is broken. She dreams of headlights and shouting voices. She wakes up with her jaw
clenched and her hands curled into fists. She has stopped attending community events because the
sight of a uniform—any uniform—makes her stomach drop. Last month, a student’s parent, a police
officer, came to pick up their child during dismissal. A. saw him at the front desk and immediately
walked the other way, leaving a colleague to handle the sign-out. She felt ashamed, but the shame
only deepened the numbness that has settled over her like a fog.

She recently told Kofi the full story, crying for the first time in a year. He held her and said
nothing, which she needed. But the relief was brief. The next day, at work, a seventh-grader
mentioned her uncle was a cop. A. felt her chest tighten. She smiled and changed the subject.

A. still goes to work every day. She still helps children. But she is no longer the same person who
sat in that car on the side of the road. She moves through her life in a state of quiet vigilance,
waiting for another moment that will confirm what she has already learned: that safety is an
illusion, and that some stories cannot be talked away.
