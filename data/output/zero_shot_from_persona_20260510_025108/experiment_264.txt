**Vignette: Dr. Marco Reyes**

The operating room was never supposed to feel fragile. Dr. Marco Reyes had scrubbed in thousands of
times—cool tile under his shoes, the steady hum of the ventilator, the precise weight of a scalpel
in his gloved hand. That afternoon, he was midway through a laparoscopic cholecystectomy when the
floor began to tremble. He assumed it was maintenance work. Then the ceiling tiles rattled free, the
lights swayed, and someone screamed in the hallway. The earthquake lasted seventy-two seconds. It
felt like hours.

He remembers dropping his instruments, pressing his body over the open abdomen, shielding the
patient from falling debris. The anesthesiologist shouted that they had to evacuate. Marco refused.
He couldn’t leave a patient open on the table. The power failed; the backup generator kicked in,
then died. For fifteen minutes, he operated by headlamp, the building groaning around them, his
hands steady but his heart racing so fast he could taste salt in his mouth. When the patient was
closed and safely wheeled out, Marco stood alone in the dark, blood on his scrubs, unable to move.

That was eight months ago.

Now, Marco avoids the hospital’s basement level entirely. He claims it’s because the break room has
bad coffee, but the truth is the low ceilings and windowless corridors press against his ribs like a
second earthquake. He takes the stairs instead of the elevator, always counting the steps. Loud
noises make him drop things—once, a sterile tray clattered to the floor during a drill test, and he
excused himself to the bathroom to breathe into his hands until they stopped shaking.

His surgical performance remains technically flawless. Colleagues still refer difficult cases to
him. But Marco has begun scheduling only morning procedures. Afternoons are spent dictating notes,
rereading them, deleting them. He avoids the OR consent form where “emergency backup plan” is
listed. At night, he wakes to the sensation of shaking, reaches for the ceiling, and lies in the
dark with his wife Elena breathing beside him, unable to tell her that he’s still in that room,
headlamp flickering, afraid the walls will crush them both.

Elena has noticed his silences. He used to tell her about his day—the gallbladders, the grateful
families, the resident who needed encouragement. Now he answers with single words. When she puts her
hand on his arm, he stiffens. He knows she interprets this as distance. He can’t explain that touch
now feels like a warning. The last time she surprised him from behind, he spun around with his fist
raised, then stood frozen, ashamed.

At the gym, he avoids the weight room—too many metal sounds, too much vibration from dropped
barbells. He stopped jogging outdoors because construction noise sends him into a cold sweat. His
concentration fractures during simple tasks: pouring coffee, he spills it when a cart rattles by. In
the OR, he is hyperaware of the ceiling’s structural support beams. He has memorized the location of
every fire extinguisher, every exit, every reinforced corner.

The nightmares are the worst. He dreams of the operating table splitting open like a fault line, and
he falls into it, still holding the scalpel, the patient’s face dissolving into rubble. He wakes
gasping, sheets twisted, certain the house is about to collapse. Elena once asked if he wanted to
talk to someone. He said he was fine.

But he isn’t fine. The PCL-5 score of 49, completed last week for a hospital wellness survey, tells
a story he already knows: the earthquake didn’t end when the shaking stopped. It embedded itself in
his nervous system, in the spaces between his ribs, in the way he now checks the ceiling before
entering any room. He is a surgeon, trained to hold steady in the face of chaos. And yet, every day,
the chaos holds him.
