Captain Maya Chen is twenty-nine years old, a combat pilot who flies F-35s out of a joint base in
the Pacific. She has been flying since she was twenty-three, logging over a thousand hours in
theater. She came to talk because she can’t sleep, because her hands shake when she holds a coffee
cup, because she has stopped talking to her mother on the phone.

It started eighteen months ago. Maya was scrambled on a night mission over hostile terrain, a
routine CAS run. Her wingman, Captain Jake Morrison—ten years older, quiet, a man who always let her
lead on the radio and bought her pad thai on R&R—was flying low cover. She saw the surface-to-air
flash on her left peripheral, heard the garbled call over comms, then silence. She orbited for
twenty minutes waiting for his beacon. It never came. The recovery team found nothing but sheet
metal.

In the weeks after, Maya threw herself into sorties. She told herself the best way to honor him was
to keep flying. She flew harder, lower, faster. Then the dreams started. In them, Jake is still
alive, sitting in the ready room, and he turns to look at her, but his face is made of chrome and
she can’t hear what he says. She wakes up with her sheets soaked, her heart pounding. She stopped
drinking coffee because it made her jump at the smell of jet fuel.

She avoids the hangar now. She drives the long way to the flight line so she doesn’t pass the
maintenance bay where Jake’s locker used to be. When she straps into the cockpit, her hands freeze
on the throttle. She has to repeat the preflight checklist three times because her mind drifts to
the radio silence, to the way the afterburner flame looked orange against the smoke. Her squadron
commander noticed a drop in her simulator scores. He suggested she see someone.

Maya lives alone in a one-bedroom near base. She hasn’t dated since the incident. She says she
doesn’t have the energy. She spends her nights cleaning her pistol, over and over, or watching old
movies on mute. She rarely eats. She lost twelve pounds. She stopped replying to her mother’s
texts—her mother lives in a city half a world away, and the last time they video-called, Maya
started shaking and said the connection was bad. She feels a constant, low-grade guilt. She should
have seen the SAM. She should have called a break turn sooner. If she had, maybe Jake would still be
alive.

At work, she is functional but hollow. She can still fly—her muscle memory is intact—but the joy is
gone. She feels like she is operating a machine that used to be her body. When the briefing officer
says “hostile territory,” she sees Jake’s canopy shattering. When she hears a siren on base, she
flinches. She has started checking the memorial page for the squadron every morning, even though she
knows no one else has died. She doesn’t know why she does it.

The therapist notes that Maya’s narrative is precise, almost clinical. She uses aviation jargon to
describe her feelings: “My affect is flat, like a dead-stick landing.” She doesn’t cry when she

sees his wedding ring on his flight glove, and she hears the last transmission, just static.

She is a combat pilot. She was trained to never leave a wingman behind. But he is gone, and she is
still flying, and that feels like the worst kind of betrayal.
