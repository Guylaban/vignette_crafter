In the months after the pneumonia that nearly killed him, Samir stopped going to the mosque. It
wasn’t a conscious decision—he just found himself unable to step inside. The first time he tried,
the smell of carpet cleaner and old prayer rugs hit him like a wave from the hospital corridor, and
his heart began to pound so hard he thought the imam might hear it. He turned around and walked
home, then called his mother and said he had a cold.

The illness started as a cough, nothing more. Samir, then thirty-five, had been working six days a
week at the salon on Alta Vista, building a loyal clientele who trusted him with their color and
their stories. He ignored the fever for three days, thinking it was flu, until a client noticed his
hands trembling as he sectioned her hair. “Samir, you look terrible,” she said. He laughed it off.
That night he couldn’t breathe, and his roommate found him on the bathroom floor at two in the
morning. The next thing he remembers is a tube down his throat and a nurse’s voice saying something
about sepsis.

Now, three years later, Samir still avoids looking at the scar on his neck where the central line
went in. When he changes shirts in the morning, he angles his body away from the mirror. He has not
gone to a doctor since the last follow-up appointment, even though he knows he should. The thought
of waiting in a clinic—the beeping machines, the antiseptic smell, the way the fluorescent lights
hum—makes his stomach clench so tight he sometimes has to sit down on the salon floor behind the
chair, pretending to look for a fallen clip.

At work, Samir is still good with his hands. He can do a balayage in under an hour, and his dry-
cutting technique is the best in the shop. But he cannot stand the scent of hand sanitizer. A few
weeks ago, the new assistant sprayed it near him, and Samir flinched so hard he nearly sliced a
client’s earlobe. He started wearing disposable gloves not for hygiene but for the feeling of a
barrier between his skin and the world. When clients cough, he feels his chest tighten. He washes
his own hands for two full minutes between appointments, counting inside his head.

He lives alone now. The breakup came six months after the ICU discharge—his partner couldn’t handle
the nightmares, the way Samir would wake up gasping and not know where he was. Samir does not blame
him. He has not dated since. Most nights he falls asleep on the couch with the television on,
because the silence reminds him of the hospital at 3 a.m. If he wakes in the dark, he touches his
own pulse at the wrist, counting beats to make sure his heart is still going. Sometimes he counts to
sixty, then starts over.

The worst part is the waiting. Not the memory of the illness itself, but the certainty that
something is coming again, lurking somewhere inside his body. Two weeks ago he found a small lump
behind his ear and spent five hours scrolling through medical websites before realizing it was just
a swollen lymph node from a mild cold. He did not sleep that night. In the morning he called in sick
for the first time in a year, then spent the day cleaning his apartment compulsively, wiping down
every surface with bleach.

His mother calls every Friday. She tells him he needs to find a nice woman, settle down. Samir says,
“Inshallah,” and changes the subject. He does not tell her about the nights he spends googling

toner. In the back room, he pressed his palms flat against the cold steel of the supply cabinet
