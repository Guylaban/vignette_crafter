He was twenty-one when the water came, and now, at twenty-two, he still smells it in his dreams. Not
the rain—that he could handle—but the thick, brown smell of the river rising through his apartment
in Bangkok. He remembers the sound first: a low hum that became a growl, then a crash as the wall
gave way. He grabbed his laptop, his phone, his grandmother’s photograph, and climbed to the roof
with three neighbors. They stayed there for fourteen hours while the water swallowed the ground
floor, swallowing the motorbikes, the street vendors, the family photographs he’d left behind.

Now he works as a UX designer for a midsize tech firm in a glass building that looks nothing like
his old neighborhood. He lives in a new high-rise on the tenth floor, far from the river. But every
morning, when the elevator doors close, his chest tightens. He takes the stairs now, all ten
flights, because the elevator feels like the roof of that building—a small, wet space with no way
out. His colleagues don’t notice. They see a quiet, meticulous young man who arranges his desk in
perfect order, who checks the weather app obsessively, who never takes the elevator.

At work, he designs interfaces for a ride-hailing app. Last month, his manager asked him to create a
flood-alert feature for the app’s safety dashboard. He nodded, opened a new file, and sat staring at
the blank screen for three hours. The cursor blinked like a heartbeat. When he finally closed the
file, his hands were shaking. He told his manager the feature needed more user research. He is still
avoiding it.

The sounds are the worst. A loud bang from a delivery truck backfiring sends him under his desk
before he can stop himself. He stays there, knees to chest, counting the seconds until his heart
slows. His coworkers have started to notice. One of them, a kind woman named Priya, asked if he was
okay. He said he was fine, just tired. He has said “just tired” so many times that he believes it
might be true. But tired people don’t wake up at 3 a.m. gasping for air, convinced the bed is
floating. Tired people don’t check the weather forecast six times before leaving the apartment.

He hasn’t been to a movie theater since the flood. The darkness, the loud sound, the way the screen
fills your whole vision—it feels too much like being trapped on that roof, watching the water rise.
He used to love action films. Now he watches only quiet shows on his laptop, in a well-lit room,
with the door open.

He hasn’t called his mother in three weeks. She lives in the same city, in a different flood zone.
He knows she is fine—she texts him every day—but hearing her voice makes him see that water again,
the way it floated her kitchen table out the door. He will call tomorrow. He said that yesterday,
too.

On good days, he can focus on wireframes and user flows. He is good at his job. He understands how
people move through digital spaces, what frustrates them, what soothes them. But lately, he has been
sketching the same thing over and over in the margins of his notebook: a small house on a hill,
surrounded by a blue line that never quite reaches the door. He doesn’t know what it means, or why
he can’t stop drawing it.

He is twenty-two. The flood was a year ago. He still checks the door is unlocked, then locked, then
unlocked again. He still sleeps with the lights on. He still finds himself holding his breath
whenever the clouds turn gray. He is tired of being tired. But he doesn’t know how to stop running
from the water that is no longer there.
