**Clinical Vignette (PTSD – Childhood Physical Abuse)**

Rhea is a 30-year-old software engineer, married for four years, who moved to the United States from
India seven years ago. She presents as articulate, careful in her speech, and slightly tense—sitting
upright with her hands folded. Over several sessions, she has disclosed a childhood marked by
chronic physical abuse from her mother.

Rhea’s earliest memories of the abuse begin when she was five or six. Her mother, a perfectionist
who believed in strict discipline, would beat her with a wooden spoon or a belt for small
mistakes—spilled milk, a misplaced toy, a low grade. The beatings were unpredictable; sometimes she
was punished for things she hadn’t even done. Rhea learned to watch her mother’s face, to read the
smallest shift in expression that preceded a beating. She remembers holding her breath, the sharp
sting, and then the silent apology that never came. By age nine, she had stopped crying during the
beatings. She would go to her room, press her back against the wall, and count the seconds until the
pain faded.

Her father worked long hours and was largely absent. When he was home, he did not intervene. Rhea
learned not to complain. “She’s your mother, she loves you,” he would say. So Rhea believed
it—believed she must deserve the punishment. She became an excellent student, trying to earn safety
through perfect schoolwork. But perfection was never enough.

In high school, the beatings became less frequent but more intense. Her mother’s slaps could still
come for speaking out of turn or wearing a skirt that was “too short.” By then, Rhea had learned to
dissociate. She would float above her body during the worst moments, watching from the corner of the
ceiling. That skill followed her into adulthood.

Rhea excelled in computer science, earning a scholarship to a top university. She left India as soon
as she could, moved to the U.S. for graduate school, and then into a software engineering role at a
large tech company. She thought distance would erase the past. Instead, the past followed her in
invisible ways.

At work, Rhea is hypervigilant. She reads her manager’s tone and body language with the same
intensity she once read her mother’s face. A curt email or a delayed response sends her into a
spiral of self-blame: *I must have made a mistake. I am going to be humiliated.* She stays late,
checking and rechecking her code, never trusting her own work. Her colleagues see her as
conscientious, even obsessive. She avoids team meetings when she can, especially if she anticipates
criticism. Her performance reviews are strong, but she feels constantly on the verge of being
exposed as incompetent.

Her marriage is a quieter battleground. Her husband, a kind and patient man, does not understand why
she flinches when he raises his hand to close a cabinet door. She does not tell him about the abuse.
When he touches her unexpectedly, she freezes. She often makes excuses to avoid sex, saying she is
tired or has a headache. At night, she lies awake, hyperaware of every sound, waiting for a threat
that never comes. When she does sleep, she dreams of her mother’s face, twisted in anger, and wakes
with her heart pounding.

glass. She tells herself she is weak for still being affected. “It was just discipline,” she says.
