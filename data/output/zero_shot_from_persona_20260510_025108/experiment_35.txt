**Clinical Vignette: Margaret**

Margaret is an 80-year-old retired social worker, still living alone in the small apartment she has
occupied for the past thirty years. She is slender, with silver hair pulled back tightly, and she
arrives for sessions wearing pressed slacks and a cardigan buttoned to the collar. She sits upright
on the edge of the chair, hands folded in her lap, and speaks with careful precision. She is single,
having never married, though she does not offer this detail freely. When asked about her history,
she says only, “I made a poor choice when I was younger. I was very young.”

She was twenty-six when she met Frank. He was charming, attentive, a fellow caseworker at the agency
where she had just started. He praised her work, brought her coffee, walked her to the bus stop.
Within six months, she moved into his one-bedroom apartment. The first time he struck her, she had
left a dish in the sink overnight. He apologized profusely, wept, promised it would never happen
again. She believed him. She had seen clients in abusive relationships—she knew the patterns—but she
told herself this was different. He was under stress. He loved her.

The violence escalated over two years. It was not constant, but unpredictable. A slammed door could
trigger a slap. A misplaced comment could lead to a shove against the wall. She learned to walk on
eggshells, to monitor his moods, to keep her voice low and her opinions inside. She never told
anyone at work. She feared losing her job, feared the shame, feared that people would say she should
have known better. The final night, he knocked her unconscious with a cast-iron skillet. She woke in
the emergency room, and a nurse—a stranger—gently asked, “Do you want to leave?” Margaret said yes.
She never went back.

Now, at eighty, she still lives with Frank in her body. She scans every room for exits, even her own
kitchen. She cannot sleep with her back to the door. She sleeps on the left side of the bed so she
can see the doorway. She wakes at 3:00 a.m. most nights, heart pounding, drenched in sweat,
remembering the sound of his footsteps in the hall. She does not open her closet at night. She
avoids romantic relationships entirely. A neighbor who once touched her shoulder to get her
attention made her flinch so hard she dropped her groceries.

Her work as a social worker kept her going for decades. She was devoted to her clients, especially
