Dr. Evelyn Marsh was seventy-two when she went to Sarajevo. It was supposed to be a medical
mission—three weeks of reading scans in a makeshift clinic, a favor for an old colleague who had
begged for an experienced radiologist. She had packed light, kissed her husband Richard goodbye, and
told him not to worry. She had spent forty years in sterile exam rooms, under fluorescent lights,
reading images of other people’s bodies. She was not prepared for the sound.

The first mortar landed three blocks from the hospital at 2:47 on a Tuesday. The concussion rattled
the windows, and Evelyn felt it in her chest before her ears caught up. She remembers the way the
ceiling tiles shuddered, the way her hands flew to the keyboard. For an instant, she thought it was
a construction blast. Then the sirens began, and a nurse pulled her away from the window.

She finished her shift that afternoon because there was no one else. She remembers the smell of dust
and cordite, the way the light turned a strange yellow through the broken glass. She remembers a
woman brought in with shrapnel in her arm—Evelyn had to hold the X-ray plate herself because the
tech was shaking too badly.

Afterward, she sat in the supply closet and cried. She told herself it was just shock, that she
would be fine once she got home. She stayed the full three weeks. The shelling continued. She
learned to stop flinching. She learned to walk fast, head down, between buildings. She learned to
sort the dead from the living by the stillness on the table.

Now she is seventy-six. She lives in a quiet suburb with Richard, in a house with a weeping willow
and a porch swing. She retired three years ago, but she keeps an old film badge from the mission in
a drawer beside her bed. She does not know why she keeps it.

She wakes at 2:47 every morning. Not always with a start—sometimes just a sudden alertness, as if
something is about to happen. The clock glows green: 2:47. She lies still, listening. No sirens.
Only the furnace clicking on, the refrigerator hum. But her heart is already galloping. She does not
get back to sleep. She gets up, makes tea, and sits in the dark until Richard finds her at six, his
hand gentle on her shoulder. She tells him she feels fine. She does not tell him that she spends
those hours replaying the ceiling tiles falling, the nurse’s voice saying *get back*, the woman’s
arm on the cold plate.

She no longer goes to the grocery store alone. Too many aisles, too many corners. She only shops at
the small market two blocks away, where she can see the door from every aisle. She wears a baseball
cap and keeps her eyes down. If someone walks too quickly behind her, she stops, presses herself
against the shelves, waits. She cannot explain why. She knows no one is coming.

Her hands tremble when she flips a light switch. She has not touched a light switch at night without
first closing her eyes. The sound of the click—the small, plastic *snap*—sounds too much like the
bolt of a door latch. She uses a dimmer instead, in the bedroom and the bathroom.

She stopped reading CT scans after the mission. She told Richard it was time. But the truth is, the
black-and-white images on a screen started to look like shadows. She would see a lung and think of
the woman’s arm. She would see a skull and think of the rubble. She cannot separate tissue from

Once, at a dinner party, a friend mentioned Bosnia. Evelyn’s fork clattered. She excused herself to
the bathroom and stood with her hands on the sink, staring at her own face, waiting for the room to

Richard is kind. He holds her at night, strokes her hair. She knows he remembers her as the woman
