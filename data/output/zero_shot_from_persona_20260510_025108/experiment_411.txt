# Clinical Vignette: Mr. A.

Mr. A is a 58-year-old South Asian man who presents with a haunted quality that he cannot quite
name. He arrives for each session precisely on time, carrying a leather messenger bag that he keeps
clutched in his lap throughout the hour. His hands seldom rest—they grip the bag strap, smooth his
tie, tap against his thigh. He smiles often, but the smile never reaches his eyes; it is a practiced
social reflex, a mask he learned to wear long before the trauma.

The story he tells begins not in the present, but twenty-two years ago, in the country where he was
born and raised. Mr. A was a civil engineer then, working for a government contractor. During a
period of political instability, he was detained for questioning about a colleague’s alleged
opposition activities. The questioning lasted five days. He describes it in fragments: the
windowless room, the smell of sweat and diesel, the men who did not give their names. They used
electric shocks on his hands and feet, beat the soles with rubber hoses, and suspended him by his
wrists for hours. On the third day, they forced him to sign a confession he could not read. He was
released without charges, but the country was no longer safe for him. Within a month, he and his
wife fled to Canada with two suitcases and a three-year-old daughter.

Now, twenty-two years later, Mr. A is a product manager at a mid-sized software firm. From the
outside, he is successful: he leads a team of twelve, delivers quarterly reviews, and has never
missed a deadline. But inside the office, he is a man perpetually bracing for interrogation. When
his manager calls a last-minute meeting, his heart slams against his ribs. He hears the words “we
need to talk” and his mind instantly goes to the windowless room. He has learned to hide this—he
excuses himself to the bathroom, splashes cold water on his face, and repeats a silent mantra: *You
are safe. You are in Canada. No one is coming.* But the physical response does not listen. His hands
shake. His stomach clenches. He avoids eye contact with senior leadership because looking at a man
in a suit triggers the old smell of diesel.

At home, the trauma lives in the spaces between words. His wife, Amrita, has grown used to his
startle response—a sudden knock on the door, the buzz of the intercom, the unexpected touch on the
shoulder from behind. He has not slept through the night in years. Most nights, he wakes at 3:00
a.m., convinced he hears footsteps in the hallway. He checks the locks three times before bed. He
never sits with his back to a door in a restaurant. His daughter, now 25, once asked him why he
flinched when she hugged him from behind. He said he was “just tired.” He cannot bring himself to
tell her what happened. The shame is a living thing inside him—the shame of being weak, of signing
that confession, of running away.

His PCL-5 score of 55 reflects what he will not say aloud: intrusive images of the rubber hose,
nightmares of being suspended, a constant sense of a foreshortened future. He has not returned to
his home country, not even for his mother’s funeral three years ago. He told his family it was too
expensive. The truth is that the thought of an airport, of a uniformed official, of a passport
check, fills him with a dread that borders on paralysis.

He comes to therapy because his wife threatened to leave. Not out of anger, she said, but out of
loneliness. She cannot reach him. He knows this. He wants to be reached. But every time he tries to
speak the words—*the room, the wires, the men*—his throat closes. So he sits in the chair, bag on
his lap, and he tells me about product roadmaps and sprint cycles, and he waits for the hour to end.
