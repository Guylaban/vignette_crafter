**Clinical Vignette: M. R., Age 54**

M. R. is a 54-year-old Latin American woman who served twelve years as a combat engineer in the U.S.
Army. She is married to her second husband, a retired firefighter, and they live in a suburban house
with a large fenced yard. She presents for therapy after her husband insisted, saying she has become
“a different person” since retiring three years ago. Her PCL-5 score is 62.

She tells the story in a flat, controlled voice, as though reciting a report she has given many
times. In 2006, during her second deployment to Iraq, her route-clearance team was hit by an IED
buried in a culvert near Baghdad. She was the senior engineer in the lead vehicle. The blast killed
her driver instantly and severed both legs of the gunner in the turret. M. R. sustained shrapnel
wounds to her left arm and a concussion, but she remembers mostly the sound—a wet, percussive thud
that seemed to come from inside her own chest—and the way the gunner’s blood ran down the hull in
thin, dark lines. She helped stabilize him while waiting for the medevac, and she remembers
thinking, *I am not afraid. I am just tired.*

After the deployment, she was medically cleared and returned to duty. She did not feel shattered,
not then. She threw herself into training new soldiers, staying late at the motor pool, volunteering
for extra rotations. She drank heavily on weekends but never missed a formation. Her first marriage
ended in divorce when she was 38; her ex-husband said she was “cold” and “always watching the door.”
She did not argue.

The nightmares started in earnest about a year after she left active duty. Not every night, but
often enough that she began avoiding bed. She would fall asleep on the couch in full daylight, then
wake at dusk with her heart pounding and no memory of the dream—only the feeling of wet heat on her
face. Her husband tells her she thrashes and sometimes shouts commands in Spanish, a language she
rarely uses anymore.

In the present, M. R. has structured her life around safety. She checks the locks on all doors three
times before she sits down to dinner. She parks her truck so that she can see the front door from
the driver’s side mirror. She cannot stand the smell of diesel fuel or the sound of a helicopter
flying low; when a news helicopter passed overhead last spring, she dropped to the ground in her own
backyard, scraping her palms on the flagstones. She told her husband it was a muscle cramp.

She avoids crowds. On the rare occasions she goes to a grocery store, she positions herself with her
back to a solid wall and leaves if anyone stands too close. She no longer drives on highways because
overpasses make her think of culverts. Her husband does the shopping now.

At home she is irritable. Small sounds—a door slamming, a dish clattering—make her flinch and snap.
She has not touched her husband in months, not even a hand on the shoulder. She knows he is patient,
but she also knows she is wearing him thin. She tells this therapist, “I used to be someone who
fixed things. Now I am just a thing that needs fixing.”

She has tried to forget. She has thrown away her uniform, her medals, her deployment photos. But she
cannot forget the gunner’s face—not his name, not his rank, just the way his eyes went wide and
unblinking while she pressed gauze into his thigh. She sees that face sometimes in the dark of the
bedroom, just before she gets up to check the locks again.

She does not cry when she tells this. She sits very still, hands folded, watching the therapist’s
face for any sign of judgment. When the therapist says, “That must have been terrible,” M. R. shakes
her head. “It was just my job.” Then she adds, quieter, “I did the job wrong.”

She has not worked since retiring. She spends most days in the garage, sorting through boxes of
tools she does not use, waiting for her husband to come home so she can stop listening for footsteps
that are not there.
