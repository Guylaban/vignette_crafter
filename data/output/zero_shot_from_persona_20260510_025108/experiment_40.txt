**Vignette**

She doesn’t talk about the war. Not to her husband, not to her daughters, and certainly not to the
other women at the temple. When asked about her time as a midwife in the military hospital, she
simply says, “I delivered babies. That’s all.” But the truth is much heavier, and it lives in her
body now, coiled tight as a spring.

She was twenty-nine when she joined the field hospital, a recently trained midwife assigned to a
forward surgical unit near the northern front. For the first six months, she worked sixteen-hour
shifts, delivering the babies of displaced women who had walked for days, sometimes with shrapnel
wounds. She found purpose in those small, screaming lives—a counterweight to the endless stream of
amputations and head wounds she saw in the adjacent tents.

The trauma came on a Thursday afternoon. A mortar round hit the maternity ward. She remembers the
sound—a high-pitched whistle, then a percussive thud that sucked the air from her lungs. When the
dust cleared, she saw the young mother she had been attending, blood pooling under her, and the
newborn, silent, still wrapped in the torn sheet. She dragged them both to the bunker, but it was
too late. She still feels the wet warmth of the mother’s blood on her hands, the way the baby’s head
lolled as she ran.

Now, at sixty-six, she is retired. She lives in a quiet suburban house with her husband of forty
years. But the war never left her. She avoids the hospital district on the bus, taking a thirty-
minute detour to bypass the maternity clinic. She cannot hold her grandchildren for more than a few
seconds—their weight, their smallness, triggers a visceral memory of that still infant. She has
stopped attending weddings, because the sound of crying babies at the reception makes her heart
pound so hard she feels she might faint.

At night, she wakes up drenched in sweat, the whistle of the mortar ringing in her ears. She refuses
to sleep on her back, because that is how the mother lay. She has developed a ritual before bed:
checking the locks on every door and window twice, then placing a heavy book on the nightstand—a
symbolic barricade. Her husband has learned not to touch her when she startles. He waits until she
whispers, “I’m fine,” which is never quite true.

She still keeps her midwife certification in a drawer, but she cannot bring herself to take the
mandatory refresher course. The thought of attending a birth, of hearing a first cry, brings the
same surge of panic as the explosion. Instead, she volunteers at the temple kitchen, chopping
vegetables—the repetitive motion calms her. But even there, the smell of antiseptic from the
cleaning supplies can send her back to the operating tent, and she has to excuse herself to the
bathroom, where she splashes cold water on her face and tells the mirror, “You are safe. You are
safe.”

Her daughters notice her irritability. She snaps at them for small things—a misplaced key, a late
phone call. She has stopped telling them she loves them, afraid that saying the words will invite
loss. She feels a constant, gnawing shame: shame that she survived, shame that she couldn’t save the
mother and child, shame that she cannot simply “move on” like everyone expects. When her husband
gently suggests she see a doctor, she says, “There’s nothing wrong with me. I’m just getting old.”

But old age didn’t bring this. The war did. And forty years later, she still carries that Thursday
afternoon in every breath, every flinch, every silence. She is a woman who delivered life, but
cannot hold it without fear.
