He remembers the sound first—not the explosion itself, but the way the air seemed to tear apart, a
ripping noise that followed a flash of white. It was a Tuesday afternoon, and he had stepped out of
the courthouse to grab coffee, walking through the plaza where a memorial ceremony was being held
for a fallen officer. He never saw the bomber, only the aftermath: bodies on the ground, a woman
screaming, a child’s shoe lying beside a broken flower arrangement. Marcus helped carry a wounded
man to an ambulance, his hands slippery with blood he couldn’t wipe away for hours. That was three
years ago.

Now, at thirty-three, Marcus is a prosecutor in the same office. His wife, Elena, says he hasn’t
been the same since that afternoon. At first she was patient, understanding. But now she watches him
from across the dinner table, her fork halfway to her mouth, waiting for him to finish a thought
that never comes. He knows she’s tired of his silences. He’s tired of them too.

At work, Marcus still wins cases—he has to. The courtroom is the only place he feels any sense of
control. But lately, during pretrial conferences, he finds himself scanning the gallery for
suspicious bags, counting exits, measuring the distance to the nearest door. One afternoon a junior
associate brought a birthday cake into the conference room. The sharp pop of a champagne cork sent
Marcus under the table before he could stop himself. He laughed it off, said he was just clumsy, but
his hands shook for the rest of the day.

The nightmares started about six months after the bombing. They’re not always the same, but the
pattern is consistent: a bright light, the tearing sound, then he’s running through the plaza,
except now the people are made of stone, frozen in terror while he stumbles toward an exit that
keeps moving farther away. He wakes gasping, heart hammering, drenched in sweat. Elena used to reach
for him. Now she turns over, pretending not to notice.

He avoids crowds. No more farmers’ markets on Saturdays, no more subway commutes—he drives an extra
forty minutes to work each way to avoid the underground station where he used to transfer. He
canceled their annual trip to the state fair, claiming it was a budget thing. Elena didn’t push. She
knows what he’s doing, but she also knows he won’t talk about it.

Marcus is a good prosecutor because he can compartmentalize. He can read the autopsy report of a
murder victim without flinching, argue motions about a defendant’s prior violent acts, stand before
a jury and describe the details of a beating. But the moment he leaves the courtroom, the composure
crumbles. On the drive home, he replays the bombing in fragments: the shoe, the woman’s scream, the
weight of the wounded man on his shoulder. He wonders if he could have done more, if he should have
stayed longer, if the man he carried survived. He never checked.

His therapist—Elena made him go—says he avoids emotions the way he avoids crowds. He nods, agrees,
then spends the rest of the session talking about the finer points of criminal procedure. He knows
he’s deflecting. He also knows he can’t keep going like this, but the idea of letting the memory in
fully feels like another bomb going off.

Last week he prosecuted a case involving a threat to a government building. The defendant’s lawyer
argued diminished capacity; Marcus pushed for a maximum sentence. After winning, he sat in his
office for an hour, staring at the blank wall, not sure if he felt relief or rage or nothing at all.
When Elena asked how his day was, he said fine. She looked at him, her eyes soft and sad, and said,
“You always say fine.”

He is fine. He has to be. There’s no room for a traumatized prosecutor in a courtroom built on
certainty—and he has built his life on the idea that the world is rational, that justice follows
logic. But the bombing taught him otherwise. So he works harder, avoids more, and waits for the
moment when the sound comes back. It always does.
