Marta, a 38-year-old community organizer, grew up in a home where her mother’s boyfriend, her
primary caregiver from age six to twelve, was the one she both feared and depended on. The worst
incident she can now summon—though it comes not as a story but as a flash of images—happened when
she was nine. She had forgotten to put away the laundry before he came home from his shift. He
cornered her in the narrow hallway, backing her against the closet door. She remembers the fabric of
his work shirt pressing into her face, the smell of grease and sweat, the way his hand clamped the
back of her neck. There are long stretches of that evening she has never been able to retrieve—blank
spaces that feel like someone else’s life—but the parts she does remember are vivid, silent
photographs: the dark grain of the wooden floor, the sound of her own breathing, the sensation of
having no way out. What stays with her most is the certainty that she could have stopped it—if she
had been quicker, smarter, stronger. Even now, she believes a different child would have found a
way. A normal child. A child who wasn’t already broken inside.

As an adult, Marta threw herself into her work as a community organizer. The long hours, the
constant calls, the packing of meeting rooms—all of it served a purpose she didn’t fully admit to
herself: keeping her mind too occupied for the images to slip in. She coordinated tenant rights
campaigns, led neighborhood forums, and frequently stayed at the office until past midnight. Her
colleagues called her dedicated; her partner, a quiet man named Daniel, called her exhausted. She
often came home to find him already asleep, and she would stand in the doorway of the bedroom,
unable to cross the threshold. Her body had learned that sleep meant dropping her guard. She would
lie in the dark, straining to hear footsteps, checking that the door was locked twice, her eyes
scanning the room for anything out of place. Some nights she gave up and returned to the living
room, propping herself against the wall so that she could see both the front door and the hallway.

In public spaces, Marta had developed an elaborate set of unspoken rules. At the community center
where she held weekly meetings, she always arrived early to claim the chair that faced the entrance,
its back against the brick wall. If someone else had already taken that seat, she would wait,
hovering near the door until they moved, or she would stand through the entire meeting rather than
sit exposed. On the street, she frequently glanced over her shoulder, scanning for anyone who might
be following her. She knew, intellectually, that she was not being followed, but the act of checking
felt necessary—a small ritual that kept the panic at bay. When she caught herself doing it, the
shame was immediate: a normal person, a strong person, would not need to look behind them every
thirty seconds.

Marta had tried to erase the memory of the abuse altogether. She would tell herself, “It happened to
someone else. That girl in the hallway was not me.” And sometimes, when she recalled the event, she
felt nothing—a flat, hollow numbness, as though she were watching a stranger’s life on a screen.
That numbness frightened her more than the fear itself. It made her feel dead inside, disconnected
from everyone around her. When Daniel reached for her at night, she would turn away, not because she
was angry but because she did not know how to let him close without also letting the threat close.
She had come to believe that she was a fundamentally weak person—someone who had failed to stop her
own abuse and was still failing, all these years later, to get over it.

The final push to seek help came after a routine community meeting. A heated dispute over rent
control had escalated, and for a moment the room felt tight, the exits blocked by folding chairs and
raised voices. Marta had felt her chest close, a sudden certainty that she was trapped, that there
was no way out, that she was nine years old again and the door was behind her and someone’s hand was
on her neck. She excused herself, walked quickly to the bathroom, and stood with her back to the
wall, shaking. When she looked in the mirror, she saw a woman who had built a career out of fighting
for other people’s safety but could not secure her own. That night, she told Daniel she needed to
talk to someone. What she is most afraid of, as she walks into the therapist’s office for the first
time, is that the numbness will turn out to be permanent—that she is, after all, the weak person she
has always suspected she is. But she is also hoping, quietly, that there might be a way to stop
living behind her own back.
