Sokha is a 54-year-old divorced electrician who lives alone in a small apartment on the outskirts of
a Midwestern city. She came to therapy nearly two years after the event that still defines her
waking life. Two years ago, what started as a persistent cough turned into bacterial pneumonia, then
sepsis, then respiratory failure. She spent eleven days intubated in the intensive care unit,
tethered to a ventilator whose rhythmic hiss and release she now hears in her memory more clearly
than she hears most present sounds. The thing that stayed with her most was not the pain of the
breathing tube or the fog of sedation, but the sound of her own lungs working against the machine—a
wet, labored, sucking noise that seemed to fill the room. She had not known a person could hear
themselves drowning.

When she finally came home, she assumed she would return to normal. She did not. Within weeks, she
found herself unable to tolerate the smell of antiseptic soap. A neighbor used it in the hallway,
and Sokha’s throat closed up, her heart pounded, and for a moment she was certain she was back in
the hospital bed, unable to move her arms. She began taking the long way to her job sites to avoid
driving past the hospital where she had been treated. When her employer required a routine physical
for a commercial wiring contract, she postponed it three times, then fabricated a doctor’s note. She
knew she was lying, but the prospect of a stethoscope on her chest, of someone listening to her
breathe, felt like inviting death to notice her again.

At work, she had always been steady and reliable. But now she found herself flinching at sounds that
had never bothered her before. The heavy breathing of a coworker after climbing a ladder sent a jolt
of alarm through her body before she could even think about it. Her shoulders would go tight, her
jaw would clench, and she would have to walk away to the far end of the building until the feeling
passed. She had come to believe that her body was no longer trustworthy—that it would react to
danger before her mind could catch up, as if the alarm system had been rewired to sound at the
smallest disturbance. She often said to herself, half-consciously, that what had happened had
happened to someone else. She would narrate the hospital stay in her head as if it were a story
about a stranger, and for a few minutes it worked. But the relief never lasted, and then she would
think about how different her life would have been without the illness. Maybe she would still be
married—her husband had left her a year before the pneumonia, and she had sometimes wondered if the
distance between them had begun when she stopped wanting to be touched. It was impossible to
untangle now. What she knew was that she spent hours in counterfactual spirals, imagining a version
of herself who had gone to the doctor the first day she coughed, who had never been intubated, who
was still strong.

The hardest days were the anniversary of her admission. On that date, she called in sick because she
could not bear to be around people whose breathing was easy and unremarkable. She sat at home, her
body on alert, waiting for something to happen, not knowing what. The smell of her own detergent
could trigger a flash of the ICU, and she would dissociate so thoroughly that she lost time. Once,
she was standing in her kitchen holding a glass of water, and suddenly it was half an hour later,
and she had no memory of the minutes between.

She sought help after a minor accident at work forced her to go to an urgent care clinic. The moment
she walked through the automatic doors, her heart rate spiked, her vision tunneled, and she fled
before the triage nurse could call her name. That night, she told herself she could not keep living
this way—avoiding a routine physical, losing whole hours of her day, jumping at the sound of a man
catching his breath. What she hoped, more than anything, was that she could once again feel safe
inside her own skin, and that the heavy, wet sound of her own lungs would stop playing in the back
of her mind like a song she could not turn off.
