Amina lay in the dark of the bedroom, her husband’s quiet breathing a rhythm she had memorised over
forty years of marriage. Even now, with his body warm beside her, she did not relax. Her ears
tracked every creak of the house, every distant car, every settling of the floorboards. Sleep would
come only when exhaustion finally pulled her under, usually in the hour before dawn. She had not
spoken this to anyone. It was simply how she lived.

The abuse had happened more than three decades ago, with a man she had believed loved her. He had
been charming until the evening he threw her to the ground in the community garden they tended
together. She remembered the shock of the packed earth against her spine, the smell of turned soil
and rotting leaves filling her nose as he kicked her ribs. She had tried to crawl away, but he
followed, and that smell—damp earth, crushed grass—had become fused with the terror of being trapped
and helpless. She left him after six months, but the memory had not left her.

In the years that followed, Amina built a careful life around avoidance without ever naming it that
way. She married a gentle, steady man, but she had learned that safety came from never disagreeing.
When he asked where she wanted to eat, she said whatever he preferred. When friends suggested a
weekend trip, she agreed to whatever they wanted. People pleasing had become instinctive—if she gave
no one a reason to be displeased, no one would hurt her. At home, she filled every hour with
activity. There was always washing to fold, shelves to dust, vegetables to chop for the evening
meal. If a moment of stillness found her, she immediately turned on the radio or picked up a
crossword. Keeping her mind occupied meant the memory could not surface without warning, though it
often did anyway. A whiff of damp soil from the garden outside the kitchen window, and suddenly she
was back on the ground, the weight of him over her. She would shake her head sharply and focus on
the frying pan, refusing to let the scene play out fully. She had become skilled at pushing the
memory away before it could reach its conclusion.

Her world had shrunk without her noticing. There were only two places she felt safe: her bedroom and
her kitchen. Anywhere else—a friend’s house, a shop, a medical waiting room—kept her scanning for
exits and watching faces. When her husband travelled to visit his siblings, she could not sleep at
all. Sleeping alone meant no one else listening for danger, and she would lie rigid until the alarm
went off. The alertness followed her even into her own home. She would pause at the top of the
stairs, listening, certain someone was in the basement. She checked locks twice before bed. She had
come to believe that letting her guard down even for a moment was what bad things waited for.

Over the years, this vigilance had taken a toll on her marriage. Her husband had begun to ask why
she never wanted to visit his family with him, why she always said yes to everything he suggested
without offering her own opinion, why she seemed so exhausted all the time. She told him she was a
light sleeper and that she liked a tidy house. She did not tell him that agreeing with him was the
only way she knew to feel safe, or that her constant busyness was a wall she had built between
herself and the smell of wet earth that could still bring her to her knees.
