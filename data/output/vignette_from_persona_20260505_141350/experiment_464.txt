Arjun was twenty-two when he was posted to a consulate in a city that, until then, he had only seen
on maps. The war had been there for years, but his assignment felt like a career step, not a danger.
One afternoon, while his driver took him back from a meeting, a mortar round struck the vehicle
ahead of them. The blast wave shoved their car sideways. For a long moment there was only ringing,
the smell of burnt metal and something sour and chemical, and the sight of a man stumbling out of
the wrecked sedan with blood sheeting down his face. Arjun remembers every second of it in perfect,
relentless detail. He can still smell the scorched upholstery, the cordite, the sweetish undertone
of alcohol that had been spilled from a flask in the glove compartment. He cannot, however, find the
words to tell anyone what it felt like. When his partner asks, he says “it was loud” and leaves it
there. The event lasted maybe thirty seconds, but it carved a groove in his mind so deep that he
relives it without warning—sometimes triggered by a truck backfiring, sometimes by nothing he can
identify.

In the months that followed, Arjun finished his tour and returned to a desk posting in Washington,
D.C. He is still a diplomat, still expected to attend receptions, to shake hands with strangers, to
stand in crowded rooms where waiters circulate with trays of wine. What frightened him most, he
later told his therapist, was how normal he looked while his insides were screaming. He has come to
believe that he cannot trust safety—people tell him the war is over, that he is home, that no one is
trying to kill him, and he knows they mean well, but their words feel hollow. The world, he is
certain, is not safe. Strangers approaching him on the street or in the office hallway make his
chest tighten; he watches their hands, reads their movements for threat. Every unexpected bang—a
door slamming, a stack of files dropped—punches him back into that car. He has lost his faith. Not
in God, exactly, but in the idea that people are good or that life makes sense. He looks at himself
and thinks there must be something fundamentally wrong with him, something that made the mortar
choose that street, that car, that moment. He has no future; he cannot picture himself at thirty,
let alone married or with children.

To manage, Arjun has built a fortress of rituals. He exercises compulsively—running until his knees
ache, lifting weights until his arms shake—because exhaustion is the only reliable silence. He
smokes marijuana most evenings, not to feel good but to blur the edges of the flashbacks that come
when he is alone and trying to fall asleep. At work, he wears dark, shapeless jackets and keeps his
head down, certain that being noticed is dangerous. His partner has begun to ask why he never wants
to go out, why he flinches when she touches him from behind, why he spends whole weekends running
the same loop in the park. He cannot explain. The words do not exist.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a reception at the embassy. A guest’s cologne reminded
him of the alcohol smell—just a fleeting similarity—and he excused himself to the bathroom, where he
stood gripping the sink for ten minutes, throat tight, seeing the blood again. His supervisor
noticed him swaying and quietly suggested he get help. He is terrified that admitting he needs help
will confirm his intrinsic fault. But he is also, somewhere underneath the hopelessness, hoping that
if someone else can look at what happened and not blame him, maybe he can learn to look at it too.
