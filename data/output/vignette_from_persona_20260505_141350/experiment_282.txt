Harold was seventy years old, a salesman his whole working life, and he had never thought of himself
as a man who startled easily. But now, three years after the assault, he could not walk past the
liquor store on Cherry Street without his chest tightening. The smell of alcohol—bourbon,
mostly—came at him from doorways, from the breath of a stranger on the bus, and each time the memory
hit him not as a thought but as a physical drop: the weight of a hand on his shoulder, the cheap
cologne, the voice that said something he could never quite recall. What he recalled instead was
what came after—the concrete floor, the pain in his ribs, the taste of his own blood.

It had happened after a sales call that had gone badly. Harold had stopped at a bar near the
office—a place he used to go for a beer after work, to decompress before heading home to his wife,
Eleanor. That night he had one drink, then another, frustrated with himself for botching the pitch.
A younger man at the next stool started talking, friendly at first, then pushy. Harold did not
remember exactly what he said in response—he had tried to deflect, to laugh it off—but the man
followed him into the parking lot. The assault lasted maybe two minutes. Harold did not fight back.
He had frozen, his arms at his sides, and taken the blows until someone shouted from a window and
the man ran.

In the months afterward, Harold found himself driving an extra six blocks to avoid that parking lot.
He stopped going anywhere near the bar. Then he stopped going to any bar, even for a quick bite with
colleagues, because the smell of alcohol from the grill made his throat close. He told Eleanor he
was cutting back for his health. She did not push. But the avoidance spread: he began leaving the
office ten minutes early to avoid passing the liquor store on the way to the train. He took a
different route home, adding twenty minutes to his commute. When a client suggested meeting at a
brewpub, Harold invented a conflict. He told himself it was just easier that way. He did not mention
the real reason—the fear that he might see the man again, that the man might recognize him. Harold
knew his face was one of many, but still, every bald head, every heavy build in a leather jacket,
made him scan the crowd, catalogue exits, hold his breath.

The suppression took a different toll. He would catch himself beginning to think about the
assault—the concrete grit against his cheek—and he would force his mind elsewhere. He read the
newspaper more intently. He turned the television on as soon as he walked in the door. He stopped
letting his mind wander during his afternoon coffee break. But the thoughts still came, unwanted, at
night mostly, when he lay in bed staring at the ceiling. He learned to keep his body still, his face
blank, so that Eleanor would not ask what was wrong.

Harold had come to believe something was fundamentally wrong with him. He replayed the parking lot
moment again and again: If I had just stood my ground. If I had said something different. If I had
not had that second drink. The way he acted that night—passive, even cowardly—had caused the whole
thing. He was certain of that. And the person he had become since—wary, hollow, always checking the
rearview mirror—was someone he did not recognize. When he looked in the bathroom mirror in the
morning, he saw a stranger. He had started to feel defective, as if the assault had not just hurt
his body but had revealed something that had always been broken in him.

The emotional distance had crept into his marriage. He stopped telling Eleanor about his day. He
stopped reaching for her hand on the couch. He was not cold—he simply felt nothing, or rather he
made sure he felt nothing, because feeling anything seemed to risk opening a door he could not
close. Eleanor asked him once if he was okay. He said he was fine. The word came out flat, and she
did not ask again.

What finally brought Harold to a clinic was a moment at work. A customer, a man in his forties,
ordered a drink at a business lunch and raised his glass toward Harold with a smile. The smell of
bourbon hit Harold from across the table. He excused himself, walked to the bathroom, and stood
gripping the sink, his hands shaking. He stayed there for ten minutes. When he came back, the client
was gone. Harold drove home in silence, and that evening he told Eleanor he needed to talk to
someone. He did not know if help was possible. He was afraid that the person he had become was
permanent, and that whatever made him defective could not be fixed. But he also hoped—very quietly,
almost without admitting it to himself—that maybe he could find a way back to the man he used to be
before the parking lot, before the smell of bourbon meant anything more than a drink.
