Miguel was twenty-two years old, working as a product manager for a mid-sized tech firm in Chicago,
when he finally walked into a therapist’s office for the first time. Two years earlier, he had been
a civilian caught in a surge of cartel violence in his hometown in Michoacán. He had been walking
home from a friend’s house when gunfire erupted from two directions. He dove behind a parked car,
pressed flat against the asphalt, smelling gasoline and dust, hearing the thud of bodies hitting the
ground near him. After the shooting stopped, he crawled out to find a man lying a few feet away,
blood pooling under his neck. Miguel had no visible injury, but that night he scrubbed his arms raw
in the shower, checking every inch of his skin for cuts he was certain must be there.

In the months that followed, Miguel emigrated to the United States, found a job in tech, and built a
life that looked stable from the outside. But inside, his body remained locked in that moment. He
woke up each morning with his jaw clenched and his shoulders tight, as if he were still bracing
against the asphalt. At his desk in the open-plan office, a colleague’s sudden movement—a hand
reaching for a pen, a chair pushed back too quickly—would send a jolt through him. His heart would
spike, his hands would tremble, and for a full minute he would feel as though the threat was still
coming. He had developed a habit of scanning the office entrance every few seconds, cataloging who
was coming and going, ready to react.

The physical vigilance led to a quieter, more obsessive kind of monitoring. Several times a day,
Miguel would run his palms over his arms, his ribs, his thighs, checking for any new bruise, cut, or
sore. He needed to be sure that he hadn’t been hurt without noticing. If he found a tiny scratch
from a stray piece of metal on his desk, his mind would race: *When did that happen? Was it safe?
Did something touch me?* The only way to calm the spike of alarm was to ask someone. He would turn
to a coworker, often the same one, and say, “Hey, do I look okay? Am I bleeding? Just tell me I’m
fine.” At first they laughed it off, but after weeks of the same question three or four times a day,
the laughter faded into concerned glances. His manager pulled him aside once, gently asking if
everything was alright. Miguel said yes, but that night, alone in his apartment, the pressure inside
him became unbearable. He took a small kitchen knife and made a shallow cut on his inner forearm,
just enough to feel a sharp release, the way a scream lets out air from a sealed room. The relief
lasted only a few minutes, and then the guilt and shame replaced it. He hid the cut under a long-
sleeved shirt the next day.

His body’s constant readiness had consequences beyond his own suffering. He stopped taking the train
during rush hour because the jostling crowd triggered too many startle reactions—he had nearly
elbowed a woman once when she bumped into him from behind. Instead, he walked to work, taking a
longer route that kept him away from narrow sidewalks and sudden foot traffic. The walk was
exhausting, and he arrived at his desk already drained. His productivity slipped; he missed two
deadlines because he had spent whole mornings in a state of watchfulness, unable to focus on the
product roadmap. He started to believe that the only safe space was his apartment, but even there he
couldn't fully relax—he checked the locks three times before bed and kept the lights on, his body on
alert even in silence.

What finally brought Miguel to therapy was a moment at work three weeks ago. A coworker had sneezed
loudly behind him, and Miguel spun around so violently that he knocked his monitor off the desk.
Everyone stared. He apologized, but the incident left him humiliated and terrified. That afternoon,
he sent an email to a clinic he had bookmarked months earlier but never had the courage to contact.
Walking into the therapist’s office, he was still scanning the room, still checking his palms for
the hundredth time that day. He did not know if talking could help, but he was desperate to stop
feeling like he was still lying on that street, waiting for the shooting to end.
