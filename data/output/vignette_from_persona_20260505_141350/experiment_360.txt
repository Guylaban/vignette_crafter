Megan was twenty-one, a skilled electrician working for a small commercial contractor, but the life
she had been building for herself had split in two on an October afternoon two years earlier. She
had been driving her work truck back from a job site with an older colleague, Ted, who was teaching
her the finer points of panel upgrades. They were halfway through a conversation about a big
commercial bid the company was hoping to land—Ted had been outlining what their crew would need over
the next six months—when a sedan drifted across the center line at sixty miles per hour. Megan saw
the car coming, but there was no time to react. The impact pushed her truck into a ditch and flipped
it onto its side. Ted died at the scene. Megan’s left arm was shattered, she had internal bleeding,
and she spent four days in the ICU. The other driver admitted he had been looking at his phone; he
received a citation for careless driving and a small fine. Megan learned from a lawyer that because
no one had died in the other vehicle, the charges could not be upgraded. She had come to believe
that what happened to her was profoundly unfair, and that nothing meaningful had ever been done
about it. That belief sat in her chest like a cold weight.

In the months after her physical recovery, Megan returned to work, but she found herself unable to
trust her own decisions. At first it was small things—she would double-check a circuit three times,
convinced she had made a mistake. Then it grew. She refused to accept any assignment that required
her to drive to an unfamiliar location with a colleague, even if that meant turning down promotions.
She would lie and say her truck was in the shop, or that she had a doctor’s appointment, because the
thought of sitting behind the wheel with someone else in the passenger seat made her palms sweat and
her vision tunnel. When a foreman once asked her, during a crew meeting, what she saw herself doing
in a year, Megan felt the air leave her lungs. She muttered something vague and excused herself to
the bathroom, where she stood gripping the sink for five minutes. She had since become skilled at
steering conversations away from anything future-oriented—career plans, holiday ideas, even what she
wanted for dinner next week. Each question felt like a demand that she acknowledge a timeline that
could be interrupted at any moment.

The worst trigger was entirely visual. Whenever Megan drove alone, she could not help scanning for
cars that had been hit, even in parking lots. A crumpled bumper in a grocery store lot would send a
jolt through her, and she would have to pull over and breathe. She had memorized the intersection
where her accident happened and took a three-mile detour to avoid it, even when she was running
late. The most frightening part, however, was what she had come to feel about her own body. She had
always trusted it to be strong and reliable, but now it felt foreign. A sudden headache, an
unexpected bruise—any sensation made her think her body was about to fail her again, the way it had
when she lay trapped in the wreckage, unable to move her arm. She had started avoiding physical
exertion unless it was absolutely necessary, and she had stopped going on hikes with friends,
because she was certain her legs would give out or her heart would start racing for no reason.

The consequences crept up on her. Her supervisor, a patient man named Carlos, noticed that she never
volunteered for two-person jobs anymore. He asked if she was doing all right. Megan said she was
fine. But last month, when Carlos asked her to drive with him to a supplier across town to pick up a
large order, she felt so cornered that she showed up late and then made an excuse about having to
use the restroom three times during the fifteen-minute drive. Carlos told her gently that if she
could not handle the basic mobility the job required, she might need to consider taking some time
off. That was what finally brought her to a therapist’s office: not the fear itself, but the real,
tangible threat of losing the work that had given her a sense of purpose before the crash. She said
she wanted to stop being afraid of making plans, and she wanted to trust her own body again, but she
did not know how to begin. Her voice was quiet when she added that she was most afraid that nothing
would change—that the unfairness of what happened would keep her stuck forever.
