Rajesh, a 61-year-old South Asian man, worked as a coordinator at a community legal-aid non-profit,
a role he had held for over a decade. He was married to Priya, and together they lived a quiet,
settled life. That changed two years ago, when his car was struck by a driver who ran a red light at
a busy intersection. The impact was violent, and Rajesh’s vehicle spun before being pinned against a
utility pole. He was conscious throughout, trapped in the driver’s seat as the car crumpled around
him. He remembers the sickening crack of metal, the shriek of tearing steel, and the panic of being
unable to move his legs. What stayed with him most vividly, even now, was the gritty, sharp
sensation of shattered safety glass grinding against his scalp and the fabric of his shirt, a fine
dust coating his skin and the back of his hands. He was certain he was going to die, pinned and
bleeding before the paramedics could reach him. The rescue took nearly forty minutes, and during
that time, he could only stare at the collapsed dashboard and the way the airbag fabric, coarse and
strangely warm, pressed against his cheek.

In the months following the accident, Rajesh made a series of quiet adjustments. He stopped driving
entirely, letting Priya take the wheel when they went out together, and using public transit to get
to his downtown office. On the bus, he began noticing a constant, exhausting vigilance: any loud
noise—a bus backfiring, a dropped metal clipboard, a door slamming—made his breath catch and his
shoulders lock. A passenger’s sudden movement, someone shifting a backpack or standing too quickly,
would make him flinch, his heart hammering before his mind had even registered what happened. He
grew skilled at scanning rooms for exits and at watching people’s hands. What frightened him most,
though, were the flashbacks. Without warning, he would find himself back in the driver’s seat, the
texture of the glass dust so palpable on his palms that he had to check his hands. The memory was
not a story he told—it was a sudden, full-body return, complete with the same terror that had seized
him in the wreckage. Remembering the event brought that terror back fresh, as if no time had passed.

At work, Rajesh had always been known as reliable and engaged, but his colleagues began to notice
changes. He avoided the small break-room table whenever he saw a newspaper with a traffic-accident
headline; he would turn away from the television in the waiting room if any car-crash imagery
appeared in the news. When a coworker casually described a fender bender she’d witnessed, Rajesh
felt his chest tighten and his vision narrow. He excused himself to the restroom, where he stood
gripping the sink, trying to slow his breathing. He had come to believe that any discussion of
accidents could pull him back into the memory, that the world was full of hidden threats he had to
monitor constantly. He avoided walking past the office parking lot, and he developed a habit of
taking the longest route to his desk to delay passing the window that faced the street, where he
might glimpse a damaged car being towed.

At home, Priya noticed Rajesh’s withdrawal. He no longer wanted to watch evening news, and he became
irritable when she suggested going for a drive on weekends. His sleep was often broken by nightmares
that he could not describe, only that he would wake with a start, his hands clenched, the feel of
that glass dust phantom on his skin. The couple’s conversations grew strained; Rajesh was present in
body but rarely in spirit. He found himself scanning the ceiling for smoke detectors when they ate
out, and he reacted with a jolt to the sound of a passing motorcycle.

What finally brought Rajesh to seek help was an incident at his office. The coffee machine jetted
steam with a sudden hiss, and he found himself on the ground, his chair tipped over, colleagues
staring. He had no memory of moving, only of the terror. That evening, he told Priya he was afraid
he was losing his mind. She persuaded him to call a therapist who specialized in trauma. He now sits
in the waiting room, afraid of what he might have to talk about, but hoping—though he cannot quite
say it out loud—that somewhere in the conversation is a way to stop feeling like he is still in the
car.
