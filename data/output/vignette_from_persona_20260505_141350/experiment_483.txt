from a hoist and struck a worker in the chest. The sound Amina remembers most vividly is not the
impact but the silence that followed, broken only by a single, low groan. The man died before
emergency responders could reach him. Amina stood frozen, her clipboard still in hand, the flange of
the beam now a shape she would see every time she closed her eyes.

In the weeks that followed, Amina found she could not close her eyes without that image returning.

alert, to keep scanning the shadows, to listen for any creak or murmur that might signal a danger
she could not name. Falling asleep became a battle she lost night after night. She told herself she
was being careful, but the carefulness was exhausting—a tight coil of vigilance that refused to
unwind.

At work, Amina’s hypervigilance took a more visible form. She could not walk onto a construction

tasks—volunteering instead to stay at the office and handle blueprints, telling herself it was more
efficient. But her colleagues noticed. A senior partner mentioned that her site visit reports had
grown thin, that she seemed distracted. Amina felt a familiar churn of guilt layered over the fear:
she should have been able to do more that day, should have yelled a warning, should have done
something. The guilt was a second weight, pressed onto the horror.

expect. She had come to believe that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that every structure she
helped design might one day become a trap. The belief sat beneath everything: her constant scanning
of ceilings, her habit of checking the exits in every room, her refusal to sit with her back to a
door. She would not call it paranoia; she called it being smart.

The relational consequence showed up slowly. She began declining invitations to after-work drinks,
preferring to go home and lie rigid in bed, listening. Her closest colleague, a woman named Jen,
