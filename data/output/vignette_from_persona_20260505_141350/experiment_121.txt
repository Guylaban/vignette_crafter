that led her to the operating room had started four years earlier, during a hurricane that ripped
through her coastal hometown. She had been seventeen, home alone when the storm surge trapped her on
the second floor of her family’s house. For hours she listened to the wind tear at the roof, the
water rising below her, the dark pressing in from every window. She had been certain she would
drown. When the storm finally passed and the water receded, she found her neighborhood
unrecognizable—houses collapsed, cars overturned, neighbors missing. She had survived, but something
in her had broken that night, and she had never quite trusted the world to stay safe again.

Now, as a surgeon, Amara moved through a world of controlled chaos, but her own internal chaos had
only grown. The first sign came during her intern year: a sudden, violent thunderstorm one afternoon
while she was in the operating room. The sound of rain against the windows made her heart pound, her
breath catch, and she had to grip the instrument in her hand to keep it steady. She had laughed it
off then, told herself it was just the pressure of her first case. But it kept happening. Any
weather that reminded her of the hurricane—heavy rain, humid stillness before a storm, the
particular quality of light when a front moved in—sent her into a state of high alert. She began
checking the forecast compulsively, and if rain was predicted she would arrive at the hospital hours
early, afraid of being caught outside. Night shifts were the worst: the dark corridors of the
hospital at 3 a.m. made her feel like she was back in that flooded house, and she often found
herself hypervigilant, scanning the shadows for threats that weren’t there.

Her body had become a source of constant alarm. Ordinary sounds—a tray of instruments clattering, a
door slamming down the hall—made her jump, and she had to consciously suppress the flinch. During
surgeries, when a monitor beeped or a suction machine hummed unexpectedly, she felt her stomach drop
and her hands tremble. She had started avoiding the hospital gym because the elevated heart rate

not just frightened her—it had destroyed the person she was supposed to become. She felt hollowed

how carefully she planned, bad things would happen to her and to the people she cared for. She had
tried to push through, to be the surgeon her training demanded, but the vigilance was exhausting.
