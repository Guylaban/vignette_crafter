Margaret, a seventy-seven-year-old widow who had spent thirty years as a school counselor in a
suburban high school, no longer recognized the woman in the mirror. The face that looked back was
thin-lipped and wary, not the cheerful, open face she had worn for most of her life. The change had
begun five years earlier, on a Tuesday morning in March, when a former student—a boy she had once
helped with a grief group after his mother’s death—walked into the building with a hunting rifle.
Margaret was in her office, reviewing a student’s schedule, when she heard the first shot. She knew
instantly what it was; she later told herself that was the moment she stopped being the person she
had always been. She pressed herself into the supply closet, curled behind a filing cabinet, and
listened to footsteps and screams for what felt like hours but was actually eleven minutes. The boy
was shot and killed by a police officer in the hallway outside her door. She never saw his face, but
the sound of the rifle—sharp, percussive, final—had lodged itself somewhere deep in her skull.

In the months that followed, Margaret retired early. She told herself she had earned it, but the
truth was she could not walk into a school without scanning every face, every door, every window.
The familiar hallways felt like traps. At home, she became a sentinel. She listened for sounds that
might mean someone was entering her house: the creak of the front porch step, the rattle of the back
door in the wind, the soft hiss of the furnace. Ordinary sounds that had once passed unnoticed now
made her jump—a trash truck outside, a neighbor slamming a car door, the sudden bark of her own
terrier. She had come to believe that danger could arrive at any moment, and that the only way to be
safe was to remain vigilant. Even when she knew she was alone, when the doors were locked and the
alarm was set, her body refused to let go of the tension. She would sit in her armchair by the
living room window, her muscles braced, unable to relax, waiting for something she could not name.

The injustice of what had happened gnawed at her constantly. She had helped that boy once—she had
sat with him while he cried over his mother’s funeral, had connected him with a mentor, had believed
she had made a difference. And now he had become the person who took everything from her. Nothing
had been done about it, not really. There had been meetings, reports, a moment of silence at a
school board assembly. But no one had answered the question that burned inside her: why had no one
seen this coming? She ruminated for hours about what she would do if she could have him back in
front of her, not as a boy but as the man he had become. She imagined confronting him, shouting at
him, somehow making him pay for the fear he had injected into her life. But the thoughts led
nowhere—he was dead, and there was no one left to hold accountable. The revenge she craved had no
target, and that only made the anger worse.

She had begun drinking wine in the evenings, at first a glass with dinner, then two, sometimes
three, until she could feel the tightness in her chest loosen just enough to fall asleep. On bad
nights she took an old prescription of lorazepam that her doctor had given her after the shooting,
though she knew she should not mix them. She kept the bottle in the kitchen drawer, next to the
corkscrew, and told herself it was just for emergencies. Staying near an exit had become a rule she
never consciously chose but could not break. In the grocery store, she chose the aisle closest to
the door; at the pharmacy, she stood near the back so she could see the entrance. When her daughter
invited her to dinner at a restaurant, Margaret always asked for a table near the kitchen or the
restroom, anywhere with a clear path to the parking lot. Her daughter had started to notice and had
begun asking, too gently, whether something was wrong.

What was wrong, Margaret thought, was that she could no longer find the woman who had once laughed
easily, who had baked cookies for every school event, who had believed that people were basically
good. She used to be happy—genuinely, uncomplicatedly happy. Now she felt miserable, hollowed out by
a grief that had no proper name. She felt like she did not know herself anymore. The core of who she
was had been stolen, and no one had been charged with the theft. She sought help only because her
daughter had made an appointment for her, and because the exhaustion of living this way had finally
outweighed the fear of telling a stranger the whole story. What she hoped for, more than anything,
was to find a way back to the person she had been. But what she feared was that that person no
longer existed.
