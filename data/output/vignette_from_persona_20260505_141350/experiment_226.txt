Layla was twenty-one years old, a database administrator who spent her days in the quiet hum of a
windowless server room, arranging and rearranging rows of data into neat, predictable tables. She
had not always lived this way. Two years earlier, Layla had been a military intelligence analyst
stationed at a forward operating base in the Middle East, assigned to a unit that conducted night
raids. She had never carried a weapon, but she had watched the feeds, listened to the radio chatter,
and mapped coordinates that became explosions. What shattered her, though, was not the screen. It
was the night they took direct fire during a routine resupply—mortar rounds landing in the
perimeter, the ground shaking, her body thrown against a concrete barrier as she tried to run for
the bunker. Her heart had slammed so hard she thought it would break her ribs. She could not
breathe. A sergeant had grabbed her by the vest and dragged her the last twenty meters. In that
moment, her body had become something dangerous to her—a thing that could panic, that could fail,
that could stop entirely.

After her deployment ended, Layla returned to the United States and took the first civilian job she
could find. She told herself she was fine. But she had come to believe that any sensation of
physical strain—a racing pulse from climbing stairs, shortness of breath from a brisk walk, the
heavy feeling in her limbs after carrying groceries—meant she was on the verge of losing control
again. She stopped exercising entirely. She took the elevator even for one floor. She avoided
anything that made her sweat or breathe hard, because what frightened her most was the feeling of
her own body speeding up without her permission. The world, she was certain, was full of things that
could ambush you from nowhere, and her own biology felt like the most dangerous ambush of all.

What she could control, she cleaned. Her apartment, a small one-bedroom, became a shrine to order.
She wiped the counters three times before bed. She organized her books alphabetically by author,
then by color, then back again. She vacuumed the same patch of carpet in slow, deliberate lines. The
cleaning was not about dirt—it was about the brief, hollow quiet that came after, when everything
was in its place and nothing could surprise her. It was the only time her mind stopped scanning for
threat.

Layla had also become convinced that darkness itself was a kind of trap. She did not go out after
sunset. She left work before the sun went down, or she did not leave at all. When her shift required
her to stay late, she asked a coworker to walk her to her car, and even then she drove with the
interior light on, checking the back seat twice. She had stopped seeing friends entirely, because
they wanted to meet for dinner or drinks in the evening, and she could not explain why the thought
of walking from a restaurant to a parked car after dark made her stomach turn into a fist. Her
social life collapsed. She spent most weekends in her apartment, cleaning.

What finally brought Layla to therapy was a moment of absurd, terrifying clarity. She had been
cleaning her kitchen cabinets, rearranging canned goods, when she felt her heart rate climb from the
effort. Her chest tightened. Her vision narrowed. She dropped a can of tomatoes and stood frozen,
certain she was about to die from a panic attack—the same certainty she had felt in that mortar
attack. She realized, standing there in her silent kitchen at two in the afternoon, that she could
not clean her way out of her own body. She had begun to understand that her entire life had been
compressed into a set of impossible rules—never exert yourself, never go outside after dark, never
stop cleaning—and that the rules were no longer working. She was not safe. She was just small. She
hoped, more than anything, that someone could help her feel less like a target in her own skin.
