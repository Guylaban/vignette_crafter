Gabriela was eight years old when she stood in the doorway of her family’s living room in San
Antonio, watching her father’s hand close around her mother’s throat. She remembered the way the
television was still on—some cartoon she had been watching minutes before—and the sound of her own
voice saying “stop” before she ran at him. He had shoved her aside with one arm, and she landed hard
against the coffee table. No one called the police that night. Her mother never pressed charges, and
when Gabriela told her aunt the next day, her aunt said, “That’s between your parents. You shouldn’t
get involved.” That was the last time she tried to tell anyone.

Now at twenty-six, Gabriela worked as a school counselor in a different part of the city, in a
bright office lined with posters about feelings and friendship. She liked the job—wanted to be
someone children could trust in a way she had not been trusted—but the work had begun to wear on her
in ways she could not ignore. A few weeks ago, a fifth-grade girl had come to her, quiet and shaky,
describing how her father had screamed at her mother the night before. Gabriela had listened,
nodded, handed her a tissue. But when the girl left, the memory swamped her without any warning: the
living room, the cartoon, the shove. Her chest tightened, her vision blurred, and she had to excuse
herself to the bathroom, where she sat on the floor until her hands stopped shaking. Later, when a
colleague asked what was wrong, she tried to explain. The words came out in fragments—“I just… when
I was little… it was my father…”—and then she lost the thread completely, the emotions pressing so
hard that she had to stop mid-sentence and walk away.

Gabriela had been on guard for so long that she had stopped noticing the effort it took. At home, a
one-bedroom apartment she kept meticulously neat, she always sat with her back against the wall,
facing the door. She checked locks three times before bed. If a friend or date touched her shoulder
from behind without warning, she flinched hard, sometimes letting out a sharp breath that
embarrassed her. She had come to believe that people could not be trusted—not because they meant
harm, necessarily, but because their intentions were hidden, and she had learned the hard way that
the people who should protect you could be the ones who hurt you. Institutions were no better. She
remembered the police never came. No teacher intervened. The adults in her life had looked away, and
she had internalised the lesson: you are on your own.

She also blamed herself. For years she had turned over the night in her mind, searching for what she
could have done differently. If she hadn’t left the room to get a glass of water. If she had called
911 from the kitchen phone. If she had screamed louder. The rumination looped daily, especially when
she saw a man raise his voice or a couple argue in a parking lot. She found herself scanning
environments constantly, evaluating who might become dangerous—a man in a hoodie, a stranger
standing too close in line, even a colleague with a quick temper.

The avoidance had become more specific over time. She had not been inside her childhood home since
she was sixteen, and she still took long detours to avoid the street where it sat, even though she
had not lived in that neighbourhood for years. At work, she avoided the storage room near the
gymnasium because its layout—narrow, with no visibility of the door—reminded her of the hallway she
had run down that night. The consequence was that she had to ask another staff member to retrieve
supplies for her, and twice she had made excuses rather than attend a meeting in a conference room
set up with chairs facing away from the entrance.

What finally brought Gabriela to seek help was a moment that frightened her more than the memory
itself. Last week, during a routine check-in with a middle-schooler who had been acting out, the boy
shoved his chair back and shouted. The sound was sharp and sudden, and Gabriela felt a surge of pure
alarm so intense that she stood up, backed against the wall, and could not speak for several
seconds. The boy looked at her, confused. She apologised, finished the session early, and closed her
office door. That evening she sat in her apartment, back against the wall, and admitted to herself
that something had to change. She was afraid of what would happen if she did not get help—afraid she
would lose the job she cared about, afraid she would never feel safe, afraid that the fear itself
was proof that she had never really left that living room at all.
