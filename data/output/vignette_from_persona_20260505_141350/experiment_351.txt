protection, though she had never liked having it in the house. There was a cloth in her hand, the
scent of gun oil, the kitchen light casting a yellow glow. Then the sound, like a door slamming
inside her skull. Then Carlos on the floor, blood spreading across the tile, his eyes wide and
trying to speak. She remembered screaming. She remembered the operator’s voice. But the fraction of
a second between the cloth slipping and the trigger catching — that was gone, a gap no amount of
replaying would fill. Carlos died on the way to the hospital. The official report called it an
accidental discharge. The word accidental did nothing to quiet the voice that had taken up residence
in Lucia’s chest: *You did this. You are the one.*

In the months that followed, Lucia began to notice that the grief did not always arrive with a
picture. Sometimes she would be sitting at her workstation, monitoring network traffic, when a wave

to no specific image. She would have to excuse herself to the bathroom and press her palms against
the cold sink until it passed. Other times a true memory would surface — the way Carlos’s hand had
looked on the stretcher, still ringed with his wedding band — and she would be flooded not just with
sadness but with a certainty that she had no right to grieve him because she was the one who had put
him there.

Her job kept her in a windowless office with three monitors and a badge that opened every door. She
had thought the controlled environment would feel safe. But danger found its own shape. One
afternoon a colleague mentioned a recent shooting at a shopping mall, and Lucia’s stomach turned to
ice. She excused herself and did not return to that floor for the rest of the week. News alerts

counter at home — any object that could be used as a weapon now made her breath shorten and her
hands tremble. She had come to believe that if she saw a weapon, something terrible would happen
again, that she would lose control and cause harm a second time. She began scanning rooms the moment
she entered them, checking for knives, scissors, heavy objects, anything that could become a weapon
in the wrong hands — her hands.

rising, Lucia felt her throat close. She heard the operator’s voice again, the sirens. She cut the
call short and later avoided the vendor’s emails altogether. At home, she stopped answering the
phone. She let her friendships wither. Dinner invitations from neighbors went unreturned. She told
herself it was better to be alone — less risk of something going wrong, less chance of another
accident. The consequence was a quiet apartment, a calendar empty except for work, and a growing
distance from the woman she used to be.

Work itself began to suffer. Lucia had always been meticulous, but now she found herself rereading
the same lines of code, distracted by the weight in her chest. Twice she missed a critical security
patch deadline. Her supervisor asked if she was okay. She said she was fine. She was not.

What finally brought Lucia to a therapist’s office was a moment three weeks ago. She was walking
through a parking lot after dark and a car backfired. For a second she was back in the kitchen — the
sound, the warmth, the blood. She dropped to her knees between two cars and wept until a security
guard found her. She realized she could not keep living this way, jumping at every loud noise,
crying without knowing why, afraid of her own hands. She came hoping to find a way to remember
Carlos without being destroyed by the memory. What she feared most was that no amount of talking
would change what she had done — that the guilt was simply the truth of her.
