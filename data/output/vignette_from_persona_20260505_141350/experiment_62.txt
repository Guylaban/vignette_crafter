Marcus is a 45-year-old agricultural worker who lives with his partner in a small rural community.
He came to therapy reluctantly, at his partner’s urging, after months of tension and a growing sense
that he was losing everything he still cared about. The first time Marcus spoke about what happened
to him, he stared at the floor and his voice dropped to a near whisper. He was eight years old when
he watched his father beat his mother in the kitchen of their home. He remembered the sound of her
body hitting the linoleum, the way his father’s belt buckle caught the light, and the fact that he
did nothing. He stood frozen in the doorway, too terrified to move or scream. He has carried that
moment with him every day since.

For decades, Marcus tried to push the memory away. He told himself it was over, that he was a child,
that there was nothing he could have done. But the harder he tried not to think about it, the more
the images would surface at unpredictable times—while he was driving a tractor across a field, or
sitting with his partner in the living room, or lying awake in the dark. He learned early that the
worst moments came when he was alone. So he made sure never to be. At work, he arranged his schedule
so that he always had another person nearby, even if that meant taking longer to finish a job or
swapping tasks with a coworker. He would never volunteer for the early-morning irrigation shift that
required walking through the orchard by himself. When his supervisor told him he was being
reassigned to a solo task, Marcus felt his chest tighten and he found a reason to refuse, not
explaining why. Over time, his boss grew frustrated, and Marcus feared he was being seen as
difficult or unreliable.

At home, he insisted on keeping lights on in every room until he was ready to sleep, and even then
he would leave a hallway light on. His partner noticed that he seemed more on edge after sunset,
that he would flinch at the sound of a car door closing or a dog barking outside. Marcus could not
explain it—he simply knew that darkness made the world feel dangerous in a way that daylight did
not. People told him he was safe, that the man who hurt his mother was long gone, that the house was
locked, that nothing was going to happen. But their words never reached that part of him that stayed
on guard. He found himself scanning the yard through the window before opening the back door,
checking locks twice, and walking through the house with his fists half-clenched. He had come to
believe that safety was not something he was allowed to feel.

The guilt was the thing that gnawed at him most. He replayed that kitchen scene again and again, and
each time he saw himself standing there, doing nothing. He told himself he would never forgive his
own cowardice, even though he knew logically that he had been a child. The anger he could not direct
at himself found its way toward his father, even though his father had been dead for fifteen years.
Marcus spent hours at night, lying next to his sleeping partner, rehearsing in his mind what he
would say if he could confront him, imagining different ways to make him pay. He knew it was
pointless, but the rumination felt like the only thing he could control. Meanwhile, the happiness he
remembered from his youth—the easy laughter, the sense of possibility—had drained away. He felt
hollow, irritable, and convinced that people were not to be trusted. He had pulled back from
friendships, stopped attending church, and rarely accepted invitations from extended family. His
relationship had become strained because his partner felt shut out, unable to understand why Marcus
could never relax, why he always needed company, why he seemed to carry a weight that could not be
put down.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a moment in the field when a coworker came up behind
him without warning and clapped him on the shoulder. Marcus spun around, heart pounding, and nearly
shoved the man before he caught himself. He saw the shock and hurt on his colleague’s face, and for
the first time he thought: I am not okay. He agreed to see a therapist because his partner said she
could not keep living this way, and because he was tired of being afraid in a world that everyone
else seemed to find ordinary. He said he did not know if he could ever feel safe, but he wanted to
stop feeling so alone even when someone was right beside him.
