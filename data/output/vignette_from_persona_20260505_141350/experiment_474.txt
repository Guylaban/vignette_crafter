When Arjun was nine years old, his father began locking him in the basement closet as punishment.
The routine was always the same: a sharp word, a shove, the heavy door slamming shut, and then the
click of the bolt. The closet was unfinished—bare concrete walls, a single bulb that stayed off
because the pull chain was too high. In winter, the cold seeped through the floorboards and settled
on his skin like a second layer. He would curl into himself, shivering, listening to the muffled
sounds of the house above, waiting for hours until his mother let him out. The cold was the worst
part. It clung to him, made his fingers stiff, his teeth chatter. And the silence. He learned to
dread the moment the door closed, the thud of metal against frame, because it meant he was alone
with the temperature and his own fear.

Those nights never fully left him. Now, at thirty-six, Arjun is a detective with the city police. He
is good at his job—meticulous, patient, able to sit through hours of interrogation without breaking
his composure. Colleagues respect him. His wife, Priya, describes him as steady. But he knows that
is a mask. Inside, he feels like the boy in the closet: cold, trapped, certain that something about
him is fundamentally wrong. His reactions since the abuse, he believes, prove that he is a lousy
coper. What kind of person, he thinks, still flinches at a door slamming thirty years later? He has
come to see himself as defective, and that belief has hollowed out his sense of who he is. He used
to know himself—a determined officer, a good husband, someone who could handle pressure. Now he is
not sure what remains.

The triggers surface in mundane moments. At the precinct, every time a door opens or
shuts—especially if it is heavy or slammed—his stomach drops. He has learned to brace himself, but
the startle response still flickers across his face. Worse is the temperature. In the winter,
walking into a cold room makes his skin prickle with dread. He keeps his office warm, but the
sensation can ambush him: a draft through a window, the chill of a metal chair. He has started
avoiding other people to minimise the risk of being caught off guard. During shift change, he stays
at his desk until the hallway is empty. He turns down dinner invitations from colleagues. Priya has
noticed he no longer wants to go out on weekends—he makes excuses about fatigue, about a case that
needs his attention. Outings mean unpredictable sounds, sudden breezes, crowds where he cannot
control what touches him.

Sleeping alone is the hardest. He and Priya share a bed, but when she works late or travels, Arjun
cannot bring himself to lie down. The silence, the empty space beside him, the faint cold of
unoccupied sheets—it all pulls him back to the basement. So he stays up, often until two or three in
the morning, watching television or scrolling through case files. He tells himself he is being
productive, but really he is delaying the moment he must face the bed. When he finally does sleep,
it is shallow and broken by dreams he cannot fully recall. The fatigue accumulates. He has missed
two morning briefings in the past month, something that never happened before. His sergeant pulled
him aside with a look of concern, but Arjun deflected. He cannot explain that the real problem is
his own memory, that he feels like he is coming apart.

What finally brought him to seek help was a minor incident that became unbearable. Last week, during
a traffic stop, a patrol car door slammed shut next to him. The sound was sharp and close, and Arjun
froze. His hand went to his weapon before he could stop it. The officer beside him gave him a
strange look. Arjun laughed it off, but that night he could not sleep at all. He sat on the couch,
his skin cold despite the heating, and thought about the boy in the closet. He realised he had been
trying to outrun that closet his whole life—by becoming a detective, by staying busy, by never
showing weakness—but it had followed him here. He has started therapy because he is terrified of
losing what is left: his marriage, his career, the thin thread of who he used to be. He hopes,
though he barely dares admit it, that maybe he can learn to let the door close without feeling the
cold.
