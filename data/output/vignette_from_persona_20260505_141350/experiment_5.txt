Kai was twenty years old when he landed in the conflict zone, a relief worker fresh out of training,
assigned to coordinate food distributions in a region where the front line shifted like a living
thing. He believed in the work the way he had believed in everything else—earnestly, fully, as if
his presence alone could hold back the chaos. That belief lasted until the afternoon the convoy
stopped at a checkpoint and the soldiers waved them through with a look that made his stomach
tighten. Minutes later, the road opened into a marketplace that had been still, too still, and then
the first mortar round hit. He remembered the sound more than the sight: a pressure that folded the
world inward, then the screaming. He spent the next three hours digging through rubble with his bare
hands, pulling out bodies and pieces of bodies, finding one child alive but bleeding so fast that by
the time a medical team reached them, the child had stopped breathing in his arms. That was the
moment he understood that he had not saved anyone. He had only been there to witness the failure.

When Kai returned to the United States and enrolled in university, he told himself the worst was
over. He was safe. But his body had not received the message. Walking across campus, he would catch
a glimpse of a police cruiser parked near the library and his heart would slam against his ribs, his
palms slick with sweat, his vision tunneling until he had to brace himself against a wall. He knew
the officers were not a threat; he told himself that over and over. But the sight of the uniform—the
crisp lines, the badge, the holster—sent him straight back to the checkpoint, back to the soldier
who had looked through him as if he were already dead. And then there was the feeling of being
watched. In lectures, he sat at the back, scanning the room, convinced that someone was observing
him—a professor, a student, anyone whose gaze lingered a second too long. He stopped making eye
contact altogether, because whenever he met another person's eyes, he saw the child's eyes staring
back, and he knew that if they looked closely enough, they would see the failure written on his
face.

He had been social before the deployment, part of a tight group of friends who met for dinner,
studied together, argued about politics. Now he avoided them entirely. He told himself he was busy,
that he had changed, that they wouldn't understand. The real reason was simpler and more painful:
being around them reminded him of the person he used to be, the one who believed he could make a
difference. That person had died in the rubble. Kai could not bear to watch his friends mourn a
stranger. So he turned down invitations, stopped answering texts, and let the distance grow into a
silence that felt permanent. On the rare occasions he walked past the student union, he took the
long way around to avoid the ROTC building and the uniformed cadets gathered outside. He even
avoided the campus security booth near the parking lot, crossing the street well before he reached
it.

None of this felt like choice. It felt like survival. There was a voice in his head that told him he
had failed in the only role that mattered—the role of someone who could protect others—and that
failure meant he was fundamentally unreliable. His body, he had come to believe, was a traitor: it
kept reacting as if the war was still happening, flooding him with adrenaline when a door slammed,
sending him into a cold sweat when he saw a police car in his rearview mirror. And no matter how far
he ran, he could not shake the certainty that bad things would keep happening, that he was powerless
to stop them, that the next mortar round could fall anywhere, anytime, even here, even now.

He sought help only after his academic advisor called him in to discuss the three courses he was
failing. Kai sat in the advisor's office, eyes fixed on the floor, and heard himself say that he
could not concentrate, that he could not sleep, that he could not stop seeing the child's face. The
advisor referred him to the counseling center. Walking out of the building, Kai passed a campus
police officer stationed at the entrance. He did not look up. He kept walking, counting his breaths,
afraid of what he would see in the officer's eyes, and more afraid of what the officer would see in
his.
