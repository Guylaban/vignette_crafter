David is a fifty-five-year-old fashion designer who walks into the office with the guarded posture
of a man accustomed to reading a room for exits. By most external measures, his career has been a
success; his collections are known for their architectural precision and clean lines. But that
precision, he would confess, is not merely a professional signature. It is a fortress. And inside
that fortress, he has been living for decades with the fragments of a childhood he cannot quite
narrate to himself.

His father was a man of terrible, watchful silences. The quiet that came before an explosion of rage
was, for David, the most dangerous part of the entire ordeal. He learned to read it the way a ground
animal reads the shadow of a hawk: a held breath, a tightening in the air that meant something
dangerous was about to happen. The physical violence itself—the belt, the back of the hand, the
shove into the corner of his bedroom—these moments he can recall with a strange and disturbing
numbness, as if they happened to a stranger, a boy he barely recognizes. He can recount the facts
without feeling, a cold and disjointed list. But other times, without warning, the numbness shatters
and the memory is suffused with an intense, immobilizing guilt. He will find himself obsessively
turning over his own childhood actions, dwelling on what he might have done wrong, the misstep that
triggered the catastrophe. What did he say? What did he fail to anticipate? Even now, he spends
considerable mental energy dwelling bitterly on what his father did to him, but it is the self-blame
that feels more insidious, more intimate, a loop he cannot escape.

This internal landscape has shaped his adult life into a series of careful fortifications. He is
certain that he has permanently changed for the worse, that the boy he might have been was destroyed
long ago and replaced by this vigilant, armored man. The notion that people can be trusted feels to
him like a dangerous and naive fantasy. His divorce five years ago was not a single explosive fight
but a slow corrosion. His wife grew weary of the distance. What frightened her most, and what he
could never adequately explain, was the way he would react with a sharp, visible alarm when she
touched him unexpectedly—a flinch, a full-body recoil that felt insulting but was purely autonomic,
a survival reflex that had never learned to switch off. She mistook his caution for coldness. He
mistook her frustration for proof that he was, indeed, beyond reach.

In his professional life, the strategies he developed to cope have begun to falter. The fashion
world demands close physical collaboration. Fittings and draping sessions require assistants to
touch him, to adjust a sleeve at the shoulder, to pin a hem at the ankle. His new assistant, a well-
meaning young woman, has a habit of approaching from the side, her fingers brushing his arm without
warning. Every time, he reacts with the same sharp alarm, his entire body tensing. He has started
rearranging his studio so that his back is always to a wall, and he avoids the vulnerable silence of
the workshop in the late afternoon, that transitional hush before the evening rush. That particular
quiet primes him for the nightmares he dreads, so he pushes himself to work until total exhaustion
forces him into unconsciousness. He feels perpetually on guard, even inside his own home, which was
supposed to be his refuge but has become just another room to scan for threats.

What finally drove David to reach out for help was a moment of profound terror that occurred in that
very home. He was standing alone in his kitchen during an unusual silence—the refrigerator hum had
kicked off, the traffic outside had paused—and a wave of sheer panic seized him for no visible
reason. He felt the old familiar dread, the held breath from childhood, and he realized he could not
tell the difference anymore between a genuine threat and the echoes of a past threat. He looked at
his trembling hands and was struck by the horrifying conviction that his reactions meant he was
going crazy, that his mind was finally giving way under the weight of the vigilance. He is terrified
walking in here that a therapist will confirm his deepest fear: that the damage is permanent and
there is nothing left to salvage. Yet beneath that fear, a small and carefully guarded hope
persists—that maybe the guilt and the rage and the startle are not the sum total of who he is, but
the shape of a wound he can finally learn to touch without being destroyed by it.
