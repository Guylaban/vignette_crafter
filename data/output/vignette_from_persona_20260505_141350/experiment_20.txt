Priya Sharma is a 44-year-old retail worker in a large suburban discount store, married for eighteen
years. The event that shattered her sense of safety occurred nine months ago, on a Tuesday afternoon
in early spring. She was stocking cleaning supplies in aisle six when she heard a commotion near the
pharmacy counter. A middle-aged man had collapsed, his face a mottled purple, his hands clawing at
his throat. A woman—his wife, Priya later learned—was screaming for help. Priya dropped her box of
detergent and ran to get the store manager, but by the time they returned with the defibrillator,
the man had stopped moving. Paramedics arrived within minutes, but they could not revive him. Priya
stood frozen behind a display of vitamins, watching the wife sob over his body, watching the
paramedics pack their equipment away. She did not know the man or his wife, but she has not stopped
thinking about them since.

At first she told herself it was just shock, that she would get over it. But within a week she
noticed changes she could not explain. When her husband, Raj, asked about her day at dinner, she
tried to describe what had happened, but the words came out jumbled. She remembered the sound of the
woman’s scream but not the order of what came next—the manager’s voice on the intercom, the sirens,
the thud of the man’s body hitting the linoleum. The sequence felt scrambled, as if the memory had
been torn apart and reassembled wrong. Talking about it made her dizzy, a floating sensation that
left her gripping the edge of the kitchen counter. Raj stopped asking after she burst into tears the
third time he brought it up. She now avoids any mention of the event, but the grief it carries still
ambushes her: a wave of sorrow for a stranger that floods her chest without warning, so intense she
has to stop whatever she is doing and breathe.

At work, Priya’s environment has become a landscape of threats. She constantly scans the aisles for
signs of distress—a customer leaning against a shelf, a child coughing, an elderly person walking
slowly. Her eyes dart from face to face, checking for the same panicked expression she saw that day.
She is hypervigilant, unable to relax even in the break room, and she startles at the sharp beep of
the cash register or the sudden clatter of a dropped item. The dizziness returns whenever she passes
the pharmacy counter, so she now takes the long way to the stockroom, adding ten minutes to every
trip. She has also noticed that she has become excessively agreeable at work and at home. She says
yes to extra shifts she does not want, and agrees to plans Raj suggests even when she is exhausted,
because refusing anyone feels unsafe—as if saying no could somehow provoke another catastrophe. She
cannot explain the logic; she only knows that going along with what others want makes her feel
momentarily less exposed.

Her preoccupation with why this happened has become almost constant. She replays the moment: Was she
supposed to have noticed the man earlier? Could she have done something different—called 911 sooner,
yelled louder? The questions circle without answers, and the lack of resolution deepens her sense
that the world is unpredictable and that she is helpless in the face of it. She has come to believe
that if she stays vigilant enough, if she checks every corner and placates everyone around her, she
might prevent another tragedy. But she also knows, somewhere beneath that hope, that it is
impossible.

The toll on her marriage has been quiet but real. Raj has grown frustrated with her constant
scanning when they are out—at the grocery store, at a restaurant, she is always looking past him,
searching for something she cannot name. She has stopped going to family gatherings because the
noise and crowds overwhelm her. She feels guilty, but the fear of being caught off guard is
stronger. It was Raj who finally suggested she talk to someone, after she woke him at 3 a.m. sobbing
from a dream she could not fully remember, only that the man’s face had merged with her husband’s,
and she had watched him fall and fall. She agreed because she is tired of being afraid, and because
she wants—though she is not sure it is possible—to stop feeling that she is still standing in aisle
six, watching a life end she never knew.
