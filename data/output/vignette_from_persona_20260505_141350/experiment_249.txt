Mai is a 52-year-old fast food worker who lives with her husband in a small apartment above a
laundromat. She came to the clinic at her husband’s insistence, though she does not really believe
that talking can help. The problem, from her perspective, is not something inside her head — it is
that the world itself is no longer safe, and she is not the person she used to be.

The trauma had happened seven years earlier, back in the coastal town where Mai had grown up before
immigrating. A typhoon had swept through the neighborhood in the middle of the night. Mai remembered
the sound first — a low, shuddering groan that turned into a roar, then the crash of something heavy
against the wall. After that, her memory came only in disconnected pieces: the sensation of water
rising past her knees, the weight of her mother’s hand gripping her arm, a ceiling fan dangling by
one chain, the smell of mud and bleach. She could never put those pieces into a single coherent
story. When the memory forced its way into her mind — always unwanted, always sudden — she felt so
overwhelmed by emotion that she had to stop whatever she was doing. Sometimes it was a wave of pure
terror; other times a thick, hollow sadness that made her want to lie down and not move.

In the months after the typhoon, Mai noticed that she could no longer feel the way she used to. She
could not feel joy at her niece’s wedding, or even sadness at the news of an old friend’s illness.
She had come to believe that her emotions had been permanently broken — that she would never again
experience anything normal. At the same time, a deep sense of not belonging had taken root. She had
felt displaced in America even before the typhoon, but afterward she was certain that she no longer
fit anywhere. Not in the old country, where the disaster had erased the landmarks of her childhood.
Not here, where people did not understand what it was like to watch your home become a sheet of
brown water. And, worst of all, not inside her own skin. She believed that bad things could happen
at any moment and that there was nothing she could do to stop them.

The consequences of these beliefs shaped every day of her life. At the fast food restaurant where
she worked the morning shift, the fry station made a violent hissing and popping sound when the
basket hit the hot oil. Every time a co-worker let a metal tray clatter onto the counter, Mai’s
entire body would lock up. She had learned to work with her back to the kitchen, and she always kept
her eyes on the door in case she needed to run. Her supervisor had noticed her flinching and had
told her to “relax” — a word that now made Mai feel angry and invisible.

Nighttime was the worst. She could not fall asleep because she was on high alert for unexpected or
loud sounds — a car backfiring, the apartment building’s old pipes groaning, her husband turning
over in bed. Even when she did drift off, she woke frequently, her heart pounding for no clear
reason. She had come to associate darkness with danger. She preferred the daylight hours, and she
had arranged her schedule so that she was home before dusk. This meant that she never saw her
husband in the evenings anymore, because he worked a later shift. He had started sleeping on the
couch so that his movements would not disturb her, and they barely spoke now.

What finally brought Mai to the clinic was a thunderstorm three weeks ago. She had been standing at
the register when a crack of lightning hit the power line behind the restaurant. The sound — a
sharp, percussive boom — had sent her to her knees behind the counter. She had not been able to stop
shaking for an hour. Her manager threatened to fire her if she “couldn’t handle the job.” Her
husband, for the first time, had said that something had to change.

Mai does not believe she can change. She is certain that she is broken, that she belongs nowhere,
and that the world will always be a place where bad things happen without warning. But she is also
tired — tired of being afraid of every loud sound, tired of being alone at night, tired of being the
person who cannot feel anything. She came to the session because she does not know what else to do,
and because a small, buried part of her still hopes that maybe she is wrong.
