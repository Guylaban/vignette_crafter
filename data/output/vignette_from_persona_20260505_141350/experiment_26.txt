Rajesh was sixty-eight years old, a divorced man who had spent the last two decades working at a
small nonprofit that supported immigrant families. He was known there as quiet, meticulous, and
unfailingly reliable—a colleague who never missed a deadline, but who always positioned himself at
the far end of the conference table, his back to the wall, eyes scanning the room as though he were
waiting for something to go wrong. What his coworkers did not know was that Rajesh had not slept
through the night in over two years, that he had learned to flinch before anyone touched him, and
that sometimes, when the memories came, he would excuse himself to the bathroom and press his
fingernails into his forearm until the pain drowned out the sounds he heard in his head.

The event that had set all of this in motion had happened late one evening after a community
fundraiser. Rajesh had stayed behind to lock up, and a man he vaguely recognized from a previous
event had followed him inside. What happened next was over in minutes, but for Rajesh it had never
ended. He could still feel the pressure of the man’s hand over his mouth, the weight pinning him to
the cold floor. He could still hear the low, deliberate breathing that had been the only sound
besides the hum of the fluorescent lights. In the months that followed, he told no one. He told
himself that he was a grown man, that he should have fought harder, that his reactions since then
just proved what a lousy coper he really was. He began to believe that he would never feel normal
emotions again—that the part of him that had once laughed easily and trusted people had been
permanently erased. He had become an outsider, even in his own life.

The symptoms arrived slowly and then all at once. At first it was just a heightened alertness: he
would listen for footsteps in the hallway of his apartment building, his heart pounding at the sound
of a door opening. Soon he could not bear to be alone in a room. He started taking his work breaks
in the communal kitchen, making small talk he did not want just to have someone nearby. At home he
left the television on all night, not for company but to mask the ambient sounds that might signal
someone entering. A sudden loud noise—a car backfiring, a dropped tray in the cafeteria—would send
him into a state of rigid vigilance. The worst was unexpected touch. When a colleague placed a hand
on his shoulder to get his attention, Rajesh would jerk away, his breath catching, his mind flashing
to that cold floor before he could stop it. He began to sit with his back to the wall at every meal,
every meeting, positioning himself so that he could see the entire room. His coworkers noticed; they
stopped touching him.

The memories themselves were not just visual. When they returned, often without warning, Rajesh
would hear the same low breathing, as though it were happening in the room with him. He would feel a
sharp pain in his ribs, in his jaw, exactly where the man had pressed him down. And when he tried to
describe any of this—to his doctor, to the therapist he finally saw once—the words would not come.
He knew what had happened, could replay it in perfect detail, but his mouth would go dry and the
sentences would dissolve. He felt trapped inside his own mind.

The self-harm began about a year in. After a particularly vivid memory that left him shaking and
nauseous, Rajesh found himself digging his fingernails into his forearm so hard that the crescents
of blood surprised him. The sharp sting brought a kind of relief, a way to externalize the chaos
that lived inside him. He started doing it more often—a small cut with a kitchen knife, a burn from
a lighter—always in private, always when the feelings threatened to overwhelm him. He told himself
it was the only thing that still felt real.

He came to therapy because his supervisor, concerned after seeing the scars on his wrist during a
hand-delivery of paperwork, had gently insisted. Rajesh knew he could not keep living like this, but
what he feared most was that he was right—that his emotions were permanently broken, that he would
always be an outsider, that no amount of talking could change what he had become. Still, he sat down
in the office, his back to the wall, and tried to find the words.
