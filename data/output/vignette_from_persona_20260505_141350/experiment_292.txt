Priya was twenty-five years old and working as a counselor at a community mental health center when
the police stopped her. She had just stepped off a city bus after a late shift, the warm spring air
carrying the smell of damp earth from a nearby park. Two officers approached her, saying she matched
a description. Before she could speak, they grabbed her arms, twisted them behind her back, and
pushed her face-first onto the gravel strip beside the bus stop. The taste of dirt and the pressure
of a knee between her shoulder blades stayed with her longer than the words they shouted. She was
released after twenty minutes, no charges, no apology.

In the weeks that followed, Priya stopped taking the bus. She started walking the forty minutes to
work, avoiding the stop where it happened, taking different streets each day so she never passed the
same corner twice. At the mental health center, she found herself studying the faces of her clients
and coworkers with a sharpness she had never had before—watching for something hidden, a shift in
expression that might signal danger. Her body would react before her mind caught up: a sudden racing
pulse when a colleague touched her arm, a flinch when a client raised their voice in session. She
began keeping her hands near her face during meetings, pressing her palms against her cheeks as if
to disappear behind them. In her car, she checked the rearview and side mirrors obsessively, certain
that a police cruiser would appear behind her. She could not fall asleep at night because staying
alert felt necessary; she lay still, listening to the building settle, her muscles taut.

Her memory of the event came in two forms that confused and frightened her. Sometimes she would be
washing dishes or sitting in supervision and the entire scene would flood back—the gravel scraping
her cheek, the officer’s breath, the smell of soil—and her body would lock into the same position
she had been in, her arms pinned beneath her, her face pressed sideways. Other times, when a friend
asked what happened, she could describe it flatly, feeling nothing, as if it had happened to a
stranger she had read about. That numbness worried her more than the reenactments.

She had always been close to her South Asian community—family gatherings, temple events, a group of
friends from the cultural association. But after the incident, she began avoiding them. She stopped

now marked or contaminated. She showered for longer than usual, scrubbing her skin until it was raw,
but the feeling of being dirty never fully left. She had come to believe that people were not what
they seemed; even the counselor inside her, who had once trusted therapeutic rapport, now scanned
every smile for a hidden threat.

Her avoidance narrowed her world. She stopped using public transport entirely, which meant she
