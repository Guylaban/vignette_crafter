Karim is a sixty-four-year-old divorced man who came to the United States as a young adult and
eventually built a career in local politics. For the past fifteen years he has served on a city
council, spending his days in meetings, public forums, and back‑room negotiations. Ten years before
entering politics, Karim had volunteered as a relief worker in a conflict zone in the Middle East.
He does not speak of that time unless forced to, and even then only in fragments. The event that
changed him occurred on an afternoon when his convoy was ambushed while distributing food in a
contested district. A mortar struck the jeep ahead of him. He remembers the world flipping
sideways—the metallic hiss of the shell, the deafening blast, the sight of a driver’s arm, severed,
still gripping the steering wheel. Then the fire. The smell of burning fuel, burning flesh, burning
rubber. He tried to pull survivors from the wreckage, but his hands found only pieces that did not
fit together. For years the memory returned as exactly that: a disjointed slide show of smoke, a
child’s sandal lying in a pool of oil, the weight of a man’s head lolling in his hands. And the
terror—the same raw, suffocating terror he felt in that moment—would wash over him without warning,
making him stop whatever he was doing, breathless, as if he were back in the heat and the noise.

When Karim returned home, he told no one what he had seen. He had come to believe that if he ever
let the feelings out, he would shatter completely. So he learned to hold his emotions in a tight
fist. He threw himself into work, first as a community organizer and then as a candidate, because
staying busy meant he did not have to sit with himself. Silence, especially at home, became
unbearable. When the apartment was quiet he felt a tightening in his chest, a certainty that
something terrible was about to happen. He began checking the locks on his doors and windows twice,
then three times, before he could sleep. In public he scanned every face, every doorway, every
parked car. The habit was automatic: he would walk into a coffee shop and catalogue the exits, the
shadows, the strangers who seemed to look at him too long. Talking to strangers became an ordeal.
Every handshake, every unscheduled conversation, felt like a potential threat. He could not place
why—it was not that he thought the other person meant him harm, but the act of meeting an unfamiliar
gaze triggered a visceral unease, as if the stranger might suddenly become part of the fragmented
memory that still haunted him.

Becoming a politician made avoidance impossible. His job required him to shake hands daily, to
attend town halls where constituents debated military policy, to stand awkwardly near the grill at
barbecues where the smell of charred meat reminded him of burning bodies. He developed a repertoire
of strategies: he kept his face neutral, refrained from showing anger or fear, and steered
conversations toward concrete tasks. When the topic turned to a new military deployment or a
veteran’s benefit, he nodded but offered no opinion, then found an excuse to move to another room.
The overworking was relentless. He took on extra committee assignments, stayed late at the office,
scheduled back‑to‑back meetings so that there was no gap for his mind to drift. By the time he came
home, he was exhausted enough to fall asleep quickly—if he first checked the locks twice and turned
on the radio to break the silence.

The cost of this system was a quiet, accumulating wreckage. His wife left him after eight years of
marriage because, she said, she felt like a stranger in her own home. He knew she was right: he had
never let her see the fragments, had never explained why he flinched when the smoke alarm went off
or why he insisted on sitting with his back to the wall in restaurants. After the divorce he lived
alone, and the solitude intensified his scanning and his checking. He found himself listening to the
silence in his empty house, straining for the sound of a footstep that should not be there.

Three months before his intake appointment, Karim attended a city council hearing on a proposal to
rezone a lot near a factory that occasionally released a burnt, chemical odor. Before the vote, a
resident—a retired military officer—stood up and spoke passionately about the need to protect the
community from industrial hazards. His words turned to the sacrifice of soldiers in recent overseas
campaigns. Karim felt his jaw lock. He did not hear the next sentences; his ears filled with a
high‑pitched whine, and the burnt smell from the factory seemed to have entered the room. He excused
himself, walked to the restroom, and stood gripping the sink, struggling to breathe. The terror had
returned—as vivid as the day of the attack, flooding him, disjointed images cascading behind his
eyes. For the first time in years, he could not distract himself out of it.

That night he found himself online, searching for a therapist who specialized in trauma. He made the
call because he was afraid of what might happen next: a panic attack during a televised debate, a
complete breakdown that would end his career. What he hoped for, though he could barely admit it,
was that someone might help him fit the pieces together so that the smell, the silence, and the
strangers would stop feeling like the prelude to an explosion. He had spent decades policing his
inner world, and he was exhausted.
