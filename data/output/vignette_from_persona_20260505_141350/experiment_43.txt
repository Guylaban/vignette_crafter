his wife’s urging after months of escalating distress. The trauma that reshaped his life occurred
eight years ago, before his current marriage, when he was in a relationship with a man named Tarek.

turned violent. Tarek’s hands closed around Karim’s throat; his voice was a low, cold whisper that
Karim can still hear, word for word, as if it were recorded in his skull. The assault lasted only a
few minutes, but what Karim remembers comes in jumbled pieces—the pressure on his windpipe, the
sting of his own teeth cutting his cheek when his head hit the wall, a single, almost surreal
detail: the distant thwop-thwop-thwop of a helicopter passing overhead that he fixated on as he
tried not to lose consciousness. He managed to push Tarek away and escape, and he never returned to
that apartment. The relationship ended, but the event never left him.

In the weeks and months afterward, Karim found himself unable to walk down the block where the
basement apartment had been. He took a different bus route, added thirty minutes to his commute, and
eventually stopped going to the neighborhood altogether. That part of the city became a forbidden
zone in his mind. At home, he began to wear clothes that made him feel invisible—oversized jackets,
dark colors, hoods that shadowed his face. He told himself it was just comfort, but he had come to
believe that if he looked nondescript enough, no one would notice him, and if no one noticed him, no
one would hurt him. The darkness of evening became a particular dread. He insisted on keeping lights
on in every room, even while sleeping, and his wife learned not to turn off the hallway lamp. When
she asked why, Karim could only say he didn’t like the dark.
