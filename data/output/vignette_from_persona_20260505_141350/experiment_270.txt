Ravi Patel was born in Kampala, Uganda, where his family had run a small pottery workshop for three
generations. He learned the wheel from his father, and by his mid-twenties he could throw a vessel
with his eyes closed, the clay rising between his palms as naturally as breath. In 1972, when Idi
Amin ordered the expulsion of Asians, Ravi, his wife Asha, and their two-year-old daughter were
given ninety days to leave. They were herded onto trucks by soldiers with rifles. Ravi’s father, who
had stayed behind to lock the workshop, never made it onto the truck. Ravi watched the soldiers beat
him and drag him away. He heard the truck’s engine rev, saw his father’s broken eyeglasses on the
ground, and then the truck lurched forward. That image—the glasses, the roar of the engine, the
uniforms—split in his mind into pieces that would never quite fit together again.

For the next year, the family lived in a refugee camp in Kenya. The camp was loud: generators,
arguments, the constant rumble of vehicles. Ravi learned to keep his head down. He never argued,
never raised his voice, never let himself hope that tomorrow would be better. When they finally
resettled in a Midwestern American city, he found work at a ceramic supply store and eventually set
up a small kiln in his garage. But something had changed. The wheel no longer felt steady under his
hands. He could not describe what he had seen—when Asha asked him about that day, his mouth filled
with sand and only fragments came out: the glasses, the engine, the soldier’s boot on his father’s
back. He remembered these pieces more vividly than his daughter’s first steps or his wedding day.
They felt more real than the present.

Fifty years later, Ravi is seventy-five. He still avoids cemeteries and funerals because the weight
of grief feels like it will crush him. When a neighbor died, Ravi did not attend the service; he
spent the afternoon in his garage, reordering clay blocks. Car sounds startle him—a backfire, a
revving engine, even the soft growl of a passing Honda makes his chest tighten. Police cruisers make
him change direction. He checks his driveway three times before opening the garage door, and if he
sees a patrol car on his block, he stays inside until it leaves. He has stopped going to Diwali
celebrations or weddings, because every time he feels a flicker of happiness, a voice inside
whispers that something terrible is about to come and take it away. He cannot name that voice, but
it is relentless.

At home, Ravi never disagrees with Asha. If she wants to repaint the living room, he agrees. If she
suggests moving to a senior apartment, he says yes, even though the thought terrifies him. He has
learned that conflict attracts attention, and attention is dangerous. But this silence has hollowed
out their marriage. Asha has begun to weep at the dinner table, telling him she feels like she lives
with a ghost. He does not argue. He just checks the front door lock again.

His pottery wheel sits unused. The clay has dried and cracked in the bag. He once told his doctor
that he did not feel capable anymore—not of working, not of enjoying life, not of handling even the
smallest upset. A dropped spoon startles him into a cold dread. He cannot shake the feeling that he
has to be especially careful, because you never know what can happen next. That lesson, carved into
him by the soldiers and the truck and the glasses, has become the rule he lives by.

What finally brought Ravi to a therapist was a funeral. An old friend from the refugee camp died,
and Asha insisted he attend. He went. He stood at the grave, unable to speak, the fragments of his
father’s face collapsing into a single unbearable image. That night he did not sleep. He sat on the
edge of the bed, scanning the shadows, waiting. Asha made the appointment. He is afraid—of hope, of
calm, of letting his guard down for even a moment. But in the quietest part of himself, he wants to
feel the clay again.
