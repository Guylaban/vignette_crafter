Michael was thirty-eight years old, a history teacher at a suburban high school, married for eleven
years to Sarah, who taught elementary music. The mugging happened on a mid-December evening after
the winter concert, when the school auditorium had been packed with parents and students. He had
stayed late to help a colleague pack up the sound equipment, and by the time he walked to the far
end of the faculty parking lot, the crowd had thinned but the lot was still busy with cars idling
and families loading instruments. A man came up fast from behind a minivan, pressed something metal
and hard into Michael’s lower back, and said, “Wallet, now. Don’t turn around.” Michael turned
anyway—he could not help it—and saw a young man in a hooded sweatshirt holding a pistol. At that
exact moment, a car backfired fifty feet away, a sharp crack like a return shot. Michael bit down on
his tongue hard enough to taste blood, and the coppery saltiness returned to him later, over and
over, whenever the memory forced its way into his mind without warning. The mugger grabbed his arm
and yanked him toward a waiting sedan; Michael struggled, and then a woman screamed from the school
entrance, and the mugger let go and ran. The police arrived, took a report, and never made an
arrest.

In the weeks afterward, Michael found himself reliving the moment in full sensory detail—the cold
metal point through his coat, the sour smell of the man’s breath, the pressure of the grip on his
arm, the taste of his own blood, and the crack of that backfire. The memory would seize him without
warning: during a quiet lesson, while he was grading papers, or in the middle of the night. Each
time, it felt as though he were back in the parking lot, his heart slamming, certain he would be
shot. He started to believe that the world had become a place where danger could erupt from any
shadow. What had happened to him was deeply unfair, and nothing had been done—the police had no
leads, and the school had not improved security. He felt abandoned by the institutions that were
supposed to keep him safe, and that sense of betrayal hardened into a conviction that he could rely
only on himself.

Michael’s daily life narrowed. He began to avoid the school auditorium entirely, taking the longest
possible route between his classroom and the office to avoid the crowded hallways during passing
periods. He stopped attending faculty meetings if they were held in the gym, which felt too large
and full of people. He used to carpool with a colleague who lived on his street; now he made
excuses—“I have to stop at the bank,” “Sarah needs the car”—and drove alone, because having a
passenger made him feel trapped, unable to escape. Loud noises became unbearable. The fire alarm
drill in February sent him into a full panic, and he had to sit in the nurse’s office for thirty
minutes, shaking. He became hypervigilant in every new setting: the moment he walked into a room,
his eyes found the exits, and he would scan the faces of strangers—reading their posture, their eye
contact, the angle of their shoulders—looking for signs of threat. Any abrupt movement nearby would
make him flinch as though a blow were coming.

His marriage suffered. Sarah wanted to go to the movies, to dinner in busy restaurants, to the
Saturday farmers’ market. Michael would agree, then find a reason to cancel, or he would stay on the
periphery, tense and watchful, unable to enjoy himself. She started to feel shut out. At work, his
colleagues noticed he no longer volunteered to chaperone field trips or supervise the parking lot
after games. He told them he was tired, that his back was acting up. Inside, he felt trapped between
the memory and the shame of being afraid.

What finally brought Michael to seek help was a panic attack in the middle of a lesson—he had been
writing dates on the board, a student dropped a textbook, and the sudden noise sent him spinning
into a re-experiencing so vivid that he grabbed the edge of the desk and could not speak for nearly
a minute. The students stared. The next day, his department head asked if he was all right. He said
he was not. He scheduled an appointment that afternoon. He was afraid that if he told Sarah what was
really happening, she would see him as broken; but he was more afraid of staying the way he was,
scanning every face and checking every exit, waiting for the next sound that would send him back to
that parking lot.
