Leila was nineteen when her two-year-old son, Amir, woke from a nap with a fever so high that his
small body trembled. She drove him to the emergency room in the middle of the night, her boyfriend,
Tariq, silent in the passenger seat. By morning, doctors told them it was bacterial meningitis.
Leila remembers the exact sound of the word—how it slid out of the physician’s mouth and landed in
her chest like a stone. She spent the next ten days in a pediatric intensive care unit, watching
numbers on monitors, holding Amir’s hand through the rails of a crib bed. When he finally recovered,
everyone told her she had done everything right. But Leila could not stop replaying the hours before
the fever. She had thought it was just a cold. She had given him Tylenol and put him down for a nap.
That delay—those hours—felt like a failure she could never undo.

In the months after, Leila returned to her warehouse job, but her mind no longer cooperated. She
stood at a packing station, scanning barcodes and sealing boxes, and found herself unable to hold a
thought for more than a few seconds. Instructions from her supervisor slipped away the moment they
were spoken. She would reach for a roll of tape and then freeze, unable to remember what she had
been about to do. The warehouse's constant hum—conveyor belts, rolling carts, the beep of
scanners—seemed to press into her skull, making it impossible to focus. More than once she had to
re-count an entire pallet because she lost track halfway through.

Small, ordinary moments triggered something she could not control. A child coughing in the grocery
store made her heart lurch and her palms go slick. The sound of a siren, even blocks away, sent heat
rushing up her neck and a tightness across her chest so sudden she had to brace herself against a
wall. Each time, a wave of fear would hit, then horror at the memory of Amir limp and gray on the
hospital bed, followed by a sharp spike of anger at herself—for not noticing sooner, for being so
stupid—and then a thick blanket of shame. She had come to believe that these reactions proved she
was a weak person. Other mothers, she was certain, would have handled the crisis with calm strength.
They would not still be jumping at every cough months later. She told herself repeatedly that she
should have protected Amir from ever becoming that ill, and she had failed. No one else blamed her,
but Leila’s own mind had become a relentless prosecutor.

Tariq tried to reassure her, but his words only deepened her guilt. She could not shake the belief
that bad things were always waiting to happen, and that she was powerless to stop them. At work, she
began to dread certain aisles of the warehouse—the one near the breakroom where someone had once
left a half-empty bottle of children’s acetaminophen on a shelf. She did not avoid it deliberately;
she simply found herself taking longer routes without thinking. But avoidance was not the problem
she named when she finally scheduled an appointment at a community mental health clinic. What she
told the intake worker was that she could not concentrate well enough to keep her job, and that her
boyfriend had started to say she seemed distant and angry for no reason. She was afraid, she said,
that she would never feel like a capable mother again, and that this sense of helplessness would
follow her forever. She wanted the physical jolt to stop whenever she saw a thermometer. She wanted
to stop feeling like a fraud when someone told her she was strong. Mostly, she wanted to believe
that she might, eventually, be able to keep something bad from happening.
