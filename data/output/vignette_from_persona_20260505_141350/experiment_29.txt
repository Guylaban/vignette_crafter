somewhere behind his nose. He stayed in the tub for three hours until the water stopped rising, and
when help came he had to be pulled out because his legs wouldn’t work.

Three years later, Jake was nineteen and working the fry station at a Burger King on the edge of
town. He was a fast-food worker, and the job was supposed to be simple, but nothing felt simple
anymore. The smell that came off the wet mop when the night crew cleaned the floors was the worst—it
hit him sometimes when he was refilling the soda machine, and suddenly he was back in that bathroom,
water up to his knees, the roar in his ears. The images would flash in his mind: the doorframe
splintering, the line of water climbing the tile, the way the light turned gray as the window well
filled. And with the images came the same raw terror he had felt that day, his heart slamming, his
hands going cold. He learned to hold his breath when someone mopped nearby, to walk quickly past the
bleach station, to never look at the drain in the floor.

shift he rehearsed the conversations he might have, silently mouthing the lines to himself in the

interactions made his chest tighten. He rehearsed ordering coffee at the gas station, too, and

to drift. But the smells found him anyway, and the pictures came in the cracks between songs, and
the terror returned no matter how hard he squeezed his eyes shut.

laughed easily, who cracked jokes in the lunch line, who didn’t plan every sentence. That person

happy, and he was miserable, and he was certain the change was permanent. He had told himself a

never be fixed. He walked through his days waiting for the next trigger, rehearsing, avoiding,

What finally brought him to the clinic was his manager pulling him aside after a third week of poor
