Marcus is a 55-year-old house painter who has been married to his wife, Denise, for thirty years. On
a Tuesday afternoon two years ago, he was on a ladder at a client’s home, finishing the trim on a
second-story window, when his phone rang. It was his brother’s wife, and her voice was barely a
whisper: “You need to come. They’re taking him by ambulance.” He jumped off the ladder, left his
brushes and paint tray where they lay, and drove to the hospital. By the time he found the right
floor, his brother was gone—a sudden massive heart attack, no warning, no chance to say goodbye.
That drive to the hospital, the sight of the ambulance’s red lights pulling away from his brother’s
house, the sterile hallway where a doctor shook his head—those images have never left him. Sometimes
they come as sharp, vivid pictures: his brother’s face on a gurney, the fluorescent lights overhead,
the rain starting to fall as he walked back to his truck. Other times, the emotion hits him without
any image at all—a wave of grief so total it doubles him over, leaving him gasping with no clear
memory attached, just the raw knowledge that his brother is gone.

Since that day, Marcus has felt the constant hum of alertness in his body. His shoulders are tight,
his jaw clenched, and he startles at sudden noises—a car backfiring, a door slamming. At night, he
finds it impossible to settle. He checks the locks on every door three times, walks through the
house with a flashlight, and lies awake listening for the siren he dreads. Even inside his own home,
the place that once felt safest, he cannot relax. He sits with his back to the wall in the living
room, scanning the windows, certain that something terrible is about to happen again. His wife has
asked him why he seems so jumpy, why he won’t sit beside her on the couch, and he has no words that
make sense to her.

His work as a painter has become a minefield. He cannot bring himself to return to the house where
he got the call—the client had to hire someone else to finish the job. Any mention of someone losing
a loved one, whether on the radio, at the dinner table, or from a friend, makes his stomach drop. He
excuses himself from conversations, walks out of rooms, changes the channel. Once, at a family
gathering, his cousin started talking about a coworker whose father had passed away, and Marcus
stood up and left without a word, driving home in silence. He has come to believe that he is no
longer the same person. He used to be the one who kept the family together, the steady, easygoing
man everyone relied on. Now he feels hollow, as if his brother took a part of him into the grave. He
feels isolated even when surrounded by people, convinced that no one understands what it means to
have your life split into before and after. A part of him is certain his life has been destroyed,
that he will never feel whole again.

The consequences have accumulated. Denise has grown quiet and hurt, unsure how to reach him. Marcus
has stopped taking new painting jobs that require travel or unfamiliar locations; he works only for
old clients who know his situation, but even then he rushes through the job, eager to get home
before dark. His income has dropped, and he has started refusing invitations to barbecues, church
functions, anything where the conversation might drift toward loss or where an ambulance might pass
by. He spends his evenings in the garage, organizing and reorganizing his paint cans, doing nothing
really, just waiting for the next wave of grief or the next sound that will undo him.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was the night he found himself standing in the driveway at
2 a.m., fully dressed, keys in hand, ready to drive to the hospital because he thought he heard
sirens. There were no sirens. Denise found him there, crying without knowing why, and she told him
quietly that she was afraid for him. He is afraid too—afraid that the person he used to be is gone
for good, that the images and the grief will never soften, that he will spend the rest of his life
jumped up and waiting for the next loss. But somewhere underneath that fear, he holds a thin thread
of hope that talking about it might loosen the grip, that he might learn to sleep through the night
again, that he might one day remember his brother without being crushed by the remembering.
