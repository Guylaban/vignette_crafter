Nadia is a 34-year-old agricultural worker who came to the United States from her home country a
decade ago, hoping for a fresh start after a difficult divorce. She now lives alone in a small
apartment near the farming cooperative where she works six days a week. For the past two years, she
has been unable to shake the memory of what she witnessed one afternoon at a local market. A man
walked up to a young mother pushing a stroller and, without a word, shot her in the chest. The woman
collapsed; the baby began to wail. Nadia was ten feet away, frozen, watching the blood spread on the
pavement. She still remembers the sound of that infant’s cry—sharp, insistent, and then muffled as
someone scooped the child up and ran. The shooter vanished into the crowd before anyone could stop
him.

In the weeks afterward, Nadia told herself it was over and she should move on. She forced herself to
think only happy thoughts—about the harvest, about the quiet evenings she used to enjoy. But the
memory kept intruding. She would be sorting tomatoes in the field and suddenly hear that baby’s cry,
even when no baby was near. She began to notice that whenever a coworker’s child visited the packing
shed, her heart would hammer and she would have to walk away. She stopped visiting the market
entirely. She also started seeing threat in ordinary behaviour: a man lingering near her car, a
woman glancing over her shoulder—anyone acting even slightly suspicious felt like a potential
killer. Nadia became hypervigilant of her surroundings, but she told herself she was just being
careful.

At work, she developed a rigid routine. She cleans her tools and worktable compulsively, scrubbing
each surface three times before she feels able to proceed. She tells herself it is about hygiene,
but she knows deep down it is the only thing that calms the knot in her stomach. She also works hard
to suppress any memory of the shooting. If an image surfaces, she immediately pictures something
neutral—a blue sky, a field of wheat—and repeats positive affirmations: “I am safe. I am strong. The
past is gone.” This works for moments, but the effort exhausts her.

Her relationships have suffered. She no longer trusts people; she is convinced that everyone hides a
dangerous side. When coworkers discuss local news or a violent event, she abruptly changes the topic
or leaves the room. She avoids any conversation that could circle back to what she saw. Her faith,
once a source of comfort, feels hollow. She used to pray regularly, but now she cannot bring herself
to believe in a higher power that allowed such cruelty. She feels she no longer belongs anywhere—not
in her community, not in her own skin. Her divorce had already left her isolated, but this event
sealed her off entirely. She stopped accepting invitations to family gatherings, saying she was too
busy.

The consequences have piled up. At work, her supervisor noticed she was avoiding certain tasks that
required her to interact with customers who had children. She started leaving early, citing
headaches. She lost a chance at a promotion because she could not handle the extra responsibility.
Last month, a baby started crying in the next row of crops, and Nadia dropped a crate of melons and
ran to the bathroom, shaking. She knew then that she could no longer manage alone.

What finally brought her to seek help was a referral from the clinic after she reported insomnia and
stomach pain. She sat in the intake office with her hands folded tightly, expecting the therapist to
tell her she was overreacting. Instead, she admitted, in a quiet voice, that she was terrified of
hearing a baby cry again, and even more terrified that one day she might stop running and just
freeze forever. She is hoping to find a way to live without the constant vigilance, but she is also
afraid that talking about it will make the memory stronger. She does not know which is worse: the
silence she has built around herself, or the noise she has been trying so hard to keep out.
