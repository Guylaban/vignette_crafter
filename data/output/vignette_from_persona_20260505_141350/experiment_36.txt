Margaret, a sixty-five-year-old widow and former military medic, still feels the weight of that
afternoon twelve years ago as though it were happening in the present. She had been riding in the
passenger seat of a Humvee, returning from a routine supply run on a dusty forward-operating base
road in Afghanistan. The driver, a young specialist named Torres, swerved to avoid a child darting
across the road, and the vehicle flipped twice before landing on its side. Margaret was pinned
between the crumpled door and her seat, her left arm trapped under the weight of the overturned
vehicle. She could hear Torres screaming, could smell diesel fuel and hot metal, and for what felt
like an eternity she was certain the vehicle would ignite. What she remembers most vividly is the
sound—the screech of tearing metal, then the deafening silence afterward, broken only by her own
ragged breathing and the distant crackle of a radio. She survived, Torres did not, and she was
medevacked with a fractured arm and a deep, invisible wound that would not heal.

In the months and years that followed, Margaret began to notice changes she could not name. Every
time she drove past a sedan that matched the sand-colored Humvee’s shape, her chest would tighten
and she would grip the steering wheel until her knuckles whitened. She found herself scanning
parking lots for similar vehicles, bracing herself for the impact that never came. The sight of any
car that had been in a collision—even a minor fender bender shown on the evening news—would trigger
a rush of images: the dashboard tilting, the sky and ground swapping places. She became deeply
uncomfortable in any vehicle, preferring to walk even when distances were unmanageable, though she
lived in a suburban area with few sidewalks. At home, she started eating large portions of whatever
was in the pantry, not out of hunger but because the act of chewing and swallowing seemed to drown
out the intrusive images for a few minutes. She developed a habit of turning on the television or a
podcast the moment she walked through the door, filling the silence with voices that kept her mind
busy. And she tried, with fierce determination, to push thoughts of the accident away whenever they
surfaced, telling herself it was over, that she was safe now.

But the memory refused to stay buried. When it returned, it came not as a story she could tell but
as a physical reliving: she would feel the pressure on her chest, taste the dust, hear Torres’s
voice as if he were in the room. At such moments, she could not find words to describe what had
happened—the experience overwhelmed her so completely that she had to stop whatever she was doing,
sometimes standing frozen in the middle of a grocery aisle, sometimes sitting on the edge of her bed
with her hands over her ears. Her body remained in a perpetual state of readiness. Unexpected
sounds—a car backfiring, a door slamming, a dropped pan in the kitchen—sent a bolt of alarm through
her; she would find herself scanning the room for an exit, her muscles tense, her heart hammering.
She had come to believe that danger was always lurking, that if she let her guard down for a moment,
something terrible would happen again. That conviction kept her vigilant, kept her body braced, kept
her from ever truly relaxing.

The consequences crept into her life slowly. She had worked as a medic in the military for over
twenty years, but after the accident she retired early, claiming her age and her late husband’s
death as reasons. In truth, she could not bear the thought of being in a moving vehicle with other
people, especially not in an emergency context where she might need to act quickly. Her
relationships narrowed. She stopped accepting invitations to dinner with friends who lived more than
a few miles away, and she rarely visited her daughter in the next state because the highway drive
felt impossible. Meals became a solitary ritual of overeating in front of the television, followed
by guilt and a vague sense of shame. She began to feel as though she was disappearing into her own
home, a woman who had once responded to crises now unable to walk past a parked sedan without
flinching.

It was her daughter, on a rare visit, who finally pushed her to seek help. She had found Margaret
sitting on the kitchen floor, tears streaming down her face, unable to explain why. Margaret had
tried to describe the accident but could only shake her head, the words stuck somewhere between her
throat and her chest. Now she sits across from a clinician, uncertain what she hopes for—only that
she cannot keep living this way, that the constant alertness and the sudden flashbacks are wearing
her thin. What frightens her most is the thought that if she stops suppressing and avoiding, the
memory might swallow her whole. Yet she also knows, somewhere beneath the fear, that she is already
being swallowed, one trigger at a time.
