Linh is a 48-year-old Vietnamese American woman who works as a private tutor, mostly for elementary
school children. She came to therapy at her husband’s urging, though she is not sure anything can
help. She is certain she has been permanently changed for the worse, and that it is only a matter of
time before someone harms her again.

The event that shattered her happened twelve years ago, when she was still with her previous
partner, a man she had been dating for two years. He had a temper, but she never expected what
happened that night. A small argument over money escalated, and he shoved her into the kitchen wall.
She hit the back of her head and slid to the floor. While she was down, he grabbed her by the hair
and forced her face to the ground. In the corner of her vision she saw a large chef’s knife on the
counter, and she was certain he would pick it up. He did not. He left her there, bleeding from a
split lip, and she managed to crawl to her phone and call a friend. That was the last time she saw
him.

The memory comes to her in disconnected pieces that do not fit together in time. She sees the knife
as if it were a photograph, a still image she can bring up any time, but she cannot recall how she
got from the wall to the floor, only the impact. When the memory surfaces, so does the same terror
she felt at that moment: her chest tightens, her hands go cold, and she feels the absolute certainty
that she is about to die. It is not a memory; it is a re-living.

In the years since, Linh has built a life of careful vigilance. She married a kind, quiet man named
Tom, but she has never been able to trust that he, or anyone, is safe. She has come to believe that
you can never know who will harm you, and that anyone could be dangerous. She is constantly scanning
every environment she enters for signs of threat. In a tutoring center, she notices potential
weapons everywhere: a pair of scissors on a desk, a heavy stapler, even a metal ruler. She does not
consider this paranoid; she considers it realistic. Her body reacts before her mind has time to
think. A door slamming shut makes her flinch and her heart race before she registers that it was
only the wind. She cannot stop it.

She checks mirrors compulsively. In her car, she glances at the rearview mirror repeatedly to make
sure no one is behind her. In public bathrooms, she checks the mirror to see if someone has entered
behind her. At home, she positions herself so that she can see the front door from the kitchen, and
she avoids sitting with her back to a room. She is hypervigilant toward people, especially men who
remind her of her ex-partner: tall, broad-shouldered, quick to raise their voice. She has become an
expert at reading a person’s build and body language from across a room.

Her work as a tutor has been heavily affected. She only accepts female students or boys under the
age of ten, because older boys and men make her too anxious to concentrate. This limits her client
base and reduces her income, but she cannot force herself to take a male teenager. She insists on
meeting at a public library and always chooses a table with her back to a wall and a clear view of
the entrance. During sessions, she finds herself looking at the child’s backpack, then checking the
nearby tables for anything that could be used as a weapon. One afternoon a father showed up early to
pick up his daughter, and when he entered the reading room without warning, Linh startled so badly
she dropped her cup of tea and felt dizzy for ten minutes. The girl’s mother gave her a strange
look.

Linh feels like a complete outsider now, even among people who have known her for years. She used to
be talkative and warm; now she is guarded and withdrawn. Tom’s family invites her to gatherings, but
she cannot relax. She watches from the corner of the room, monitoring who comes in and out, and she
often leaves early. She has come to believe that she is fundamentally different from others, that
the abuse broke something inside her that can never be repaired. She does not talk about it because
she assumes no one would understand.

What finally brought her to therapy was a conversation with her husband after a particularly bad
week. She had screamed at him for coming up behind her in the kitchen, and afterward she saw the
hurt in his eyes. He said he could not live like this anymore. She agreed to come, but she is not
hopeful. The part of her that still wants to believe she can feel safe is the same part that she
learned to silence a long time ago. She is terrified that if she lets her guard down, she will be
hurt again — and she is equally terrified of living the rest of her life this way.
