fall light and sirens. She had been pushing three-year-old Liam in a stroller through the park that
ran along the edge of the neighborhood where his parents lived, the one with the tall iron gates and
the stone fountain that the children liked to toss pennies into. Two men had come out of the trees
near the gazebo, shouting, and before Mona could pull the stroller around, one of them pulled a gun
and shot the other twice in the chest. She had dropped to her knees, shielding Liam with her own
body, her hand pressed over his eyes so hard he cried. The shooter ran. The other man died there, on
the gravel, a few feet from the stroller. Mona later learned his name from the news—a store clerk, a
father of three—and that the police never made an arrest.

In the months that followed, Mona found herself unable to go near that park. She took a different
route to the grocery store, even though it added twenty minutes to her walk. If the family she
worked for suggested a picnic or playground, she made excuses—her knees, her back—and offered to
stay home with the baby instead. She began agreeing to everything anyone asked of her, nodding when
she wanted to say no, taking extra shifts she did not want, because it felt safer to keep people
happy. Conflict, she had come to believe, could turn a moment ordinary into a moment deadly. She
tried hard not to think about the shooting, but the images came anyway—the sound of the gun, the
gravel dust, the way Liam’s small body had trembled against her chest. When a thought surfaced, she
would turn on the television or start scrubbing a counter, anything to keep her mind on something
else.

At the same time, Mona became acutely aware of the world behind her. She checked over her shoulder
constantly—waiting for a bus, unlocking her car, walking from the front door to the garage. She
watched people’s hands, their eyes, the way they stood too close in lines. Every man in a hoodie,
every sudden move on a sidewalk, made her stomach drop. She began scanning faces for the specific
kind of anger she had seen just before the shots—a tightening around the mouth, a fixed stare. At
work, she found herself studying the parents of the children she cared for, reading their moods for
any hint of volatility. She had come to believe that if she could just see danger coming early
enough, she might keep someone safe this time.

pulled Liam away faster, should have yelled, should have thrown herself between the shooter and the
victim. She was a nanny, a protector—that was what she was supposed to be. Instead she had frozen,
shielded, survived. The men who owned the park, the police who never found the shooter, the system
that let the killer walk free—none of them had faced a single consequence. Mona saw the unfairness
everywhere, and it made her feel small, hollow, as though she had no real power in the world because
she was just an old woman, just a nanny, just someone who had failed at the one thing that mattered.

her own mind felt like a room she could not leave. She was afraid of what might happen if she let
