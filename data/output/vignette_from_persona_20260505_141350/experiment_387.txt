Amara is a thirty-three-year-old park ranger who, fourteen months ago, lost her younger brother Kofi
in the backcountry of the very park where she works. They had been hiking a remote trail she knew
well—one she had patrolled for years—when a section of unstable cliff face gave way beneath his
feet. She watched him fall nearly sixty feet, and by the time she rappelled down to him, he was
barely conscious. He died in her arms before the search-and-rescue team could reach them, his head
trauma too severe. She stayed with his body for two hours, holding him, listening to the wind.

In the months that followed, Amara found that the memory did not arrive as a story she could tell.
It arrived in pieces. She would be standing at the kitchen counter and suddenly see his face in that
moment—not the whole scene, just that one photograph-quality image, so clear it made her gasp. Then
it would vanish, leaving her disoriented and flooded with a grief so raw it felt like the event was
still happening. Other times, what surfaced was the sound of the rock breaking, or the weight of his
head in her lap, each fragment as sharp and still as a photograph projected onto her mind. When
these images came, the grief that followed was not a memory of sadness but the same overwhelming
despair she had felt on that cliff, undiminished by time.

She had come to believe, quietly and without ever saying it aloud, that she was no longer the person
who could do this job. She had always been the ranger other people called when something went
wrong—steady, quick, dependable. Now she was certain that whatever had defined that version of
herself was gone. When she looked in the mirror, she saw someone hollow, someone who had been
permanently damaged by what she had witnessed. She told herself she felt dead inside, and the
numbness frightened her more than the grief did, because at least the grief felt like something.

Amara stopped taking backcountry assignments. She told her supervisor she preferred front-country
duties for a while, and she meant it—she could not imagine returning to the trail where Kofi had
died. But the avoidance spread. She began to avoid the hiking trails entirely, even the easy ones on
the opposite side of the park. She stopped leading the weekly guided nature walks she had once
loved. She gave up her role on the search-and-rescue rotation without explanation, and her
colleagues noticed. She had always been reliable; now she was the ranger who left early, who
deflected questions, who sat in the visitor center with her radio turned low and her eyes fixed on
the door.

At home, she had stopped relaxing. Even in the evenings, when her husband Marcus would sit down to
watch television or read, Amara could not join him. Relaxing felt dangerous, as though letting her
guard down invited something terrible to happen. She kept her mind occupied constantly—podcasts
during her commute, music while she cooked, a running internal monologue during any quiet moment.
The silence was unbearable because the silence was where the photographs appeared. At night, sleep
came slowly, if it came at all. Her body stayed tense and ready, even in bed, as though the cliff
were still beneath her feet and the next step could be the one that gave way.

She experienced strong negative emotions throughout her days—fear that she would never feel like
herself again, anger at herself for not reaching Kofi faster, shame that she could not do her job
anymore. Her body felt constantly on alert. Her shoulders stayed tight, her jaw clenched, her
breathing shallow. She scanned the trails around the visitor center not with the calm attention she
had once had, but with a vigilance that exhausted her. Every loose stone on the path, every uneven
patch of ground, every hiker who walked too close to an edge made her pulse race.

The consequence that finally pressed her toward help was both occupational and relational. Her
supervisor had gently suggested she take a desk reassignment until she was "feeling more like
herself," and Amara had agreed immediately—too quickly, she now realized. She had been working the
front desk for three months. She no longer felt like a park ranger. And at home, Marcus had started
sleeping in the guest room because she would wake him at all hours, thrashing or crying out, and she
could see in his face that he did not know how to reach her anymore. Last week, while driving home
from a shift where she had done nothing but stamp entry passes, she had realized that she could not
remember the last time she had felt present in her own life. That was what finally brought her to
the clinic: not the memory fragments or the vigilance or the guilt, but the quiet certainty that she
was watching herself disappear and did not know how to stop it. She said she wanted to feel like
herself again, but she was afraid she had already changed too much to ever go back.
