one, and on that trip he insisted they take a canoe out on the reservoir just north of town. Kwame
had been uneasy—the sky was gray, the water a flat, steely sheet—but he agreed. Halfway across, a
sudden squall overturned the canoe. Kwame, a strong swimmer, made it to shore. Moses did not. By the
time rescue crews found him, it was too late.

In the immediate aftermath, Kwame did what he had always done: he returned to work, trusting that
routine would restore him. But the world had changed. He found himself unable to sit in the faculty

himself, and his throat would close. Worse was water. The campus had a small ornamental pond near
the humanities building, and Kwame now took a half-mile detour every morning to avoid it. He had
come to believe that if he even saw a puddle after a rainstorm, he would lose control—his heart

canoe.
