At thirty-eight, Mai had spent more than a decade as a welder, her hands steady and her eye precise,
working in a fabrication shop outside Portland. The accident happened on a Tuesday afternoon. She
was welding a steel beam, her visor down, the arc hissing and spitting. A coworker named Tom stepped
into her work zone without warning—she never heard him approach. When she turned, the torch
followed, and the flame caught the sleeve of his flannel shirt. The fabric ignited instantly. She
remembered the sound of his scream, the smell of burning cotton and skin, and then a taste in her
mouth—sharp, metallic, like hot copper and smoke, a taste that would return without warning in the
months that followed, coating her tongue even when she was nowhere near a shop.

What happened next was fragmented. She recalled dropping the torch, pulling off her gloves,
shouting. But the few seconds between the flame catching and Tom collapsing on the concrete floor
were missing, a blank space she could not fill no matter how many times she tried. She knew she had
dialed 911. She knew she had knelt beside him, his burns already blistering, and that her hands had
been shaking so badly she could barely press a towel to his arm. But the sequence was wrong, the
details scattered, and that incomplete picture haunted her more than the parts she could see. Every
time the memory surfaced, guilt flooded through her chest like a wave—hot, suffocating, absolute.
She had done this. She had turned without looking, without warning. She had known better.

In the weeks after, Mai tried to push the event out of her mind. She avoided thinking about it
deliberately, filling her days with mechanical tasks—cleaning her apartment, organizing
drawers—anything to keep her thoughts busy. But the rumination slipped in anyway, especially at
night. Lying awake, she would replay the moment over and over, searching for what she had done
wrong. Why hadn't she checked her blind spot? Why hadn't she called out that she was about to move?
She dwelled on every mistake, turning them over like stones in her hands, each one heavier than the
last. Sleep became a war. She would drift off only to jolt awake with the taste in her mouth, her
heart hammering, the bedroom dark and silent but her body convinced she was back in the shop.

She stopped going to work. At first she told her supervisor she needed a few days, then a week, then
she simply stopped answering the phone. The shop was full of people she had worked with for years,
but she could not face them. She was certain that if she walked in, everyone would be staring at
her, that they all knew exactly what she had done—and worse, that they were right to judge her.
Avoidance spread beyond the shop. She stopped seeing friends, stopped returning calls from her
mother, who lived three states away. When she had to go to the grocery store, she chose late hours
when the aisles were empty, and if she saw anyone she vaguely recognized, she ducked into another
aisle or left without buying anything. She had come to believe that being around people was
dangerous—not because they might hurt her, but because she might hurt them. What frightened her most
was the possibility that she was fundamentally careless, that the accident had revealed something
true about her that she could not undo.

Concentration became a problem. She would start a task—folding laundry, paying a bill—and find
herself staring at the same point for ten minutes, the guilt so loud in her head that she could not
follow a single thought through to completion. Physical reactions came without warning: a gust of
wind from a passing truck could make her flinch and start sweating; the hiss of a steam radiator
sent her pulse racing. She had not touched her welding equipment since the accident, and she could
not even enter the garage of her apartment building because the smell of oil and metal triggered a
cascade of sensations—the heat of the torch, the taste, the scream.

By the time she finally called a therapist, she had been unemployed for four months, her savings
nearly gone, and her isolation so complete that she had not spoken to another person face-to-face in
over two weeks. She said she did not know if she wanted to feel better—she was not sure she deserved
to—but she could not keep living with the taste in her mouth and the missing seconds and the weight
of what she had done. She was terrified that talking about it would only make the memories sharper,
more real, but she was more terrified of staying like this: a woman who had once built things, now
unable to hold anything steady.
