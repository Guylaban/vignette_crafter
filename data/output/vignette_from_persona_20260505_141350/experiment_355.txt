circling, she sat beside his body and thought, *This is my fault. I did something wrong. Somebody
else would have saved him.*

That belief did not fade. It calcified over the months after she returned stateside, honorably
discharged. Aisha moved into a small apartment and started her counseling program, but she found
herself avoiding the classmates and friends she had known before deployment—people who might ask

had caused the sergeant to die. She stopped watching television entirely, because a news segment
about the war or a movie with a helicopter scene would send her heart slamming into her ribs. She
started leaving lights on in every room at night, because darkness made her feel the weight of that
riverbed again, the smell of blood and dust, the sound of the rotors bearing down.

client mentions family tensions or a parent’s disappointment, because any comment on family makes

capable of doing the things that defined me.*

dating feels impossible—how would she explain what she did, what she failed to do? She imagines the
look on someone’s face if they learned the truth, and she knows they would see it too: the flaw, the
failure. Her world has shrunk to a one-bedroom apartment, a few shifts at the clinic, and the
careful management of her triggers. She drives a different route to work to avoid a stretch of road

to settle down?” and the question feels like an accusation.

living this small, this afraid. She wants to stop feeling that she caused everything bad that
happened. She wants to believe she might be capable again. But when she walks into the therapist’s
office, what she is most afraid of is that the therapist will nod and think, *Yes, there is
something wrong with her,* and that will confirm what Aisha has known all along.
