Ravi is a 41-year-old graphic designer who has been married for twelve years. He comes to therapy
because his wife told him she cannot watch him like this anymore—always looking over his shoulder,
never agreeing to weekend plans, and spending hours in his home office rehearsing conversations with
clients out loud before he can bring himself to call them. The event that changed everything
happened eighteen months ago, during a routine traffic stop on his way home from a late deadline at
the studio.

He was driving through a suburban intersection when a patrol car’s lights flashed behind him. Ravi
pulled over, certain it was a mistake—he had not been speeding, his registration was current. The
officer approached, a young man in a crisp uniform, and asked for his license and registration. Ravi
handed them over and began to explain that he had just come from work, that he was a graphic
designer, that perhaps there was some confusion. The officer’s tone shifted rapidly. He accused Ravi
of being argumentative, ordered him out of the car, and within seconds Ravi was on the pavement, his
face against the asphalt, his hands cuffed behind his back. He heard a sharp click and then a
burning, acrid smell filled his nostrils—something electrical, like ozone and melted plastic. Later
he learned it was a taser being deployed against someone else at a nearby stop, but in that moment
he believed it was for him. He lay there, unable to speak, while the officer berated him. The
betrayal was absolute: he had trusted that uniform, that authority, and it had turned on him without
cause.

After the incident, Ravi was released with a warning, but he did not feel released. The memory
arrives not as a story he can tell but as a wave of guilt—he should have said something different,
should have stayed calmer, should have prevented the whole thing. Yet when he tries to describe what
happened to his wife or to a therapist, the words vanish. He knows exactly what took place, but his
mouth goes dry and his mind blanks, and he trails off mid-sentence, unable to finish. The burnt
smell returns without warning—from a passing car’s exhaust, from a neighbor’s grill—and he freezes,
heart pounding, scanning for a uniform that might be coming for him.

Ravi’s work as a graphic designer once meant collaborating with clients, presenting ideas, and
sketching out future campaigns. Now he refuses to plan anything beyond the immediate week. Making a
future plan feels like inviting disaster: if he commits to a vacation or a project deadline,
something terrible will happen. He spends hours in his home office, role-playing every possible
conversation with a client before he can press dial. At the local coffee shop he keeps his back to
the wall and watches the door, cataloging anyone who might be a dangerous person—someone in cargo
pants, someone with a stern expression, anyone who moves too quickly. When he sees a police car, he
turns down a side street or pulls into a parking lot until it passes. His wife has noticed he no
longer visits his brother, who lives near a precinct.

What finally brought Ravi to seek help was a moment in a grocery store. A security guard in a blue
uniform walked past the cereal aisle, and Ravi dropped his basket and walked out without buying
anything. He stood in the parking lot, shaking, unable to go back inside. That night his wife told
him he could not go on like this. Ravi agreed, but what he is most afraid of is that he will never
trust anyone again—that the world really is as dangerous as it feels now, and that the people he
thought were safe were never safe at all. He hopes, cautiously, that talking about it might somehow
give him back the words he lost that night on the pavement.
