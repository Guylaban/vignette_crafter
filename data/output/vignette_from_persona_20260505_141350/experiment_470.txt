Priya, a 32-year-old chaplain at a community hospital, first came to the clinic after her self-
harming behaviors became impossible to hide. She had been assigned to a humanitarian mission in a
conflict zone three years earlier, where she provided spiritual comfort to displaced families. One
afternoon, armed men in military fatigues stormed the aid compound, separated her from the group,
and held her in a concrete bunker for several days. They restrained her wrists with zip ties so
tight they left deep bruises. They used sound deliberately—loud metal scraping, a generator humming,
boots thudding on concrete—to disorient her. When they finally released her, she was ordered to
leave the region immediately.

In the months that followed, Priya returned to the United States. Her marriage, already strained by
the deployment, ended in divorce. She began her current role as a hospital chaplain, but the job
places her in a constant state of hypervigilance. Uniformed hospital security officers trigger an
immediate freeze response; she has learned to memorize their schedules so she can walk different
corridors. Hospital equipment—the beeping of monitors, the hiss of oxygen tanks, the clatter of
metal trays—sends her heart racing, because those sounds remind her of the bunker where she could
not predict what would happen next. During a recent physical exam, when a nurse gently held her arm
to take blood pressure, Priya felt the restraint wrap around her wrist and nearly vomited.

She has also come to dread open spaces. The hospital’s main lobby has a large glass ceiling; she
enters through a side door every morning. On weekends she cannot bring herself to walk through the
park that was once her favorite route to the grocery store. She is certain that if she were exposed
in an open area she would be seen and captured again. To avoid this fear, she leaves her apartment
only for essential errands, and takes back alleys and underground parking garages whenever possible.

The memory of the torture remains fragmented and wordless. When a coworker once asked her what
happened “over there,” Priya opened her mouth but found only the shape of panic. She knows the
facts—where it happened, the faces of the men—but the sequence slips away. Her mind shows her
images: a concrete floor, a harsh light, the feel of the zip ties. But when she tries to explain,
she loses the thread, her sentences collapse, and she stops mid‑attempt. She has come to believe
that if she cannot put it into words, then she is broken in a way that no one can understand.

The avoidance of social contact has hollowed out her life. Before the torture, she had a close
circle of friends from her church and from the humanitarian organization. Now she screens calls,
cancels plans, and tells herself that these people belong to a version of her that no longer exists.
She stopped attending services altogether. The isolation leaves her alone with the intrusive
thoughts, and when they become unbearable, she cuts the inside of her thigh with a razor blade. The
pain, she tells herself, is something she can control; it forces the memories to pause.

What finally brought Priya to the clinic was a shift during which she saw a hospital security guard
rounding a corner and completely lost her ability to speak. She stood frozen in the hallway for
nearly two minutes, unable to move or respond to a colleague’s question. Later that night, she cut
deeper than usual and had to bandage herself. She realized she could not keep her job—or her
sanity—if she continued this way. She hopes that talking might help her find the words that have
eluded her, but she is terrified that putting the event into language will make it more real, more
permanent. For now, she is willing to try.
