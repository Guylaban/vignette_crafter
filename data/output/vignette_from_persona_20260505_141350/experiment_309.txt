Robert O’Malley, a sixty-eight-year-old state senator, had spent his career convincing people he was
in control. The autumn night that shattered that certainty began at a campaign fundraiser in a hotel
ballroom. He’d had one glass of red wine, then driven home through a light snow. On a curve he knew
well, the car hit black ice. For a moment the world seemed to slow—the headlights sweeping toward a
bare oak, the passenger-side door crumpling inward—and then there was only pressure and the smell of
his own blood mixing with the wine he’d spilled across his shirt. He was trapped for forty minutes,
conscious the whole time, believing his wife would become a widow. Surgeons rebuilt three ribs and
part of his pelvis; the phantom pain in those bones still arrives unannounced.

Months later, Robert cannot let the story leave his mouth. When a colleague asks what happened, he
starts with “I was driving home,” then stops. The next image appears—the tree rushing toward him—and
the words dissolve. He feels a hot ache in his side, as if the metal is still pressing. The memory
refuses to be narrated; it comes instead as a series of snapshots: the glass spiderwebbing, the
steering wheel jammed against his chest, the silence after the engine died. That silence haunts him
most. An empty hallway at the capitol building or the quiet in his study after midnight makes his
pulse spike. He has come to believe that stillness means something terrible is about to happen, so
he keeps a television on in the bedroom, or he wakes at 2 A.M. and walks the house, checking locks,
making sure his wife is breathing.

The smell of alcohol undoes him completely. At a dinner with donors last spring, a woman’s cabernet
glass was refilled near his elbow, and he had to excuse himself to the restroom, panting, gripping
the sink. He now asks his wife to scan restaurant tables before they sit down. He has stopped
attending fundraisers altogether; his chief of staff explains away the absences as health issues.
Driving is impossible. He cannot be behind the wheel, and he flinches when his wife accelerates
through a yellow light. She drives him everywhere now, a role she never wanted and one that has
frayed their marriage. He sees the fatigue in her face and tells himself he is a burden, that people
like him cause their own disasters.

Robert has come to believe that the accident happened because of the sort of person he is—someone
who cuts corners, who chose to drive after a drink, who was too arrogant to wait for the roads to be
salted. He replays the evening constantly, searching for the moment he could have chosen
differently, and each time he arrives at the same verdict: he failed. The rumination is a loop he
cannot break. At his desk in the office, he stares at a bill summary for twenty minutes without
absorbing a word; his concentration has become so poor that staff members now double-check his notes
before they go to committee.

He notices that others seem to see it too. In meetings he feels that senators across the table can
read the whole night on his face—the fear, the blame, the broken ribs. He keeps his guard up,
scanning rooms for exits, watching for any sudden movement. The hypervigilance exhausts him, and yet
lowering it feels like inviting the next crash.

What finally brought Robert to a therapist’s office was a hearing last month. He was supposed to
testify on a transportation bill, but when he walked into the hearing room the smell of
coffee—something about the heat and the steam—turned into the smell of wine and blood. He froze,
turned around, and left without a word. His wife told him that if he would not get help for himself,
he should do it for the people who still depended on him. He came to this intake because he is
terrified that he will lose his career, his marriage, and any sense of who he used to be. He hopes,
though he cannot say it aloud, to stop feeling like the accident is still happening to him, right
now, every day.
