David is a 52-year-old married man who has spent the last sixteen years as principal of a suburban
middle school. Eight months ago, during what should have been a routine disciplinary meeting, a
father whose son had been suspended for fighting became agitated in David’s office. The man stood up
abruptly, swept a heavy brass paperweight from David’s desk, and struck him twice across the face
before school security arrived. David does not remember the seconds immediately after the second
blow—his deliberately recalled memory of that afternoon is fragmented, as though a curtain has been
drawn across the middle of the film. Yet the film itself plays without his permission. At
unpredictable moments, he sees the man’s reddened face looming toward him; he feels the shock of the
first impact, then a strange blankness where the second should be. When these images tear through
his mind, his body locks into the position he was in that day: half-risen from his chair, left hand
reaching for the phone, shoulders braced against the window behind him. Even now, months later, if a
memory surfaces during a faculty meeting, he will catch himself gripping the armrests of his
conference chair, knees pressed together, as if waiting for another blow.

David’s daily environment has become a landscape of perceived threats. Every door he enters triggers
an automatic sweep: where are the exits, is this route obstructed, could I get out if someone came
through that entrance. In his own office, which he once considered a sanctuary, he has rearranged
the furniture so his desk faces the door and his chair is backed against a wall with a clear path to
the side exit. He notices potential weapons everywhere. During a recent tour of the art room, he
found himself staring at the scissors on the supply counter, the X‑Acto knives, the sharpened edge
of a metal ruler. He had to step into the hallway to calm his racing heart. He has come to believe
that any room can become a trap if he is not constantly alert, and that his survival depends on
knowing, at every second, where the nearest weapon might be hidden and how fast he could reach the
door.

At home, his wife, Sarah, has watched him change. He startles violently when she walks up behind him
in the kitchen, and he has begun insisting that she announce herself before entering a room. He no
longer watches the evening news because a story about a school incident would send his chest tight
and his hands shaking. He has stopped attending weekend barbecues with friends, telling Sarah he
needs quiet time, though what he actually fears is being in a backyard full of people where he
cannot see all the exits at once. His work has narrowed. He now delegates every difficult parent
meeting to his assistant principal, fabricating reasons about scheduling conflicts. When he must
walk through the school’s main hallway during class changes, he keeps his back to the wall and scans
the faces of passing students, looking for the flash of anger he saw in that father’s eyes.

What finally brought David to seek help was a moment in the school parking lot two weeks ago. A
staff member accidentally dropped a stack of metal trays near his car. The clatter sent David diving
behind his vehicle, heart hammering, ready to fight or flee. A group of eighth graders saw him;
later, he overheard them laughing about how the principal had “freaked out.” The humiliation,
layered over the exhaustion of constant scanning and the numbing replays of the assault, pushed him
to call his physician for a referral. He told Sarah he was going “to talk to someone about the
anxiety,” but what he did not say is that he is terrified the memories will never stop looping, and
that he cannot imagine ever feeling safe in his own school again. What he hopes—though he cannot
quite bring himself to believe it—is that a therapist can help him find a way to walk into a room
without counting the exits, and to sit in his office without feeling the phantom weight of a
paperweight in his hands.
