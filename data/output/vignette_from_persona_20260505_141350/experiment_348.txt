Moana came home from the hospital in late autumn, after seventeen days in the ICU for a rare
bacterial pneumonia that had nearly stopped her breathing twice. She was a singer—had been since
childhood, leading the church choir in her Samoan community, later performing with a local band on
weekends—and the first thing she noticed when she walked through her front door was the way the air
felt wrong. Not thin or heavy, just wrong, as though her lungs were listening for something that
wasn't there. She stood in the kitchen, one hand on the counter, and waited for her chest to
tighten. It did not. But she could not shake the feeling that it would.

In the months that followed, Moana learned to read her own body like a threat. A slight catch in her
breath while climbing stairs sent her heart racing. The smell of antiseptic soap in a public
restroom made her nauseous and alert, her palms sweating before she could name why. She began
cancelling gigs that required long vocal sets, telling her bandmates she was still rebuilding her
stamina. What she did not tell them was that she had started to believe she did not deserve to sing
again—that the illness had been a kind of punishment for something she could not name, and that her
survival meant she was simply postponing the real reckoning. The thought came to her most clearly at
night: you got sick because you are not strong enough, and now you are a lousy coper because you
cannot even breathe without panicking.

Her husband, Tanielu, noticed she would not let him drive past the hospital where she had been
treated. She took a longer route to the grocery store. When her primary care physician called to
schedule a follow-up, Moana said she would call back and never did. She told herself she was
fine—her lungs had cleared on the last scan—but the idea of lying on an examination table, of
someone pressing a stethoscope to her chest, filled her with a terror that felt older than thought.
Her body seemed to know the danger before she did. She would be washing dishes and suddenly her
shoulders would lock, her breathing would turn shallow, and only when the moment passed would she
realize she had been smelling something—dish soap, maybe, or the faint metallic tang of tap
water—that her mind had connected to the hospital.

Safety became a place. Moana could feel calm only in the corner of her living room where a certain
armchair sat, or in the small back garden where the air smelled of jasmine and nothing else. She
started waking at three or four in the morning, not with a start but with a quiet, certain
alertness, as though her body was checking a perimeter. She would lie still, listening to Tanielu
breathe, and count her own breaths until she was sure she was not suffocating. If she coughed even
once, she got up and walked to that armchair, refusing to go back to bed until dawn.

The counterfactual thoughts came when she least expected them—while chopping vegetables, while
watching television. She would find herself imagining a version of her life where the bacteria had
never entered her lungs, where she had not cancelled that summer tour, where she was still the
person who could belt a ballad without checking her air supply. That version of Moana did not lie
awake fearing her own body. That Moana trusted herself. The real Moana had come to believe she could
not trust a single decision she made, because if she had made the right ones, she would never have
gotten sick in the first place.

It was Tanielu who finally said something, after she turned down a paid residency at a small venue.
He did not push, but he asked gently: “When did you decide that you don't get to have your life
back?” The question stayed with her. She started reading about trauma online, recognizing fragments
of herself in the descriptions, though she resisted calling it that. A week later, she called a
clinic and made an appointment. She was terrified—of what the therapist would think of her, of
whether she would be able to talk without breaking down, of the possibility that she was too broken
to fix. But she was also exhausted from the night-waking and the smallness of her world. She wanted
to sing again. She wanted to stop feeling that she deserved her own suffering. She did not know if
she could, but she showed up anyway, and that, for now, was enough.
