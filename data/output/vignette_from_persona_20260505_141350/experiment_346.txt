Caleb was twenty years old when he deployed with a faith-based relief organization to a conflict
zone in the Middle East. His team ran a food distribution point on the outskirts of a city that had
been shelled for weeks. On his third afternoon there, a military convoy rolled up to the
checkpoint—not the local forces he recognized, but a different unit. The men jumped out shouting in
Arabic, weapons raised. Caleb didn’t understand the words, but the tone was unmistakable:
accusation, panic, threat. A soldier grabbed him by the collar, shoved him to the ground, and
screamed inches from his face. Another relief worker yelled something back, and for a long minute
Caleb was certain he would be shot. Then the soldier released him, the unit remounted, and the
convoy drove off. The whole thing lasted maybe ninety seconds. It took him months to stop feeling
those hands on his collar every time he closed his eyes.

Back home, Caleb threw himself into his job as a social ministry worker. He married his fiancée,
Sarah, and tried to pick up his life. But the memory of that afternoon didn’t stay in the past. When
he recalled it now—and he recalled it often, without meaning to—the terror flooded back as if the
soldier were still shouting. It felt less like remembering and more like being pulled back into the
moment: the grit of the pavement, the smell of diesel, the ringing silence after the convoy left.
Part of him knew it was over, that he was safe in his own living room, but his body didn’t seem to
know that. His heart would hammer, his palms would sweat, and he would find himself checking the
exits.

The worst part was how unreal it all seemed. He would tell himself, *That didn’t happen to me, that
can’t have happened*, even as the images played on a loop. The disconnection only made it more
frightening—if his own mind couldn’t accept it, how could he trust anything he felt?

He began to avoid anything that might bring the memory closer. Military topics were everywhere: a
client’s son considering enlistment, a news segment about veterans, a stranger in a camo jacket at
the grocery store. Each time, Caleb found a way to leave the room or change the subject. Shouting in
Arabic—in a movie, in a crowded street, even in a language class on TV—made him freeze, then flee.
And if someone asked directly about his time overseas, he would shut down with a terse “I don’t talk
about it.” Talking about the event felt like handing someone a loaded weapon.

Strangers now felt dangerous. A person approaching him on the sidewalk, even with a friendly face,
made his chest tighten. He started scanning rooms for exits, for anyone who moved too quickly.
Sudden movements—a hand reaching for a wallet, a child darting across a room—sent him into a full
alert, heart racing, fists clenching. He woke multiple times a night, silent and rigid, listening
for footsteps or voices that weren’t there. To delay the nightmares, he stayed up until two or three
in the morning, scrolling his phone, keeping himself exhausted so he wouldn’t dream.

He also avoided anything that might make him feel happy or hopeful. Positive events felt dangerous,
like a trap. A good day with Sarah would be followed by a wave of guilt and a conviction that
something terrible had to happen to balance it out. He stopped planning weekend outings, turned down
invitations from friends, and let his hobbies wither. Sarah tried to reach him, but he pulled
further away, spending more time in his own head, endlessly replaying the scene and asking how it
could have been prevented. If he had stood to the left, if he had said something, if he had never
taken that assignment—on and on, a loop that gave him the illusion of control while draining every
ounce of energy.

What finally brought Caleb to a clinic was Sarah’s quiet ultimatum: she couldn’t live in a house
where her husband was a ghost. He came because he was afraid of losing her, but underneath that he
was afraid of something else too—that the memory would never feel real, and that the terror would
never stop feeling present. He wanted to believe it was possible to live without checking the door
every five minutes, but he wasn’t sure he could.
