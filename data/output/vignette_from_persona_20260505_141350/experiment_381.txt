Sione was fifty-five years old when he finally walked into a therapist’s office, though it had taken
him nearly four years to get there. He sat on the edge of the chair, hands folded in his lap, and
tried to explain what had happened. But as soon as he started to say the words—"I was watching Leo,
he was four, and we were at the lake"—his throat closed. The sentence dissolved. He shook his head,
pressed his palms flat against his thighs, and said, "I can't. I just can't finish it." The
therapist waited, but Sione could only hear the water. The hollow, sucking sound of a child going
under. The silence afterward, thick and wrong. He heard it in his head almost every night, and
sometimes during the day when he wasn't expecting it—a sudden rush of that sound, and with it, the
weight of Leo’s small body in his arms as he tried to pump life back into it.

Sione had been a nanny for most of his adult life. He was good with children—patient, gentle,
watchful. That was why the family had hired him for Leo. They had trusted him completely. And Sione
had believed he could keep any child safe. But on that afternoon at the lake, while Leo was playing
near the water’s edge, Sione had turned away for what felt like seconds to answer a phone call. When
he looked back, Leo was gone. He found him floating face-down, just beyond the reeds. He pulled him
out, screamed for help, started CPR, but it was too late. The paramedics said later that Leo had
likely been under for three or four minutes. Sione knew that time was his fault. He should have been
watching. He should have protected him.

In the months after, Sione divorced. His wife had tried to be supportive, but he had become someone
she didn’t recognize—silent, jumpy, hollow. He stopped going to family gatherings. He stopped
answering calls from friends. He was certain, deep down, that he was a weak person. A man who
couldn’t even do the one thing he was supposed to do. He felt less like a person and more like a
shell, an object that moved through the world but didn’t belong in it. When he looked in the mirror,
he didn’t see a man anymore—he saw something that had failed, something that should have been
stronger.

The hardest part was that he needed to keep working. He had bills, no savings, no support. So he
took another nanny job, this time with a family who had a ten-year-old girl. He told himself it
would be different—she was older, more independent. But every time she reached for a glass of water,
he saw Leo’s small hand. Every time she ran ahead on the sidewalk, his chest seized. He began
avoiding the park where she liked to play. He made excuses: the weather, a headache, homework. He
started to dread bath time, because the sight of her wet hair against her neck reminded him of the
lake. He stopped watching TV altogether because any news story about a missing child or a grieving
parent sent him into a spiral of guilt and grief. He couldn’t even listen to a friend talk about
losing their mother without feeling that same overwhelming wave—loss themes, the therapist later
called them, but Sione just called it the hole that opened in his chest whenever anyone mentioned
death.

The auditory memories were the worst. Not just the water—sometimes he heard Leo’s laugh, the way he
had called "Sione, look!" just before he ran toward the lake. Or he heard his own voice, shouting
for help, desperate and thin. He had come to believe that he was somehow unworthy of being around
children. He monitored every child he saw for signs of danger, constantly scanning their
bodies—their arms, their legs, their faces—looking for something that might go wrong, and each time
he saw a child’s hand or foot, the memory slammed back. He could not explain to anyone why he
flinched when a toddler reached for a ball. He could not finish a sentence about what happened
without the grief rising so fast he had to stop breathing.

What finally brought Sione to therapy was a moment at work. The ten-year-old he cared for had a
playdate with a friend. The friend’s mother, unaware, started talking about a neighbor who had lost
a son in a drowning accident. Sione felt his vision narrow. He excused himself, locked himself in
the bathroom, and sobbed for twenty minutes. He knew he couldn’t keep going like this. He was afraid
he would lose this job too. He was afraid he would never feel like a person again. But sitting in
that therapist’s office, trying to say the simplest thing, he wondered if he even deserved to get
better. He had failed to protect Leo. What right did he have to peace?
