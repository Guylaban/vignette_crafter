Margaret is a 73-year-old woman who works as a quality assurance engineer—a role she has held for
decades, long past the age when most of her peers have retired. She came to therapy reluctantly,
referred by her primary care physician after her blood pressure spiked during a routine checkup and
she admitted she had not slept more than a few hours a night in months. What finally pushed her to
call was a moment of quiet desperation: her husband had asked her to accompany him to a memorial
service for an old colleague, and she had frozen in the kitchen, unable to speak, unable to move,
her heart hammering as if she were a child again.

The abuse began when she was six and continued until she left home at seventeen. Her father was the
one who beat her—her mother worked long shifts and either did not know or chose not to see. Margaret
can still see the scenes the way photographs sit in an album: the pattern on the linoleum floor, the
way the light fell through the venetian blinds, the angle of his hand just before it came down. She
does not have to close her eyes to conjure these images; they are simply there, as immediate as the
moment they happened, and yet sometimes when she tries to recall them she finds herself hollowed
out, a witness watching a stranger’s life, feeling nothing at all. When she has tried to describe
what happened to her husband, the words evaporate. She knows the facts—she could write them down in
a list—but the sounds and the sequence refuse to arrange themselves into language that feels real.

Her father’s violence was never predictable. He could be calm for weeks and then erupt over a
dropped glass or a misplaced book, or for no reason she could ever identify. That uncertainty became
the thing she feared most. As an adult, she learned to scan every environment for clues: the way a
man’s shoulders tensed, the speed of his turn, the shift of weight from one foot to the other. Her
body reacts before her mind has time to register the threat—her neck tightens, her breath shortens,
her hands go cold. She is always watching, always waiting. At work, uncertainty in her own domain is
unbearable; when a test case fails unexpectedly or the specifications are ambiguous, she feels a
surge of panic that she cannot explain, and she buries herself in rechecking data, sometimes staying
hours past her shift to eliminate every possible variable.

She has become expert at ensuring she is never alone with a man. In the open-plan office she manages
this by keeping her desk in a busy corner; if a male colleague asks her to step into a conference
room, she finds an excuse—a phone call, a deadline, a bathroom break—or she leaves the door ajar and
positions herself near the exit. The same vigilance follows her home: she is relieved that her
husband, who is gentle and slow-moving, has never frightened her, but she still cannot tolerate
sudden gestures from him, and she has asked him never to approach her from behind. When he forgets,
she yells before she can stop herself, and then is flooded with shame.

Funerals and cemeteries have become impossible. She avoided her father’s years ago, telling herself
it was because he did not deserve her grief, but the truth is more complicated. Standing near a
grave, she is overwhelmed by a sense of helplessness—the same crushed feeling she had as a child,
convinced that nothing she did would ever stop the bad things from happening. She believes she will
never forgive herself for not doing more to stop the abuse, though she was only a child. The
betrayal runs deeper: the man who should have protected her was the one who hurt her, and that
knowledge sits inside her like a stone.

Over the decades, she has quietly withdrawn from anything that might bring her close to these
triggers. She stopped attending family reunions where her uncles would be present. She declined
invitations to birthday dinners if they were at restaurants with private rooms where men might
corner her. Her social world has shrunk to her husband, a few women she trusts, and the structured
predictability of her work. Her husband has grown frustrated—he wants to travel, to visit old
friends, to honour the dead—and Margaret feels the distance between them widening. She is terrified
that she will drive him away, and equally terrified that if she tries to change, she will be pulled
back into those scenes that live like photographs inside her skull.

She came to therapy because she could no longer pretend she was coping. What she wants, she said in
the first session, is to stop being afraid all the time. She hopes that maybe, if she can learn to
tolerate uncertainty, she might be able to walk into a room without scanning the exits first. But
she is also afraid that revisiting the past will shatter her completely, and that she will never
find the words to make anyone understand.
