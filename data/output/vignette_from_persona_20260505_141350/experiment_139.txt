Mariana was a twenty-two-year-old accountant who had learned to measure safety in noise. Silence,
for her, was not peaceful—it was the moment before something terrible revealed itself. She worked in
a mid-sized firm, and her cubicle sat at the end of a corridor where the fluorescent lights hummed.
She preferred that hum. On days when the office was quiet, when no one was typing or talking, she
felt her chest tighten and her ears strain for the first sign of bad news. Delays, too, had become
unbearable. When a report took longer than expected to finalize, when a colleague was late to a
meeting, Mariana’s mind leapt immediately to catastrophe: something had gone wrong, someone was
hurt, a phone call was about to come.

The event that had reshaped her world took place six years ago, when she was sixteen. Her little
brother, Mateo, then eight, had been hospitalized with bacterial meningitis. For ten days she had
lived in the hospital waiting room, and the silence there had been the worst part. The hallways were
eerily quiet between crises. When doctors took too long to return with test results, she had learned
to interpret every minute of delay as confirmation that he was dying. She could still taste it: the
bitter, metallic flavor of the vending-machine coffee she drank to stay awake, mixed with the dry
air of the ICU. That taste came back to her now, unbidden, when she was anxious—a sudden copper tang
on her tongue that made her want to rinse her mouth. And yet, when she tried to explain what had
happened to anyone, even to her own mother, the story came out in fragments. She could not seem to
put the fear into words. She would start, and then something in her would shove the rest of the
memory away before she could reach the end. She had become skilled at stopping herself mid-thought,
at leaving the story unfinished.

After Mateo recovered, Mariana had changed. She used to be a happy person—she remembered that,
dimly—but now she felt trapped in a permanent grayness. She could not stop bad things from
happening; she had learned that lesson in the hospital. No amount of vigilance had saved her
brother; he had survived by luck. She felt isolated, set apart from the other young women at her
firm who talked about weekend plans and dating. She was single, and that seemed right to her,
because she could not imagine letting anyone close enough to see how broken she had become.

At work, the consequences of her avoidance had crept up gradually. She started asking for only
quiet, routine tasks, because any uncertainty sent her heart racing. Her manager noticed that she
avoided client meetings, that she flinched when a project deadline shifted. She had begun arriving
early and leaving late, trying to control her environment, but the effort exhausted her. One
afternoon, a printer jammed and the IT department took forty-five minutes to fix it. Mariana spent
those forty-five minutes in the bathroom, breathing fast, waiting for a disaster that never came.

It was her mother who finally suggested she talk to someone. Mariana had come home from work and
found herself crying over a silent kitchen. She could not explain why. All she knew was that she was
tired of being afraid of quiet rooms and waiting, tired of the taste that haunted her, tired of
feeling like a stranger to her own life. When she made the appointment, she was uncertain what she
could possibly say. The story was still locked somewhere inside her, half-formed and unspeakable,
but she hoped that if she could sit with someone who was willing to wait through the silence,
something might finally come out.
