Minh was seventy-six years old and still working the night shift at a regional distribution
warehouse, stacking boxes of canned goods onto pallets. He had been a civilian in Phnom Penh during
the years of forced evacuation and labor camps, and what he remembered most clearly was the smell of
smoke and wet earth, and the feeling of rope around his wrists. He had been taken from his village
by soldiers when he was eighteen, tied with other young men to a wooden beam in a barn, and held
there for three days without food. The rope was coarse and tight, and every time he tried to shift
his weight it bit deeper into his skin. He could still feel it, sometimes, when he woke in the dark.

The memory came without warning. One minute he would be sliding a box onto a shelf, the next he
would be back in that barn, the smell of diesel exhaust from a forklift suddenly becoming the smell
of the soldiers’ truck, his chest tightening so fast that he had to put down the box and lean
against a stack of pallets. The vividness of it—the exact grain of the wood, the angle of the light
through a crack in the wall—terrified him more than the memory itself, because it meant he had lost
control. He would stand there, shaking, his breath shallow, until a coworker called his name or a
supervisor clapped him on the shoulder, and then he would flinch away from the touch. Being held
down by a sudden grip, even a friendly one, made his stomach drop.

Confrontations were worse. In the warehouse, disputes over shift schedules or misplaced inventory
were common, and Minh had learned to keep his head down. But one afternoon a younger worker accused
him of leaving a pallet in the wrong aisle, raising his voice, stepping closer. Minh’s hands went
numb. He could not speak. He backed away, turned, and walked out the back door, leaving his jacket
and his lunch behind. He stood in the alley for twenty minutes, staring at a brick wall, repeating
to himself that he was not tied up, that he could leave anytime. The man had not touched him, but
the raised voice and the looming posture had been enough. After that, Minh started taking his breaks
alone in the bathroom, eating a banana over the sink so no one would approach him.

The smells were the hardest. The warehouse stored cleaning supplies, and the scent of bleach
sometimes drifted across the loading dock. It reminded him of the chemical smell in the camp
latrines, which mingled with the smoke from burning garbage. His nose would fill with it, and
suddenly the memory was not just a picture but a full-body experience—the ache in his shoulders from
being bound, the dry grit in his mouth, the terror of not knowing when it would end. He had learned
to breathe through his mouth when he entered certain aisles, and he avoided the cleaning supply
section entirely, even if it meant taking a longer route to the break room. His supervisor had
noticed, and twice had asked him why he was walking around the long way. Minh had shrugged and said
his knees hurt.

The consequences of these habits had grown quietly over the years. He lived alone in a small
apartment with a kitchen table he never sat at, because the chair had a straight back that reminded
him of leaning against the wooden beam. He ate standing at the counter. He had no contact with his
surviving relatives—he had stopped answering the phone years ago, because hearing a voice he did not
expect, even a familiar one, felt like an ambush. He had never married. The warehouse was the only
place he went outside of his apartment, and even there he had become invisible, arriving early,
leaving late, speaking to no one unless forced.

What brought him to the clinic was a panic attack so severe that a coworker called an ambulance.
Minh had been walking past a pallet of bottled water when a loose plastic strap caught his ankle.
The sudden resistance, the sensation of being held in place, had thrown him into the past so
completely that he fell to his knees, sobbing. The EMTs found him curled against a stack of boxes,
repeating a Khmer word they did not understand. He was taken to the emergency room, cleared
medically, and referred to a specialty clinic for trauma. When the intake worker asked him what he
hoped for, Minh sat in silence for a long time. Then he said, very quietly, that he wanted to be
able to walk past a pallet of water without falling apart. He did not say that he was also afraid of
what would happen if he ever had to feel the rope again—because that, he was certain, would break
him entirely.
