Eleanor had been five years old the night her father’s fist met the kitchen wall, then her mother’s
cheekbone. She remembered the sound—a wet, hollow crack—and the way her mother’s body folded into
the linoleum like a doll dropped from a great height. Her father had turned, and for one frozen
second his eyes found hers through the doorway. Then he had looked away, wiped his hand on his
trousers, and walked past her as if she were furniture. No one ever spoke of it. Eleanor never told
anyone. Not then, not ever. What she carried forward was not a set of images that she could replay
like a movie, but something far worse: a sudden, unbodied flood of terror that would arrive without
warning, as if the air itself had turned to that night. She would feel her stomach drop, her mouth
go dry, and the certainty that she was about to be destroyed—and she would have no idea why. The
memory blocked itself before she could reach it. She learned to push it away the moment she sensed
the familiar pressure building, and for decades that strategy worked well enough.

Now she was seventy-six, a divorced city council member whose name appeared on public notices and
whose face people recognized at the grocery store. The council chambers were full of men who cleared
their throats before they spoke, who used the flat of their hands on the table when they wanted
emphasis. She had been on the council for twelve years and had learned to keep her voice steady, to
meet their eyes, to never let them see her flinch. But lately the old terror was finding new entry
points. During a routine colonoscopy preparatory appointment, the sight of the nurse laying out
latex gloves sent her heart hammering so hard that the blood pressure cuff alarmed. She cancelled
the procedure. She told herself she would reschedule next month, but she knew, somewhere underneath
the rationalizing, that she was not going to reschedule at all. The helplessness of lying flat while
someone performed an examination—the way the table tilted, the fluorescent lights overhead, the
smell of antiseptic—felt exactly like being small and unable to stop something from happening.

The second trigger was harder to name. A man in the waiting room of her bank, tall, grey-haired,
with the same heavy brow her father had carried, and Eleanor had to press her palm against the
counter to keep from walking out. She knew it was not him—her father had died twenty years ago—but
that did not matter. Her body knew the shape of danger, and it responded the same way it had at
five. She had begun avoiding the downtown branch, taking the longer route to the suburban one, and
she was aware that this took time she did not have. The third trigger was the most bewildering:
comments on family. A fellow council member remarked casually at a committee meeting, “Must be nice
having a family that supports you,” and Eleanor felt the temperature in the room drop. She could not
explain why those words—perfectly ordinary, even kind—made her want to stand up and leave. She had
come to believe that any mention of family carried a hidden accusation: that she had not stopped it,
that she had not spoken, that she had somehow been complicit by her silence. She began excusing
herself from informal conversations, claiming phone calls, taking the stairs instead of the elevator
so she could be alone.

The memories, when they did surface, were more vivid than anything that had happened yesterday. She
could feel the grain of the linoleum under her bare feet, the damp chill of the hallway, the
particular way the kitchen clock ticked through the silence after the blow. But she could not hold
onto the memory for long. The moment she recognized it, her mind slammed a door shut. She was left
with the emotion—the raw, wordless dread—and no image to anchor it, so that the dread attached
itself to whatever was in front of her: the stethoscope, the man at the bank, the colleague’s well-
meaning remark. She had begun to scan every room for potential threats, her eyes moving from faces
to doorways to hands, a low-level vigilance that exhausted her by noon. At home, she kept the

could no longer ignore. She had missed three council meetings in a row—one because of a dental
cleaning she had rescheduled, one because she had convinced herself she had a fever, one because she
had seen a grey sedan that reminded her of her father’s car and had turned around at the parking lot
entrance. Her colleagues had noticed. The mayor had asked if she was unwell. Eleanor was terrified
that they would see her as weak, as incapable, as the scared child she still felt herself to be. She
wanted to go back to the meetings. She wanted to schedule the colonoscopy. She wanted, most of all,

folded in her lap, waiting to see if this room would feel safe or if it, too, would become another
