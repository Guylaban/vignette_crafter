Mai was fifty-seven years old, a married interior designer who had built her career on creating
spaces where people felt safe, comfortable, at ease. That sense of ease had evaporated on a Saturday
afternoon three years earlier, when she and her husband had stopped at a crowded outdoor market in a
neighborhood known for its weekend food stalls and craft vendors. She remembered the smell of
grilled meat and flowers, the sound of a live band playing pop covers, the way the sunlight fell
through a canopy of string lights. Then a pickup truck barreled through the barrier at the south
entrance, and everything became noise and dust and screaming. Mai had been thrown against a concrete
planter and had lain there, half-conscious, as the truck continued its path, pinning a man against a
produce stand. The gasoline from the truck's ruptured tank had pooled and ignited, and the air
filled with a sharp, sweet chemical odor that Mai would come to associate with the smell of alcohol-
based hand sanitizer and cheap liquor.

In the months that followed, Mai found that she could not bear to look at old photographs—not only
those from that day, which her brother-in-law had taken just before the chaos, but any photo from
the past. The act of flipping through an album made her feel as if the moment in the image were
about to shatter into violence. She also discovered that the smell of alcohol—a glass of wine at
dinner, the rubbing alcohol in a first-aid kit, a friend's perfume with a heavy ethanol base—sent
her heart racing and her hands shaking. She stopped going to restaurants where servers might carry a
tray of drinks past her table, and she began to avoid social events where she might be asked, “How
did you get through it?” The question alone, the mere mention of the event, made her feel as if she
were falling through a trapdoor back into that market.

To manage the overwhelming feelings that came with these intrusions, Mai developed routines that she
kept entirely to herself. After a triggering day—an old photo unearthed while cleaning, a whiff of
hand sanitizer in a client's bathroom—she would run on the treadmill until her legs burned and her
lungs ached, sometimes for two or three hours, until she was too exhausted to think. On other days,
she would eat, mechanically and without pleasure, working through an entire loaf of bread or a pint
of ice cream in the kitchen while her husband watched television in the next room. The eating was a
way to fill time, to keep her hands busy and her mind dull. But what frightened her most was the
urge to hurt herself physically. She had scratched at her forearms with her fingernails, dug her
thumbs into the soft flesh of her thighs, and once, in a moment of frantic despair, she had struck
her own head against the bathroom wall because the pressure of the impact was the only thing that
felt real.

Mai had come to believe that she could not handle even the smallest upset. A late delivery from a
fabric supplier, a mildly critical comment from a client—these sent her into a spiral of trembling
and self-doubt. She was certain the world was a dangerous place, that danger could burst from any
ordinary scene, and so she spent much of her waking life on guard. At work, while measuring a living
room for new curtains, she watched the homeowner's movements out of the corner of her eye,
cataloguing what could be a weapon, what could be a hiding place. In her own home, she jumped when
her husband touched her shoulder unexpectedly, and she had snapped at him more than once,
apologizing later but unable to stop the reaction. She monitored crowds at the grocery store, the
parking lot, the post office, scanning faces for the subtle signs she believed would warn her of an
attack.

Her marriage had become strained. Her husband wanted to talk about what happened, wanted her to see
a therapist, but talking about the event was itself a trigger, and she had learned to deflect, to
change the subject, to leave the room. She had come to the present session only because her
compulsive exercise had resulted in a stress fracture in her foot, and her doctor had insisted she
stop running. Without that outlet, the overeating and the intrusive thoughts had intensified, and
Mai had finally, reluctantly, acknowledged that she could not keep managing on her own. She was
afraid of what she might do to herself if she continued this way, but she was equally afraid that
therapy would force her to relive the market, the smell, the sound, the sight of the man pinned
under the truck. She wanted to feel less afraid, but she was terrified of the price.
