David, a 52-year-old film director of European descent, had built his career on being able to
orchestrate chaos into controlled, beautiful frames. That had changed one night, about fourteen
months ago, when he was driving home from a late location scout through a winding coastal road. His
headlights caught the eyes of a deer just as another vehicle crested the hill ahead. He swerved, the
car left the asphalt, rolled twice, and came to rest upside down in a dry ditch. He remembered the
shriek of tearing metal, the glass exploding inward, and then the dark—absolute, suffocating,
smelling of gasoline and damp soil. He had been trapped in the overturned car for nearly forty
minutes, suspended by his seatbelt, unable to see anything, hearing only the hiss of the engine and
the distant sound of someone shouting his name. When the rescuers finally cut him out, he was
covered in blood from a scalp wound, but physically intact. The paramedics said he was lucky. David
did not feel lucky.

In the months that followed, David found that he could not shake the images. The memory of the
accident was not a story he could tell; it came as a series of vivid snapshots—the glare of
headlights, the dashboard crumpling toward his chest, the taste of copper and dust. If he tried to
describe it to his partner, the words felt hollow, as though the real event existed only in pictures
his mind could not translate into language. He found himself jumping at ordinary sounds: the clatter
of a dropped coffee cup on set, the hiss of a pressure valve, even the sharp crack of a director's
slate. Each time, his heart would lurch, and he would feel a flush of shame. He had come to believe
that his reactions proved something damning about him—that he was a lousy coper, that he was
fundamentally inadequate, that the person he had become was unrecognisable to himself. A man who
once relished the controlled chaos of production now startled at his own assistant's voice.

David's work as a director forced him into environments thick with sudden noises and unpredictable
lighting, and he had begun to adapt in ways that worried his crew. He insisted on keying every scene
with at least a three-quarter fill light; no shadows, no darkness, even when the script called for a
moonlit bedroom. He had a standing rule that no one could approach him from behind without verbal
warning. He stopped attending night shoots altogether, claiming creative differences, but really
because the darkness outside the soundstage triggered a visceral sense of being trapped in that
overturned car. Instead, he buried himself in post-production—spending twelve-hour days in the
editing bay, headphones on, cutting and colour-grading, anything to keep his mind occupied. The
distraction worked, but it came at a cost. His partner had begun to feel shut out; dinners were
cancelled, weekends disappeared into the cutting room. David knew he was hiding, but the
alternative—sitting alone with his thoughts, drifting into the *why* of it all—felt worse. He would
catch himself asking why this had happened to him, as if the universe had singled him out, and then
he would push the question away with another task.

What finally brought David to seek help was a meeting with his producer, who told him bluntly that
his avoidance of night shoots was costing the studio money and that his behaviour on set had become
"a liability." David had snapped at a sound engineer who dropped a sandbag, and the incident had
spread through the small film community. His partner had also sat him down and said she didn't
recognise him anymore—those were his own words, echoing back. He was terrified that he was ruining
the career he had spent thirty years building, and even more terrified that if he stopped and really
looked at the accident, he would find something even worse: proof that he was, after all, a man who
could not handle what life threw at him. He sat in the therapist's office with his hands clasped,
and for a long moment he said nothing. Then he looked up and said, quietly, "I used to know exactly
what scene we were shooting. Now I just want to know who I'm supposed to be."
