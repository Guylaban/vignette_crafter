Amara was seventy-eight when she finally let her husband drive her to the clinic, though she made
him park three blocks away so no one would see her enter. She had been a pastor for forty-two years,
first in a small Liberian church in Monrovia, then in a storefront congregation in a Midwestern city
after she fled. Her parishioners knew her as dignified, soft-spoken, always dressed in long, flowing
skirts and a matching head wrap that framed her face like a curtain. They did not know that the head
wrap was also a kind of armor—that Amara chose fabrics that were dark and loose, that she pulled the
edges forward so that strangers on the street would glance past her rather than at her. She had come
to believe that being seen was dangerous, that visibility invited a kind of attention that could
only lead to harm.

The harm that had taught her this happened on a bus. It was 1991, during the First Liberian Civil
War. Amara was thirty-seven, a young mother of three, traveling with her children to a town where
she had heard there was food. The bus was crowded, and at a checkpoint soldiers in mismatched
uniforms climbed aboard. They carried machetes and rifles, and they demanded papers, demanded money,
demanded to know who was from which tribe. Amara watched as they pulled a man from his seat and beat
him with the flat of a blade. She heard the sound—a wet, heavy thud—and then the screaming. She held
her children close, their faces pressed into her skirts, and she tried to make herself small. One
soldier looked directly at her, then past her. She did not know why they were spared, only that when
they finally stepped off the bus hours later, her legs would not hold her. She had felt, in that
moment, like a sack of grain, like something to be moved or discarded, not a person. That feeling
never fully left.

Now, at eighty, Amara still could not ride a bus. The sight of one pulling to a curb sent a cold
spike through her chest, and she would cross the street, circle the block, walk an extra mile to
avoid the stop. In church, when a visiting deacon once propped a wooden collection pole against the
wall, she froze during the offertory prayer; the pole was long and dark, and for a split second it
became a machete in her mind. She blinked hard and finished the prayer, but afterward she could not
remember a word she had said.

The memories did not ask permission. They arrived without warning—the sound of a door slamming would
become the crack of a rifle; a whisper in the congregation would turn into the desperate murmur of
passengers on that bus. Sometimes she heard the screaming again, right inside her head, as clearly
as if she were still sitting on that hard seat with her children’s breath hot against her arm. And
in those moments, the memory felt so extreme, so surreal, that she could not reconcile it with the
woman she was now—a grandmother, a pastor, a wife of fifty years. It did not seem possible that such
a thing had happened to her. Yet she also felt that if she let herself think about it, truly think
about it, she would not be able to hold herself together. So she pushed the thoughts away, again and
again, and told no one.

Not her husband, not her grown children, not her fellow clergy. She had learned that silence was
survival. In Liberia, speaking out could get you killed. In America, speaking out might bring pity,
or questions, or the kind of attention that felt like being seen again. She wore her concealing
clothes, avoided doctors (she had not had a physical in seven years, even as her knees ached and her
blood pressure climbed), and watched other people constantly—their hands, their sudden movements,
the direction of their gaze. She looked over her shoulder when she walked to the market. She sat
with her back to the wall in every room. She did this so automatically that she no longer noticed
the effort.

What finally brought Amara to the clinic was not a breakdown, but a quiet conversation with her
husband. He had noticed she no longer visited the sick in the hospital, even though it was part of
her pastoral calling. She told him she could not bear the smell—but she did not tell him that the
hospital’s fluorescent lights and narrow corridors reminded her of a refugee camp she had passed
through, or that the sight of a wheelchair could send her into a floating, detached state where she
felt as if she were watching herself from above. She told him only that she was tired, that she felt
old, that nothing seemed fair.

“They got away with it,” she said to him one evening, and he did not ask who “they” were. “They took
everything, and nothing was done.” She felt the injustice like a stone in her stomach. She had heard
of survivors who testified at truth commissions, who told their stories aloud. She did not know how
they did it. The thought of speaking the story out loud made her feel as if she might dissolve.

Now she sits in the therapist’s office, twisting the edge of her head wrap. She is terrified of what
will happen if she begins to talk. But she is also tired—tired of crossing streets, tired of looking
over her shoulder, tired of the way her own life feels unreal. She hopes, though she cannot say it,
that someone might finally see her and not as an object, but as a person who survived.
