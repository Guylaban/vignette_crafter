has been married to his wife, Linh, for sixteen years. Eight months ago, after a long double shift,
he walked toward his car in the hospital's dimly lit parking garage around three in the morning. A

handed it over, but the man shoved him anyway, hard enough that Minh's head struck the concrete
pillar beside him. The man grabbed the wallet and ran. Minh lay on the ground for several minutes,
dazed, tasting blood from where his cheek had split. He had a small fracture in his jaw that healed
in six weeks. But the memory did not heal.

What stayed with him, what returned again and again, was not the story of the event but the exact,
still images he could summon at will—the grain of the concrete pillar under his cheek, the pattern
of the man's sneakers as he ran away, the angle of the fluorescent light bars above him. The
memories came as photographs, silent and sharp, and when they arrived he felt a sharp ache along his
left jaw and a pressure in his ribs, precisely where the man had pushed and where his head had
struck. He had come to believe that there must be something about him—the way he carried himself,
the way he had walked too slowly, some inherent carelessness—that had made the man choose him. That
thought burrowed into him over the weeks that followed. Nothing good can happen to me anymore, he
started telling himself, and then, later, more quietly, I have no future.

Minh stopped leaving the hospital after dark if he could help it, but he could not change his shift
entirely. On the nights he did walk out, he scanned the garage from the moment he pressed the
elevator button, rotating his head constantly, checking blind corners, turning to glance behind him
every few steps. He began to sit in his car for ten minutes before driving, watching the rearview
mirror for anyone approaching. That mirror-checking spread into other parts of his life: at home, he
caught himself looking at the bathroom mirror to see if someone was behind him; at the grocery

his breaks in the staff lounge, he always chose a seat against the wall, one where he could see the
door and the window at the same time, even if it meant sitting away from his colleagues. If someone
sat behind him, he could not focus on his food.

The consequences accumulated quietly. Linh noticed that he no longer accepted invitations to join
friends for dinner at restaurants, because he would spend the whole evening checking the entrance
and the windows. He told her he was tired, and he was—falling asleep had become a struggle, and
staying asleep was worse. He would jolt awake at two or three in the morning, convinced he had heard
footsteps on the driveway, and would lie in the dark, staring at the bedroom door. He started to
keep his mind occupied all the time: podcasts during his commute, music at home, the television on
low all night. Silence left room for the images.

patient was brought in after a street assault, and as Minh helped clean the man's facial
