Michael was twenty-three when the abuse began, and he was already a patrol officer, already trained
to read threat in the flicker of a muscle. His partner, a woman he had been with for nearly two
years, had never raised a hand before the night she came home from a bar reeking of cheap whiskey
and accused him of hiding something. He tried to calm her, and she swung. The first punch caught him
across the jaw; the second, as he turned, landed on his kidney. He did not fight back. He was a cop.
He knew how these calls went. He stayed still, let it happen, and when she finally stumbled into the
bedroom, he sat on the couch for hours, pressing a bag of frozen peas to his ribs and listening to
his own breathing. That single night, he told himself, would be the end. It was not. Over the next
fourteen months she hit him, shoved him, threw glass at him, and once locked him in the bathroom for
six hours. He never reported it. He believed he could manage it, contain it, keep it separate from
the job. But what he could not contain was the way his own body began to remember things he did not
want recalled.

Now he is twenty-six, a detective in a busy precinct, and he is married to a different woman—someone
gentle, patient, a teacher who leaves sticky notes on his coffee mug. He loves her, but he cannot
let her touch him without flinching. Any form of physical contact—a hand on his shoulder, a hug from
behind—sends a spike of cold through his chest, and he has learned to step sideways, to pivot, to
keep distance. He tells himself it is better that way. Safer. He has also stopped relaxing. After
work, he used to sit on the back porch with a book; now he cannot. Relaxing feels dangerous, like
lowering a shield in enemy territory, so he stays upright, stays alert, watching the door. He has
come to believe that people are not what they seem—that the person you trust can become the person
who hurts you without warning. That belief has hardened into a kind of permanent suspicion, a
feeling that he is an outsider now, set apart from the ordinary trust that other people share. He
lost faith in people, in the idea that the world is predictable or fair. He does not pray anymore,
though he used to.

His memories of the abuse are relentlessly clear and detailed. He can still see the pattern in the
linoleum floor as he lay on his side. He can hear the sound of her voice, the exact pitch of the
accusations, the scrape of a chair leg. The memory forces its way into his mind during quiet
moments—at his desk, in the car, in the four a.m. stillness of his own kitchen—and when it comes, he
sometimes hears the sounds again, the thud of a fist against drywall, the shatter of a bottle. The
auditory recall is so vivid that he will jerk upright, certain the noise was real.

At work, the triggers are constant. He is the first officer at a domestic call, and the smell of
alcohol on a suspect’s breath sends a wave of nausea coiling through his stomach. He sees visible
injuries on a victim—a split lip, a bruise blooming along a jawline—and he has to excuse himself to
the restroom, where he breathes into his palms until the shaking passes. He has learned to numb his
feelings deliberately, to seal them off behind a professional flatness that his colleagues mistake
for composure. But that numbness leaks into his marriage. His wife has started to ask why he never
seems present, why he flinches, why he refuses to sit still on the couch with her. He does not tell
her. He cannot. The words would require him to admit what happened, to own the shame of being a man
who could not protect himself.

He is seeking help now because his captain noticed the way he went pale at a crime scene and quietly
recommended a therapist. Michael came because he was told to, but also because something is wearing
thin. He is tired of the vigilance, the nausea, the way his own mind ambushes him. He is afraid of
what he might become if this goes on—afraid that the outsider he feels himself turning into will
eventually be all that is left. He wants to stop flinching when his wife reaches for him. He does
not know if that is possible, but he walked into the office anyway, and that is more than he has
done in three years.
