Robert Jensen, a 78-year-old database administrator, had a heart attack in his kitchen eighteen
months ago. He remembers the crushing pressure in his chest, the cold sweat on his forehead, and the
way the world narrowed to a single thought—this is it. The paramedics arrived, and as they worked on
him, the sharp antiseptic smell of the ambulance hit his nostrils; that smell lodged itself
somewhere deep. In the emergency room, he could taste the oxygen mask, hear the beeping, feel the
hands pressing on his ribs. He survived, but his body had betrayed him. Now, whenever he recalls
that morning—and he recalls it often—the same antiseptic odor floods his senses without warning, and
the memory does not feel like a memory at all. It feels as real and immediate as the moment itself.
He will be sitting at his desk, running database queries, and suddenly he is back in the stretcher,
gasping, the world tilting. The reliving hits him with such force that he has to grip the arms of
his chair to remind himself he is at home, that the attack is over. But his body does not believe
that. His heart races, his palms sweat, and every twinge in his chest becomes a potential second
act. He has come to see his own body as an enemy that remains on the offensive even when he is safe.

Robert lives alone now; his divorce predated the illness, but the heart attack deepened the
isolation. He used to enjoy going to the office, chatting with colleagues over coffee. Now he works
exclusively from home, and even there he feels on guard. He has checked his home’s smoke and carbon
monoxide detectors four times this week. He paces from room to room, listening for any irregular
sound. The hypervigilance has a relentless quality—he cannot sit still for more than a few minutes
without scanning for signs of danger. He has stopped going to the grocery store because the
fluorescent lights remind him of the hospital ceiling. He has not visited his primary care physician
for a routine checkup in over a year; the thought of stepping into a waiting room sends him into a
full-body panic. The world, he has concluded, is fundamentally dangerous. You can be healthy one
minute and dying the next; there is no safety, no predictability. The faith he once had in life, in
people, in the notion that he might have some control, has eroded completely. He feels a profound
guilt, too, for not preventing the attack, for having ignored the early warning signs, and that
guilt surfaces as tight-jawed anger at himself.

The consequences have accumulated quietly. Robert’s productivity has dropped by half. He cannot
concentrate on work because he is constantly monitoring his heartbeat, waiting for the next
flashback. His manager has noticed, and there have been conversations about performance targets that
Robert dreads. He avoids video calls now because he is afraid his face will betray his terror. His
daughter, who lives an hour away, has grown frustrated with his refusals to visit—he always says he
is “not feeling well.” He knows she worries, but he cannot explain why he cannot leave his house.
What would he say? That the world is a trap, that his own body is a ticking bomb? He has lost faith
not only in the structure of his life but in the people who might help him; he is certain they will
not understand the raw immediacy of his terror. Only last month, he smelled the antiseptic while
brushing his teeth—some strange association with the mint—and he spent the next hour on the floor of
his bathroom, trembling, the heart monitor on his watch flashing a red warning.

Robert finally came to the clinic because his daughter gave him an ultimatum: either see someone or
she would stop calling. He is here, but he is skeptical. He is afraid that talking about the heart
attack will make it come back, will make him relive it again. He is hoping, though, that someone can
tell him why he cannot feel safe in his own living room, why his own body will not listen to reason,
and perhaps—though he barely dares think it—why he feels so utterly alone in a world that keeps
threatening to collapse.
