working animals in villages caught between shifting frontlines. She had been there for three months,
and the work felt meaningful. Then came the mortar attack on the market town where she and two
colleagues were running a mobile clinic. She remembered the first explosion—a deafening crack that
seemed to come from inside her skull—and then the chaos of people and animals screaming. She was
thrown to the ground, and someone grabbed her arm, yanking her toward a truck. She could not recall
the evacuation clearly; the memories came in disconnected pieces: the feel of a rough hand on her
wrist, the smell of smoke, a fragment of a prayer, the sight of one of her colleagues lying still on
the ground. She was certain she had done nothing useful. She had frozen. When she later learned that
her colleague had died, she felt a shame so deep it seemed to live in her bones. Nothing about that
day had been fair—she had gone to help, and instead she had been helpless, and no one had ever been
held accountable for the attack.

Back home in the United States, Malia tried to resume her life as a veterinarian in a suburban
animal hospital. She was married to a quiet, patient man named Tui, and they had a comfortable
house. But within weeks, the symptoms took hold. The loud, sudden bark of a large dog in the exam
room would send her heart racing and her vision tunneling, and she would have to excuse herself to
the bathroom to breathe. She began to flinch when clients or staff touched her unexpectedly—a hand
on the shoulder, a tap on the arm. She could not bear to have Tui drive her anywhere; the sight of

brought back the jostling ride in the evacuation truck. So she drove herself everywhere, gripping

alert to every creak and whisper, unable to let herself fall deeply asleep because she needed to be
ready. Even when she knew the house was locked and safe, her muscles would not relax.

The memories came like a film that played over and over, but the film had missing frames. She could
not piece together the moments between the second explosion and the truck ride. What she did
recall—the shame of her inaction, the feeling of being grabbed, the sound—felt more vivid and more
real than the present. She began to believe she could not rely on herself. If she had failed then,
what was to stop her from failing again? She could not stop bad things from happening, no matter how
carefully she prepared.

husband grew worried when she stopped wanting to go out to dinner with friends; the noise of the
restaurant, the possibility of someone brushing past her chair, was too much. He asked what was
wrong, and she could not explain it in a way that made sense. She began to feel that she was
disappearing into her own vigilance, that the world had become a series of threats she had to
monitor.
