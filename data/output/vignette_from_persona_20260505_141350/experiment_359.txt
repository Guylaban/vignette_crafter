Samir is a 48-year-old fashion designer who came to therapy after a series of nights he could no
longer outrun. The event that changed everything happened three years ago, at the downtown studio
where he was overseeing final alterations for a runway collection. A short circuit ignited stored
bolts of silk and chiffon; the fire spread so fast that by the time Samir smelled smoke, the main
exit was already blocked. He managed to crawl through a side door, his hands blistering from a door
handle that had turned to iron, his throat raw from the chemical tang of melting synthetics. For
years afterward, that taste would rise in his mouth without warning—bitter and acrid, impossible to
swallow away. The memory never arrived whole. Instead, Samir would be sketching a new hemline or
driving home from a client meeting, and suddenly he would feel the searing heat on his skin, a
precise temperature that belonged only to that door handle, and his stomach would turn violently,
leaving him pale and hunched over the nearest sink. The taste would come next, and then the
fragments—screams from the other end of the hall, the pop of ceiling tiles, the weight of a body
against his back as someone pushed past. It never felt like remembering. It felt like it was
happening right then, the sweat breaking out on his forehead, his hands searching for something cool
to press against his face.

In the months that followed, Samir’s life contracted. He kept himself moving constantly—12-hour days
at the studio, then freelance projects in the evenings, then gym sessions at midnight—because
stillness was where the taste returned and the heat crawled up his arms. If he stopped, the
fragments would come; if he kept working, he could drown them in fabric swatches and seam
allowances. But busyness had a price. His concentration frayed. He would stare at a pattern draft
for twenty minutes, the lines blurring as his eyes went elsewhere—checking the studio’s vents,
searching for smoke, calculating how fast he could reach the fire escape. Every new location
required a mental map of exits before he could breathe. Restaurants, theaters, even a friend’s
living room: Samir would scan the room for hazards, the way a designer scans a mannequin for flaws.
He had started checking the locks on his apartment door three, four, five times before he could
sleep, and then he would lie awake, running escape routes through his head until dawn.

There were nights when the pressure built to a point he could not think his way out of. Then Samir
would lock the bathroom door and press the edge of a scissors blade against his forearm—just hard
enough to break skin, just deep enough to turn the taste of smoke into something cleaner, more
immediate. The pain grounded him, pulled him out of the hot, sick fog of the memory and back into
the cold tile floor. He had told no one. His divorce had already happened before the fire, his ex-
wife citing the long hours and the way he had grown distant, but he knew this was something else. He
had become a man who checked every window twice, who could not focus on a conversation because he
was watching the ceiling for sprinklers, who sometimes had to excuse himself from a fitting when a
client’s perfume reminded him of paraffin and ash.

What finally brought Samir to the clinic was a Tuesday morning in his studio. A new intern had
brought in a space heater, and the moment the heating element clicked on, Samir smelled it—that same
chemical warmth—and he vomited onto a half-finished gown. He stood there, shaking, the fragments
swarming, the taste thick on his tongue, and he knew he could not design his way out of this one. He
made the appointment with the number his primary care doctor had given him months ago. He is afraid
that if he slows down enough to talk about the fire, he will be pulled back into it permanently,
trapped in that hot, choking moment forever. But he is also tired—tired of mapping every room, tired
of the nightly checks, tired of the taste that never quite leaves his mouth. He wants to feel like
the man who used to sketch without looking over his shoulder. He wants to walk into a room and see
the clothes, not the exits.
