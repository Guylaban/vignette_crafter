Sofia Rivera is a fifty-seven-year-old physical therapist who lives alone in a modest apartment on
the second floor of a building she chose specifically because it sits on high ground. She divorced
nearly a decade ago, and her two adult children live in another state. For the past three years, she
has worked at a busy outpatient rehabilitation clinic, where she treats patients recovering from
surgeries and injuries. But the Sofia who shows up to those sessions is not the same woman who once
greeted every patient with a warm, confident smile and a steady hand.

The change began during Hurricane Maria, when Sofia was still living in a ground-floor home near a
river in a coastal town. She had refused to evacuate—she had weathered storms before—but this one
was different. The water rose faster than anyone anticipated, pouring under the door and through the
walls in thick, muddy surges. She climbed onto the kitchen counter, then onto the refrigerator, as
the current dragged furniture past her. The sound of the flood was enormous: a deep, grinding roar
punctuated by the crash of unseen objects and the screams of neighbors she could not reach. She
remembers the smell of wet earth and gasoline, and the way her stomach turned and heaved until she
vomited into the brown water below. A piece of debris struck her shoulder, nearly knocking her off
balance. For hours she clung to a pipe that ran along the ceiling, certain she would die, until a
rescue boat finally arrived. Her home was destroyed. Two people on her street did not survive: a
woman named Lidia and her young son, Mateo.

In the months that followed, Sofia tried to rebuild. She found a new apartment, returned to work,
and told herself she was fine. But the world had become a place of hidden dangers. The first time
she felt the nausea return was at a supermarket, when she rounded an aisle and saw a display of
bottled water stacked to the ceiling. Her stomach clenched, and she had to leave the cart and walk
out. She began to avoid large bodies of water entirely—lakes, rivers, even the decorative fountain
in the town square. She stopped going out after work altogether, except for essentials, and even
then she planned her routes to avoid the few safe zones: her clinic, her apartment, and a small café
two blocks away where the restrooms had no windows and felt enclosed and secure.

Her sleep fractured. She would lie awake for hours, her body rigid and listening, because any
unexpected sound—a car backfiring, a dropped pan in a neighbor's kitchen—sent her heart into a
gallop. She came to believe that safety was an illusion, that disaster could strike again at any
moment, and that only her hypervigilance kept her alive. So she stayed indoors, and in the quiet of
her apartment she began to dwell on the person she used to be: the Sofia who laughed easily, who
drove to the beach on weekends, who trusted the world. That woman felt like a stranger now.

What frightened Sofia most was the way her own memory betrayed her. Sometimes, without any image or
trigger she could identify, the full emotional force of the flood would wash over her—the terror,
the helplessness, the grief—and she would find herself weeping on the floor of her living room,
unable to connect the feeling to a specific thought. At other moments, when she deliberately tried
to recall the event, the memory felt detached, as though it had happened to someone else, a story
she had been told rather than lived. The grief that surfaced when she did remember—especially when
she thought of Lidia and Mateo—was so heavy that Sofia learned to push the memory away on purpose.
She would blank out, leaving her body, letting her mind go empty, because staying present was
unbearable.

The dissociation became a strategy she used at work, too. During a session with a new patient whose
name was Lidia, Sofia felt the nausea rise and her vision tunnel. She excused herself to the
restroom, sat on the floor, and deliberately emptied her mind until the world went gray. She
returned to the treatment room with a calm, practiced smile, but she knew she could not keep this up
forever.

Last month, a fire alarm malfunction at the clinic sent a piercing tone through the building. Sofia
dropped a therapy ball, grabbed the nearest wall, and did not move until the sound stopped. Her
patient asked if she was all right. Sofia said yes, but she was not. That night, she barely slept,
and in the morning she called a therapist who specialized in trauma. She is terrified that talking
about the flood will bring it back, that she will drown in the grief she has tried so hard to
contain. But she is also tired of living inside a shrinking bubble, of being a stranger to her own
life. She hopes, more than anything, that somewhere inside her there is still the woman she used to
be, waiting to be found.
