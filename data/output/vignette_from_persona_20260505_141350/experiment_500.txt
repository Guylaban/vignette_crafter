anticipated, not in forty years of midwifery. The room was already tense; the mother was exhausted,
the baby’s heart rate dropping. Then the mother’s partner, a large man who had been pacing in the
corner, began shouting at the staff, accusing them of incompetence. He shoved a doctor hard against
the wall before security arrived. In the chaos, a junior nurse, trying to steady herself, knocked
over a bottle of concentrated disinfectant that had been left on the counter. The liquid splashed
across the floor and up Helen’s forearm. She felt the sting immediately, then a wave of nausea so
powerful she had to brace herself against the bed. She did not stop working—she couldn’t—but by the
time the baby was out and safe, her hands were trembling and the room seemed to tilt. She finished
the shift, went home, and spent the night vomiting, her skin burning where the chemical had touched.
The next day she was told the substance was a known irritant, that she would be fine, but something
in her had already begun to shift.

Over the following months, Helen found herself scanning every room she entered—for smells, for
bottles, for anything that might signal danger, but also for raised voices, for sudden movements,
for the kind of anger that could turn physical. At work, she started avoiding the supply closet
where the disinfectants were stored and asking colleagues to handle cleaning instruments. She also
began avoiding certain visitors, especially men with loud voices, and would ask to be reassigned if
a family member seemed agitated. Her own body became a source of alarm: whenever she felt dizzy, or
her stomach turned, or a headache came on, she was certain she had been exposed again. She would
check her skin for redness, her mouth for a metallic taste. Even a mild cold sent her into a spiral
of checking and rechecking. But the most vivid triggers were violent cues. A slammed door in the
hospital corridor made her flinch. A delivery room argument on television caused her to change the
channel, her heart racing. She had been to a grocery store where a man shouted at his child, and she
had to leave her cart and walk out, shaking, convinced she was back in that moment.

What frightened her most, though, was how she felt inside. She no longer recognized herself. The
woman who had loved her work, who had felt strong and sure, had vanished. In her place was someone
who agreed with everything her partner said—what to eat, where to go on weekends—because disagreeing
felt too risky, as if any friction could break the fragile safety she was trying to hold together.
She had stopped making plans for the future. Retirement was three years away, but she could not
imagine it, could not bear to think about what would come next. Nothing in her life felt meaningful
anymore. The births she attended, the families she supported—they were just tasks, obligations. She
went through the motions, smiling, nodding, saying the right things, but inside she felt hollow. She
told herself she was weak, that a stronger person would have shaken off the exposure and moved on.
That belief had calcified into a quiet, constant self-contempt.

It was a delivery last month that brought her to therapy. A routine birth, but the mother had a
small hemorrhage. The sight of blood pooling on the pad, the smell of it mixed with the antiseptic
in the room—Helen’s stomach lurched and she had to excuse herself, claiming a sudden illness. At the
same time, the father, who had been calm throughout, suddenly raised his voice to call for the
doctor, and that sharp, angry tone sent a jolt through her chest. She sat in the staff bathroom for
twenty minutes, unable to stop shaking, replaying both the chemical sting and the shove. Her
supervisor noticed and gently suggested she take some leave. Helen agreed, then spent the next week
at home, avoiding any thought about the incident, about the therapy she had finally scheduled. When
she came to the first session, she was polite, agreeable, answering questions with careful, minimal
responses. She said she hoped therapy would help, but what she thought was: *I don’t know who I am
anymore, and I’m afraid that if I talk about it, I’ll find out I’ve been gone for a long time.*
