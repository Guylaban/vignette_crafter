Margaret is a 78-year-old graphic designer, married and still working part-time from the home studio
she built in the basement of the house she and her husband bought fifty years ago. Two years ago,
during a late-summer wildfire that swept through their county, she was alone in that studio when the
evacuation order came too late. The fire had jumped the highway. She could see smoke pouring under
the door at the top of the basement stairs—the heavy oak door that led to the kitchen. She ran up,
but the wind had sucked the door shut and the metal latch jammed. She remembers pounding on it,
screaming for her husband who was at the grocery store, then the sound of the door rattling in its
frame as the fire trucks arrived and someone finally forced it open from the outside. That door—its
slam, its shudder, the way it sealed her in—has never stopped echoing.

Now Margaret cannot bear the sound of any door opening or closing abruptly. When her husband comes
home and lets the front door swing shut, her chest tightens and she feels the heat of that afternoon
on her skin. She has taken to sitting in the living room where she can see the front door at all
times, and she has asked him to open and close it slowly, without letting it catch. He tries, but
sometimes he forgets, and the sharp click sends her heart racing. She knows it is irrational—she is
not trapped now—but that knowledge does nothing to stop the feeling that the fire is happening
again, that she is back in the smoke and the panic. At other times she can recall the event exactly,
but the memory feels flat, almost as though it happened to someone else, and she wonders if she has
gone numb. Then, without warning, the memory will snap into the present: she is once again pounding
on the door, the smoke stinging her eyes, and she cannot convince herself that it is over.

The fire itself was contained within a few hours, but the aftermath has been slow and corrosive.
Margaret’s neighbourhood was spared, yet she lost something less tangible. She had always believed
that if you prepared carefully, stayed calm, and followed the rules, you would be safe. That belief
is gone. She now watches the weather alerts with a vigilance that exhausts her, and she has started
cleaning and reorganising her studio repeatedly—rearranging her drafting table, wiping down her
monitors, stacking her sketchbooks in precise piles—because for a few minutes the orderliness pushes
the dread aside. But the relief never lasts, and afterward she berates herself for wasting time. She
also finds herself dwelling, sometimes for hours, on how the disaster could have been prevented: if
the county had cleared more brush, if the evacuation routes had been announced sooner, if she had
kept a crowbar by the basement door. The dwelling does nothing to change anything, but she cannot
stop.

One of the hardest daily reminders comes from conversations with her neighbours or the news. Any
mention of someone losing a loved one in a disaster—a family in California who lost a child, a widow
who lost her husband in a hurricane—stops her cold. The talk does not directly remind her of a loss
she suffered; she and her husband survived unharmed. But the injustice of it, the randomness, feels
like a personal accusation. She has come to believe that the world is fundamentally dangerous, that
no amount of preparation matters, and that what happened to her—the terrifying moments of
entrapment—was deeply unfair, and nothing has been done about it. No one was held accountable for
the poor planning, and the county still has not improved the emergency alerts. That sense of
injustice gnaws at her more than the fear sometimes.

The smell of alcohol is another unexpected trigger. During the evacuation, a firefighter who had
been directing traffic smelled strongly of liquor—Margaret recalls it distinctly as he helped her
out of the house. She never learned if he was drinking on duty, but now the scent of beer or
whiskey, whether from a neighbour’s barbecue or a passerby, yanks her back into that moment of
helplessness. She avoids walking past the local pub, and she has asked her husband not to drink at
home, which has created a quiet strain between them. He complies, but she knows he resents it, and
she feels guilty, yet she cannot make herself tolerate the odour.

Her attempts to push the memory away have taken another form: she tries to erase it by deliberately
not thinking about it, by refusing to say the word “fire” out loud, by changing the subject when
anyone brings up natural disasters. It does not work. The memory stays, sometimes unreal, too
extreme to have happened to her—a story that belongs to a different person. The effort of
suppression has made her more tired, less focused at work. She is a graphic designer, and her
projects require concentration; recently she missed a deadline because she spent an hour cleaning
her studio instead of finishing a layout. Her client was understanding, but Margaret felt a deep
shame.

What finally brought her to seek help was the door. Last week, her husband slammed the kitchen
cabinet door—not the basement door, just a cabinet—and she found herself on the floor, her hands
over her ears, sobbing. She decided then that she could not keep living this way, jumping at every
sound, scrubbing her house into a false safety, running from a memory that never stays buried. She
is hoping that talking about it will make the memory feel like it really is over, and not happening
right now. But she is also afraid that if she lets herself feel the full weight of that afternoon,
she will break apart—that the numbness will vanish and the terror will flood in and never stop.
