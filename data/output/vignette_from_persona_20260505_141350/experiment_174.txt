Leilani is a 66-year-old network engineer who works for a mid-sized telecommunications firm,
managing their server infrastructure from a quiet cubicle on the fourth floor. She has been widowed
for eight years, and for the first six of those she considered herself reasonably content — she had
her routines, her adult children visited on holidays, and she took quiet satisfaction in solving
complex routing problems. That changed two years ago, when a contractor came to her home to fix a
leak under the kitchen sink. He was talkative, polite, and smelled sharply of whiskey — something
she registered but dismissed because he was supposed to be there. After the work was done, he
lingered. He asked about the framed photographs on her mantel. Then he moved closer, put a hand on
her shoulder, and when she tried to step back, he grabbed her wrist. The assault happened in her own
living room, on the carpet where she and her husband used to watch Sunday football. The contractor’s
breath was sour with alcohol; his grip left bruises she stared at for weeks afterward. She never
called the police. She cleaned the carpet herself. She told no one except her daughter, six months
later, and only the barest facts.

In the months that followed, Leilani noticed a shift in what she could tolerate. A colleague
touching her elbow to get her attention made her jerk back so hard she nearly knocked over her
monitor. She began scanning rooms at work — the break room with its coffee mugs and letter openers,
the conference table with its pair of scissors clipped to a whiteboard. Every object could be
weaponized in her mind; she registered the scissors, the heavy stapler, even the edge of a glass
water pitcher, and she could not stop. People told her she was safe — her daughter, her supervisor,
a kind security guard who walked her to her car — but the words landed like sounds from underwater.
It was impossible to believe them. Her body had a memory that her mind could not talk out of.

She started avoiding anything that felt close. When a friend from work suggested drinks after hours,
Leilani invented a stomach bug; the thought of being near someone who might have alcohol on their
breath made her chest tighten. She stopped attending her weekly book club and let her garden go to
weeds — pleasant activities she had once looked forward to now felt dangerous, because they required
letting her guard down. The worst was the physical affection. Her daughter tried to hug her goodbye
at the front door, and Leilani stiffened, her arms pinned at her sides, her entire body wired for a
threat that did not come. She could see the hurt in her daughter’s eyes, but what frightened her
more was the wave of revulsion that rose in her own chest — revulsion at being touched, at having
her body touched, at the memory of that hand on her wrist.

She had come to believe that she was fundamentally incapable of preventing harm. Bad things
happened, and she could not stop them; she had not stopped it then, and she could not stop it now.
Worse still, she was convinced she had acted in ways that violated her own values — she had frozen,
she had not screamed, she had not fought, and afterward she had cleaned up as though it were a
spill. That passivity felt like a betrayal of the person she thought she was. And there was a deep,
specific shame about her body itself — what had been done to it, how it had been used without her
permission, how she now occupied it like a stranger in a borrowed coat. To control something, she
began restricting what she ate, carefully counting calories and skipping meals, because when her

At work, she started leaving her cubicle door half-closed, and she flinched audibly when a courier
set a package on her desk without warning. Her supervisor noticed the wince, the sudden reluctance

near people without tracking their hands, without scanning for danger, without feeling the old
helplessness rise in her throat. She finally scheduled an appointment with a therapist not because
she thought she deserved to feel better, but because her daughter had wept on the phone and asked
her to try. Leilani is afraid of what talking will uncover — afraid that naming the shame will make
it permanent, and afraid that if she ever lets herself cry, she will not stop.
