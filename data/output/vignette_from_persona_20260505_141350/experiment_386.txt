James is a 62-year-old stock broker who came to therapy on the recommendation of his physician after
a routine check-up revealed dangerously elevated blood pressure. He had lost twenty pounds in the
past two months and admitted he was sleeping only three or four hours a night. For a long time he
had told himself he was fine, that his life was simply hard and he needed to push through, but
something had shifted after a recent trading day when he found himself standing on the balcony of
his firm’s thirty-second-floor office, gripping the railing and wondering whether it would be easier
just to let go. He had no plan, he was quick to insist, but the image of himself leaning forward had
scared him enough to make the appointment.

The event that had unhinged James happened eighteen months earlier, on a cold Tuesday in March. His
younger brother Daniel, fifty-seven, had been driving home from his own accounting job when a
delivery truck ran a red light and T-boned his sedan at an intersection less than a mile from the
house where they had grown up. James received the call from Daniel’s wife while he was still at his
desk, watching bond yields climb. He remembered the precise second he heard the words — the digital
clock on his monitor read 4:17 — but after that the memory fragmented. He could not recall driving
to the hospital, or the conversation with the doctor, or even the moment he identified the body.
What stayed were disjointed snapshots: the fluorescent hum of the hospital corridor, the cold metal
of the chair he sat in for hours, the way his own reflection in the elevator mirrored back a face he
did not recognize. He tried deliberately to reconstruct that night, to put the pieces in order, but
the more he strained the more the images slipped away, leaving only that empty, buzzing feeling.

In the weeks and months that followed, James developed a set of habits that he considered common
sense. He stopped listening to jazz because Daniel had been a drummer and every saxophone riff felt
like a punch. He avoided the grocery store aisle where Daniel’s favorite brand of hot sauce was
kept. When his sister called to reminisce about their childhood, he let the voicemail pile up until
the inbox was full. The hardest thing to avoid was his own mind. For hours at his desk he would
catch himself running the same loop: why had he not answered Daniel’s last text? Why had he canceled
their usual Wednesday lunch because a client meeting ran long? The question — *why did this happen
to me, to us?* — played over and over, a scratched record he could not lift the needle from. He
tried to push these thoughts away by focusing on the market, squeezing his eyes shut and repeating
stock symbols like a mantra. It worked for a few minutes, sometimes.

But the memories did not stay banished. They arrived as fragments, unbidden. The sound of a car horn
on the street could suddenly throw him back into the hospital lobby, his brother’s wife’s hand
gripping his arm, the antiseptic smell. His heart would pound, his palms would sweat, and for a
dizzying moment he would be there again, not quite knowing where he was. These episodes came without
warning and vanished just as quickly, leaving him disoriented and ashamed. He learned to scan his
environment constantly — the hum of engines, the ring of a phone, the sight of a blue sedan like
Daniel’s — so he could brace himself. At work, colleagues noticed he had become edgy, quick to snap,
and unpredictably daring. He started making trades that were reckless even by his own standards:
leveraged positions that could wipe out a quarter’s profit in an hour. When a junior analyst warned
him against one bet, James snapped that the kid did not know what real risk looked like. He felt a
perverse thrill in the danger, as if tempting something to punish him further. The guilt was a
living thing inside his chest, and the risky behavior was the only way he could feel anything
besides it.

His social world contracted. He had been divorced for ten years and had no daily contact with his
grown daughter, who lived in another state. His few close friends stopped calling after he declined
every dinner invitation, and his drinking crept upward — a glass of whiskey became three, then four,
though he never considered it a problem because he never missed a trading day. The consequences of
his avoidance accumulated quietly: his apartment grew stale and empty, his mail went unopened, and
he found himself skipping holidays entirely, claiming he had to work.

What finally brought James to seek help was not the isolation or the drinking or even the weight
loss. It was a moment two weeks before his physician’s appointment when a loud truck backfired while
he was crossing the street. He dropped to his knees on the sidewalk, heart cannonading, convinced
for an instant that he was back in the hospital corridor and the fluorescent lights were about to
flicker out. A stranger helped him up, and James realized he could not remember the last time he had
felt truly awake without the fog of a flashback or the grip of his own endless rumination. He was
afraid of what he might do next, of the part of him that kept reaching for danger, and he was
desperate to know whether the pieces of his life before the accident could ever be put back
together.
