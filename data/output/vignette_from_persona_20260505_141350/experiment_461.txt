Kai Nakamura was seventy-seven years old, a retired firefighter in a small Midwestern city, but the
war that had ended half a century ago still owned him. In the spring of 1975, as a young man in a
coastal village in southeastern Asia, he had been a civilian in a war zone. He remembered the
morning his neighbor’s house took a direct hit from an aerial bomb. The blast had thrown him against
a wall, and for a long moment the world had been nothing but pressure and a high, ringing silence.
When his hearing returned, he heard a woman—Mrs. Tran from two houses down—screaming her children’s
names over and over. *Minh! Anh!* He never found out if they had survived. He had fled the village
that same night, walked for three days, and eventually made his way to a refugee camp, then to
America.

The memory had never faded. It arrived without warning—a vivid, full-color replay of the explosion,
the screams, the smell of burning wood and flesh. And yet, when Kai tried to hold it in his own
hands, it felt foreign, as though it belonged to a different man entirely, someone else’s history
playing behind his eyes. He had learned to avoid the memory altogether, because he was certain that
if he let himself think about it, he would come apart. So he did not think about it. He kept moving,
working, scanning.

For decades, being a firefighter had suited him. The job gave him a reason to stay alert. Even after
a long shift, when he came home to his wife, Mayumi, he found himself checking the shadows in the
backyard, the corners of the living room, the parking lot outside the grocery store. He did not call
it hypervigilance; he called it being careful. But there was a darker current, too. Late at night,
when sleep refused to come, Kai would lie still in the dark and imagine the people responsible for
the bombing—the pilots, the commanders, whoever had given the order. He pictured them suffering,
pictured himself making them suffer. The fantasies were vivid and detailed, and each time they left
him feeling hollow, as if he had ceased to be a person and become only a container for rage.

The triggers had become harder to avoid as he aged. Hearing certain Vietnamese names in
conversation—even just on television or in a store—would send him spinning back to that morning,
Mrs. Tran’s voice piercing through the ringing in his ears. Ambulance sirens were even worse. They
had never bothered him on the job; he had ridden in plenty. But now, in retirement, the high-low
wail passing his house made his chest lock. And bangs—a car backfiring, a door slammed too hard, a
child’s balloon popping—these sounds produced a physical response he could not control: a flinch, a
cold sweat, a need to drop to the floor.

Sleep had become the enemy. Kai could not lie in the dark without the memories forcing their way in.
So he stayed up until exhaustion overwhelmed him, usually in the armchair with the television on and
all the lights blazing. Mayumi had tried to coax him into bed, but he had snapped at her more than
once, and the guilt afterward was a separate weight. He told himself that someone else—a stronger
man, a soldier, a real survivor—would never have let himself get trapped in that situation in the
first place. He had been a civilian, unarmed, helpless. That thought corroded him.

What finally brought Kai to a therapist’s office was not his own decision. Mayumi had found him in
the garage an hour after an ambulance tore past their house at 3 a.m., sitting on a crate with his
hands over his ears, rocking. She had not threatened or begged; she had simply asked him, “What are
you so afraid of?” and he had not been able to answer. Now, as he sat in the waiting room, he was
terrified of what he might uncover if he spoke about the memory at all. But he was also tired—so
tired of being a man who could not lie down, who checked door frames for threats in his own home,
who clung to revenge fantasies like they were the only proof he was still alive.
