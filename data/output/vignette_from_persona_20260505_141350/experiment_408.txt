Amanda is twenty-five years old and has been driving a city bus for the past three years. Before
that, she served a single deployment as a tactical vehicle operator in a combat zone. One morning,
during a routine supply run, the lead vehicle in her convoy struck an improvised explosive device.
The blast wave slammed into her own vehicle before she could react—she never saw it coming, never
heard a warning. The world went silent and spinning. She came to with blood in her mouth, her ears
ringing, and the realization that she had no idea where the next attack might come from. That moment
of disorientation—the dizziness that had no clear cause, the utter unpredictability of the
explosion—never fully left her.

In the months after returning home, Amanda found that ordinary stress began to feel like the
precursor to an ambush. A sudden deadline at work, a raised voice in traffic, an argument with a
friend—her body would react before her mind could catch up. Her heart would pound, her palms would
sweat, and she would feel a familiar lightheadedness, as if the ground were tilting beneath her. The
dizziness was the worst part, because it made her feel out of control, and out of control meant
vulnerable. She began to scan her surroundings constantly, even in places that should have been
safe: the grocery store, her apartment hallway, the break room. She learned to position herself in
rooms so that her back was against a wall, where she could see every door, every window, every
approaching face. At home, she rearranged her furniture. On the bus, she could not, and that was a
problem.

As a bus driver, Amanda is exposed to unpredictability every shift. A car swerving into her lane, a
passenger shouting, a child running across the street—each sudden event triggers a visceral
reaction. Her chest tightens, her hands grip the wheel, and she feels the world spin again, just for
a second. She has learned to suppress the outward panic, but inside she is always on edge. She scans
the road ahead twenty times a minute, watching for anyone who might step off the curb, any driver
who might change lanes without a signal. She checks her mirrors obsessively, searching for threats
from behind. The smallest surprise—a horn honk, a brake light flash—makes her flinch. She has begun
to dread her own route, because she cannot predict what will happen, and unpredictability now feels
like the moment before the blast.

Her sense of herself has crumbled. Before the deployment, Amanda had been proud of her driving
skills—she was the one people called when something needed to get through. She could navigate a
seventeen-ton truck through a narrow alley in the dark. Now she is terrified of a school bus full of
children. She has come to believe that she is no longer capable of the things that used to define
her: decisiveness, calm under pressure, competence. She tells herself that her life has been
destroyed by the trauma—not just the event itself, but the person she became afterward. Nothing
feels meaningful anymore. She goes through the motions of her job, her conversations, her evenings
alone in her apartment, but it all feels hollow. She used to enjoy fixing up old bicycles in her
spare time; now the tools sit untouched in the corner. She has stopped returning calls from friends
because she cannot explain why she is so tired, so jumpy, so changed.

What finally brought Amanda to a clinic was a morning a week ago when she could not bring herself to
step onto the bus. She sat in the driver’s seat of the parked vehicle, her back pressed against the
seat as tightly as she could manage, and felt the dizziness surge. She called in sick, then sat on
her floor with her back to the wall for two hours. She realized she could not keep living this way.
She is terrified that the therapy will ask her to relive the blast—she is certain that if she
remembers it clearly, she will fall apart. But she is also desperate to feel capable again, to find
something that matters, to stop scanning for danger in every quiet moment.
