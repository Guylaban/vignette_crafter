James is a 36-year-old tax consultant, married for eight years, living in a midsized Midwestern
city. He walked into the office on a Tuesday afternoon, clutching a referral from his primary care
physician. His wife had made the appointment, but now that he was here, he wasn’t sure he could
explain himself. What he could say was that the metallic taste of blood still flooded his mouth
whenever his wife raised her voice during an argument—that same copper tang from the morning his
convoy was ambushed twelve years ago outside a small village in eastern Afghanistan. He had been a
specialist, riding in the second Humvee. The noise, the shouting, the dust, and then the taste of
blood from biting his tongue when the blast threw him against the seatback. He could still taste it,
vividly, as if it were happening right then, and the feeling would overwhelm him so completely that
he had to stop whatever he was doing, sometimes mid-sentence in a client meeting, and sit perfectly
still until the wave passed.

After the ambush, James tried to return to normal life. He finished his deployment, left the
service, married his college girlfriend, and found steady work preparing individual and small-
business tax returns. For a few years, things felt manageable. Then, gradually, the world began to
close in. His work as a tax consultant required interactions with clients, and he soon noticed that
any conflict—a client disputing a deduction, a colleague questioning his numbers—sent his heart
racing. He began avoiding certain conversations altogether: anything about veterans, overseas
deployment, or even loud political debates. When a coworker brought up a news report about military
casualties, James would excuse himself to the restroom, his mouth filling with that familiar taste.
He started feeling physically unwell more often—headaches, stomachaches—and he noticed that those
bodily sensations themselves became triggers. If he woke up nauseous, he’d immediately flash back to
that Humvee, the disorientation after the blast, and he’d spend the rest of the day on edge.

To cope, he developed a set of habits that at first seemed practical to him. He began avoiding any
physical contact with others. Handshakes felt unbearable; he would stiffen and pull away. If a
colleague clapped him on the back, James would flinch and take a step back, earning him a reputation
as cold or standoffish. At home, his wife’s gentle touches on his shoulder or arm made him react
with alarm, and he would withdraw into himself, apologizing softly. He also found himself pushing
away positive emotions. When his wife told him she loved him, or when he felt a moment of
contentment watching a sunset, a creeping sense of danger would rise—he was certain that something
bad was just around the corner, that letting himself feel good was an invitation for disaster. So he
stayed flat, neutral, unreachable. He never talked about the ambush, not to his wife, not to his
brothers-in-arms, not to anyone. When asked, he changed the subject or said he didn’t remember much.

The silence itself became a threat. James worked in an open-plan office, and during lulls when no
one was typing or speaking, the quiet would press in on him. He would scan the room, identify the
nearest exit, and mentally trace the route to the stairwell. He did this everywhere now: every
restaurant, every movie theater, every parking lot. He had to know his escape route before he could
feel even slightly comfortable in any space. His wife had noticed him doing it during dinner at a
friend’s house, and she had asked why he kept looking at the back door. He couldn’t explain it.

Sometimes, when the memory returned, he would feel nothing at all—a stark, hollow numbness, as if he
were watching a scene from a movie about someone else. Those moments were almost more confusing than
the overwhelming ones. He would sit at his desk, staring at a spreadsheet, and realize he had just
relived the blast without any emotion, like a robot. It made him wonder if he was broken in a deeper
way.

The consequences accumulated. At work, he had been passed over for a promotion to senior consultant
because he had refused to lead a team meeting that he thought might involve a contentious discussion
about new IRS guidelines. He told his supervisor he wasn’t feeling well, which was true—just not for
the reasons they assumed. His marriage had become a careful dance of avoidance. His wife stopped
reaching for him, stopped asking about his day, stopped suggesting they attend social events. They
lived in a quiet, tension-filled coexistence. He knew she was hurt, but he couldn't find a way to
explain why he recoiled from her touch or why he seemed to fear happiness. The thought of telling
her the truth—the taste of blood, the overwhelming flashbacks, the watchful dread—felt like a second
ambush he had no strength to survive.

What finally brought James here was a minor illness. He caught a cold that left him feverish and
dizzy, and the physical vulnerability triggered a three-day spiral of intrusive memories that he
could not stop. His wife found him sitting in the dark in the living room at 2 a.m., trembling, and
she wept. She begged him to get help. He agreed, but only because he was exhausted. What he fears
most now is that talking about the event will make it worse, that the numbness will disappear and
he’ll drown in the emotion he has been pushing away for years. What he hopes for—though he barely
admits it even to himself—is that someone can help him feel safe in his own skin, so that a touch
from his wife no longer feels like an attack.
