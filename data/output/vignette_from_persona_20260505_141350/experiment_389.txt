Mai was thirty-two when the abuse began, and she did not recognise it for what it was until much
later. Her partner had always been intense, but the first time he grabbed her wrist and forced her
to her knees, she told herself it was a misunderstanding. He was angry about something she had
said—she could not remember what—and he wanted her to apologise. When she hesitated, he pressed an
ice pack from the freezer against the back of her neck and held it there, saying she needed to cool
down. She stayed frozen, not because of the cold but because she believed that if she moved, he
would hurt her more. That moment—the specific sensation of that cold pressure, the feeling of having
no choice, the grip of his hand—became a template for everything that followed.

Over the next year, the incidents multiplied. He would trap her in rooms, demand she repeat
apologies, grab her arm when she tried to leave. She learned to comply. But the memory never felt
like something in the past. When she was triggered—a patient’s hand on her shoulder, the chill of a
therapy ice pack, a supervisor telling her to change a treatment plan—the scene would return as if
it were happening again. Her chest would tighten, her vision would narrow, and she would need to
excuse herself to the staff bathroom, where she would sit on the floor until the shaking stopped.
She could never explain what happened if a colleague asked. The words would tangle, and she would
trail off, overwhelmed by a rush of emotion that left her silent.

Mai began to avoid. She stopped accepting evening shifts because the parking lot felt unsafe. She
requested to work only with female patients, though she could not say why. When a male patient
needed hands-on therapy, she felt a flash of heat and dread, and she would hand him off to a
coworker with a vague excuse about her schedule. At home, she avoided her husband’s touch in certain
ways—flinching when he reached for her, sleeping on the far edge of the bed. He did not know about
the abuse; she had never told him. She was certain that if he knew, he would see her the way she saw
herself: defective, humiliated, someone who had failed to prevent what happened.

The self-blame was relentless. She replayed the arguments, the moments before the ice pack, the
times she could have left. She dwelled on the person she used to be—confident, easy with her hands,
quick to laugh—and saw her current self as a stranger. She was vigilant in every room, scanning for
men who seemed angry or controlling, cataloguing exits. The rumination about prevention exhausted
her: if only she had not said that, if only she had left earlier, if only she had been stronger. She
believed the abuse had happened because of the way she acted, and that belief made the world feel
dangerous in a way she could not control.

The consequences accumulated. Her supervisor noticed she was avoiding certain patients and asked if
she was burned out. Mai said yes. Her marriage grew distant. She stopped meeting friends for dinner.
The hypervigilance bled into her sleep, and she woke each morning already tired. What finally
brought her to therapy was a moment at work: a patient with a mild stroke needed passive range of
motion on his left arm, and when Mai took his hand, the temperature of his skin—cool, slightly
damp—sent her straight back to the kitchen floor, the ice pack, the grip. She froze for several
seconds, then excused herself and vomited in the bathroom. That evening, she searched online for a
trauma therapist and made an appointment.

She is afraid of what she will find in the memories she has tried so hard to contain. But she is
also afraid of staying as she is—of losing her career, her marriage, the last pieces of the person
she used to be. She hopes the therapy will help her stop living in a moment that already ended,
though she cannot yet imagine how that would feel.
