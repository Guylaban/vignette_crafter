Elena Ramirez was fifty-four years old, a combat engineer who had spent twenty years in the Army
before retiring, but the mission that ended her career never left her. It happened during a route
clearance patrol in a province she still could not name aloud. The lead vehicle hit an IED, and
within seconds the whole convoy was under fire. She remembered the heat—a specific, searing
temperature on her face and arms, as though the air itself had turned to metal. She remembered
shouting, men screaming orders and pain, and then the flames: orange and black, climbing from the
burning hull. That was all. She could not tell a clear story of what happened even to herself. The
memory arrived without warning, triggered not by a deliberate thought but by something in her
surroundings: the flicker of a gas stove, a colleague’s raised voice in the workshop, the sudden
warmth of sunlight through a car window. When it came, she heard things in her head—the shouts, the
crackle of fire, a sound like her own heartbeat.

In the months after she was evacuated, Elena tried to push it all away. She learned to avoid
anything that could be stressful, which meant almost everything. She stopped watching news that
showed combat footage. She stopped attending the unit’s memorial ceremonies. At home, she turned the
television off whenever a scene involved fire. Her husband, Marco, noticed that she had become
quiet, distant. She would sit beside him on the couch but feel nothing—a hollow numbness that she
had come to rely on. She told herself it was better than the alternative. She had also developed a
habit of scanning every room she entered, checking doorways, windows, the faces of strangers,
looking for signs of threat. Her body felt constantly tense, ready to react, as if danger were
always just a moment away. Silence unsettled her most. An unexpected quiet in the house—the furnace
turning off, the neighbor’s dog falling silent—made her certain something terrible was about to
happen. Her concentration suffered; she could not read a book or follow a conversation without her
mind drifting back to that heat, those shouts.

She took a job as a safety inspector at a manufacturing plant, thinking it would be controlled and
predictable. It was not. The plant had welding stations where flames hissed, and workers often
called out to each other across the warehouse floor. Every time she heard a shout, her chest
tightened and she had to force herself not to flee. The heat from a furnace line made her skin
prickle with old terror. She began to avoid entire sections of the facility, delegating inspections
to a junior colleague. At night, she checked the house three times before she could lie down. Marco
asked her what was wrong, and she said she did not know. She did know, but the words felt useless.

What frightened Elena most was the growing conviction that there was something wrong with her as a
person. She had survived, but she felt broken in a way that could not be fixed. The injustice of
what happened—the ambush, the chaos, the lack of accountability—gnawed at her. Nothing had been done
about it. The world, she was certain, was a dangerous place, and she was defective for having been
caught in it. She came to the clinic only after Marco told her he could not watch her suffer
anymore. She did not know if she wanted to be fixed, but she was tired of the constant scanning, the
heat on her skin that came from nowhere, the chaos in her head that refused to become a story. She
hoped, at least, that someone might tell her she was not the only one.
