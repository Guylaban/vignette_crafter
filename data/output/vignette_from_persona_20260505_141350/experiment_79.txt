Minh is a 47-year-old park ranger who lives with his wife in a quiet town at the edge of a national
forest. For the past three years, he has been unable to shake the morning he drove his twelve-year-
old son, Kai, to the emergency room. Kai had woken up with a high fever and a stiff neck, and by the
time they reached the hospital, he was slipping in and out of consciousness. The diagnosis was
bacterial meningitis. At the time, the attending physician, a man with grey temples and a calm
demeanor, told Minh it would be fine. But Minh could smell alcohol on the doctor’s breath—faint,
like whiskey—and he remembers feeling a cold certainty that the man was not fully present. The next
twelve hours were a blur of beeping monitors, antiseptic stinging his nostrils, and the sight of Kai
lying still while doctors rushed around him. Kai survived after a course of aggressive antibiotics,
but the medical team later acknowledged that a lumbar puncture had been delayed, and Minh has never
stopped believing that the delay was because the doctor was impaired. He has not pursued a formal
complaint; he has not confronted anyone. Instead, he spends long stretches of the day—sometimes
while patrolling a trail or standing at a scenic overlook—imagining the precise words he would say
to that doctor in a confrontation, or worse, the humiliating public exposure he could engineer. He
dwells on it until his jaw aches. He knows these thoughts are not productive, but they feel like the
only justice he can claim.

At work, Minh is often alone, which he prefers. The woods are quiet, and he can control his
environment. But the job is not a fortress. Twice a week he restocks the first-aid kit in his patrol
truck, and the moment he opens the plastic lid the sharp smell of antiseptic washes over him. He
finds himself holding his breath, then feeling lightheaded. The dizziness started about six months
after Kai’s discharge and has worsened. It now happens whenever he enters a room with a strong
chemical smell, or even when he thinks about the hospital too vividly. Once, during a ranger-led
hike, a visitor pulled out a small flask of whiskey and offered it to his friend. Minh caught the
scent from ten feet away. His vision swam, he lost his balance, and he had to sit down on a log. He
told the group it was the heat. He now deliberately avoids the area near the campground where he
knows alcohol is common, and he has asked his supervisor to reassign him from any programs that
involve first-aid demonstrations. His supervisor has noticed a pattern of cancellations but has not
pushed.

Minh’s avoidance extends into his personal life. His wife, Linh, sometimes brings up Kai’s illness
in passing—mentioning how close they came to losing him, how grateful they should be. Minh cannot
tolerate this. He cuts her off, or leaves the room. He has stopped visiting Linh’s family because
her sister is a nurse and someone always brings up a hospital story. He has also drifted away from
his closest friend, a fellow ranger who once asked casually, “So, how’s Kai doing now?” Minh saw
that question as a reminder of the event, as if the friend was reopening the wound on purpose. He
answered curtly and has since avoided shared shifts. Linh has grown frustrated; she says he has
become distant and angry. Minh knows this is true, but he feels that if he stops being angry, he
will have to face the terror of those hours alone in the hospital, and he cannot.

What finally brought Minh to a therapist’s office was a panic attack that occurred while he was
driving home from work. A car in front of him swerved, and the sudden motion made him dizzy. He
pulled over, certain he was going to pass out, and for twenty minutes he sat with his head between
his knees, replaying the moment the doctor came out of Kai’s room with that slight, boozy breath.
The next morning, Linh told him she could not live like this anymore. Minh is afraid that talking
about the event will make it worse—that the memories will take over completely. But he is also
afraid that if he does not change, he will lose his wife and his job, and that the revenge fantasies
will become the only thing he has left.
