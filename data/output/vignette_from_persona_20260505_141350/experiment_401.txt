Mark is a 58-year-old dentist who has been practicing in a midsize suburban town for nearly three
decades. He lives with his wife, Laura, in the same house where they raised their two children, now
grown and living out of state. What he cannot bring himself to talk about, even to Laura, began on a
Saturday evening two years ago. Driving home from a rare late appointment, he was pulled over by a
patrol officer. The official report states that the officer misidentified Mark’s vehicle as matching
a suspect from an earlier incident. Within seconds of approaching the car, the officer ordered him
out, forced him to the ground, and struck him repeatedly with a baton while shouting commands Mark
could not process. He felt cracking pain in his ribs and a searing blow to his lower back. The
encounter ended only when a supervisor arrived and recognized the mistake. No charges were filed.
The officer was placed on administrative leave and later reinstated. There was no apology, no
disciplinary action that Mark ever saw.

In the months that followed, Mark noticed a change he could not name. The memory of that night felt
as though it belonged to someone else—something he had watched on a screen rather than lived. Yet
when the memory returned, unbidden, it was anything but distant. He would hear the officer’s voice
yelling, the thud of the baton, the gravel scraping his cheek. And he would feel the same sharp pain
in his ribs and lower back, even though the bruises had healed long ago. He began to believe that
there must be something about him that had caused the violence. He replayed the moment he reached
for his license, wondering if his hand had moved too fast, if his tone had been wrong. He told
himself that if he had been a different kind of man—calmer, more submissive, less noticeable—it
would not have happened.

He started to avoid anything that might stir emotion, positive or negative. He stopped listening to
music in the car because a song could lift his mood, and he found that unacceptable. He also began
steering clear of any potentially stressful situation: he declined invitations to family gatherings,
stopped attending his weekly poker game, and limited his dental practice to routine cleanings and
fillings, referring complex cases to colleagues. At the office, he found himself scanning every
waiting patient as a potential threat. Strangers approaching him in ordinary settings—a man asking
for directions, a woman at the grocery store checkout—made his heart race and his hands tremble. He
could no longer sit with his back to a door. At restaurants, he would insist on a booth facing the
entrance, and at home he moved his armchair to face the hallway. His sleep became fragmented; he
would lie awake for hours, replaying the officer’s shouted commands, or jolt awake in the dark,
convinced someone was entering the bedroom.

These strategies eroded his life quietly. His wife began to complain that he was distant, that he
had become a stranger in their own home. His practice lost a few longtime patients who sensed his
unease. He stopped going to the local hardware store where the clerk always greeted him too
cheerfully. The world outside his dental chair had grown treacherous, and the only way to manage it
was to shrink his orbit.

What finally brought Mark to seek help was a moment of quiet desperation. He had spent a Sunday
afternoon in his home office, curtains drawn, refusing to answer the phone when his sister called.
He looked at the framed photograph of his children on his desk and felt nothing. His life, he
thought, had been destroyed. What happened to him was deeply unjust, and nothing—absolutely
nothing—had been done about it. He was not angry anymore; he was hollow. The next morning he called
a therapist who specialized in trauma. He did not know if talking could change anything, but he was
afraid of who he had become, and more afraid that this was all that was left.
