Kwame was sixty-eight years old when he finally walked into a therapist’s office, a policy analyst
whose careful, data-driven mind had always been his greatest asset. Now it felt like a trap. The
traumatic event that had upended his life was not a single moment of violence but a creeping,
invisible enemy: a life-threatening illness that had nearly killed him two years earlier. He had
been hospitalized with a severe bacterial infection that spiraled into sepsis, his organs beginning
to fail while doctors fought to stabilize him. He remembered the fluorescent lights of the ICU, the
beeping monitors, the prick of needles, and the terrifying sensation of his own body turning against
him. That was the moment his relationship with his own flesh had fractured. He survived, but
something inside him had not.

In the months that followed, Kwame returned to his job at a state agency, surrounded by policy
briefs and spreadsheets. But the concentration that had once made him a reliable analyst had
evaporated. He would stare at a page of text, the words blurring, unable to hold a single policy
argument in his head before his mind veered back to the hospital room. His body felt constantly
tense, as if coiled for a threat he could not see. Even inside his own home—the quiet condo he had
shared with his late wife before she passed away a year after his illness—he could not relax. He
found himself scanning the rooms, checking his own pulse, listening for any change in his breathing.
His body, which had once carried him through decades of work and marriage, now felt like a foreign
agent, unreliable, a source of danger.

The memory of his illness did not behave the way he expected. Sometimes, when he tried to recall the
hospitalization, he felt absolutely nothing—a flat, numb detachment, as if he were watching a
stranger die. He would describe it to himself as “not my own story.” And yet, at unpredictable
moments, the memory would slam into him with full sensory force: he would be sitting at his desk, or
opening the refrigerator, and suddenly he was back in that bed, the sterile smell in his nostrils,
the weight of the oxygen mask. In those moments, the past became the present. He would be so
overwhelmed by fear and helplessness that he had to stop whatever he was doing, gripping the edge of
the table, waiting for the wave to pass.

Kwame had come to believe that he would never feel normal emotions again. The joy he used to take in
his work, the warmth of remembering his wife’s laughter—all of it felt sealed behind a wall. His
body kept reacting as if he were still fighting for his life, even when he was perfectly safe. He
began to mistrust every sensation: a racing heart meant another crisis, a twinge of pain meant a new
infection. He stopped going to the gym because he did not want to feel his heart pound. At home, he
avoided looking at his own reflection too long, afraid of what his body might reveal. The
hypervigilance was exhausting, and he became isolated. Colleagues noticed his distractibility; he
had been passed over for a project lead because he could not complete a report on time.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was a morning when he woke up already on high alert, his
fists clenched, his jaw tight, before he had even opened his eyes. He realized he could not go on
like this. His wife’s death had left him without a confidante, and he had no one to tell that he was
afraid of his own body, that he felt like a man trapped in a faulty machine. In the therapist’s
office, he sat rigidly, his hands resting on his knees, waiting for the moment he would be told he
was beyond help. He was afraid of the answer, but he was more afraid of spending the rest of his
years as a stranger to himself.
