Kimo is a 71-year-old cashier at a busy supermarket in Honolulu, married for forty-seven years to
his wife, Leilani. Three years ago, on a humid evening during his shift, a man walked through the
automatic doors and within seconds shot a customer in the checkout line over a trivial argument.
Kimo stood frozen at his register, ten feet away. The customer collapsed; the shooter fled. A young
woman with a baby was standing nearby, and the baby began to wail—a high, piercing cry that seemed
to go on for hours. In reality it was only a few minutes until paramedics arrived, but that sound
has never left him. The perpetrator was never caught. Store management told employees not to discuss
the incident and offered no counseling; the police investigation went nowhere. To Kimo, the people
who should have protected him—the store, the security guards who arrived too late, the police who
never made an arrest—simply failed. The shooter faced no consequences, and that felt like a second
wound.

Since that night, Kimo’s memory of the event has remained fractured. He can recall the door sliding
open and the argument, but the order of what happened next is a blur. When Leilani asks him about
it, he starts to explain, then loses the thread—the words scatter. He cannot tell a clear story even
to himself; there are gaps where the terror should be, only a blank. He has stopped trying.

At work, the automatic doors are a constant source of dread. Each time they slide open or slam shut,
his body jerks as if the shooter has returned. Unexpected movement—a customer reaching quickly for a
wallet, a child darting past him—triggers the same jolt, flooding him with adrenaline and the
conviction that danger is imminent. He has become hypervigilant, scanning every face and hand in the
checkout line, watching for any sign of threat. He reads hostility in ordinary gestures: a man
adjusting his jacket, a woman shifting a bag. Kimo knows this is irrational, but he cannot stop. The
sound of a baby crying—any baby, anywhere in the store—sends a spike of fear through him, instantly
transporting him back to that high-pitched wail mixed with the gunshots.

He has grown emotionally numb. He feels dead inside, going through the motions of work and family
life without any real connection. Leilani has noticed he no longer laughs at her jokes, no longer
reaches for her hand. He avoids the back aisle of the store where the incident occurred, but as a
cashier he must stay at the front, near the doors. He has asked his manager to let him work in the
back office, but his seniority doesn’t allow it, and he cannot explain why without tearing up.
Instead, he endures each shift in a state of coiled tension, taking frequent breaks in the stockroom
to calm his racing heart.

What finally brought Kimo to seek help was an incident two weeks ago: a customer moved unexpectedly
toward him to grab a dropped receipt, and Kimo shouted at him to get back. The customer complained
to management, and Kimo was written up. Leilani insisted he see a therapist. He agreed, but inside
he fears that talking will bring back the parts of the memory he cannot piece together, and that he
will never feel alive again. He hopes, barely, that someone can help him make sense of it—that
maybe, after all this time, he can find a way to live without watching every movement, waiting for
the next door to slam.
