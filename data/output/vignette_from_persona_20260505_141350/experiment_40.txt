Priya Sharma is sixty-six years old, a midwife who has guided thousands of women through the most
vulnerable moments of their lives, yet she cannot recall the last time she felt truly safe in her
own body. The trauma happened during her brief service in the Army decades ago, when she was a young
woman who believed in duty and structure. A superior officer, a man whose uniform gave him
unquestioned authority, cornered her alone in a storage room after a long shift. She remembers the
heavy click of the door, the way his rank insignia caught the fluorescent light, the flat certainty
in his eyes as he watched her. She never reported it. What would have been the point? He was an
officer, she was a nurse, and the uniform made him untouchable. She learned that night that men in
authority could take what they wanted, that being watched meant danger was already upon you.

After leaving the service, Priya poured her considerable discipline into midwifery. The work felt
like redemption, a world of women focused on life. But the sanctuary crumbled quickly. In the
brightly lit corridors of the large teaching hospital where she works, the triggers press in from
every side. She cannot be alone with a man. When a male anesthesiologist needs to review a patient's
chart, she insists a female nurse join them in the consultation room, or she leaves the door wide
open. She is certain that isolation with a male colleague is an invitation to harm. Authority
figures—the Chief of Obstetrics, hospital security guards, anyone in a uniform—cause her stomach to
drop. She has to fight the urge to walk away mid-conversation. She feels watched constantly: the
security cameras in the hallways, the eyes of husbands and partners in the waiting room, a nurse
glancing her way. She interprets these gazes as evaluative, predatory, a prelude to something
terrible.

To manage the constant pressure of this threat, Priya has constructed a rigorous internal system.
She throws herself into her work with an intensity that exhausts her colleagues. She takes back-to-
back shifts, takes on the most difficult high-risk deliveries, stays late to write notes. Exhaustion
is the only thing that quiets her mind. She makes sure never to be isolated. She eats lunch in the
bustling cafeteria rather than the breakroom, and she always walks to the parking lot with someone
else. She is perpetually vigilant for dangerous people, scanning the faces and postures of everyone
around her. A man walking too close, a raised voice in triage, a sudden gesture from a patient's
partner—her body shoots into full alarm before her mind can catch up. She is unable to relax, even
at home. Her husband of forty years, Raj, will reach out to her on the couch, and she flinches. A
sudden movement from him puts her immediately on guard. He has learned to announce himself before
entering a room. The distance between them has grown into a chasm she does not know how to cross. He
sees her exhaustion and her fear, but she has never told him the full story, and the silence has
become a permanent feature of their marriage.

What finally drove her to seek help was a moment during a routine delivery. A young father stood up
abruptly from his chair to see his laboring wife. The sudden movement was all it took. Priya's
vision tunneled. The hospital room dissolved, and she was transported back to that supply room. She
did not drop the baby, she did not flee the room, but for several seconds she was completely frozen,
a terrified young nurse in an army uniform again instead of the seasoned midwife she had worked so
hard to become. Afterwards, she locked herself in a supply closet and sobbed. She realized then that
the strategies she had relied on for decades—the overwork, the isolation management, the constant
scanning—were no longer containing the fear. They were hollowing her out. At sixty-six, with a
career she loves and a marriage she is losing, Priya is here because she is terrified that if she
does not find another way to live, she will spend the rest of her life waiting for the attack that
already happened. She is desperately hoping that somewhere in the telling, she might finally find a
way to let her guard down.
