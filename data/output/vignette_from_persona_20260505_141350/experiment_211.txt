Sister Margaret is a fifty-five-year-old nun who has lived in a small religious community in the
Pacific Northwest for nearly three decades. She is married to John, a fellow member of the
community, and together they served at a convent school where she taught sixth-grade history.
Eighteen months ago, during a weekend retreat at a forest camp the community had used for years, a
heavy branch snapped from an old Douglas fir and struck John as he walked a few paces behind her. He
died before help could arrive.

Margaret remembers the falling branch with terrifying clarity—the way the light caught the needles,
the angle of its descent—but the moments after she turned and saw him on the ground are a complete
blank. She cannot recall screaming, calling for help, or even reaching his side. The paramedics told
her later that she had performed CPR, but she has no memory of it. That gap in her mind has become a
source of deep shame. She is certain that if she had only shouted louder or walked more slowly, he
would have heard her warning and moved. The event happened, she believes, because of the way she
acted—or failed to act. She failed to protect him, and now she can no longer trust herself to keep
anyone safe.

Since the accident, Margaret’s memory of the event returns without any warning. The cracking sound
of a wooden floor settling, the scent of pine needles after rain, even the sight of a tall tree
through a classroom window can plunge her back into that instant. She feels the impact in her chest,
sees the branch falling, and experiences the same helpless terror. Her days are spent scanning her
surroundings for potential danger—a loose gutter, a swaying limb, any sound that might signal harm.
She has come to believe that her vigilance is the only thing preventing further tragedy.

To manage this constant fear, Margaret has developed a private ritual. Every evening, after evening
prayers, she recites a specific verse three times in her mind, repeating it until she feels a kind
of mental quiet. She is convinced that missing a repetition or saying the words wrong would invite
disaster—another sister might fall ill, a child might be hurt. The ritual takes only a few minutes,
but it leaves her exhausted and doubting her own mind. She avoids the retreat site altogether,
taking a longer route to town to steer clear of the road that leads to the camp. When the community
plans gatherings there, she invents reasons to stay behind. She has also stopped attending memorial
services for John and has removed his photographs from her room. Any reminder of him—a favorite
hymn, a shared joke, even the mention of his name—feels unbearable, so she steers conversations away
or leaves the room. Over the months, her friends have stopped bringing him up, and she has grown
isolated. She senses their concern, but what frightens her most is the possibility that if she lets
herself grieve openly, the memories will overwhelm her completely.

The consequences have crept into her work. She declined to chaperone a class field trip to a state
park, knowing the forest would trigger her. Another teacher had to take her place, and the students
noticed her absence. She feels she has let them down, just as she let John down. The moment that
finally drove her to seek help came on a Tuesday morning during a history lesson. A student snapped
a wooden ruler in half, and the sharp crack sent Margaret into a full panic attack in front of the
class. She fled to the restroom, trembling and disoriented, and sat on the floor until her breathing
slowed. The humiliation was acute, but deeper than that was the sinking recognition that she could
no longer control where or when the terror would find her.

Margaret arrives at the clinic with a tight grip on her rosary. She wants to find a way back to her
work and her community without the constant vigilance. But she is afraid that speaking about the
event will make the gaps fill in, that she will have to relive what she cannot remember, and that
she will discover something even worse about what she did or did not do.
