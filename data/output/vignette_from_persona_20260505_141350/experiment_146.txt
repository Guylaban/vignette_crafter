The crash happened on a Tuesday evening in late October, two years ago. Amir had been driving home
from a routine audit at an auto-body shop—nothing unusual, just a stack of receipts and a headache
from the fluorescent lights. He remembers the intersection, the way the light turned green, the
shape of a black sedan hurtling through the red. The impact flipped his car onto its side. The smell
of alcohol was thick in the air when the other driver stumbled out of his vehicle, and Amir, pinned
and bleeding, had that smell in his nostrils for the entire forty minutes before the fire department
cut him free. He spent three weeks in the hospital with a collapsed lung and a fractured pelvis, and
another month at home learning to walk again.

When he finally returned to work at the tax office, he tried to act as if nothing had changed. But
almost immediately, small things began to feel wrong. A car backfiring in the parking lot made him
drop his coffee and crouch behind a filing cabinet. A colleague’s cologne—something with an alcohol
base—sent his heart hammering so hard he had to excuse himself to the bathroom. He could not shake
the image of that black sedan, the crunch of metal, the tilted view of the dashboard from inside his
mangled car. It came to him without warning, especially when he saw a similar make and model on the
street, or when he passed a fender bender on the highway. His supervisor assigned him a few field
audits, but Amir found himself making excuses: traffic, bad weather, a sudden headache. He started
taking the bus, but even as a passenger he flinched at every honk and screech of brakes. Eventually
he stopped leaving the office altogether. He spent his lunch breaks staring at his phone, scrolling
through news feeds and playing puzzle games, anything to keep his mind from drifting back to that
intersection.

The undereating began as a way to control something when everything else felt out of control. He
would skip breakfast, then forget lunch, and by evening he was too tired to cook. It was easier to
lie in bed, headphones on, letting videos play one after another until his eyes burned and he
finally fell asleep. His then-wife had tried to talk to him about it—about the way he jumped at
ordinary sounds, about how he never seemed to be fully present. He told her he was fine, but he was
secretly certain that he could no longer trust himself to react the right way. He used to be the
person who handled tax audits calmly, who could negotiate with angry business owners and keep a
clear head. Now he could not even sit through a two-minute commute without his hands shaking. That
sense of capability had slipped away, and in its place was a hollow certainty that he would never
feel normal again—that some essential part of him had been crushed in that car and would not come
back. His wife left him six months after the accident. She said he was not the same man she married,
and he knew she was right.

Amir’s current life is a narrow loop of work and apartment. He has stopped driving entirely. He
takes a single bus route to the office, sitting near the driver and gripping the railing the whole
way. Any unexpected sound—a door slamming, a dropped clipboard—makes him startle so badly that his
colleagues have stopped sitting near him. At home, he spends hours on his phone into the early
morning, because the alternative is lying in the dark with the image of that black sedan, the smell
of alcohol, the feeling of being trapped. He eats just enough to function, usually a protein bar and
coffee, because a full meal feels like too much to manage.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a formal warning from his employer. His productivity had
dropped; he had refused three field assignments and had not filed a single audit report in two
weeks. His supervisor said if he did not get help, he would lose his job. Amir made the appointment
because he did not know what else to do, but he is not sure he believes therapy can change anything.
What he is most afraid of is that the man he used to be—the one who could drive anywhere, who could
feel excitement or grief or even boredom—is gone permanently. What he hopes for, though he does not
say it aloud, is that maybe some version of that man is still inside, and that a way exists to reach
him again.
