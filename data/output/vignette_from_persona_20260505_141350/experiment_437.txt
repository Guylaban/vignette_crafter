Robert, a 77-year-old delivery driver, had always taken pride in his reliability. He knew every
street in his territory, every shortcut, every house with a dog that barked too long. But that was
before the Tuesday afternoon in late October when a man he’d never seen before stepped out from
behind a parked van and slammed a crowbar into his shoulder. Robert had been carrying a heavy box
toward a front porch; he didn’t hear footsteps, didn’t see the swing coming. The blow knocked him
sideways, and before he could get his bearings, the man grabbed the box, then turned and ran. Robert
lay on the concrete for what felt like an hour, though it was probably only minutes, his arm
throbbing, his mind blank with shock. A neighbor called an ambulance. The police came, took a
report, never found the man.

In the weeks that followed, Robert told himself he was fine. He went back to work after three
days—the doctor said nothing was broken, just deep bruising. But something had changed. When he
pulled up to a delivery address, he started scanning the street before he got out. He would sit in
his truck for an extra minute or two, gripping the wheel, his stomach tight. He began to notice
every person who walked near his vehicle, every shadow between houses. His wife, Carol, noticed he
was quieter at dinner; he’d push his plate away after a few bites, then later, around ten o’clock,
he would stand at the kitchen counter eating crackers or leftover pasta without really tasting it.
He told himself it was just nerves, nothing serious.

What frightened Robert most was the thought that he might not be able to handle what happened. He
had always considered himself a calm, practical man—someone who dealt with problems, not someone who
fell apart. Now, if a memory of the assault crept into his mind—the sound of the crowbar scraping
against the van’s side, the jolt of pain—he would immediately switch on the radio or start mentally
planning his next route. He learned to keep his mind occupied hour after hour: after work he would
find small jobs around the house, rearranging the garage, cleaning out the gutters, anything to
avoid sitting still. Even sleep felt dangerous; lying awake in the dark left too much room for
replaying the scene. So he would get up and make himself a sandwich, eating slowly until exhaustion
took him.

His beliefs about the world had been quietly dismantled. Robert had always felt safe in his own
neighborhood, on his own streets. Now he saw threats everywhere. A group of teenagers laughing on a
corner made his hands shake. He refused to deliver to a certain block two miles from the attack
site, claiming the addresses were confusing. His supervisor grew frustrated, and Robert began
arriving late to avoid those streets, taking longer loops. He started to doubt his own judgment: if
he had missed the man behind that van, what else was he missing? He couldn’t trust himself to decide
where to park, whether to get out of the truck, whether to walk up to a door. The self-doubt became
a constant hum in the back of his mind.

He never spoke to Carol about the moment he froze. She asked why he was eating so much at night, why
he seemed distracted. Robert said work was busy. He felt a heavy shame about being so shaken by
something that, as he told himself, “wasn’t even that bad.” He started to avoid going out with her
to the grocery store or the hardware store—places where he might feel trapped or exposed. She went
alone, puzzled and hurt.

Robert finally came to therapy because Carol told him gently that she thought he needed help. He was
tired, he admitted—tired of the constant scanning, the extra weight around his middle, the way his
mind went blank the moment a memory surfaced. He was afraid that if he started to talk about the
assault, he would lose control entirely, fall into something he couldn’t climb out of. But he also
knew something had to change. For the first time in seventy-seven years, Robert was not sure he
could manage on his own.
