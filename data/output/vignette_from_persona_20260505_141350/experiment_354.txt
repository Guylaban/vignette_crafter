For eighteen years, Malia had been the spiritual anchor of a small but devoted congregation in
Portland. At fifty-eight, she was known for her measured voice and steady hands—hands that had
blessed newborns and held the hands of the dying. None of that remained after the accident. It
happened on a rainy March night, driving home from a grief support group she had led at the
synagogue. A pickup truck ran a red light and T-boned her sedan, spinning it into a utility pole.
The engine compartment crumpled and caught fire. Malia remembers the smell before she remembers the
impact: burnt wiring, melted rubber, the acrid sweetness of gasoline. She clawed at her seatbelt as
flames licked the hood, and by the time a bystander pulled her through the shattered passenger
window, her face was slick with tears and soot. She was hospitalized for smoke inhalation and a
fractured collarbone, but physically she healed within weeks. The smell never left her.

At first, Malia tried to manage the aftermath with sheer will. She forced herself to say only
positive things when congregants asked how she was recovering. “I’m grateful to be alive,” she would
tell them, and she meant it in the moment. But at night, she would wake gasping, the burnt smell
thick in her nostrils, her hands flying to her face as if to block out the memory. She would lie in
the dark, heart pounding, feeling the sheets around her like the jammed seatbelt. She began to dread
going to sleep. The nightmares didn’t always feature the crash itself—sometimes it was just the
smell, or the sensation of being pinned, and she would wake with her hands pressed against her face,
covering her eyes. During the day, when the kitchen at the synagogue prepared the weekly Shabbat
dinner, the slightest whiff of scorched bread or a forgotten pot would send her heart racing. She
learned to stay out of the kitchen entirely, and eventually stopped attending the communal meals
altogether, claiming a busy schedule.

The person she saw in the mirror became a stranger. Malia, who once took pride in her calm counsel,
now snapped at the synagogue’s administrative assistant for a misplaced paper. She yelled at a
congregant who touched her arm to offer condolences—a gentle touch that made her flinch as if
burned. “Don’t touch me,” she had hissed, and then immediately apologized, but the damage was done.
She started eating excessively after those outbursts, consuming whole trays of kugel and challah in
her office, alone, telling herself it was comfort food. The weight gain only deepened her sense of
being a stranger in her own skin. Her body, which had always been reliable, now seemed to betray her
at every turn: the racing pulse, the sweaty palms, the startle reflex that made her drop her siddur
when someone came up behind her.

She began to take risks that alarmed even her. Two months after the accident, she insisted on
driving herself to a hospital visit despite still feeling shaky behind the wheel. She sped through
yellow lights, tailgated the car ahead, and felt a perverse thrill in the danger. It was as if she
were testing fate, daring the world to hurt her again. Her daughter, who lived across the country,
called after hearing about the outbursts and asked if Malia was okay. Malia said she was fine, that
she was focusing on the positive. But she knew she was lying. She had become isolated from her small
community—she cancelled one-on-one meetings, stopped attending board meetings, and began wrapping up
services early. She told herself it was better for everyone if she kept her distance, that her
irritability would only drive people away. In truth, what frightened her most was that her own body
kept reacting as if the fire were still happening, even when she was standing in her quiet, safe
living room, the candles unlit, the house still.

What finally brought Malia to seek help was a Shabbat morning in early autumn. A child in the
congregation had lit the memorial candles for her grandmother, and the small, warm flame sent Malia
into a full panic. She covered her face with both hands, dropped to her knees in the sanctuary, and
wept. A few congregants rushed to her side, and she flinched away from their hands, shouting at them
to leave her alone. After the service, the board president gently suggested she take a leave of
absence. Malia sat in her office afterward, surrounded by the smell of old books and candle wax, and
realized she could not lead a congregation if she could not even sit through a service. She called a
therapist that afternoon. She is afraid—terrified, really—that she will never get back to who she
was, that the fire has burned away the rabbi she spent decades becoming. But she is also desperate
to stop covering her face, to stop waking in the dark with the smell in her nose, to feel like a
person who belongs among her people again.
