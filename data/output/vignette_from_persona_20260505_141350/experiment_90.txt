legs. Their methods alternated between brutality and feigned kindness: one torturer would bring him
water, speak softly, and then tighten the restraints. That disorienting switch—a moment of relief
that turned into more pain—left a deep confusion inside him. When he finally escaped during a
transfer, he fled north, crossed the border, and was granted asylum. He has not returned.

Everyday life is a minefield. Javier sees dark sedans everywhere—in the hotel parking lot, outside
the grocery store—and each one makes his chest tighten. He cannot watch news reports about his home
country; the images of police or military convoys send him into a cold sweat. Most puzzling to him
is the way positive emotions trigger a sinking dread. If he feels a moment of genuine happiness—a
guest complimenting his work, a song he used to love—his mind instantly scans for the coming
betrayal. He has come to believe that feeling good is a trap, a lull before pain. He cannot trust
it.

To manage these intrusions, Javier has built a rigid life. He avoids being reminded of his past: if
a coworker asks about his background, he changes the subject or walks away. He is especially wary of
anyone in uniform or in a position of authority. His hotel manager wears a blazer and a badge, and
Javier speaks to him only when absolutely necessary, keeping his eyes down. He has quit two previous
jobs because a supervisor raised his voice once, and that was enough. He smokes a pack and a half a
day now—the only thing that steadies his hands when the memories surge. He knows it hurts his lungs,
but the alternative—the panic—is worse.

strangers: anyone who approaches him on the street, even a lost tourist asking for directions, makes
his pulse race and his gaze dart for an exit.

not let himself laugh at a movie, why every unexpected hug felt like an attack. Eventually she left.
Javier knows he drove her away, but he could not explain it then; he could not explain it now.

feel anything but fear.
