Kwame is a 30-year-old curriculum designer who came to therapy after a year of growing distress that
had begun to interfere with his work. The traumatic event occurred two years ago, during a
relationship with a man named Marcus, who had become increasingly controlling and volatile. One
night, after Marcus had been drinking heavily, an argument escalated. Kwame remembers the sharp,
sour smell of alcohol on Marcus’s breath as he was pinned against the wall, the searing pain in his
ribs as he was struck repeatedly, and the terrifying sensation of hands around his throat. The
apartment had a faint burnt smell from a candle that had been knocked over earlier, and that
odour—mingled with alcohol—became seared into his memory. He managed to escape and ended the
relationship the next day, but he never reported the abuse. He told himself it was a one-time event,
and he tried to move on.

In the months that followed, Kwame found that he could not simply leave the experience behind. When
he least expected it, the memory would crash over him with a force that left him breathless. He
would feel the exact same physical pain in his ribs and throat, as though the blows were landing
fresh, and the terror would return—the same helpless, frozen panic he had felt in that moment.
Sometimes, without any apparent trigger, he would catch a smell that seemed to come from nowhere:
alcohol, or that specific burnt odour from the apartment, and he would be back there, his heart
pounding, his skin crawling.

The smells soon began to appear in his daily life as real, external triggers. Walking past a bar,
the whiff of beer on someone’s breath would send him into a state of hypervigilance. At work—an
open-plan office for an educational publishing company—a colleague once burned popcorn in the
breakroom microwave, and Kwame had to excuse himself to the bathroom, shaking, for nearly twenty
minutes. He began to avoid the breakroom altogether, eating lunch at his desk. He also started to
dread conversations that touched on family. When a coworker asked, “So, are you close with your
parents?” or someone joked about family gatherings, Kwame would feel a surge of anger and shame that
he could not explain. He had come to believe that he was somehow dirty, contaminated by what had
happened. The injustice of it—that Marcus had walked away without consequence, that no one knew,
that nothing had been done—gnawed at him daily. He felt that the person he had become was
unrecognisable: the confident, easygoing curriculum designer who once led team workshops had turned
into a man who flinched at comments and avoided lunch with colleagues.

The avoidance had consequences. He stopped dating entirely, certain that any romantic partner would
see the stain of his past. He turned down invitations to after-work drinks, and his supervisor noted
that he seemed withdrawn and less collaborative. Kwame had always taken pride in designing
inclusive, engaging learning materials, but now his creativity felt blocked. He worried that his own
emotional state was affecting his work, and that he could not bring himself to write scenarios that
required empathy for characters in conflict.

What finally brought him to therapy was a moment at his desk when a colleague made an offhand
comment about “family drama” and Kwame felt a flash of such intense physical pain in his ribs that
he gasped aloud. He realised he could no longer manage on his own. He is afraid that therapy will
force him to relive the abuse, and he is ashamed that he never reported it. But he is also desperate
to feel like himself again—to be able to sit through a team meeting without scanning for alcohol
smells, and to believe that he is not defined by what Marcus did to him. He hopes, above all, to
stop feeling dirty.
