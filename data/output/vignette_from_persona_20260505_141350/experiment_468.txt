enlisted in the Army right out of high school, completed a fast-track medical program, and deployed
as a battalion surgeon to a forward operating base in a conflict zone. Six months into his tour, the
base came under a coordinated mortar and small-arms attack. He was in the aid station when the first
rounds hit. The dust, the screaming, the sudden pressure in his chest as he ran to help—he
remembered the feeling of not being able to get enough air, his own breath ragged and useless. Then
came a stretch of silence after the explosions stopped, an eerie, ringing quiet that felt more
dangerous than the noise. He saw a fellow soldier, a young sergeant he had treated for a minor
infection the day before, lying crumpled against a wall, his body torn open. Kwame had tried to save
him, but the image of that violence—the raw, wet color of it—stayed burned behind his eyes.

Now, back in the United States, working as a resident in a busy urban emergency department, Kwame
found himself struggling in ways he had not expected. He had always been calm under pressure, but
lately, when a patient arrived short of breath—an asthmatic child, a COPD exacerbation—his own
throat would tighten. He would feel a familiar breathlessness, as if the air had turned thick, and
he had to force himself to focus. The worst moments came during the lulls. Between traumas, the
department would fall into a sudden quiet—a pause after a code, the silence of a hallway at three in
the morning—and his heart would hammer. He had come to believe that silence meant something terrible
had just happened or was about to. He avoided sitting alone in the doctor’s lounge. He kept the
television on in his apartment at all times, even when he was trying to sleep.

What frightened Kwame most was that he did not recognize the person he had become. He used to be
someone who trusted his instincts, who believed that if he worked hard and stayed prepared, he could
handle anything. Now he was certain that catastrophe was always waiting. He scanned every waiting
room, every corridor, every patient’s face for signs of violence. When he sutured a laceration or
placed a chest tube, the memory of the sergeant would flicker—just a flash, a fragmented piece—and
then he would shove it away so fast that he felt dizzy. There were parts of the attack he simply
could not retrieve: the moment between the first mortar and the silence, the face of the soldier who
had yelled his name. He knew the gaps were there, but every time he tried to reach them, his mind
slammed shut.

His avoidance had become a quiet habit. He volunteered for the overnight shifts because the
emergency department was busier then, less chance of dead air. He stopped going to the hospital gym
because the exertion left him panting, and that feeling of breathlessness was too close to the panic
he dreaded. His colleagues had noticed that he seemed distant, that he flinched when someone
approached from behind. He had no energy for the social life he once enjoyed; he told himself it was
exhaustion, but underneath he knew he was terrified of being caught off guard.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was an incident in the trauma bay. A young man was brought
in after a gang-related shooting, multiple gunshot wounds. Kwame worked the code for nearly an hour.

monitors stopped. In that vacuum of sound, Kwame felt his chest seize, his vision tunnel, and he had
to excuse himself to the bathroom, where he sat on the floor, gasping, certain he was about to die.
He realized then that he could no longer do his job without falling apart. The next morning, he
called the hospital’s employee assistance program. He did not know if he could ever feel safe again,
but he was desperate to stop the cycle—to be able to draw a full breath, to sit in a quiet room, and
not be thrown back into that night. He hoped, more than anything, that there was a way to find
himself again.
