Miguel is a twenty-two-year-old HVAC technician from a close-knit Latin American family, and for the
past fourteen months he has been living in a state he does not recognise as his own. The accident
happened on a Tuesday afternoon in late July, when he was on a rooftop performing a routine
compressor replacement for a commercial building. His coworker, an older man named Raúl, was on the
ground handing up tools. Miguel had the new compressor balanced on the dolly when his grip
slipped—his gloves were slick with condensation, or maybe his hand just cramped, he would replay
that microsecond a thousand times—and the two-hundred-pound unit tipped over the edge. He heard Raúl
shout, then a wet, heavy sound, then a silence that lasted less than a second before the screaming
started. The air filled with the sharp, sweet-chemical smell of refrigerant mixed with something
metallic and coppery. Raúl survived, but his pelvis was shattered and he spent six weeks in the ICU.
Miguel visited him once in the hospital. He could not look at Raúl’s wife. He has not visited again.

In the weeks afterward, Miguel could not stop going over the details. He sat on his couch for hours,
replaying the identical sequence: the dolly, the tilt, the weight, his hand. He searched for the
exact moment he could have done something different—tied a safety rope, waited for help, tightened
his grip. Every version ended the same way, but he kept searching. He began to feel that the memory
itself was not his own; it was like watching a video of someone else making a mistake, except that
someone was also him. Sometimes, without warning, the smell would come back. He would be standing in
a client’s basement or driving his van, and suddenly the air would go chemical and coppery, and his
heart would slam against his ribs, his palms would soak, and he would have to pull over and wait for
it to pass. His body had become a traitor. He could no longer trust his hands, his reflexes, his
judgment. He was certain that at any moment he could do something catastrophic again.

Silence became a poison. In the quiet between HVAC cycles, in the stillness of his apartment at
night, he would freeze, listening for the sound that meant something terrible was about to happen.
He learned to keep noise on—television, music, a fan—but even then his muscles stayed tight. He
started refusing service calls that felt stressful: anything with a tight deadline, a difficult
customer, an unfamiliar rooftop. Then he started calling in sick on days that reminded him of the
accident—the anniversary itself, the day Raúl was discharged, the day he first went back to work
after the accident. His supervisor noticed. Miguel was given a warning. He quit before they could
fire him, telling himself he needed a break. That was eight months ago. Now he lives off savings and
the occasional under-the-table repair for neighbors, but only in the apartment complex where he
rents, because that is the only place he feels even close to safe. He does not drive his van
anymore.

The person he used to be—a reliable young man who helped his mother with the bills, who played
soccer on weekends, who laughed easily with his cousins—feels like a stranger. Miguel looks in the
mirror and sees someone hollow-eyed and jumpy, someone who cannot forgive himself for not doing
more. In his worst moments he replays the scene and imagines himself reaching out and catching the
compressor, or shouting a warning a second earlier, and the guilt is a physical weight on his chest.
He has begun to believe that he does not deserve to feel safe, that the constant vigilance is a kind
of punishment he has earned.

Two weeks ago, his mother found him sitting in the dark at four in the morning, staring at the wall,
a fan blowing directly on his face. She made an appointment at the community mental health clinic
and told him she would drive him there herself. Miguel agreed because he could not find a reason to
say no. He is afraid of what the therapist will think of him, afraid that telling the story out loud
will make it more real, but he is more afraid of another night like that one, waiting for a silence
that is never really silent.
