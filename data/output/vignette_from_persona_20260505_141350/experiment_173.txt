other stretches were simply gone, as if her mind had refused to record them.

In the weeks that followed, Meera tried to resume her life. She returned to her job at a suburban
department store stacking shelves and helping customers, but the world had become a landscape of
hidden threats. The smell of smoke from a customer’s cigarette outside the entrance would stop her
cold, her throat tightening as if she were back in that room. When she saw people misbehaving—a man
shouting at a cashier, a teenager cutting in line—a wave of revulsion and fear would wash over her,

the back. Her supervisor noticed and warned her about “customer service standards,” but Meera could
not explain why her heart pounded so violently when a man stood too close.

Her avoidance seeped into every corner of her life. She stopped attending the temple gatherings she
had once enjoyed, because there were men there she knew only slightly and she could not guarantee
she would not be left alone with one. She no longer answered her door after dark. She had come to
believe that she could not cope with even the mildest upset—a broken appliance, a rude comment on

the fire. What frightened her most was the growing certainty that nothing in her life held meaning
anymore. The rituals of her job, the conversations with her adult daughter, even the memory of her
late husband had grown flat and colorless. And beneath all of this was a consuming sense of
injustice: what had happened to her was deeply unfair, and when she had finally gathered the courage
to report it, the police had said there was insufficient evidence. The neighbor denied everything.
No one was held accountable. The unfairness burned inside her, but she had no words for it.

It was her daughter who finally insisted she come to the clinic. Meera had been waking up drenched
