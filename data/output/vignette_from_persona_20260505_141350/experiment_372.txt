George was sixty-seven years old and had managed the same downtown hotel for nearly two decades. The
mugging happened on a Tuesday night in late autumn, after he had locked the front doors and walked
to his car in the employee lot behind the building. Two men came out of the alley between the dry
cleaner and the bar next door. One of them smelled sharply of alcohol. The other carried a tire
iron. They took his wallet, his watch, and the cash from the night deposit bag he had forgotten to
leave in the safe. They also took something George could not name afterward, a kind of certainty he
had carried all his life that other people were essentially decent. He did not fight. He handed over
everything and stood still, his hands raised, while the man with the tire iron laughed and said
something about old men who should know better. Then they were gone.

For the first few weeks after the police report and the insurance forms, George told himself he was
fine. He went back to work the next morning. But he began noticing things he had never noticed
before. A guest lingering in the lobby longer than necessary, glancing in his direction — that was
suspicious. A housekeeper who seemed to be watching him from the end of the hall — she was up to
something. He found himself scanning the faces of everyone who entered the hotel, reading threat
into any man who walked with his hands in his pockets or any woman who looked away too quickly. The
smell of alcohol, even a faint whiff from a guest’s breath at the front desk, made his chest
tighten. He would step back, excuse himself, and retreat to his office. Loud noises in the building
— a dropped tray in the kitchen, a door slamming in the corridor — made him flinch so sharply that
his staff started noticing. Once, when a busboy touched his shoulder to get his attention, George
spun around with a hand raised, his heart pounding, and he saw the young man’s startled face and
knew he had frightened him. He apologized, but the humiliation was already there, a hot flush that
spread from his collar to his scalp. That humiliation stayed with him. He had stood there, passive,
while two men took everything, and now he could not even be touched without reacting like a
frightened animal.

Months passed. George began to feel that the world itself had changed, or that he had only now seen
it for what it was. People could not be trusted. Every friendly smile might be a prelude to a
threat. The hotel, once his domain, became a place of constant vigilance. He stopped walking through
the lobby without first checking the exits. He started arriving an hour early so he could inspect
the parking lot alone. At home, he could not relax. He sat in his armchair facing the front door,
listening for footsteps on the porch. He checked the locks three times before bed. His wife, Ellen,
tried to reassure him, but he found himself irritated by her casual ease. She did not understand. He
was certain that anyone could be a danger, that the safety they had taken for granted had always
been an illusion.

His avoidance seeped into everything. He delegated the evening cash deposits to a junior manager,
even though that had always been his responsibility. He stopped attending the weekly managers’
meeting at the corporate office, claiming he was too busy. The consequences appeared slowly: a terse
email from regional about the meeting absences, a quiet conversation with Ellen about why he no
longer wanted to visit their daughter in the city. Each time, George felt a familiar shame
tightening around his throat. He was retreating from his life, and he knew it, but the thought of
going back to the parking lot after dark, or walking through a crowd, or trusting a stranger’s
intentions — that felt impossible.

What finally brought him to seek help was a morning in late spring. He was in the hotel lobby,
talking to a guest about a reservation, when a delivery driver entered through the side door
carrying a large box. The sound of the door slamming, the sudden figure in his peripheral vision,
the faint smell of beer on the driver’s breath — it collapsed the distance between that Tuesday
night and the present moment. George froze mid-sentence, his mouth dry, his hands shaking. The guest
asked if he was all right. George could not answer. He walked to his office and sat in the dark for
twenty minutes, and when he came home that evening, he told Ellen he needed help. He is most afraid
that this is now who he is — a frightened, untrusting man who sees danger in every corner. But there
is also something else, a quieter hope he has not said aloud: that maybe, somewhere beneath the
hypervigilance and the shame, the person he used to be is still there, waiting to be reached.
