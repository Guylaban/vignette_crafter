Sione was fifty-one, a welder, his hands scarred from years of sparks and steel, and he had not
driven a car in eight months. The crash had happened on a Tuesday evening, just after he’d finished
a long shift at the fabrication shop. He was heading home, taking the familiar route past the old
cannery, when a sedan ran a red light and slammed into the driver’s side of his pickup. The impact
spun the truck into a utility pole. Sione remembered the screech of metal, the shatter of glass, and
then the smell—sharp, sour, unmistakable—as the other driver staggered to his window, reeking of
alcohol, asking if he was okay. Sione was not okay. His ribs were cracked, his left femur fractured,
and he was pinned against the steering wheel, unable to move. For what felt like an eternity he hung
there, half-conscious, the world tilting, the smell of beer and whiskey filling the cab.

The physical injuries healed slowly over months of physical therapy, but the memory of that evening
did not. When the memory came—and it came without warning—Sione’s body would go rigid. He would feel
his shoulders pressed back against an invisible seat, his legs locked in that same trapped angle,
and the sharp, grinding pain would return to his ribs and thigh, even though the bones had long
since mended. It was not just remembering; it was reliving—the shriek of metal, the flash of
headlights, the sour stench of alcohol, all at once, so vivid that for a few terrible seconds he was
back in the truck, waiting to die.

At work, the sounds of the shop became a minefield. A grinder hitting a steel beam, the clang of a
dropped tool, the hiss of an air hose—each could send that full sensory wave crashing over him. He
had learned to flinch, to freeze, to brace himself against the welding bench until the flashback
passed. The other welders noticed; they started calling him jumpy, told him to relax. He could not
explain. What frightened Sione most was that the memory seemed to own his body. He did not choose
when it came, and while it lasted he was helpless.

He began to avoid anything that might trigger it. He stopped driving altogether—his wife, Leilani,
drove him to and from work now, a forty-minute detour because she had to take back roads to avoid
the intersection where it happened. He stopped going to the local bar where his old crew gathered
after shifts; the first whiff of beer from someone’s glass had sent him into a full reenactment, his
hands gripping the edge of the table, his breath short, his ribs aching. He told himself it was
safer to stay home. But home was not safe either. A car accident on the evening news, the screech of
brakes from the street outside, even the sound of Leilani opening a bottle of wine—each could
trigger the same cascade of smells, sounds, and physical pain.

The consequences grew slowly. His supervisor pulled him aside and warned that if he kept freezing
mid-task, he would be moved to inventory, a job with no welding, no future. His marriage grew quiet.
Leilani would ask why he flinched when she hugged him from the driver’s side, why he couldn’t ride
in her car without gripping the door handle. Sione could not put words to it. He only knew that the
memory was still happening, as real as the day it occurred, and that he measured his life by the
hours between flashbacks.

He finally agreed to seek help after Leilani said she could not keep driving him forever, that she
was exhausted. In the therapist’s office, Sione sat stiffly, his hands on his knees, afraid to let
his body relax. He was hoping, without quite believing, that someone could teach him to sit in a car
without feeling the impact all over again. He was afraid that if he gave up the hyper-vigilance, the
next time the memory came he would drown in it. But the weight of the avoidance had become heavier
than the fear.
