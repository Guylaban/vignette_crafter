Minh is a 69-year-old widowed man of Vietnamese descent who spent most of his working life as a
nanny, caring for other people’s children. The trauma that reshaped his world occurred four years
ago, when he was watching three-year-old Leo, a boy he had looked after since infancy. One
afternoon, Leo became suddenly unresponsive, his skin hot and mottled. Minh called 911, but the
minutes before the paramedics arrived stretched into an eternity he could never later reconstruct.
The boy was diagnosed with a severe bacterial infection that led to septic shock. Leo survived after
weeks in the pediatric intensive care unit, but Minh never recovered.

Now, when he tries to explain what happened, the story comes out in fragments. He cannot tell it
clearly, even to himself. The memory does not unfold like an ordinary recollection. Instead, it
arrives as a full sensory assault — the smell of Leo’s sweat, the odd angle of his small arm, the
sound of his own voice on the phone with the dispatcher — and it feels more real than anything
around him. Minh will be washing dishes, and suddenly he is back in that living room, the boy limp
in his arms, the sirens still blocks away. The memory has no past tense. It is happening now, and he
cannot stop it.

To cope, Minh learned to snap his mind away from these intrusions. He keeps himself occupied from
the moment he wakes until he collapses into bed. He watches television shows he does not follow,
folds laundry that is already neat, replays old songs in his head. He avoids any thought that might
lead back to that afternoon. But the effort exhausts him, and the memories still break through. He
also avoids medical care entirely. He has not seen a doctor in over three years, even for routine
checkups. The sight of a clinic waiting room, the smell of antiseptic, the sound of a child crying —
any of it can trigger a flashback so vivid that he feels he might pass out.

The consequences have been severe. Minh stopped working as a nanny shortly after the incident. He
could not bear to be around any child who might become ill; every cough or fever felt like the
beginning of another collapse. His employer understood, but without his income, he struggled to
maintain his small apartment. He became withdrawn. He had always trusted people easily — it was part
of why he was good with children — but now he finds himself scanning faces for hidden dangers.
People are not what they seem, he believes. They carry illness, or they hide their true intentions.
Even his own daughter, who calls every week, feels distant to him. He cannot explain to her what
happened because he cannot explain it to himself.

What frightens Minh most is the person he has become. He used to be warm, patient, quick to laugh.
Now he is rigid and watchful. He feels permanently damaged, as if the event carved something
essential out of him and left a stranger in its place. That stranger does not recognize his own
reflection. He finally agreed to seek help because his daughter pleaded with him, and because he is
terrified of spending the rest of his life in this state. He does not know if therapy can undo what
was done. But he is desperate enough to try, even if it means walking into a clinic where the air
smells faintly of disinfectant and a child’s cough echoes down the hall.
