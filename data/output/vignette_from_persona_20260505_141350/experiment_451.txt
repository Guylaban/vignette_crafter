someone who had trained himself never to stop moving. He had come to believe that stillness was
dangerous—that the moment his hands were quiet and no one was speaking to him, the memory would
surface in pieces he could not control. The accident had happened eighteen months ago, on a damp
February night, while he was driving to a club in East LA. A pickup truck ran a red light and
T-boned his sedan on the driver’s side. Mateo remembered the sound of metal crumpling, the
windshield cracking, the paramedic’s voice asking him to squeeze a hand. But when he tried to recall
the sequence in order, to tell the story start to finish, the memory would not cooperate. He could
bring up only fragments—the taste of blood, the sensation of being pinned, the paramedic’s face—and
they never seemed to fit together. It was as if the event had been broken into pieces and scattered,
and he could not find all of them. This had troubled him deeply, because he felt he ought to be able
to remember what had happened, ought to be able to make sense of it. Instead, he lived with the
vague certainty that something important was missing.

In the months after the accident, Mateo had developed a relentless schedule. He lined up every night
with a band or a session, then stayed out afterward with whoever would stay with him—bandmates, old
friends, even strangers at the bar. He had become terrified of his own internal world. When he was
alone, the fragments would push in without warning, and he would feel a wave of heat rising in his
chest, a tightness in his jaw that frightened him. What scared him most was not the anger itself but
the possibility that he would lose control of it—that he would say something brutal, or worse, act
on it. He had always been a steady, easygoing man; now he felt like a live wire. The injustice of
the accident had calcified inside him. The other driver had been arrested but released on bail, and
the court date kept getting postponed. Nothing had been done. No one had apologized. The world had
moved on as if what happened to Mateo meant nothing, and that sense of unfairness gnawed at him
every day.

He avoided talking about the event with anyone, even his wife, Carla. When she asked how he was
doing, he said “fine” and changed the subject. He did not want to think about it either—did not want
to risk the memory surfacing, or the rage that might follow. He could not practice guitar alone at
home anymore because the silence of the practice room invited the fragments. So he practiced with
other musicians, always in company. He booked more gigs than he could comfortably handle, often
playing four or five nights a week, sometimes double-shifts. The constant socialising kept the
thoughts at bay, but it had cost him. Carla had started sleeping in the guest room; she told him she
felt like a stranger in her own home, that he was never really there even when he was physically
present. He had missed her birthday because he was at a jam session with a group he barely knew, and
he had not even felt guilty about it at the time—he had only felt relief at not being alone.

Mateo’s life as a musician had once been his joy. Now it had become his shield. He took every
opportunity to perform because performing meant being surrounded. But the music itself had lost its
colour; he played mechanically, his mind elsewhere, watching for the gap between songs when the room
would go quiet and he might hear his own thoughts. The accident had stolen something he could not

inside him. He no longer trusted himself to be alone. He no longer trusted himself not to explode.
He came to therapy only because Carla had given him an ultimatum: get help, or she would leave. He
sat in the chair with his hands clenched, ready to deflect, but a part of him was desperately hoping
that someone might show him how to put the pieces back together.
