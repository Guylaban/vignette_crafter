For eighteen months, the accident had lived inside him without words. Mark could not have described
what he saw that afternoon even if he had wanted to, and he did not want to. He was a carpenter,
thirty years old, married, steady—or he had been. The job site was a three-story frame-up on the
edge of town, and he was on the ground floor running trim when the foreman yelled. Mark looked up
just as a co-worker named Derek lost his footing on a ladder propped against an open second-floor
joist. Derek fell sideways, his head catching the edge of a concrete footer on the way down. The
sound was wet and wrong. Mark dropped his nail gun and ran over. He was the first one there. Derek
was on his back, blood pooling under his skull, his eyes open but not tracking. Mark knelt and
reached out without thinking, his hand finding Derek’s shoulder, then Derek’s fingers curling around
his wrist with a grip that felt like desperation. The metallic taste of his own blood flooded Mark’s
mouth—he had bitten his tongue hard when the body hit the ground, and he had not even noticed until
that moment. He stayed there, frozen, until the paramedics pulled him away.

In the weeks that followed, Mark found he could not talk about it. When his wife, Carrie, asked what
happened, he said “I saw something bad” and then stopped, because the words were not there. What was
there instead was the sensation of Derek’s hand on his wrist, a phantom pressure he could not shake.
He started wearing long sleeves even in summer, and he flinched when Carrie touched his arm. He
stopped eating lunch in the break trailer because the taste of the sandwich he had been
eating—turkey with mustard—now arrived without warning in his mouth whenever he saw a drop of blood
on a bandage or a scrape on a child’s knee. And the worst part was that he could not control when
the memory came. It would slam into him while he was measuring a piece of lumber: the full sensory
replay—the crack, the weight of Derek’s hand, the copper taste, the red pooling on gray concrete—all
of it alive again, as if time had folded. He would stand frozen, heart pounding, until he could
force his mind to go somewhere else. He learned to do that deliberately. When the wave started, he
would imagine his body emptying out, becoming a shell, nothing inside. He would stare at a point on
the wall and feel himself drift upward, blank and weightless. It worked, mostly, but it left him
exhausted.

Food became his other escape. At night, after Carrie had gone to bed, Mark would sit in the kitchen
and eat whatever was in the pantry—cereal, crackers, frozen pizza—mechanically, until his stomach
ached. Eating dulled the edge of the dread that lived in his chest. He gained nearly thirty pounds
over the year, and Carrie started making comments about his health. He knew she was worried, but he
could not explain that he was trying to kill something inside himself with every bite.

On the job site, Mark had become a different carpenter. He could not trust himself to work on
scaffolding anymore. He would check and recheck the locks, and even then his hands would shake. His
foreman noticed and moved him to ground-level work, which meant fewer hours and less pay. Mark told
himself it was temporary, but deep down he believed he had permanently changed for the worse. He was
certain his life had been destroyed by that single afternoon. He used to think of himself as
capable, someone who could handle anything. Now he felt like a stranger in his own body—someone who
could not rely on himself to stay present, to eat normally, to let his wife touch him. The worst
thought came in the quiet moments: *I am broken, and I will never be fixed.*

What finally brought Mark to the clinic was a Tuesday evening when he came home from a half-day of
work, avoided Carrie’s goodbye kiss, ate an entire bag of chips without tasting them, and then sat
on the couch trying to disappear from his own mind. Carrie sat down across from him and said,
quietly, “You are not here. You haven’t been here in a year. I don’t know who you are anymore.” Mark
looked at her, and for a moment he wanted to tell her everything—the taste, the touch, the way he
had to leave his body to survive the day—but the words would not come. So he just nodded. The next
morning, he called the number on the card she had left on the counter. He did not know if talking
could help. He was afraid it might make things worse, that describing the event would bring it back
fully and he would never return. But he was more afraid of losing his wife, of losing the last
thread that tied him to the man he used to be. So he came, sat in the chair, and waited to see if
there was any way back.
