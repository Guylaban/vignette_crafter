Liam was twenty years old when he watched his older brother Marcus die. It happened in the parking
lot of a gas station just off the interstate—a stupid argument over a parking space that spiralled
into something none of them could have predicted. The other man pulled a knife. Liam saw the blade
flash once, then disappear into Marcus’s chest. What stayed with him, what he could still see in
photographic stillness whenever he closed his eyes, was the way Marcus’s hand had flown up to the
wound, fingers splayed, blood welling between them. That hand. The fingers. The blood on the
knuckles. He could not describe the event in words—every time he tried, the sentences scattered, and
all he had were those frozen images, one after another like slides in a tray. And the smell:
metallic, thick, the coppery scent of blood mixed with hot asphalt. It came back to him at odd
moments—while cooking ground beef, while walking past a construction site, while his wife Sophie was
on her period. He would gag and his heart would hammer and he would be back in that parking lot, the
fluorescent lights buzzing overhead, the sound of his own voice screaming for someone to call 911.

In the months after, Liam stopped watching television altogether. He used to love crime dramas, but
now even a trailer for a hospital show made his palms slick. Sophie would turn on a comedy, and he’d
sit beside her on the couch, but his gaze would drift to the window, scanning the street for anyone
who moved too quickly, too deliberately. A neighbour bending to pick up a newspaper made him flinch.
A jogger rounding the corner made him hold his breath. He could not be alone in the apartment. If
Sophie worked a late shift, he would walk to the all-night diner three blocks away and sit there
nursing a coffee until she came home, because the silence of the empty rooms pressed against his
ears and his mind threw up images of Marcus’s hand, over and over, until he felt he was back in the
parking lot, watching it happen again in real time. He began waking at 3 a.m., drenched in sweat,
the smell of blood so vivid he had to check his own hands. Sleep became a battleground. He lay
beside Sophie, listening to her breathe, terrified of what awaited him when his eyes closed.

He had no job now. Before the incident, he had been a mechanic at a local garage—good with his
hands, proud of his work. But after, he could not bear the sight of grease under his nails. It
reminded him of something, though he could not name it. He quit without explanation. His boss called
twice; Liam did not call back. He had come to believe that his body had betrayed him. It kept
reacting as if the danger were still there—heart racing, muscles taut, hands trembling—even when he
was safe in his own living room. That meant he could not trust himself. He could not rely on his own
judgment, his own reactions. The things that had once defined him—fixing engines, being the
dependable younger brother, showing up for his family—felt like costumes he had shed and could not
put back on. He had become someone who hurt himself. When the overwhelm grew too sharp, when the
flashbacks pinned him in place and the smell filled his nostrils, he would lock himself in the
bathroom and dig his fingernails into his forearm, or press a lit match against his thigh, just to
feel something other than the terror. The physical pain gave him a moment of release, a clean
sensation that cut through the chaos, and that was worth the bruises and burns Sophie would find
later.

His marriage was straining under the weight of it. Sophie was patient, but she was also only twenty,
and she did not understand why a comment about her own mother—a harmless remark about visiting for
Thanksgiving—could send Liam into a cold silence for hours. He could not explain it. The word
“family” landed in his chest like a stone. He avoided conversations about brothers, about holidays,
about anything that touched the shape of what he had lost. He had stopped visiting his parents. His
mother called and he let it go to voicemail.

that he was drowning her, too, and because he could not shake the terror that the next time he
closed his eyes he would not be able to wake up. He was afraid of what he might do, but he was also
afraid of never escaping the parking lot. He sat in the intake office with his hands clasped between
his knees, unable to look the therapist in the eye, and said only, “I don’t know how to make it
stop. I don’t even know how to tell you what happened.”
