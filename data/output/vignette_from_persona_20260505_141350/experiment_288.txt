Michael O’Shea, a 45-year-old military medic, still wears the same desert combat boots he had on
three years ago when the earthquake hit. He isn’t sure why he can’t let them go. They remind him of
something, but he cannot put the memories of that day in the right order—only fragments that rush
back with the same sickening terror he felt then, and when they do, he has to stop whatever he is
doing or he will shake apart.

The earthquake struck during a routine rotation at a forward operating base in a coastal region.
Michael had just finished a morning sick call when the ground lurched. He remembers the sound
first—a deep, grinding roar, like the earth was tearing itself apart. After that, the images come in
a flood, jumbled: a concrete wall buckling, dust so thick he could not see his hands, a young airman
trapped under a support beam, screaming. He worked for hours, prying debris with his bare hands,
trying to keep the man talking until extraction arrived. He remembers the man’s name was Riley, but
the sequence of events before and after is a smear. He remembers Riley going quiet. He remembers a
second aftershock that nearly buried them both. He remembers walking out of the rubble at dusk,
covered in other people’s blood, and feeling nothing at all.

That changed. Within weeks, Michael could not shake the feeling that the world had become a place
where the ground could fall away without warning. He started to startle at any unexpected or loud
sound—a dropped pan, a helicopter overhead, even a door slamming in the barracks. His heart would
hammer, and he would scan the room as if ready to dive for cover. He knew he was safe; the base had
been rebuilt, the drills were over. But his body did not know. He could not relax, not even in his
own quarters at night, because his ears kept straining for the next crack, the next shudder.

His work suffered. As a medic, he had always been steady in a crisis; now he found himself avoiding
the trauma bay when he could, inventing reasons to stay in the supply closet. The loud clatter of
medical instruments being sterilised sent him into a cold sweat. He stopped making eye contact with
patients. The job that used to give his life meaning felt hollow. Nothing seemed meaningful
anymore—not the men he was supposed to treat, not the letters his wife had written him before she
died of cancer two years before the quake. He had once believed in the order of things: that if you
worked hard and kept your head, you could make a difference. That belief had been destroyed. The
earthquake had shown him that chaos could swallow anyone, and he had been helpless. He felt like he
did not know himself anymore. The calm, competent medic was gone. In his place was a man who
flinched at a cough and lay awake staring at the ceiling, listening for aftershocks that would never
come.

The avoidance spread. He stopped going to the mess hall at peak hours because the roar of
conversation sounded too much like the noise after the collapse. He declined invitations to play
cards with his old unit, claiming fatigue. His commanding officer noticed the change and suggested
he take leave, but being home alone was worse—the quiet made him jump at the creak of the house
settling. He had come to believe that the ground, or something, was always about to betray him.

What finally brought him to the clinic was an accidental blast of the base’s outdoor warning siren
during a maintenance test. The sound hit him like a physical blow. He dropped to his knees in the
middle of the parade ground, gasping, convinced the demolition was starting again. Two fellow medics
carried him inside. That night, he called the chaplain and asked for a referral. He is terrified
that he is losing his mind, that he will never be the man his wife once knew, that the memories will
never stop feeling as fresh as the moment they happened. But he is also afraid to hope that talking
about them could change any of that.
