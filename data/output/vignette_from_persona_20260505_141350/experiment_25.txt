Amir, a 29-year-old train conductor, still remembers the precise temperature of that February
afternoon—the way the air bit at his cheeks as he leaned out of the cab, and then, seconds later,
the shocking cold of the young woman’s skin when he knelt beside her on the gravel. She had stepped
onto the tracks just before the express curve, and by the time Amir saw her, the train was already
past the point of braking. The impact threw her clear of the wheels, but the damage was done. When
he reached her, blood was pooling beneath her head, dark and thick against the white stones. Her
left arm was bent at an angle he knew was wrong. He touched her wrist to check for a pulse and felt
only the damp chill of her skin, already cooling in the wind. She was gone before the ambulance
arrived.

In the weeks that followed, Amir tried to tell himself it was over. The investigation cleared him of
any fault. His supervisor said, “You did everything right.” His coworkers clapped his shoulder and
told him to go home and rest. But Amir found that no amount of reassurance could quiet the feeling
that the world was not safe—that at any moment, something terrible could happen again, and this time
it might be his fault because he had not seen it coming soon enough. He began to scan the tracks
obsessively, his eyes darting from rail to rail, checking for shadows or figures. Even when the
train was stopped at a station, he would catch himself listening for a sound that did not belong,
straining for a scream or a thud that never came. The hypervigilance followed him off the job. At
night, lying in his apartment, he kept his bedroom door open so he could hear the street. He
convinced himself that he needed to stay alert, that the moment he let his guard down would be the
moment something went wrong. Sleep came only in shallow, fitful stretches after the streetlights
dimmed and the last car passed.

The triggers were everywhere, and they felt as if they were designed to ambush him. A sudden chill
on his skin—from an air conditioner, or the handle of a cold drink—would pull his mind back to that
gravel bed and the feel of her wrist. Blood was the worst. Once, a passenger on the train cut his
hand on a broken window latch, and Amir saw the red streak across the man’s palm. He had to lock
himself in the crew restroom for ten minutes, hands shaking, until the image of that earlier blood
faded. Visible injuries on others—a child’s scraped knee, a commuter’s bandaged arm—made his stomach
clench and his chest tighten. He started avoiding eye contact with passengers, especially during the
evening rush when people were tired and more likely to have minor wounds or patches on their skin.
His boss noticed that Amir had become jumpy, that he double-checked doors and brakes long after they
were secure. “You’re fine,” the boss said. “The system is safe.” But Amir did not believe it.
Safety, for him, had become a word that meant nothing unless he could see, hear, and feel every
possible danger for himself.

The social cost crept up slowly. He stopped going out with friends, because at a bar the sight of a
napkin stained red from someone’s salsa would spike his heart rate. He avoided his sister’s house
because her toddler had a habit of scraping his knees and showing off the scabs. He told himself it
was easier to stay home, where he could control the temperature, the lighting, the level of threat.
But home wasn’t safe either; it was just less unpredictable. He spent most evenings pacing his
apartment, checking the locks twice, then lying on his bed with his eyes open, listening.

Amir finally decided to seek help after an incident three weeks ago. During a routine inspection, he
found a small puddle of coolant under one of the train cars—it had a film of red hydraulic fluid,
and the sight of that oily red on the concrete sent him into a full panic attack. He could not
breathe, could not think, could not move until another conductor pulled him away. That night, he
realized he could not go on like this. He made an appointment with a therapist who specialized in
trauma. Sitting in the waiting room, he held his hands tightly in his lap and thought about what he
wanted most: to feel, even for a minute, as if the world were not a place where danger was hiding
around every corner. But he was also afraid—afraid that if he let himself believe he was safe, he
would stop being watchful enough to keep everyone else alive.
