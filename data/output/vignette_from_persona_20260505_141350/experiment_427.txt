Luke is a 28-year-old social media manager who lives with his girlfriend in a mid-sized city. He
spent eighteen months in Afghanistan as part of a convoy security unit. What happened one afternoon
near a dried riverbed is something he can recount only in pieces—there was an explosion, a vehicle
ahead of him lifted and fell, and then he saw the driver’s leg. The memory does not come as a
continuous story. Instead, he feels a sudden wash of heat and nausea, his heart slamming, without
any clear picture attached. Other times a fragment surfaces: the sound of the rotor blades from the
medevac helicopter, the way the dust swirled, the sight of a boot that was still laced. He knows the
event happened—he was there—but the whole thing can feel so extreme that he sometimes has to tell
himself it really did occur.

When Luke returned home, he threw himself back into his civilian job, managing brand accounts and
scheduling posts for a regional outdoor gear company. The work itself is safe, but the visual
environment is not. He scrolls through user-generated content and sometimes comes across a photo of
a hiker with a bloody gash on their leg from a fall. The image hooks him: his throat tightens, his
palms sweat, and he has to look away fast. He has learned to flag such content without really
looking. His girlfriend, Jen, had asked him to drive them to a weekend getaway a few months ago, and
he felt his chest seize. Being in the car with her, the passenger seat that should be empty, the
road ahead—it triggered a physical reactivity he could not talk through. He muttered that he was
tired and asked her to drive instead. Now she always drives when they go out together, and he knows
she wonders why, but he cannot explain it without sounding broken.

Avoidance has become a kind of invisible architecture in his life. He never walks past a military
recruiting office or a police car if he can help it. If one passes him on the street, he turns away
or crosses a block early. He has stopped going to the gym entirely because any sensation of his body
under strain—muscles burning, breathing hard—makes him feel as if he is back in that moment of
adrenalized survival. Before any conversation with a coworker or even with Jen, he runs through what
he might say, over and over, to make sure nothing catches him off guard, because spontaneity feels
dangerous. At night, he lies in bed with his back to the door, his ears tuned for the sound of a
helicopter overhead—rare, but when it happens, he is instantly, rigidly awake. Jen has accidentally
touched his shoulder from behind twice in the past month, and both times he flinched violently,
nearly knocking over a glass. She says she feels like she has to give him a warning before she comes
near him.

What frightens Luke most is the conviction that he has permanently changed for the worse. He looks
at his coworkers and his girlfriend, people who have not been through what he has, and he believes
he is now a different species. Somebody else, he tells himself, would not have let the situation
happen in the first place—would have seen the threat, made a different call, not been where that IED
was waiting. He has come to feel like an outsider in every ordinary setting, watching from the other
side of a glass wall. His relationship with Jen has grown quieter; he knows she senses the distance,
but he cannot bridge it.

He finally sought help after a week when he rehearsed every single conversation at work and still
felt flattened by a nausea that came without warning. The helicopter sound had passed overhead three
nights in a row, and he had not slept. More than anything, he wants to feel as if he belongs in his
own life again, but he is terrified that the damage is already done.
