Zuri sat across from me with her hands folded tightly in her lap, the knuckles pale against her dark
skin. She had come because her husband Marcus had told her, gently but firmly, that he couldn’t
watch her disappear into the small hours of the morning night after night, and that the credit card
statements were becoming something they could no longer ignore. She knew he was right. But what
frightened her most was the thought that if she stopped replaying every moment of that morning, she
would be forgetting Elijah.

Two years ago, on a clear Tuesday in October, Zuri had left for work before dawn. She was a
commercial fisherman on a trawler out of a small Gulf Coast harbor, and the season demanded early
departures. Elijah had been six, with a smile that made her forget the cold and the wet and the long
hauls. Marcus had agreed to drive him to school because Zuri’s shift started at five. She remembered
crouching by Elijah’s bed, pressing a kiss to his forehead, whispering that she would see him after
dinner. She remembered the way his small hand had patted her cheek. Then she had gone.

A drunk driver ran a red light at the intersection of Bayou Drive and Main. Marcus walked away with
a broken collarbone. Elijah did not survive the ambulance ride.

In the months that followed, Zuri became certain that the accident had happened because of the way
she acted. She should have taken the day off. She should have driven him herself. She should have
known, somehow, that letting Marcus take the wheel that morning was a failure of protection that
could never be undone. Her mind had turned into a trap: every night, when she finally climbed into
bed only to lie rigid and staring at the ceiling, she would go through the sequence again—the alarm,
the kiss, the door closing—and look for the moment where she could have changed it. If she had just
called in sick. If she had made Marcus wait. If she had held Elijah back for one more minute. The
rumination had no end; it only shifted shapes.

Sleep had become something she delayed deliberately. She would stay up until two or three in the
morning, scrolling aimlessly on her phone, then clicking through online stores. The purchases came
in waves: a new jacket she didn’t need, kitchen gadgets still in their boxes, a set of fishing
knives she already owned. Each purchase gave a few minutes of quiet in her chest, a small hit of
control before the guilt surged back and she realized she had spent another hundred dollars she
didn’t have.

At work, the triggers were everywhere. A crewmate talking about a fender bender on the highway would
make Zuri’s stomach drop and her hands go cold. She would walk to the bow and grip the railing until
the physical reaction passed—the pounding heart, the shortness of breath, the hot shame that
followed. Once, rounding the corner near the docks, she saw a car with its front end crumpled from a
minor collision, and she had to sit down on the curb, her vision swimming, the image of Elijah’s
small body superimposed over the twisted metal.

The anniversary was the worst. As October approached each year, Zuri would feel a mounting dread
that made her irritable and short-tempered. She snapped at Marcus for leaving a dish in the sink,
then felt a wave of self-disgust so strong she had to leave the house. She would drive aimlessly,
then find herself parked outside the cemetery, unable to go in.

She had come to believe that forgiving herself would mean saying it was okay that Elijah died. That
was something she could not do. And so she held onto the guilt like a duty, replaying the morning
over and over, staying awake to keep the nightmares at bay, spending money to fill the silence, and
lashing out because the anger had to go somewhere. When she finally sat in the chair across from me,
what she said was not “I want to feel better.” She said, very quietly, “I don’t know who I am
without this. But I can’t keep living like this either.”
