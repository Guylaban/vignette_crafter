Carlos, a 70-year-old dentist who lost his wife to cancer three years ago, still runs his small
practice two days a week in a suburban strip mall. The rest of his time he spends alone. The trauma
that brought him to therapy happened five years earlier, on a warm September afternoon when he had
taken his wife to a community festival downtown. He remembers the music, the smell of grilled corn,
the way she leaned on his arm. Then he remembers the first sound — not a firecracker, he knew that
immediately — and the second, and the third. He remembers the crowd surging, people falling, and the
man in the dark vest who kept walking forward with the rifle. Carlos was not hit. He pulled his wife
behind a concrete planter and covered her with his body until the shooting stopped. Nine people
died. Twenty-three were wounded. He has never told anyone the thought that came to him in that
moment, pinned under the weight of his own uselessness: *I should have done something. I should have
run toward him instead of hiding.*

That thought has not left him. For five years, Carlos has revisited the moment of decision — that
split second when he chose to protect his wife rather than intervene — and each time he arrives at
the same verdict. He blames himself for not acting, even though he knows, intellectually, that he
would likely have been killed. The rumination has a specific rhythm: a trigger will surface, and
then the replay begins, and then the self-criticism, and then the surge of heat in his chest that
forces him to stop whatever he is doing. When the memory returns in full, it arrives across all his
senses at once: the pop-pop-pop of the rifle, the smell of the man's sweat as he passed within ten
feet, the weight of his wife's body trembling under him, the bright blue sky that did not change. He
cannot describe the event in words without the sentences breaking apart. In session, he often stops
mid-sentence and shakes his head. "I can't," he says. "It's not— I can't make it make sense."

The triggers that ambush him are everywhere. Any sound like a gunshot — a car backfiring, a door
slamming, a dropped metal tray in his own dental operatory — sends his heart racing and his palms
sweating. He has learned to brace himself before opening a drawer of instruments. Authority figures
in uniform, especially police officers and security guards, make him feel suddenly exposed and
suspicious, though he cannot explain why; his mind goes to the officers who arrived at the festival
afterward, the way they shouted and pushed, the way he felt herded and helpless. Military topics on
television or in conversation produce a tightness in his throat, and he will change the subject or
leave the room. He has stopped watching the news entirely because he cannot tolerate the inevitable
coverage of shootings or bombings, even those on the other side of the world. The news is a door he
keeps closed.

He has come to carry a weapon himself — a small handgun he keeps in his car console or in a shoulder
bag when he is out — because the idea of being unarmed again feels intolerable. He tells himself it
is practical, that it gives him options, but he also knows he checks the bag several times a day to
make sure the gun is still there. When he walks into any building, his eyes go immediately to the
exits. He notes the number of doors, the placement of windows, the thickness of walls. In his dental
office he has rearranged his chair so that he faces the door, and he watches every person who enters
for signs of threat — the way they hold their hands, the direction of their gaze, the shape of a
bulge under a jacket. He is always scanning.

The avoidance has cost him. He stopped attending the community center where he used to play dominoes
with other retired men; too many loud voices and sudden movements. His daughter, who lives two hours
away, calls more often now because his responses to her invitations have become vague and
deflecting. He says he is busy, or tired, or that he needs to stay near home because of the
practice. The practice itself is shrinking; his remaining patients are long-standing ones who
tolerate his occasional flinch when they walk in unannounced. He has not made a new patient
appointment in months.

What brought Carlos to therapy was not a crisis, but an accumulation. He found himself, one Tuesday
morning, sitting in his car in his own driveway for thirty minutes, unable to turn the key in the
ignition because he thought he heard a sound like a firecracker three blocks away. He knew it was
irrational. He also knew he could not make himself move. That evening, he called his daughter and
told her, for the first time, that he thought something was wrong with his mind. She found him a
therapist who specialized in trauma. He came to the first session with his hands folded tightly in
his lap, and said, almost in a whisper, "I need to stop living like this. But I don't know how to be
anywhere else."
