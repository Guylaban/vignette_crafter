Erin was thirty-three years old and had been a paramedic for nearly a decade when it happened. She
responded to a multi‑car pileup on the interstate, a typical winter scene of twisted metal and
shattered glass. The driver in the second car was trapped, conscious, terrified—a man in his forties
who kept saying his neck felt wrong. Erin knew the protocol for suspected cervical spine injury. She
and her partner prepared to extricate him with a backboard and cervical collar. In the chaos of the
scene, with sirens still wailing and another vehicle leaking fuel, Erin moved to stabilize his head
while her partner worked the jaws of life. She was trying to hold his head perfectly still, but as
the crushing metal gave way, his body shifted without warning. Her hand slipped. His neck rotated
sharply, and she heard him cry out. Later, at the hospital, the neurosurgeon told her the movement
had caused a complete spinal cord injury. The man would never walk again. Erin had accidentally
caused that injury.

At first, she tried to keep working. She went to the next shift, answered the next call. But the
memory of that moment kept arriving without warning—a sudden, vivid fragment: the feel of his hair
under her fingers, the angle of his head, his eyes locking onto hers as he screamed. Each time it
came, the same raw terror flooded her body as if she were back in that moment. She remembered it not
as a story but as a series of snapshots, each frozen image seared into her mind. She could not think
about it deliberately without her heart racing and her hands shaking, so she stopped letting herself
think about it at all.

She began drinking after every shift. A glass of wine became two, often three; sometimes she added a
sleeping pill from the old prescription in the bathroom cabinet because the alcohol alone did not
keep the images away. She told herself the drinking helped her relax, that she needed it to
function. When her husband asked about her day, she gave one‑word answers and changed the subject.
If he pressed about the accident, she walked out of the room. She stopped talking to her colleagues
about any call that involved trauma, and when the formal debriefing was scheduled at the station,
she claimed she had a doctor’s appointment. She distracted herself with constant activity—cleaning
the house at midnight, streaming shows she did not really watch, scrolling social media until her
eyes burned.

The hypervigilance set in within weeks. At work, she found herself scanning every intersection
before the ambulance rolled through, watching the faces of approaching drivers as if they might
suddenly swerve into her path. At night she could not rest; she kept the bedroom lights on and
checked the locks on every door twice before she allowed herself to lie down. The worst of it was
the hyperalertness on scene: every patient’s movement seemed like a potential disaster waiting to
happen. Her concentration suffered. She missed a note during a handoff at the hospital, and later
the charge nurse called her supervisor to clarify a dosage. Another time she forgot to check the
defibrillator batteries before a shift. She had never made such errors before. She came to believe
that she had lost something essential—that maybe she had always been reckless and the accident had
simply revealed it.

The consequences crept up on her. Her husband grew quiet and then frustrated; they stopped having
dinner together because she would rather drink in front of the television than sit across from him
and pretend. At work, her supervisor asked if she needed time off, but Erin said no. She was
terrified that if she stopped, the snapshots would stop coming as fragments and start consuming her
entirely. What frightened her most was not the image of the man’s face—it was the certainty that she
would make the same mistake again. She could feel the threat in her own hands. Night became the
hardest; she had come to believe that darkness itself was dangerous, that if she let her guard down
the accident would repeat itself in some new form.

She finally sought help after a near‑miss during a routine transport: she reached for the wrong vial

was thinking of leaving. She sat alone in the living room, lights blazing, and realized she could
not keep living this way. She made an appointment with a psychologist who specialized in trauma. She
was afraid of what she might discover about herself, but she was more afraid of causing another
injury—and of losing the life she still had left.
