Minh was forty-eight when it happened, though he sometimes felt as though the years before and after
had been erased and only that night remained in fragments. He had been with Lina for two years, and
he had learned to read the tilt of her jaw, the way her voice would go flat just before she struck.
But that evening he had come home late from the office—an audit file had taken longer than expected,
and he was tired, so tired that the usual vigilance slipped. She was waiting in the kitchen. The
first blow caught him across the cheekbone, and then she was on him, her fists and knees finding his
ribs, his thighs, his back. He remembered a white light, the smell of cooking oil, the sound of her
breathing. There was a moment when he tried to push her away, and she laughed. Then nothing. A gap.
The next thing he knew, he was on the floor, his shirt torn, and she was gone. He never pressed
charges. He told himself it was a one-time thing, that he had provoked her, that he was a man and
should be able to handle it. But when he looked in the mirror that night he did not see a person; he
saw an object, something that had been used and discarded.

The memory did not stay in that night. It arrived in pieces—a flash of her hand, the cold tile
against his cheek—without warning, often when he was alone and least expecting it. He would be
sitting at his desk at the Revenue Department, reviewing a return, and suddenly the image would push
through, and his heart would hammer, and he would have to excuse himself to the restroom. There were
long stretches when he could not recall the event at all, not because he was avoiding it, but
because the memory simply would not come together; whole minutes were missing, as if someone had cut
out a section of film. He would try to reconstruct the sequence, but all he found were gaps, and the
effort left him hollow.

What frightened him most, though, was the growing sense that danger had not ended. He began to feel
unsafe standing in open rooms; he needed his back against a wall, needed to see every door and every
window. At work, he chose a desk in the corner and always sat facing the entrance. He caught himself
looking behind him as he walked down the hall, and sometimes he spun around for no reason, certain
someone was following. His wife, Thanh, noticed. She asked why he flinched whenever she entered a
room quietly. He said it was nothing, but he stopped coming out of the bedroom until he could hear
her voice first. At home, he felt most secure with his back to the headboard, scanning the doorway.
The false alarms came daily: a car backfiring, a dropped file, a sudden change in a taxpayer’s
tone—each time his body braced as if for impact. His chest would tighten, and he would be drenched
in sweat before he could reason his way back to calm.

The exhaustion made everything worse. Tiredness became a trigger in itself; if he felt fatigue
settling in, he knew the fragments would come more easily, and so he began to avoid rest. He stayed
up late, working through spreadsheets, because sleep felt like surrender. He also avoided any
situation that felt unpredictable—changing his route to work so that he never encountered a
surprise, declining dinner invitations because he could not control the seating arrangement. His
supervisor noticed that Minh had stopped taking on new audits, and Thanh grew quiet, unsure how to
reach him. He had come to believe that nothing good could happen anymore; the world had shown him
what it was, and he was merely moving through it, a shell.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a morning in the office when an intern tapped him on the
shoulder from behind. Minh spun around and shoved the young man against a filing cabinet before he
knew what he was doing. The intern was shaken, and Minh was asked to take leave. That afternoon,
sitting alone in his car, he thought of Lina, of the betrayal, of the feeling that he had been
broken and there was nothing left. He called a number Thanh had left on the fridge months ago. He
did not know if he could be fixed, but he could not keep living like this—back against the wall,
waiting for a blow that never came.
