Amari had always thought of himself as someone who read people well—it was part of why he was good
at UX design, anticipating what users needed before they knew it themselves. That belief shattered
six months ago, during his junior year of an internship at a small tech firm in Chicago. A senior
designer named Derek had taken an interest in Amari’s work, at first offering mentorship, then
gradually inserting himself into Amari’s evenings with texts asking about his day, his schedule, his
apartment building. Amari had shrugged it off, even felt flattered—until the messages turned into
photographs: Derek standing outside the coffee shop where Amari studied, Derek’s car parked across
from the gym. The moment that became the event was the night Amari found Derek waiting in the
hallway outside his apartment door, holding a printed wireframe Amari had designed, saying he’d
“fixed a usability issue.” Amari’s hands still shook when he remembered the way Derek smiled as if
this were normal.

Amari had moved out of that apartment within a week, changed his phone number, and filed a
restraining order. But the sense of safety never returned. In the months since, he had come to
believe that he should have known better—should have seen the signs, should have been less friendly,
should have kept his personal life locked away. The event happened because of the way I acted, he
told himself. I was too open, too trusting. I gave him my number, told him where I lived. That
thought repeated like a broken loop, especially late at night when he checked his door lock three
times before bed.

The world now felt like a place where danger was always hiding just behind the next interaction.
Every new colleague at his current UX design job triggered a checklist of wariness: Where did they
stand? Were they looking at his screen when he walked by? Did they ask too many personal questions?
Amari had become a master of the escape route—choosing desks with clear sightlines to exits, sitting
with his back to the wall in the open-plan office, scanning faces in the lunchroom before committing
to a seat. What frightened him most was the possibility that he might miss a sign again, that his
own judgment had been proven unreliable. So he stayed on guard constantly, telling himself he had to
be especially careful because you never knew what could happen next.

The vigilance bled into his work. He found it hard to concentrate on user flows and interface
sketches because part of his mind was always tracking the people behind him, the notifications on
his phone, the unexpected knock on a meeting-room door. He stopped attending team socials and after-
work events, claiming he was busy, but the truth was that any group gathering felt like a room full
of potential observers, people who might take a strange interest. His productivity slipped, and a
junior designer was asked to assist on a project that would have been Amari’s alone. He told himself
it didn’t matter—better to be safe than to make another mistake.

His days narrowed: home, commute with headphones on and eyes on the train car, work, home. He
stopped dating, stopped messaging old friends. The only person who knew the full story was his
mother, who lived two states away. She urged him to see a therapist. He resisted for months,
insisting he could handle it by being careful. But the constant mental math—calculating escape
routes, checking shadows, replaying conversations for hidden meaning—had worn him down to a thin,
exhausted wire.

What finally brought Amari to a clinic was a moment at work: a senior designer he barely knew walked
past his desk and said, “Hey, you’re usually the first one here, huh?” The words were harmless, but
Amari’s heart slammed into his throat, and he spent the next hour hiding in the bathroom, trembling.
He realized he could not keep living like this—scanning every friendly voice for a predator’s
intent. He wanted to trust again, to sit at his desk without his shoulders up around his ears. But
what he was most afraid of, as he sat in the waiting room, was that the therapist might tell him the
world really was as dangerous as he feared.
