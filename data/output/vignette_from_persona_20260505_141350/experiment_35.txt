Eleanor was eighty years old when she finally walked into a therapist’s office, though the decision
had been forming for months. The door had slammed again—this time at the grocery store, a heavy
metal door closing behind a young man who had held it open for her. The sound had sent her heart
into her throat, and she had stood frozen in the produce aisle, unable to move until the manager
asked if she needed help. She did not need help; she needed the man to step farther away, to stop
being so close, to stop being male.

The event that had shattered her life happened more than fifty years ago, but it lived in her body
as though it were yesterday. She had been with a man she trusted—a romantic partner, her first
serious relationship after leaving her parents’ home. He had come home angry, and she could still
see the door frame splintering as he slammed the kitchen door, the noise splitting the quiet
evening. She remembered his hand, the way it looked raised in the air, but she could not remember
what came before or after. The memory came as snapshots: his knuckles white, the door handle gouging
the wall, the feeling of her back against the counter. She could not order them. She could not make
them into a story. When the snapshots surfaced, she felt an overwhelming grief that left her
breathless, a sorrow not just for herself but for the person she had believed she was.

For decades, Eleanor tried to erase the memory. She would catch herself mentally shoving the images
into a box, slamming a lid down, and then forcing herself to think about her case files, her
clients, anything else. She avoided the hours of night when dreams were most vivid, staying up until
two or three in the morning until exhaustion finally pulled her under, and even then she often lay
awake, watching the shadows on the ceiling. Sleep had become a battleground. When she did drift off,
the snapshots would replay, jumbled and relentless.

Being a social worker had once been her identity, but over the years she found herself shrinking.
She began scheduling home visits only in the mornings, when she could plan an escape route before
entering a client’s building. She scanned every room for exits—not as a professional assessment, but
as a reflex, her eyes darting to doors and windows before she could help it. She stopped taking male
colleagues along on joint visits; being alone in a car with a man, even a trusted coworker, made her
skin crawl with a sense of contamination that she could not explain. She began to feel dirty when
men stood too close, as though something of the old trauma had seeped into her pores and could not
be washed out.

Her belief that people were fundamentally good had been destroyed. She no longer trusted anyone,
least of all men she knew personally. Her nephew—her sister’s son, a gentle man in his fifties—had
once visited, and Eleanor found herself unable to sit in the same room with him. She told her sister
she was tired, but the truth was that being alone with any man, even a relative, felt like a threat
requiring constant vigilance. She found herself staring at the floor, the walls, anywhere but at his
face, and she kept a path to the front door clear at all times.

Why had it happened to her? The question looped through her mind for decades. She had been a good
person, a helper, a believer in trust. She had been raised to believe that the world was safe if you
made good choices. The abuse had erased that belief utterly. She felt contaminated by her own
history, as if the dirt of that night clung to her no matter how many showers she took.

Her life contracted slowly. She retired early from social work because she could no longer tolerate
the unpredictability of male presence in the office. She lived alone in a small apartment with a
deadbolt and a chain. She stopped accepting dinner invitations from her sister if her brother-in-law
would be home. She stopped going to the evening senior center events because doors opened and closed
constantly, and each slam sent a jolt through her chest.

What finally brought her to seek help was not a crisis—it was a quiet recognition that at eighty,
she was running out of time. She was exhausted from the hypervigilance, from the grief that still
welled up when she least expected it, from the endless search for meaning that never ended. She
wanted, more than anything, to be able to sit in a room with a door closed and not feel her heart
race. She wanted to be able to trust that the next door that opened would not bring a raised hand.
She was not sure she could ever fully believe that, but she had come to see that carrying it alone
was no longer possible.
