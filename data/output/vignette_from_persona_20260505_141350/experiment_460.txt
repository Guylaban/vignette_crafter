pitched roofs under the heavy sun. That sense of competence was shattered three years ago, late on a

coworker, a man Kai had trusted, approached him near the tool trailer. The man reeked of
alcohol—cheap whiskey, familiar from countless other after-work beers. Before Kai fully understood
what was happening, the coworker shoved him against the trailer, overpowered him, and sexually
assaulted him while a motorcycle roared past on the street below, its engine popping and stuttering.
Kai froze, disbelieving, until it was over. He told no one. He washed himself off in the client’s
backyard hose, drove home, and sat in the dark garage for an hour, trying to convince himself it
hadn’t happened.

Over the following weeks, Kai’s world began to narrow in ways he couldn’t name at first. The
motorcycle noise that had been an ordinary part of his day—kids joyriding, delivery bikes—now made
his chest lock and his breath go shallow. More than once he stopped his truck at an intersection,
hands white on the wheel, because a bike idled two cars ahead. He also found himself avoiding the
break room at the supply yard whenever he caught the whiff of alcohol on someone’s breath; the smell
of it from a grocery aisle could send him walking out of the store without his cart. And when the
local news ran a segment about a workplace sexual assault case in another state, Kai switched off
the television so fast that his wife Leilani asked if he was ill. He told her he was just tired.

His memories of the assault had an unnerving vividness. Ordinary recollections of other days—lunch
with Leilani, a roofing job he’d finished—felt faded and flat in comparison, while the image of the
trailer’s rusted hitch and the feel of gravel against his cheek rose up with a presence that seemed
more real than his actual present. Sometimes, without any picture at all, Kai would be seized by a
wave of pure emotional intensity—shame, helplessness, fear—that left him trembling in the middle of
a conversation or standing still on a roof, gripping his hammer. Worst of all were the moments when
remembering brought not fear but a crushing, overwhelming grief, a sorrow that felt as if something
irreplaceable had been stolen from him. He couldn’t explain it, even to himself.

To cope, Kai developed a set of habits that, at first, felt like sensible self-protection. He began
to avoid any physical exertion that pushed his body into stress—refusing to carry the heaviest
bundles of shingles, declining overtime that required extra lifting, even pulling out of the weekend
basketball games he had played for years. On the job he made excuses, saying his back was acting up,
and his foreman, though skeptical, let it slide. At home, he started controlling his eating with
strict, almost ritualistic precision—eating the same small meals at the same times, never snacking,
never allowing himself to feel out of control around food. And he filled every empty hour with
busyness: staying late to clean tools, taking on odd projects at home, volunteering to help
relatives move furniture. If he kept moving, he reasoned, the past couldn’t catch up.

The consequences accumulated quietly. Leilani noticed that he no longer sat still with her in the
evenings; he was always pacing, doing something, and when she tried to talk he’d say he was too
busy. Their conversations grew thinner. At work, his avoidance of physical stress made him less
reliable; his crew leader started giving the more demanding jobs to younger roofers, and Kai felt a
slow erosion of his identity as a capable workman. He began to feel like a stranger in his own life,
hollowed out by a secret he could not speak and a grief he could not name.

What finally brought Kai to a clinic was an incident three weeks ago. He was at a traffic light when
a motorcycle pulled up beside him, the rider revving the engine; the sound, the smell of exhaust,
and a random whiff of alcohol from a nearby pedestrian all hit him at once. He had a full panic
reaction—pounding heart, sweating, a sense that he was back at that trailer—and he drove straight
home and sat in the garage again, just as he had on that first night. Leilani found him there. This
time, he didn’t lie. He told her something bad had happened, and she listened without pressing. She

the event as a constant, invisible weight.
