Leila is a 43-year-old pharmacist, married for fifteen years and the mother of a twelve-year-old
daughter. She grew up in a home where her father, a heavy drinker, would fly into rages after coming
home smelling of alcohol. From the age of seven until she left for college, these episodes meant
beatings—belt, fist, whatever was near—delivered with shouted commands to stand still and take it.
Her mother, too afraid to intervene, would retreat to another room. Leila learned that the smell of
alcohol, the sound of an order, the presence of any authority figure meant danger. She learned that
hope was a trap.

Now, decades later, Leila works in a busy retail pharmacy. Every shift, she braces for the moment a
customer leans across the counter with whiskey on his breath—the acrid scent hits her and the
pharmacy dissolves. The fluorescent lights seem to dim, the chatter fades, and she is eight years
old again, frozen in the kitchen doorway, her father’s voice bellowing commands. The memory is not a
distant recollection; it is more real than ordinary things. It fills her senses entirely. Sometimes
the emotion overwhelms her so completely that she has to excuse herself, lock the staff bathroom
door, and press her knuckles into her thighs until the pain pulls her back. Other times,
unexpectedly, she can recall the scene in perfect detail but feels nothing—just a dull deadness, as
if the girl in the memory is a stranger she is watching on a screen. Both forms terrify her.

Leila has learned to navigate her workdays by avoiding her manager whenever possible. Authority
commands trigger the same visceral response: her chest tightens, her mind goes blank. She will take
the long way around the store to bypass his office, fill a quiet corner of the pharmacy when he
walks through. But avoidance has crept into every part of her life. She has stopped attending family
gatherings or dinner with friends because the prospect of feeling happy or hopeful makes her
anxious—joy feels like an invitation to disaster. The worst nights are the ones when the memory
returns with full force. She has begun hurting herself physically to interrupt the loop: a hidden
cut on her upper arm, a burn from a lighter against her ribcage. The pain is a relief, a way to stop
thinking.

At home, her sleep is shallow and vigilant. She lies awake, listening for noises, keeping herself
alert, certain that letting her guard down will mean something terrible happening. During the day,
her concentration is shot. She has misread prescription labels twice—once dispensing a wrong
dose—and caught it only after the patient had left. She reacted to a sudden loud voice in the store
with a full startle, knocking over a bottle of pills. False alarms happen constantly; her body
screams danger when nothing is wrong. She has come to believe that bad things will happen no matter
what she does, that she is helpless to stop them. Nothing feels meaningful anymore. Her life, built
around family and profession, feels hollow. She is certain she has failed in her most important
role—as a mother. When her daughter asks for help with homework or wants to share a happy moment,
Leila pulls away, afraid that any closeness will somehow lead to harm.

What finally brought her to seek help was the near-miss: she caught herself about to hand a child
the wrong medication because her mind had slipped away into a flashback. Her husband found her
crying in the car that evening and saw the fresh marks on her arm. He did not get angry; he asked
her to talk to someone. Now she sits in the clinic, exhausted, ashamed, unsure if anything can
change. She is most afraid that she will damage her daughter the way she was damaged. But beneath
that, something thin and quiet hopes that maybe, just maybe, there is a way to stop feeling like she
is still that terrified child waiting for the next blow.
