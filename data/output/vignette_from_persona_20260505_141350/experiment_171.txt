Minh was sixty-two when it happened, though he still remembers every second with a clarity that
feels like a curse. He had been a roofer for forty years—since he came to this country from Vietnam
as a young man—and he knew the work, knew the heat of tar and the sting of solvents. That morning he
was sealing a flat commercial roof, the kind of job he had done a thousand times. A wind caught a
spray of sealant and drove it straight into his face before he could turn away. The chemical burned
his eyes and throat instantly, and he felt his chest seize as if a hand had closed around his lungs.
He collapsed on the roof, gasping, and his foreman called 911. The next thing Minh knew was a
fluorescent ceiling in the ICU, a tube down his throat, and the sharp, clean smell of antiseptic
that he now cannot stand even for a moment.

In the months that followed, Minh tried to go back to work, but something had shifted. The memory of
that day played like a film he could not stop—the sudden sting, the panic of not being able to
breathe, the sirens. Sometimes the feeling returned without any image at all: a wave of terror would
wash over him while he was just sitting at the dinner table, his heart pounding for no reason he
could name. He began to notice that he could no longer relax, even at home with the door locked and
his wife Lien beside him. His body stayed tight, waiting. He had trouble concentrating on simple
tasks—measuring shingles, counting tiles—and his boss noticed that Minh started making mistakes he
never made before. Minh came to believe that he could not rely on himself anymore. If his own body
had betrayed him on a routine job, how could he trust his judgment about anything?

The triggers multiplied. The darkness of early morning when he woke before dawn became unbearable;
he associated it with the dim ICU room where he had lain helpless. Any hospital smell—even a bandage
or a doctor’s waiting room—sent a jolt through him. And related smells, like bleach or paint
thinner, made him gag and turn away. He started refusing jobs that required chemical sealants, then
jobs on unfamiliar roofs where he could not predict the dangers. Eventually he stopped taking any
new projects at all, sticking only to the handful of houses he had worked on for years. His income
dropped, and he felt shame piling on top of fear.

At home, Minh changed too. He used to argue with Lien about small things—money, chores—but after the
exposure he stopped disagreeing altogether. He had become convinced that conflict was dangerous,
that any confrontation could somehow trigger another catastrophe. So he nodded when Lien complained,
said nothing when she made plans he did not like. She began to feel that he had disappeared into
himself. Minh tried hard not to think about the trauma, but the film kept playing, and the feelings
kept rising without warning. The world felt like a dangerous place now, not just the roof but
everywhere; he avoided unfamiliar streets, unfamiliar faces, because he could not tell what might go
wrong. He dwelled on who he used to be—a steady, capable man who could handle anything—and the gap
between that man and the man he had become felt like a chasm he would never cross.

What finally brought Minh to a clinic was Lien’s insistence, but also his own quiet despair. He had
started to feel that there was no future worth planning for. At sixty-six, he thought, maybe this
was just what old age felt like. But Lien told him it was not like that before, and she was right.
He came because a part of him still hoped—though he was afraid to say it—that he could find a way to
stop the film, to let his shoulders drop, to sit in the dark without his heart racing. He wanted to
be able to disagree with his wife again, to take a new job without scanning the horizon for what
might kill him. He was not sure he believed it was possible, but he showed up anyway, and that was
the first step.
