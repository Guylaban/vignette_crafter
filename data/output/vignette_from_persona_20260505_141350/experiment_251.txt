Miguel was a 62-year-old cardiothoracic surgeon who had spent thirty-five years with his hands
inside chest cavities, repairing hearts and patching lungs. He was married to his wife, Elena, and
had three adult children. The accident happened on a rainy November night as he drove home after a
twelve-hour shift. A pickup truck ran a red light and T-boned his sedan, spinning it into a highway
guardrail. Miguel was conscious throughout the extraction, pinned against the driver’s door with his
left arm twisted under the steering wheel. He remembered the odour of gasoline, the sound of
crumpling metal, and—most vividly—the slick, cold feeling of the torn upholstery pressed against his
cheek. That texture, a damp synthetic weave, became as indelible as a scar.

The physical injuries healed within months, but the psychological ones took hold more quietly.
Miguel began noticing changes in the weeks after he returned to the operating room. His first
intrusive memory came during a routine coronary bypass: he reached for a clamp and the rubberised
grip of the instrument reminded him, for a split second, of the car’s ruined seat. The image of the
guardrail rushing toward his driver’s side window forced itself into his mind before he could push
it away. He set down the clamp, took a breath, and finished the case, but from then on he was never
fully at ease in surgery. The memory appeared without warning—not as a faint recollection but as a
vivid, immediate reliving. He could feel the impact, smell the wet upholstery, hear his own
breathing. As soon as he recognised what was happening, he would mentally block the scene, refusing
to let it play out to the moment the car stopped spinning. That incomplete recall left him with a
nauseous tension, like a sneeze that would not come.

He began avoiding certain textures outside of work. He stopped wearing his old corduroy jacket
because the feel of the fabric against his forearm was too similar. He asked Elena to replace the
throw pillows on the living-room couch with something smoother. More troublingly, he quit his
Wednesday-night basketball games, claiming his knees bothered him, when in truth the physical
exertion—the pounding heart, the shortness of breath—mimicked the bodily panic he had felt while
trapped in the car. He did not want his body to feel that way again, so he avoided anything that
raised his heart rate beyond a leisurely walk.

The crash imagery came at unpredictable moments. Driving past a minor fender bender on the freeway
was enough to flood him with the image of crumpled cars. He began taking surface streets, adding
twenty minutes to his commute, and he refused to watch any television show that featured a car
accident. At the hospital, he found himself checking the surgical schedule to ensure none of the
other doctors were the ones who had treated him in the emergency room that night. Those faces,
though kind, were now part of the memory, and seeing them in the hallway triggered the same
uncontrolled sense of being back in the wreck.

His relationships grew strained. Elena noticed that Miguel had stopped suggesting weekend hikes or
dinner parties with old friends. When she asked why, he said he just did not feel like it. He had
quietly given up nearly everything that had once brought him pleasure: golf, woodworking, playing
the guitar. The activities felt hollow, even threatening, as though enjoyment itself had become a
kind of vulnerability. He spent his evenings in the den, reading medical journals or watching nature
documentaries—anything that did not involve people or effort or the risk of a physical sensation
that might trip the alarm.

What finally brought Miguel to seek help was an episode that frightened him more than the accident
itself. During a particularly long aortic repair, he had to pause mid-surgery because the tactile
feedback through his gloves—the warm, slippery surface of the aorta—briefly merged, in his mind,
with the wet upholstery from the crash. He froze for three full seconds. His scrub nurse asked if he
was all right. He finished the operation, but the next day he could not bring himself to schedule
another case. Elena found him sitting in the dark living room, his hands resting on his knees, and
she told him he could not go on like this. He agreed to see a therapist, though he was terrified
that talking about the accident would finally let the memory complete itself—that he would have to
watch it all the way through, and then he would never be able to stop.
