Robert was sixty-eight, a school counselor who had spent thirty years helping teenagers navigate
their worst moments. He had always believed he could handle anything—until the assault. It happened
late one afternoon, after a parent conference. The father of a student he’d been trying to support
came to the meeting already drunk. Robert smelled the alcohol on his breath as the man leaned across
the desk, then the shouting, then the chair hitting his ribs, then the floor. He remembered only
snapshots after that: the cracked linoleum, a shoe, the antiseptic sting of the ER later. Those
moments came back as still images, each one separate, each one carrying the same raw terror he had
felt on the floor. He could not string them into a story. They just appeared—sudden, vivid, then
gone, leaving him breathless.

In the weeks that followed, Robert did not talk about what happened. He returned to work because he
had to, but he began to move through the hallways differently, keeping his eyes down, stepping into
empty classrooms whenever a student lingered near his office door. Emotional closeness had become
something dangerous. He had always prided himself on being the counselor who listened without
judgment, but now if a student leaned forward, confiding in him, his chest would tighten and his
mind would go somewhere else—a deliberate blanking out, a leaving of his own body that he could do
in a second. He had learned to do it during the attack. It had helped then. Now it happened whenever
someone got too close, and he could not stop it.

At home, his wife noticed the distance. He stopped sitting next to her on the couch. He stopped
telling her about his day. When she tried to talk about their son’s visit next month, Robert felt a
wave of something he could not name, and the next thing he knew he was staring at the wall, gone. He
had come to believe that being known by someone meant being vulnerable, and being vulnerable meant
being hurt. Intimacy, even kindness, now felt like an invitation to disaster.

The triggers were everywhere. One morning a custodian mopped the hall with a solution that smelled
like the hospital where Robert had spent four hours getting stitches. He froze, the snapshots
came—the linoleum, the shoe—and he had to excuse himself to the bathroom to dissociate until the
terror passed. Another day a student walked past him reeking of hand sanitizer. That was enough. He
began avoiding the main office, where the antiseptic smell was strongest, and took to eating lunch
alone in his car. The alcohol smell was worst of all. A parent’s breath, a spilled drink at a
faculty event—he could not be near it. He stopped attending any gathering where alcohol might be
present, which included every retirement party and after-school meeting. His colleagues noticed.
They said he seemed different, withdrawn. Robert said he was fine.

The consequences accumulated. His caseload of students dropped because he referred them to other
counselors without explaining why. His wife cried one night and said she felt like she had lost him.
Robert did not know how to tell her that he had lost himself. What had finally brought him to a
therapist’s office was a moment three weeks ago: a student—a shy boy who had been bullied—came to
him with tears in his eyes, asking for help. Robert felt the warmth of the boy’s trust and his own
mind began to slide away. He sat there, smiling blankly, while the child spoke. Later, he could not
remember a word the boy had said. That was the moment he understood he could no longer do his job,
could no longer be present for anyone. He was afraid of what the therapy would ask of him—afraid
that talking about the assault would bring the snapshots back and that he would not survive them.
But he was more afraid of living the way he was living: hollow, hidden, alone in a house with the
woman he loved.
