Elena was forty-five and alone when she finally walked into the clinic, though she had been living
with the aftermath of that day for nearly three years. As a combat pilot, she had flown missions
over hostile territory countless times, but the attack that rewired her world did not happen in the
air. It happened on the ground, in the base mess hall, during a routine shift change. A truck bomb
detonated just outside the reinforced doors. The noise was not a blast—it was a physical pressure
that drove through her skull and into her chest, so loud that for several seconds she heard nothing
at all, only a ringing that seemed to come from inside her own bones. People were everywhere,
pressed against her, screaming, pushing. She remembered the smell of diesel and the feel of grit in
her mouth. Later, in the triage tent, a medic handed her a cup of water; she vomited before she
could drink it. The physical sensation of her own body rebelling—the nausea, the shaking, the raw
throat from yelling for people to stay down—had lodged itself in her gut like a stone.

In the months that followed, Elena found that her body remembered what her mind tried to push away.
A mild cold, the ache of a muscle strain, any hint of physical unwellness brought the stone rolling
back into place. She would feel the nausea rise and the ringing start in her ears, and she would be
back in that crowded hallway, unable to breathe. Crowded spaces became unbearable. She stopped going
to the base commissary, the gym during peak hours, the briefing room before it emptied out. Each
time she walked into a room and found people clustered, her chest tightened and her gaze flicked to
the exits. Loud noises—a truck backfiring, a dropped metal tray, a door slamming—sent her heart into
a gallop and left her scanning the room for the source, certain something worse was coming.

She had always been disciplined about her health, but now she smoked. It started as something to do
with her hands in the evenings when sleep would not come. She would sit on her small apartment
balcony, watching the base lights go out one by one, and chain-smoke until the urge to look over her
shoulder subsided. She did not live on the base anymore—she had moved off it, into a building with
wide hallways and a twenty-four-hour front desk, but even there she could not stop checking behind
her. Walking from her car to the entrance, she turned her head every few paces, knowing it made no
sense, knowing there was no one there, but unable to override the feeling that someone was
following, closing in. Her sleep was a shallow, fractured thing. She woke at three in the morning,
four, five, her body braced for an explosion that never came. She had tried to tell herself that
others had survived worse, that she was lucky to be alive, that at least she could still walk and
talk and fly. She had said it so often that the words tasted false. The downward comparison was a
mantra that had lost its comfort.

Her flying had not stopped—she was still cleared for duty—but now the cockpit felt like a different
kind of enclosure. The canopy closing over her head, the sealed press of the oxygen mask, the narrow
confines of the seat: all of it pressed on her in a way it had not before. She had requested a shift
to administrative roles, away from combat rotations, and the request had been granted without
question. Colleagues assumed she was burned out. She let them think that. What she could not say was
that the small window of the cockpit had begun to feel like the crawl space she had been trapped
beneath during the attack, a collapsed partition that had pinned her for almost an hour until rescue
teams cut her free. She had never told anyone about that hour. In her own mind, she minimized it. It
could have been worse. She had not lost a limb. She had not lost a friend. But the world no longer
felt safe, and she had come to believe that no amount of vigilance could keep the next bad thing
from finding her.

The final strand that brought her to therapy was a trivial event: a sudden stomach bug that kept her
home for two days. She could not eat, could not stop trembling, could not stop the conviction that
she was about to fall apart completely. "I can't deal with even the slightest upset," she said in
the first session, her voice flat. "I can't stop bad things from happening." She was tired of the
hypervigilance, tired of the smoke-stained clothes, tired of sleeping in a chair because lying down
made her feel too exposed. She wanted to fly again without the dread, to walk into a supermarket
without her spine turning to ice. She did not know if that was possible, but she had come to ask.
