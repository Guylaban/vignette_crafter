Kenneth had always taken pride in his work. For twenty-six years, he drove the same route through
the northern suburbs, knowing every pothole, every stoplight, every regular face that climbed aboard
with a nod or a quick “Morning, Ken.” The bus was his domain—a predictable, controlled space. That
was before the night shift three years ago, when he gave a lift to a man he vaguely recognized from
the depot. The man asked for a ride to the far end of the line, and Kenneth, tired from a double
shift, agreed. He didn't think twice until the man moved into the seat behind him, until the engine
idled at the empty terminus, until the unexpected hand on his shoulder. By the time it was over,
Kenneth's world had shrunk to the smell of diesel and the sound of his own shallow breathing. He
never reported it. He never told anyone. He just drove home, showered, and lay awake until dawn,
certain that if he said the word out loud, the shame would swallow him whole.

In the months that followed, Kenneth discovered that the memory would not stay buried. It played
like a film he could not switch off—the rough fabric of the seat, the weight, the low murmur of the
man's voice, and worst of all, the terror that rushed back each time, as fresh as the moment itself.
He began to notice that certain things pulled the film to the surface without warning. The sound of
a bus door hissing shut could do it. A passenger clearing his throat in a particular pitch. And the
exhaustion: when he was tired, the images seemed to break through the dam, flooding his mind until
his hands trembled on the steering wheel. He learned to dread the late-afternoon runs, when fatigue
crept into his bones and the film started to roll.

Driving with people became unbearable. Every passenger was a potential threat. He watched them in
the rearview mirror—studying their hands, their posture, the direction of their gaze. He had come to
believe that strangers were not simply unpredictable but dangerous; that any one of them could
decide to cross a line. The bus, once his sanctuary, now felt like a trap. He started taking
unscheduled breaks, pulling over to calm his racing heart. He switched to the earliest morning shift
to minimize contact with rowdy riders, but the isolation cost him. His wife, Diane, noticed he was
distant, that he flinched when she touched his shoulder from behind. He told her he was just
stressed from work. He could not bring himself to explain the shame that clung to his body—the sense
that he was dirty, damaged, that if anyone knew what had happened, they would see it written on his
skin.

The lost faith was the quietest wound. Kenneth had never been particularly religious, but he had
believed in a basic decency, a kind of unspoken contract between people. That contract was broken.
He no longer trusted the goodwill of others, and in darker moments he saw the entire world as a
place where someone could smile at you and then hurt you. He stopped attending the monthly drivers'
social. He stopped greeting passengers with more than a grunt. When a young man offered him a coffee
at a layover, Kenneth declined with a curt shake of his head, his jaw tight.

It was a sound that finally drove him to seek help. A few weeks ago, sitting in his idling bus at a
red light, he heard a muffled thump from the luggage compartment—a passenger's bag shifting. The
sound echoed something from the assault, a dull impact he had tried to forget. He lurched forward,
slammed the brakes, and a woman in the front seat stumbled and nearly fell. She yelled at him,
called him reckless. Kenneth pulled over, handed the wheel to a supervisor, and walked home. That
night, Diane told him he needed to talk to someone. She said she was scared for him. She said she
didn't know who he was anymore.

At fifty-three, Kenneth sits in the therapist's office with his hands clenched on his knees. He is
afraid of what he might find if he opens the door to that memory—afraid that the shame will be too
heavy, that the terror will never stop, that he will lose the last shreds of control he has left.
But he is also tired, so tired, of living inside a film that never ends. He wants to get behind the
wheel again without watching every face. He wants to look at Diane and feel her touch without
flinching. He does not know if that is possible, but for the first time, he is willing to try.
