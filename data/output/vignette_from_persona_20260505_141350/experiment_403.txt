Layla was thirty-one when the convoy she was driving in was hit by an improvised explosive device on
a road just outside Mosul. She remembers the dust, the silence after the blast, and then the
shouting. What she cannot remember, no matter how many times she has tried, is the moment of impact
itself. There is a gap in the film of her mind, a skip, and then she is on the ground, ears ringing,
watching the translator—a quiet man named Khalid who always wore a faint spicy cologne—slumped
against the Humvee door, not moving. She has never been able to piece together the seconds between
the noise and the stillness. The memories come in fragments: the smell of diesel and copper, the
heat, the weight of her weapon, but never in the right order, never forming a whole story.

For the past fourteen years, Layla has lived with those fragments. She is an auditor now, working
for a state agency in a glass-and-steel office building downtown. The open-plan floor makes her skin
crawl. Every morning she walks the long way to her cubicle, hugging the wall, scanning each face,
noting every exit. She has memorized the building's stairwells and fire doors. She never sits with
her back to the room. Colleagues have joked about her being paranoid; she has learned to smile and
say she likes to see who's coming.

The worst moments come without warning. A coworker's perfume—something floral with a warm base
note—can flood her with a panic so immediate she has to excuse herself to the restroom and stand
with her hands gripping the sink until her breathing slows. Khalid's cologne was like that, she
thinks, though she cannot be certain. Every sudden bang—a filing cabinet slamming, a car backfiring
in the street—sends her heart into her throat and her gaze darting to the nearest cover. She knows
these reactions make no sense in a suburban office park, but her body does not seem to care what she
knows. It acts as if the danger is still there, as if she is still the person who could not protect
the people around her.

That belief has taken root deeply. Layla is convinced that the attack happened because of who she
is—a woman, an Arab who chose to serve, someone who was not strong enough or quick enough. She
replays counterfactual scenarios constantly: if she had taken a different route, if she had stopped
to check the road, if she had been more alert, Khalid would still be alive. The marriage she had
before the deployment ended a few years after she came home. Her husband could not understand why
she needed the bedroom door locked, why she jumped at his footsteps, why she could not sit in the
backyard without her shoulders tensed. They tried counseling, but she could not explain the gaps in
her memory, the way the story would not hold together long enough for her to tell it.

She has built a narrow life that feels manageable. She lives alone in a small apartment with a
deadbolt and a chain lock. There are exactly three places where she feels safe: her bedroom, her
bathroom with the door closed, and her car with all doors locked. Everywhere else requires
vigilance. She checks behind her on the street, identifies escape routes in grocery stores and
waiting rooms, avoids open plazas and parking lots where there is nowhere to hide. The consequence
has been a slow shrinking of her world. She no longer visits her sister, who lives in a house with
large windows and an open front yard. She has turned down promotions that would require travel to
new cities. She is reliable at her desk work but has no close colleagues, no after-work drinks, no
one who knows what she does when the lights are off and the dream residue of a nightmare leaves her
gasping at 3 a.m., the ghost of Khalid's cologne still in her nostrils.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning two weeks ago, when a loud truck backfired
outside her office. She dropped to the floor, halfway under her desk, hands over her head, and
stayed there for several moments before she realized what she had done. A security guard came over;
she said she had tripped. That evening, sitting in her locked apartment, she understood that the
fragments were not going to sort themselves out, that her body would keep reacting as if the convoy
were still burning, and that she was tired of living inside what she could not piece together. She
is afraid of what she might find if she looks at the gaps too closely, but she is even more afraid
of spending another decade hiding from a smell she cannot forget.
