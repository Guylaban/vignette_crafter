Mai is a forty-four-year-old tattoo artist who runs her own small studio in a converted garage
behind her home. She is married, of South Eastern Asian descent, and until two years ago, she had
built a loyal clientele who valued her steady hand and her ability to work in silence for hours.
Then, during a routine cleaning session, she accidentally mixed a potent industrial-grade
disinfectant with a leftover chemical stripper beneath her work table. Within minutes, the fumes
overwhelmed her—her throat closed, her eyes burned, and she collapsed, gasping, onto the floor. Her
husband found her and rushed her to the emergency room.

In the hospital, everything moved too fast. Staff held her down to insert an intravenous line; she
could not speak, could not wave them off. She remembers the bright overhead lights, the beeping
monitors, and the sharp, overlapping smells of antiseptic and latex. She remembers a nurse gripping
her wrist firmly, another pulling her gown. What she remembers most vividly is the feeling of being
watched—by doctors who leaned in, by her husband’s terrified face through the glass—while she had no
ability to say no, to pause, to stop any of it. She was discharged after two days, physically
recovered, but something inside her had changed.

In the weeks afterward, Mai found herself avoiding the hospital. That was easy enough. Harder to
manage was the return to her studio. The first time a client arrived early and stood in her doorway,
watching her set up her station, Mai’s stomach dropped. She felt the same prickling sensation of
being observed—of someone seeing her every move and waiting. She started to schedule long gaps
between appointments so she would never feel rushed, but then a client asked for a design change ten
minutes in, and Mai felt a surge of panic. *I can’t do this*, she thought. *They’re making me do
something I don’t want to.* She knew it was irrational—she could have simply said no—but the
pressure of the request felt exactly like being held down on that gurney, unable to refuse.

The smell of antiseptic returned unpredictably. A bottle of rubbing alcohol left uncapped in the
sink, a cleaner a client used on their own bandage, a hospital soap in a public restroom—these would
trigger instant dizziness and a racing heart. Mai began washing her hands only with plain soap, then
stopped taking on clients who needed aftercare instructions involving alcohol wipes. She started
leaving the studio door open while she worked, so the air would stay fresh, but that only made her
feel more watched by neighbors and passersby.

Over months, the avoidance became a quiet erosion. She stopped accepting custom requests that
required her to learn a new technique—that felt too much like being *forced* to adapt. She turned
away clients who asked to watch her sketch the design beforehand, preferring they choose from pre-
drawn flash. Her income dropped, and her husband noticed her irritability after every appointment.
He asked what was wrong, but Mai could not explain without feeling foolish. *It’s fine*, she said.
*I just need things to be predictable.*

What finally brought her into the clinic was a morning when she opened a fresh shipment of surgical
gloves—plain white, with a faint powdery smell—and immediately retched into the sink. She stood
there, shaking, and realized she had not done a single tattoo in the past two weeks. Her savings
were running out. She was terrified that the smell would never stop surprising her, that she would
never again feel at ease with someone standing over her shoulder, that she had become, in her own
words, “a prisoner of the watchful room.” More than anything, she wants to sit with a client, needle
in hand, without the constant scanning for a scent or a stare that might undo her.
