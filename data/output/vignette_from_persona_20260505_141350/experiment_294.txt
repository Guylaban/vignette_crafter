Amara Okafor, a 63-year-old financial analyst who emigrated from Nigeria decades ago, still carried
the memory of the day soldiers came to her village. She had been helping her mother pack food for a
journey they did not want to take, because the militia had ordered everyone to leave within the
hour. A young soldier pushed her mother to the ground when she moved too slowly, then pointed his
rifle at Amara and shouted that she would be shot if she argued. She was forced to kneel in the red
dust, hands on her head, while he counted down the seconds until she obeyed. Later, they fled
through a dense forest, hiding in the underbrush whenever they heard footsteps or vehicles, never
knowing whether the people approaching were soldiers or rebels. She spent two nights pressed against
the roots of a baobab tree, afraid to cough, afraid to move, certain that any sound would bring the
uniformed men back.

In the years that followed, Amara built a disciplined life in the United States. She earned a degree
in finance, landed a stable job at a mid-sized investment firm, and kept her head down. But the old
fears never left. She avoided parks, hiking trails, any open natural space that reminded her of the
forest. Even a quiet suburban greenbelt made her chest tighten, and she would quickly turn around
and walk home. She also avoided police officers, crossing the street when she saw a patrol car,
never driving because she could not bear the thought of being pulled over. On the rare occasions she
had no choice—once when a bank teller called security because she had forgotten her ID—she froze,
unable to speak, while her hands shook.

At work, her colleagues saw her as quiet to the point of invisibility. She never challenged a
supervisor’s decision, never voiced disagreement in a meeting, even when she spotted an error in a
quarterly report. She had learned that confrontation could escalate into something dangerous, and so
she swallowed her corrections and let others take credit. Her performance reviews noted that she was
“reliable but reserved,” and she was passed over for team-lead roles three times. She told herself
that was fine; visibility only drew attention, and attention invited trouble.

The worst part, from her own perspective, was the unrelenting worry about her two adult children.
They lived in different cities now, and she called them every evening. If her son did not answer by
the third ring, she imagined him in a hospital bed. If her daughter mentioned a disagreement with a
coworker, Amara would lie awake constructing entire scenarios of retaliation, violence, loss. She
knew her worrying made them pull away—her son once told her she sounded like she expected the
worst—but she could not stop. It felt like the only way to protect them was to anticipate every
danger.

Last month, a small incident pushed her to finally seek help. A police cruiser parked outside her
apartment building for an hour while officers conducted a welfare check on a neighbor. Amara could
not step out of her door. She called in sick to work, then spent the day pacing her living room,
afraid that they would knock on her door for some reason, afraid she would be forced to explain
herself, forced to comply with an order she could not refuse. That evening, her daughter did not
answer the phone for six hours because she was in a movie theater. Amara left fourteen voicemails,
then emailed her daughter’s roommate in a panic. She knew this was not normal. She knew she was
driving everyone away.

When she first sat down in the therapist’s office, she said she wanted only one thing: to stop
feeling that every uniform, every patch of grass, every unexpected demand meant that she was about
to lose everything again. She was terrified that if she let go of her hypervigilance, something
terrible would happen to the people she loved. But she was equally terrified of living the rest of
her life this way—alone, silent, trapped in a forest that no longer existed.
