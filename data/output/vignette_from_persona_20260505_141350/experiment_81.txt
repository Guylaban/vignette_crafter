Michael was twenty years old when the Humvee in front of his convoy hit the IED. He remembers the
bang—air tearing apart, then the ringing, then the heat. He remembers the smell: burning rubber,
diesel, something organic he still cannot name. He remembers being lifted out of his seat, the dust
cloud, the shouting. A medevac helicopter came in low, rotors thumping the air, and he helped load
the driver from the lead vehicle onto the bird. That driver had no legs from the knee down. Michael
does not remember the flight back to base. He knows he was on it, because people told him later, but
his memory skips from the dust and the smell directly to sitting on a cot in the aid station,
staring at his boots. The pieces do not connect. When he tries to tell someone what happened, the
words stop in his throat. He knows the facts. He cannot say them.

He was discharged three months later and came home to a small city in the Midwest. He got the bus
driver job through a veteran preference program. It seemed straightforward—sit in the cab, drive a
route, drop people off. He thought the structure would help. Instead, every day became a gauntlet. A
backfiring car in the next lane sent his hands jerking on the wheel. The diesel exhaust from his own
bus, especially on a warm afternoon, brought the burnt smell back so vividly that he had to pull
over and breathe through his mouth. One morning a medical helicopter flew low over the depot, and
Michael sat frozen in the driver’s seat for fifteen minutes, the route supervisor tapping on the
window, until he could make his hands let go of the steering wheel.

He had come to believe that the world was simply a place where things exploded without warning.
Anyone could be fine one second and gone the next. He had to be on guard all the time. On the bus,
he watched passengers’ hands, their bags, the way they moved. Crowded stops made his stomach clench.
He started taking a longer, quieter route that avoided the shopping centre and the university
campus. His supervisor noticed and asked why. Michael said the other route had potholes. He began to
dread weekends, when crowds swelled downtown. He told himself he just did not feel like going out.

At night he sat in his apartment with the lights off and the phone screen glowing. He scrolled
endlessly through gaming forums, played the same first-person shooter until two or three in the
morning, anything to stop the film that played behind his eyes. The film was the same every time:
the Humvee pitching sideways, the dust, the driver’s face—but then it would skip, and he would be
back in the aid station, not knowing how he got there. He could not stand the gaps. He could not
stand the replay. Eventually he would fall asleep on the couch, phone still in his hand.

He stopped going to the small church his mother had attended. He had no words to explain why. The
smell of incense reminded him of something, or maybe it was the quiet, or the way people stood and
knelt at the same time. He just knew he could not walk through that door. His mother called and
asked if he was alright. He said he was busy. He said he would come next week. He did not.

What frightened Michael most was the thought that he had already died over there and was now just
going through the motions. He did not want to die, exactly, but he was not sure his life was worth
living anymore. He woke up most mornings feeling like he had not slept, the smell of burnt rubber
already in his nose. The day stretched ahead: another route, another backfire, another helicopter,
another shift where he would have to watch everything and everyone. He had stopped answering texts
from his old Army buddy because he could not talk about what happened, and he could not talk about
anything else.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a Tuesday afternoon. A kid’s birthday balloon popped in
the back of the bus—sharp, sudden, loud. Michael slammed the brakes. A passenger fell. No one was
hurt, but the passenger complained to the company, and Michael was suspended pending a fitness-for-
duty evaluation. His supervisor told him it was standard procedure, just get a note. Michael sat in
the parking lot for an hour after the meeting, phone in his hand, not knowing who to call. He read
the number of the VA clinic his mother had given him months ago. He dialed before he could talk
himself out of it. He was terrified that they would say he could not drive a bus again—that the one
thing he had left would be taken—but he was more terrified of the next balloon, the next backfire,
the next night alone with the film that would not stop. He hoped, more than he expected to, that
someone would understand what he could not find the words to say.
