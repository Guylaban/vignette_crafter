Priya, a 66-year-old security guard, had been stationed at a downtown office building for nearly a
decade, her days shaped by quiet routines and the steady rhythm of employees coming and going. That
changed two years ago, on a humid October evening when her son Ravi, then 38, offered to run an
errand she had asked him to do. She had insisted he go, despite his saying he was tired. A block
from the grocery store, a driver ran a red light and struck Ravi’s car at full speed. He died at the
scene. From that moment, Priya’s world collapsed into a single, unbearable loop: she had sent him.
She had acted. The event was her fault.

In the months that followed, Priya found herself unable to tolerate even the smallest disagreement.
When her husband gently suggested she speak to a grief counselor, she felt her chest tighten and her
throat close. She learned to nod and say nothing, because any hint of conflict—any difference of
opinion—now carried the weight of that final argument. She had told Ravi to go; she would never
argue again. At work, she avoided the break room whenever her colleagues talked about a minor fender
bender they had seen on the news. The words “car,” “crash,” and “accident” landed like blows. She
would slip into the supply closet until the conversation shifted, her hands shaking, her pulse
racing.

Her job as a security guard had always required vigilance, but now it felt like a trap. Every shift,
she scanned the parking garage for any sign of danger—a car moving too fast, a driver’s eyes not on
the road, the glint of a bumper out of place. Her body stayed tense, shoulders hunched, jaw
clenched, waiting for something terrible to happen. When a delivery truck backed up too quickly near
the loading dock, she felt a surge of adrenaline so strong she had to sit down, her legs weak, her
heart pounding as if the accident were happening again. She began to believe that her body would
never be calm, that she was permanently on alert because danger was always waiting.

The memory of the accident was not a memory. It was a presence. When it surfaced—often without
warning, triggered by a car horn or a glimpse of brake lights—it felt as vivid as the moment itself:
the phone call from the hospital, the drive to the ER, the doctor’s face. The emotions—grief,
terror, guilt—washed over her so completely that she would stop mid-step or freeze at her desk,
unable to speak. She told her husband she felt like the event was happening right now, not years
ago. Ordinary memories of Ravi’s childhood seemed faded, but the accident was sharp, real, and
inescapable. She tried hard not to think about it. She would say a phrase in her mind—“stop, stop,
stop”—and push the image away, but it always came back.

She also avoided anything connected to grief. She had not visited Ravi’s grave once. When a funeral
procession passed her on the street, she turned her face away and walked in the opposite direction.
When her husband mentioned Ravi’s name, she changed the topic or left the room. She had come to
believe that if she let herself feel the full weight of her sadness, she would never recover—that
her emotions were broken for good. “I will never feel normal again,” she whispered to herself one
night, staring at the ceiling. She also believed she had failed as a mother. She should have
protected him, she should have driven him herself, she should have known better. The event happened
because she insisted, because she was impatient, because of the way she acted.

Over time, Priya’s avoidance took a toll on her marriage. Her husband, himself grieving, grew
frustrated with her silence. She stopped going out with him to social gatherings, afraid that
someone would bring up the accident or that she would hear a car crash in a movie. She felt
isolated, trapped between the constant alertness of her work and the emptiness of her home. She knew
she was losing the person she used to be, but she could not see a way out.

What finally brought her to seek help was an incident at the security desk. A delivery driver struck
a concrete pillar in the parking lot, and the sound—the metallic groan and the shatter of glass—sent
Priya into a full panic. She dropped to the floor, shaking, gasping, convinced she was back at the
intersection where Ravi died. Her supervisor called her husband, who took her home and told her
gently that she could not go on like this. She agreed to see a therapist, but she arrived full of
fear. She was afraid of what would happen if she let the memory come fully. She was afraid she would
fall apart and never be put together again. But somewhere beneath the fear, she wanted to believe
she could feel differently—that she could someday talk about Ravi without the memory consuming her.
That was all she hoped for.
