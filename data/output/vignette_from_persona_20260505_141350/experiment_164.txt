In the months that followed, Zuri found that she could not speak about what had happened. She tried,

happened to someone else entirely, but when it surfaced—and it surfaced, uninvited, in fragments of
sound and sensation—the terror returned at full force, unchanged, undimmed. She had come to believe
that she was permanently damaged, that something essential had leaked out of her that evening and
could never be replaced.

pooling in her stomach. If she allowed herself to feel good, if she let herself hope, she believed
she would be struck down again. The world had taught her that safety was an illusion, and she had no
interest in being tricked twice. So she declined invitations, pulled away from laughter, refused the
small pleasures that once had colored her days. Her partner had grown quiet, then frustrated, and
now she sensed his patience wearing thin.

Her work as a tutor became a minefield. She specialized in elementary reading, and her sessions were

learned to excuse herself quickly, to retreat to the bathroom and press a cold paper towel to her
neck while the room spun. She had also come to dread uncertainty in any form—an unexpected schedule
change, a student who arrived late, a parent who did not return her message. Each unknown felt less
like a minor inconvenience and more like a ledge she might step off into the dark.

She blamed herself, day after day. She had been certain she could make it home. She had not fought
hard enough. She had not screamed. The self-blame loop was constant, a quiet music playing beneath

not to raise the subject, not to ask about her day, not to probe. She had made it clear: if he

about the assault properly, if she let the memory fully in, she would shatter beyond repair. She

up that Thursday evening, and nothing had been replenished.

What finally brought her to therapy was a Tuesday when a new student arrived with his mother. The
boy was seven, with large glasses and a nervous smile. He did not cry, but his mother did. She
hugged him goodbye, her eyes wet, and whispered that he would do fine. Zuri felt the familiar surge

there for twenty minutes, unable to stop trembling. She realized then that she was losing ground,
that her life had narrowed to a room with one exit she was too afraid to use.

She was hoping, now, to find a way back to herself. She was terrified that there was no self left to
find. But she had come, and that was something she had not thought she could do.
