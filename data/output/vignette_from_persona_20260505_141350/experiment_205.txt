Nadia, a 55-year-old HVAC technician, had been divorced for over a decade when she finally walked
into a therapist’s office. She lived alone in a small apartment in a Midwestern city, and her days
were spent repairing heating and cooling systems in office buildings, schools, and sometimes private
homes. She was good at her job—methodical, thorough—but for the past few years, her work had become
increasingly difficult to do without a knot of dread tightening in her chest.

The event that had fractured her life happened twenty-two years ago, during the final days of the
war in her home country. Nadia, then in her early thirties, fled with her husband and young
daughter. The route took them through a flooded valley where a bridge had been destroyed. They waded
through chest-deep, muddy water for hours, holding their daughter above the surface. The water was
thick with debris—broken branches, dead animals, and once, the floating body of a stranger. The
texture of the mud and wet fabric clinging to her legs became seared into her senses. The
uncertainty of whether they would make it to the other side, whether the water would rise further,
whether soldiers were waiting in the silence ahead—that was the worst part. She remembered the
strange, heavy silence after the sounds of shelling stopped, a silence that felt more dangerous than
the explosions themselves. They eventually crossed, but Nadia never felt safe again.

In the months that followed, she began to notice changes. She could not stand the feeling of wet
clothing against her skin; she would change her shirt immediately if she got splashed. The sight of
a full bathtub or a large puddle made her heart race. She started scanning every room she entered
for an exit, even her own living room. At work, when she had to go into a basement to inspect a
water heater, she would first walk the perimeter, checking for flood drains and alternative ways
out. Her coworkers noticed she always sat with her back to the wall during breaks, her eyes moving
constantly. She told herself she was just being careful.

The shame was harder to explain. When memories of the crossing surfaced—and they did, without
warning, leaving her gasping and disoriented—she felt a deep, burning shame. She had been helpless,
terrified, and she had not been able to protect her daughter from the cold and the fear. The memory
felt unreal, as though it had happened to someone else, yet the emotions were too raw to deny. She
would replay moments of the flight, obsessively wondering if she had made the wrong choices, blaming
the smugglers, the soldiers, the chaos that had torn her life apart. Rumination became a nightly
ritual.

The silence became a trigger in itself. In the quiet of a basement utility room, Nadia would freeze,
certain that the stillness meant danger was imminent. She began to work with music playing loudly
through earbuds, partly to drown out the silence, partly to keep herself grounded. But the
flashbacks still came: the sensation of water rising to her chin, the weight of her daughter on her
shoulders, the smell of mud and rot. When this happened, she had to stop everything, lean against a
wall, and breathe until the world returned to something recognizable.

Over the years, her divorce had been partly fueled by her husband’s inability to understand why she
could not just “move on.” He had not felt the same way about the crossing; he seemed to have left it
behind. Nadia had retreated into herself, avoiding any conversation about the past, refusing to
attend social gatherings where she could not control her environment. Her daughter, now an adult,
lived in another state, and their calls were brief and stilted. Nadia was terrified that her own
fear had damaged their relationship beyond repair.

What finally brought her to seek help was a panic attack in a client’s flooded laundry room—a burst
pipe had sent an inch of water across the floor. She had stumbled out, trembling, and her boss told
her she needed to take time off. Sitting in the waiting room, Nadia was still scanning for exits,
still feeling the phantom wetness on her legs. She was terrified that if she let herself talk about
the crossing, she would shatter. But she was equally terrified of losing the only work that gave her
life structure. She hoped, more than anything, that someone could help her make the memories feel
less real, less present, so she could walk through a quiet room without feeling like the water was
about to rise again.
