Priya was thirty-one when she killed a cyclist. The word “killed” nested in her chest like a stone,
though the man had not died at the scene—he had died three days later in the ICU, while Priya sat in
a hospital chair, her husband’s hand on her shoulder, her own hands still raw from gripping the
steering wheel. She had been turning left on a green arrow, and the cyclist had come out of the sun,
from a direction she had not checked, because she had been thinking about her next lesson plan,
about whether the photocopier would be free, about nothing that mattered. The impact was a single
thud she felt through the axle. What she remembered now, two years later, was not the man’s face but
the smell of hot rubber and something metallic, and the way the windshield had splintered into a
thousand tiny squares—a snapshot she could call up at any moment, each square a picture in her mind.
The memory did not feel like a memory. It felt like a loop that played with no warning, and when it
played, Priya was not in her classroom or her kitchen; she was inside that moment, the air bag
deploying, the silence after the sound.

She had always known herself as a teacher. It was not just a job; it was the list of things she was
good at: explaining fractions, calming a child’s tantrum, writing report-card comments that made
parents cry with gratitude. Teaching was her way of being useful in the world. After the accident,
that Priya disappeared. She still showed up at school, graded papers, gave lessons, but she no
longer felt capable of any of it. When a parent stopped her in the hallway to say, “My son has never
loved reading until your class,” Priya’s stomach dropped as though she had been pushed. The praise
landed like an accusation: you do not deserve this. You destroyed a family. You are not a good
person. She started to dread parent-teacher conferences, then morning greetings, then the staff
room, where someone might compliment her bulletin board. She found herself turning away from smiles.

Positive emotions became treacherous. Once, during a staff lunch, someone told a joke and Priya
laughed—a real, involuntary laugh—and then the guilt hit so hard she had to excuse herself to the
bathroom. She stood over the sink, gripping the porcelain, repeating to herself, “His mother will
never laugh again. You laughed.” She had come to believe that she was permanently different, marked,
an outsider who could no longer share ordinary human feelings because ordinary people had not done
what she did. She stopped joining conversations about weekend plans. She stopped going to department
happy hours. Her husband, Raj, tried to reassure her: “It was an accident. You didn’t mean it.” But
Priya knew that was not the point. The event happened because of the way she acted—because she had
been distracted, because she had not looked twice, because she had not been careful enough. The word
“accident” felt like a lie.

The avoidance grew quietly over months. She took different routes to school to avoid the
intersection. She muted the television when a traffic report came on. And at school, when the
fourth-grade safety unit came around and the principal scheduled an assembly about bike lanes, Priya
called in sick even though she was healthy. She had figured out that any talk about crashes—even
abstract, even about statistics—sent her heart pounding and her palms sweating. It was harder to
avoid being praised. Students occasionally left notes on her desk: “You are the best teacher ever.”
She could not throw them away, because that felt cruel, but she could not read them either. She
shoved them into a drawer and closed her eyes.

It was a parent-compliment that finally brought her to the clinic. A mother had emailed the
principal to say how much her child had improved, and the principal forwarded the email to Priya
with a congratulatory note. Priya stared at the screen for twenty minutes, then shut her laptop.
That night she told Raj, “I can’t do this anymore. I feel like I’m living inside the crash every
time someone tries to be nice.” She was not sure what she hoped for. Mostly, she was afraid that
therapy would confirm what she already believed: that she was no longer capable of being the person
she used to be, and that the only way out of the guilt was to disappear entirely from the life she
had built. But she came anyway, because she had noticed that when she stopped laughing, Raj started
sounding tired, and she could not stand the thought of hurting someone else.
