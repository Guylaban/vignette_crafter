Carlos was sixty-eight when the torture happened, though it felt as if it had happened yesterday, or
was happening right now, or would happen again the moment he let his guard down. He had been a
welder for forty years, a quiet man who kept his head down and his hands steady, living alone since
his wife passed a decade earlier. The men who came for him were from a gang that had been extorting
the neighborhood. He had refused to pay, believing he had nothing worth taking. They took him from
his garage workshop one evening, thrown into the back of a van, and held him for three days in a
windowless room. He could still feel the cold concrete floor against his back, the smell of gasoline
and sweat, the way the door sealed shut like a coffin. They beat him with pipes, they put a gun to
his temple and pulled the trigger on an empty chamber just to hear him beg, they told him no one
would find him, no one would care. What stayed with him most was the sound of the hammer clicking
back, a dry metallic snap that now came back to him at the clatter of a dropped wrench or the hiss
of a welding torch.

For two years Carlos tried not to think about it. He threw himself into work, filling orders,
welding gates and railings, not speaking unless spoken to. He detached from the memories the only
way he knew—by pushing them into a room in his mind he never entered. When a customer complained or
a foreman raised his voice, he felt his spine soften, his voice go small, agreeing before he could
even register what he was agreeing to. He became overly compliant, nodding, apologizing, offering
discounts, desperate not to provoke anyone. It made sense to him: if he kept his head down, kept
everyone happy, he would stay safe. But the safety never lasted. A sudden bang from a backfiring
truck outside the shop would send him ducking behind a bench, heart pounding, vision tunneling, the
memory surging back so vividly he could feel the rope on his wrists, the weight of a boot on his
chest. He would stand up, trembling, and tell himself it was over. But his body did not believe him.

The worst part was the rage. When the memory came—and it always came, no matter how hard he
suppressed it—he felt a heat behind his eyes, a pressure in his ribs that made him want to smash
something. He would clench his fists until his nails bit into his palms, and then hate himself for
the violence that lived inside him now. He told himself that what happened was his own fault: he
should not have tried to be brave, he should not have said no, he should have done more to stop
them, he should have fought back harder, he should have died trying. The guilt was a weight he
carried everywhere. He believed that you could never know who would harm you—a neighbor, a client, a
stranger on the street—and that the only way to survive was to expect betrayal. But expecting it did
not stop it. It only made him watch the door, scan every face, flinch at every loud noise.

His work suffered. He started refusing jobs that required him to work in enclosed spaces—boilers,
storage tanks, basement vents—because the feeling of having no way out was unbearable. His boss
noticed, then complained. Carlos lost hours, then lost the job. He spent his days at home, sitting
in a chair facing the window, telling himself he was fine. But he was not fine. He could not explain
what happened when someone asked: the words scattered, the timeline dissolved, and he would stop
mid-sentence, lost, waving a hand as if to say *forget it*. No one pushed. He had no one left to
push.

Last month, a neighbor’s television blared a war movie. The crack of a rifle sent Carlos to the
floor, gasping, soaked in sweat. That night he barely slept, replaying the moment the gun was
pressed to his skull, the click, the acrid breath of the man who laughed. The next morning he found
himself at a community health clinic, hands shaking, unable to say why he had come. He told the
intake worker he wanted the memories to stop. What he did not say, what he could barely admit to
himself, was that he was terrified they would never stop, and that if they did not, he was not sure
he could keep living this way—waiting for a sound that had already happened, already ruined him,
already proved that the world was nothing but a series of locked rooms and loaded chambers.
