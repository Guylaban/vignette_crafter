Robert, a 62-year-old potter living in a small New England town, had spent thirty years coaxing
beauty from clay. He and his wife, Margaret, raised two children, and their son, Daniel, had grown
into a quiet, gentle man who loved working alongside his father in the studio. On a clear Tuesday
afternoon two years ago, Daniel died of a sudden cardiac arrest while at work. The call came to
Robert at the wheel of his truck; by the time he reached the hospital, his son was gone. There was
no warning, no illness to prepare for—just a gap where Daniel’s voice used to be.

In the weeks that followed, Robert found himself moving through the house as though walking through
invisible walls. He stopped going into Daniel’s room, stopping even to glance at the closed door.
When Margaret left a photograph of Daniel on the kitchen counter, Robert quietly slid it into a
drawer without saying a word. He began to notice that any object, any sound, any scent that carried
even a trace of Daniel—the smell of wood shavings from the studio, the creak of the porch swing
where Daniel used to sit—sent a cold spike through his chest. So he began to eliminate them. He
moved the swing to the shed. He bought new brushes for his pottery, discarding the old set Daniel
had given him for his birthday. He stopped listening to the radio station they had both enjoyed. He
didn't explain these choices to Margaret; he simply made them, one by one, and hoped the silence
would protect him.

What startled Robert most was what happened when something good came his way. A friend stopped by
with a glazed bowl he had finished, full of praise. Robert felt a warm surge in his chest—and
instantly shut it down. He turned away, muttered something about needing to clean his tools, and
walked out of the room. Positive emotions had become treacherous, as though feeling even a flicker
of contentment meant forgetting Daniel. He had come to believe that if he let himself feel happy, he
would be punished, or worse, that the happiness would somehow erase his grief. So he refused every
invitation, every opportunity for joy. Margaret suggested a weekend trip to the coast; Robert said
no without explanation. The thought of planning anything—a dinner date, a doctor’s appointment, a
retirement party—filled him with a low, persistent dread. He stopped making any plans at all, living
day to day in a gray, uncommitted present. He told himself it was simpler that way, though it was
not simpler. It was just less dangerous.

The studio, once his sanctuary, became a place of avoidance. He could not touch the clay without
seeing Daniel’s hands pressing into it. He could not sit at the wheel without hearing Daniel’s laugh
from the stool beside him. Within months, Robert had stopped working entirely. The wheel sat still,
the kiln cold. Margaret had to take on extra hours at the library to cover the lost income, a fact
that Robert knew but could not bring himself to address. He spent his days in the back room of the
house, reading old magazines, feeding the woodstove, waiting for the hours to pass. Conversations
with Margaret grew shorter. He knew she was hurting too, but talking about Daniel felt like pulling
the scab off a wound that had not yet healed. So he said nothing.

It was Margaret who finally insisted, six months ago, that he see someone. She had found him staring
at a cracked mug in the kitchen—a mug Daniel had made as a teenager—and without a word, Robert had
thrown it in the trash. She wept, and for the first time he could not turn away. He agreed to come
to this office because he had run out of ways to keep the world at a distance, and because the
emptiness he had built around himself had begun to feel more terrifying than the grief it was meant
to contain. He is afraid of what will happen if he lets himself feel—afraid the grief will swallow
him whole—but he is also, in a small corner of his chest that he barely acknowledges, hoping that
something else might still be possible.
