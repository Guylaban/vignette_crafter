who worked part-time as a peer counselor at a community youth center—a role he took seriously,
because listening to other people’s problems gave his own life structure. Then came the fever that
would not break, the headache that felt like his skull was splitting, and the emergency room visit
that turned into a week in intensive care. Bacterial meningitis, the doctors said. They used words

worst. He survived, but the memory of those days was not a story he could tell; it was a series of
snapshots he could not control. The sharp cold of the lumbar puncture needle. The antiseptic smell
that clung to the plastic walls of his isolation room. The sight of the IV line snaking into his own
forearm, and the way his body had trembled uncontrollably even when the fever broke.

In the months after discharge, Leo tried to go back to normal. He returned to his counseling shifts,
he saw his girlfriend Maria regularly, and he told himself the worst was over. But the triggers
began to surface in ways he could not predict. A client mentioned that their grandmother was in the
hospital, and Leo felt his throat close. He walked past a first-aid kit in the staff break room and
his heart started hammering. He started steering clear of the clinic down the street from the youth
center, taking a longer route even when he was running late. His girlfriend suggested they watch a
medical drama, and he snapped at her without meaning to. What frightened him most was the way his

attack, and his heart would pound for no reason he could name. He began to believe that his body was
no longer something he could trust, that it was an enemy that kept sending danger signals even when
he knew, logically, that he was safe. Nothing in his life felt meaningful anymore. He went to work
because he had to, came home because he had nowhere else to go, and spent the evenings sitting in
the dark.
