Claire first noticed him in late October, two years ago, when she was still riding her bicycle to
the graphic design studio downtown. He was always there, leaning against a lamppost near the bike
rack, or sitting on a bench outside the coffee shop she stopped at every morning. At first she told
herself it was coincidence—a fellow commuter, maybe someone who worked in the same neighborhood. But
the pattern sharpened too quickly to ignore. He began appearing at the grocery store two blocks from
her apartment, then at the farmers’ market on Saturdays, then on the pedestrian bridge she crossed
on her way home from the studio. She never saw him arrive or leave; he was simply there, his eyes
fixed on her, his mouth set in a thin, expressionless line. She started taking different routes,
riding through side streets, even leaving the studio an hour earlier, but he would find her anyway.
Once he stood directly in her path on the bike path, close enough that she had to swerve into the
grass to avoid hitting him. She called the police, but he had no identification, no criminal record,
and by the time officers arrived he was gone. The case never went anywhere. No charges were filed.

That was the last time she felt safe on a bicycle. She sold hers within the month and began taking
the bus to work, but the bus brought its own kind of terror. Every person who stood too close to her
at the stop, every passenger who lingered near her seat, every head turned in her direction sent a
jolt through her chest that she could not control. She would feel her heart race and her hands go
cold even when she knew logically that nothing was wrong. What frightened her most was the certainty
that the next person who brushed against her might be him, or someone like him—someone whose face
said nothing but whose intentions were anything but harmless. She had come to believe that people
were not what they seemed. The man had worn a calm, ordinary expression the whole time; he could
have been anyone. Now she looked at neighbors, coworkers, even strangers in line at the pharmacy and
wondered what they were hiding.

Her husband, David, noticed the changes within weeks. She no longer wanted to walk to the corner
store together after dinner. She stopped accepting invitations to parties or gallery openings. The
place where it had all culminated—a narrow alley between two buildings near the bus terminal, where
he had cornered her one evening and simply stood, blocking her exit, saying nothing for what felt
like an eternity—she avoided entirely. She would walk an extra twenty minutes around the block
rather than pass that alley. But avoidance did not make her feel safer. Instead, she began scanning
every environment before she could relax: the front entrance of the studio, the windows, the
bathrooms, the fire exit at the back of the break room. She always chose a seat where she could see
the door, and she always checked where the exits were before she sat down. At her desk, she
rearranged her workspace so that her back faced the interior wall and she could watch the hallway.
When David suggested they go out for dinner, her first thought was about where the nearest exit was
and whether she could slip out unnoticed.

Her job as a graphic designer suffered in ways she had not anticipated. She found herself unable to
concentrate on client layouts because her attention kept drifting to the doorway or the sound of
footsteps in the corridor. She had to ask a colleague to sit in on a client meeting because the
conference room had no windows and only one door, and the thought of being trapped in there with
another person—even a friendly one—made her stomach turn. She had begun to react as if she were in
actual danger when nothing was happening: a co-worker’s hand touching her shoulder made her flinch
violently; a sudden bike bell on the street made her drop her coffee; a car backfiring sent her
diving toward the wall. David tried to reassure her that the man was probably gone, that the police
said he had no history of violence, but Claire found no comfort in that. The people responsible had
faced no consequences. He was still out there, somewhere, and no one had held him accountable. That
meant the world was exactly as dangerous as she feared—more dangerous, in fact, because no one else
seemed to see it.

It was after a minor incident at the supermarket—a customer standing too close in the checkout line,
his shopping cart bumping her foot—that Claire finally agreed to seek help. For the first time in
months, she left the store without buying anything, shaking, convinced that she had been followed
inside. She sat in her car for twenty minutes before she could start the engine, scanning the
parking lot for a face she might recognize. She called David from the driver’s seat and told him she
could not keep living this way. She was tired of checking exits, tired of jumping at nothing, tired
of feeling like her own life was not her own. She said she wanted to stop being afraid every single
minute. She did not know if that was possible, but she was willing to try.
