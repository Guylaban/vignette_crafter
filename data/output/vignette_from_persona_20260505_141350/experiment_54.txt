career in international relations, a path she had chosen out of a genuine belief that dialogue could
bridge divides. That belief had died in a concrete bunker on the outskirts of a war-torn city, three

armed men who accused them of spying. She remembers the rifle butt slammed into her ribs, the zip

language she barely understood. They did not hurt her further, but they talked about what they would
do if the "foreign agents" did not cooperate. In that darkness, she had felt the last traces of her
personhood dissolve. She was no longer Isabel Garcia, diplomat; she was an object, a bargaining
chip, a body that could be used or discarded. The memory of being physically restrained—the pressure
of the ties, the weight of a boot on her back when she tried to shift—still visits her at night,
waking her with a gasp, her hands clenched into fists.

returned home physically unharmed but hollowed out. She tried to resume her post, but the world had
changed colour. Nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore; the treaties and summits she had once
cared about now struck her as a pointless charade. She found herself going over and over why the
event had happened to her—was she assigned to the wrong region? Had she failed to read the danger
signs? The questions circled without answers, yet she could not stop asking them. At the same time,
she tried hard not to think about the trauma, pushing the memories down whenever they surfaced, only
to have them return with greater force. She became convinced that she could no longer trust her own
judgment: if she had misjudged that situation so badly, how could she trust herself to do the right
thing in any high-stakes setting? This self-doubt made her freeze during meetings, hesitant to voice
opinions she had once been known for.

security, and any mention of military topics—troop movements, arms deals, even a general’s name—sent

delegating to colleagues and staying in her office with the door closed. More insidiously, she
developed a visceral reaction to moral violations of any kind: a colleague cutting a corner, a
minister lying to the press, even a friend being rude to a waiter. Each instance of "people
misbehaving" echoed the lawlessness of her captors and deepened her sense that the world was
meaningless and cruel. She started to withdraw from social events, fearing that someone’s unethical
comment would trigger a panic she could not control.

called them, because every conversation felt like a performance she had to manage.
