Mabel is a seventy-eight-year-old potter who has lived alone since her husband died twelve years
ago. She spends most of her days in the small studio she built onto the back of her house, shaping
clay on the wheel and firing pieces in the kiln that hums beside the garden. It was there, in that
studio, that the assault happened. A man she did not know forced open the side door just after dusk.
He demanded money. When she told him there was none, he struck her twice across the face and pushed
her to the floor. He left without taking anything, but Mabel lay on the cold concrete for what felt
like hours before she could stand.

In the weeks afterward, she began to notice that she could not stop checking the locks. Every time
she walked past the front door, she had to touch the deadbolt to be sure it was fastened. She would
check the windows three or four times before bed, then again in the middle of the night when she
woke with her heart pounding. Sometimes she found herself staring into the mirror in the hallway,
not to see her own face but to scan the reflection behind her—to make sure there was no one standing
in the doorway. She had come to believe that the world was a dangerous place, that danger could
appear without warning, and that she had to be constantly ready. What frightened her most was the
way her body kept reacting: her hands would shake even when she was sitting safely in her kitchen,
her stomach would knot at a sudden creak of the floorboards, and she could not relax no matter how
many times she told herself she was alone.

The memory of that evening forced its way into her mind without permission. She would be working a
lump of clay on the wheel, the rhythmic pressure of her palms grounding her, and then suddenly she
would see the man’s face, the way his eyes had locked onto hers, and she would feel the impact of
his fist as if it were happening right now. The memories were mostly pictures—the angle of the
light, the scuff of his boot on the tile, the smear of blood on her palm when she touched her mouth.
She tried hard not to think about the event, telling herself that if she went over it again she
would not be able to handle it, that she would fall apart entirely. But the images came anyway,
mostly during the quiet of her studio or in the dead hours of the night.

The consequences of her vigilance crept into her daily life. She stopped working in the studio after
four in the afternoon, because the fading light made her feel exposed. When she did throw clay, she
kept the studio door locked from the inside, and every few minutes she would stop and listen for
footsteps, for the click of a latch. Her production slowed, and she began to turn down commissions.
Her daughter, who lived three hours away, noticed that Mabel no longer answered the phone after
dark. When she visited, she found that Mabel had removed the mirror from the studio because she kept
catching a glimpse of her own reflection and spinning around in fear.

What finally brought Mabel to seek help was a night when she woke to the sound of her own scream.
She had been dreaming that the man was back, that she was on the floor again, unable to move. In the
dream she had tried to call out but no sound came. She woke sitting up, gasping, her hands gripping
the sheets so tightly that her knuckles ached. She turned on every light in the house and checked
every lock twice, then sat in the kitchen until dawn, certain that she was still in danger even
though she knew logically that the house was empty. The next morning she called her daughter, who
drove down that weekend and helped her find a therapist. Mabel is afraid that she will never feel
safe again, that her work—the only thing that has felt like her own since her husband died—will be
taken from her by the fear. But she is also hoping that maybe, if she can tell someone the story in
full and not push it away, the images will stop feeling like they are happening now, and she will be
able to sit at her wheel without looking over her shoulder.
