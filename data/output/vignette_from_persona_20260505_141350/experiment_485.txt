Carmen was seventy-eight years old, a widow who still wore her wedding ring on a thin chain around
her neck, tucked beneath the collar of her blouse. She worked the afternoon shift at a health
insurance call center in a mid-sized city, taking calls from customers who were often anxious,
confused, or grieving—people who had lost coverage, lost a parent, lost a spouse. She had been doing
this for twelve years, long enough that the scripted phrases came automatically, even as certain
words began to land like small, sharp stones in her chest.

The event that had reshaped everything happened on a Saturday morning seven years ago. Carmen and
her husband, Miguel, had gone to the open-air market downtown, as they did most weekends. Miguel was
buying avocados from a vendor when a car bomb detonated fifty feet away. The blast threw Carmen
against a concrete pillar; she felt a searing pain in her neck and heard nothing but a high,
sustained ring. When the ringing faded, the world was dust and screams. She crawled toward where
Miguel had been standing, but she could not find him. What she found instead, she later learned, was
his body beneath a collapsed table. That part of the morning she could not remember. She knew only
fragments: the smell of diesel and burnt fabric, a stranger’s hands pulling her away, a paramedic
asking her name over and over while she stared at a shoe that looked like Miguel’s.

For months afterward, Carmen could not describe what had happened. When a counselor at the hospital
asked her to talk about the attack, she opened her mouth and found nothing. The words were there
somewhere—she knew the facts—but they would not form. She would shake her head and say, “I can’t. I
just can’t.” The images were not in sentences; they were in her body. The worst was the neck pain, a
deep ache that had never fully healed. Every time she turned her head too fast, or sat too long at
her desk, the pain flared, and with it came the ringing and the dust. She never connected the two
consciously, but she had learned that if she kept her head very still and avoided certain movements,
the flashbacks stayed at bay.

The anniversary of the bombing became a day of dread. The first year, her daughter invited Carmen
over for dinner, and Carmen went, but she spent the whole evening silent, her neck rigid, her eyes
flicking to the clock. After that, she began to make excuses: she was tired, she had a headache, she
had to work. She never said why. She stopped going to the market neighborhood altogether, taking a
longer bus route to avoid the street where the market had been. The city had rebuilt it, but Carmen
could not bear to see the new awnings, the same produce stands, the life that had returned to a
place that still held her husband’s blood.

At work, the triggers were harder to avoid. A caller would say, “I lost my husband last month,” and
Carmen’s throat would close. She would manage the script—I’m so sorry for your loss, let me check
your benefits—but her voice would go flat, and her hand would press unconsciously against the ring
under her shirt. She had come to believe that she had failed in the most important role of her life:
she had not protected Miguel, had not even been able to find him in time. What kind of wife lets her
husband die alone among avocados while she crawls away? The thought looped daily, quiet and
automatic. She also believed, with a certainty that felt like bone, that she had no future worth
wanting. She was seventy-eight, alone, and bad things happened without her being able to stop them.
She had tried to stay close to her two children after the bombing, but every time they hugged her
too long or asked how she was really doing, she pulled back. Being emotionally close felt like
standing at the edge of a pit; one wrong word and she would fall in again. So she kept them at a
careful distance, and they stopped asking.

What finally brought Carmen to a clinic was the neck pain. It had grown worse in the past six
months, and her doctor had referred her to a pain specialist, who asked about stress. Carmen
mentioned the anniversary—it was coming up again—and the doctor gently suggested a therapist who
worked with trauma survivors. Carmen agreed only because the pain was interfering with her work; she
had started missing calls, misrouting them, her fingers clumsy on the keyboard. She was afraid of
losing her job, and strangely, she was afraid of something else: that she might finally have to put
the bombing into words, and that when she tried, there would be nothing there at all.
