Marcus is a 55-year-old bomb disposal technician, a role that for decades had demanded precision,
calm, and a near-superhuman tolerance for the unpredictable. His trauma, however, came not from a
wire or a detonator, but from the sky. Two years ago, during a drive through a midwestern town, he
was caught in a sudden, violent tornado. He had pulled the car under an overpass, but the wind tore
the vehicle sideways, and debris smashed the windows. He remembers the roar—a deep, deafening
freight-train sound—and the screaming of a woman trapped in a car nearby, her cries swallowed by the
wind. What struck him most was the utter unpredictability: the sky had been grey but not
apocalyptic, and within minutes the world inverted. He tried to anchor himself, to think
strategically as his training dictated, but there was nothing to disarm. The tornado lifted the
other car and hurled it into a field. The woman survived, but Marcus has never been able to hold the
entire event in his mind. When he tries to recall what happened next—how long he lay there, how he
got out—the memory splinters. He pushes it away before it fully forms, as if his brain refuses to
run the whole tape. What remains is a searing, wordless rage: at the storm, at himself, at the sheer
indifference of the event. When the memory surfaces, even in fragments, his fists clench and his jaw
locks; he has to leave the room to keep from shouting or breaking something.

In the months since, Marcus’s life has narrowed. He used to enjoy quiet evenings with his wife,
Carol, but now he dreads any situation that might bring unpredictability. He has started avoiding
family gatherings where conversations might turn argumentative—shouting, even in jest, makes him
jump. A car backfiring on the street can send him into a full-body flinch, his heart hammering
before his mind registers the sound as harmless. He has caught himself reacting to the garage door
closing too fast, his body coiled for danger before he can think. At work, he once froze for a full
second when a colleague slammed a metal locker; the false alarm felt real, and he had to excuse
himself. He is terrified that one day his body will betray him at the wrong moment—that he will
react to an imaginary threat and make a fatal error with a live device. To keep the fear at bay, he
keeps his mind occupied constantly. He listens to dense podcasts during his commute, plays puzzle
games on his phone during breaks, and volunteers for the most technical, detail-heavy assignments
because they leave no room for intrusive images. But the distraction drains him, and at home he
turns to food—he eats mechanically, often past fullness, as though filling himself physically might
quiet the internal vigilance.

Carol has noticed the changes. He used to be engaged, teasing her about her gardening, but now he
retreats to the garage or turns the television up loud. She has tried to talk to him about the
tornado, but he cuts her off, saying he doesn’t want to “go there.” The overeating has added twenty
pounds, and his primary care doctor has flagged his blood pressure. Marcus knows the avoidance is
costing him, but he is certain that if he stops distracting himself, the missing pieces of that day
will flood back and overwhelm him. What finally brought him to seek help was a flashback three weeks
ago. He was in the grocery store when a child shouted near the cereal aisle. For an instant, he was
back under the overpass—the roar, the screaming, the helpless rage. He dropped his basket and stood
frozen, panting, until a store employee asked if he was okay. He felt humiliated and terrified of
what might happen next time. He told Carol he needed to “fix this,” and she found him a therapist
who specializes in trauma. Marcus is afraid of what he will find if he lets himself remember fully,
but he is also tired—tired of eating his fear, tired of jumping at shadows, tired of the rage that
lives just beneath his skin. He hopes someone can help him put the pieces together without breaking
him further.
