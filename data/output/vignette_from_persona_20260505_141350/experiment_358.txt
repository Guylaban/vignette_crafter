Six months ago, Robert was driving to an early-morning budget meeting at the high school where he
had been principal for over a decade. He was approaching an intersection he had navigated perhaps
ten thousand times before, thinking about the upcoming teacher evaluations, when a driver ran the
red light. The impact tore through the driver’s side door. Robert remembers the sound most of all —
a deep, metallic roar folding into the shatter of glass — and then the crushing pressure of the
airbag against his chest. He was extracted from the wreckage, hospitalized for a bruised sternum and
a concussion, and told he was lucky. The other driver was fine. For the first few weeks, Robert told
himself the same thing. He forced himself to focus solely on the positive narratives: he was alive,
he was walking, he would return to work. He repeated this as though saying it forcefully enough
could seal the crack he felt opening in his own mind.

By the second week at home, the crack had widened into a chasm. He began to replay the seconds
before the collision obsessively, the film reversing and starting over before he could stop it. In
these replays, he saw himself checking his blind spot more carefully, slowing down, avoiding the
intersection altogether. A cruel certainty took root: somebody else would not have gotten into this
situation. He began to feel that the crash had not been a random accident but a symptom of some
fundamental failing in his own vigilance. The world outside his front door began to feel saturated
with threat. Every car approaching an intersection was a potential missile. Every delay — a long
traffic light, a stalled train on his commute — sent a surge of cold dread through his chest, a
conviction that something terrible was unfolding just out of sight. He could not relax. Even in his
living room, with the door locked, his body remained braced for impact.

Then came his first attempt to return to the school. He walked into the main office, and a student’s
mother in the waiting area began to shout at the secretary about a disciplinary issue. Robert froze.
The raw, loud sound hit him like a physical blow, and the memory of the crash — the roar of metal —
slammed into his present moment so vividly he could taste the dust from the airbag. He retreated
into his office and closed the door, heart pounding, feeling the entire scene happening right now
all over again. The building itself had become an engine of triggers. The clatter of lunch trays in
the cafeteria was the sound of breaking glass. The loud cheer of a basketball game in the gym was a
sonic assault. The drop-off line, where cars idled and bumped fenders, became a daily gauntlet of
crash imagery. He found himself unable to put what he was feeling into words for his assistant
principal or his wife. The experience was entirely sensory, a wash of pure terror that vanished the
moment he opened his mouth to explain it.

To manage the overwhelming distress, Robert began to avoid the school altogether. First, he called
in sick the days after the assembly. Then he took a formal medical leave of absence. The positive
reframing he attempted — I just need rest, I will bounce back — rang hollow against the daily
experience of his own mind. Sleep was a battleground; lying down meant being delivered directly back
into the memory of the crash, a film that played on a loop until three in the morning, when he would
finally give up and walk to the kitchen. There, he ate. He ate everything — leftovers, cereal,
loaves of bread — a compulsive, mechanical attempt to fill the hollow dread that occupied his chest.
His wife began to worry. She asked him to talk about it, but the words were not there. He told her
he had permanently changed for the worse, that the old Robert — the decisive, steady leader his
teachers relied on — was gone.

The school board has been calling, gently pressing for a return date. His assistant principal is
struggling to hold the building together without him. Robert sits in my office, a large man who
seems to be trying to fold into himself. He came not because he believes he can change, but because
his wife asked, and because the absence from work is becoming a visible failure he cannot reframe.
He is afraid of what he might find in therapy. He is deeply afraid of returning to the school, where
he expects the loud noises and the shouting will put him right back in the driver’s seat of that
car. But he is even more afraid of what it means if he cannot go back. He wants to believe that
therapy might help him find the version of himself he lost on that intersection, but deep down, he
is certain the world is still a dangerous place, and that he is no longer equipped to lead anyone
through it. That is where he begins.
