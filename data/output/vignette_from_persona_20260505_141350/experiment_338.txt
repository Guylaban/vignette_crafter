Minh is a 41-year-old contemporary dancer who spent two years as a relief worker in a conflict zone
before returning to his wife and his career in a major city. The event that changed everything
happened at sunset, during a distribution of medical supplies near the front line. A mortar round
struck a nearby fuel depot, and the explosion sent a wall of fire and black smoke rolling over the
compound. Minh was knocked flat; the air turned thick with the smell of burnt fuel and something
else—medical alcohol from a shattered supply crate that had ignited. He remembers scrabbling to his
feet, seeing a colleague pinned under a collapsed wall, and then he ran. He ran away from the fire,
away from the screams, and hid behind a concrete barrier until the attack was over. He did not go
back until the smoke cleared and it was too late. The colleague died. Minh has never stopped
believing that his body made a choice that his mind could not endorse—that he acted in ways that go
against who he is, that his instincts as a dancer, trained to move with grace and purpose, failed
the one moment they were needed for something real.

Back home, Minh tried to return to the studio as if nothing had happened. But the memory of that
sunset, of the alcohol and burnt smells, began ambushing him without warning. He would be stretching
before rehearsal when the scent of a nearby bar drifting through a window would snap him back—not
just remembering, but reliving: the heat on his skin, the metallic taste in his mouth, the shame
that he had not been the person he thought he was. The same time of day became a trap. Every
evening, as the light slanted and the sun began to drop, his pulse would spike and his chest would
tighten. He started scheduling all his rehearsals for the morning, telling the artistic director he
preferred the light. Within months, he had stopped accepting evening performances entirely. His
career, which had been building steadily, began to narrow. His wife noticed he no longer wanted to
meet friends after work at the bar down the block; he made excuses about his diet, about needing
early sleep, but what he could not say was that the smell of alcohol—any alcohol, even a single
beer—filled him with a dread that felt like a physical blow. At home, he would leave the room if she
burned toast, and he insisted she cook with the exhaust fan on high. She began to feel she was
living with a stranger who could be unmoored by a scent in the air.

Minh’s shame has only deepened. He sees his avoidance as proof that he is a lousy coper, that his
reactions since the event reveal a fundamental weakness. He used to trust his body implicitly—it was
his instrument, his source of pride. Now he cannot rely on himself to simply stay in a room where
someone is drinking wine, or to walk through a parking lot at dusk. His wife has tried to be
patient, but she has started to ask why he won’t talk about what happened, and he has no answer that
does not feel like more failure. What finally brought Minh to seek help was a recurring moment in
rehearsal: the director asked him to perform a solo at a sunset-themed show, and Minh could not even
bring himself to say no. He walked out of the studio, shaking, and sat in his car for an hour,
gripping the steering wheel. He is terrified that the shame will never lift, that he is stuck with a
version of himself he despises. But he is also, beneath everything, hoping that somewhere in the
wreckage of his memory there is a way to stop the reliving—to remember the event without feeling,
even for a second, that it is still happening now.
