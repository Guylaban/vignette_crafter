Leilani was twenty years old when she deployed as a combat nurse, young and steady-handed, proud of
her role. The base hospital was a repurposed schoolhouse; she worked twelve-hour shifts under
flickering lights, learning to work quickly, to hold pressure, to look away when she had to. The
attack came at dusk. Sirens first, the high wail that meant incoming mortars, and then the ground
shook. She remembered a soldier she had been tending to—a boy from Nebraska, nineteen, with dirt in
his hair—and then the ceiling collapsed on them. She did not remember crawling out, but she
remembered the silence afterward, and the sound of her own heart railing against her ribs, and the
rage that rose up and had nowhere to go. She was fine, she told herself. She had done her job. She
had saved who she could.

Back home, Leilani returned to civilian nursing at a busy emergency department. At first she thought
she was just tired. But the sirens hit her differently now—the ambulance bay doors would swing open
and the sound would knock the breath from her chest. She would freeze for a second, three seconds,
her hand on a tray of gauze, her mind somewhere else entirely. Loud noises—a metal tray dropped, a
cart banging against a door—sent her heart into a sprint, and then that racing heart was itself a
trigger, and she would feel the panic rising, the hot surge of rage that had no target. She had come
to believe that her body was a liability, that if she lost control of her heart rate she would lose
control of everything.

So she stopped running. She used to jog the trail near her apartment—the sound of her own breath and
the pounding of her feet had felt good—but now each beat of her pulse threatened to pull her back.
She stopped agreeing to go out with friends; the idea of feeling happy, even for a moment, seemed
dangerous, like she was betraying something. She was certain that if she let herself feel hopeful,
the bottom would drop out again. Better to stay flat. Better to stay scanning. She had developed a
constant habit of surveying every room she entered: exits, shadows, the faces of strangers. She
noticed it in the grocery store, at the nurses’ station, even in her boyfriend’s apartment. He would
ask what she was looking at, and she would say nothing, because she could not find words for it. The
rage she felt when the memory surfaced was so thick it stole language from her. When the flashbacks
came—suddenly, without warning, a full sensory return to that schoolhouse—she would be so
overwhelmed by emotion that she had to stop whatever she was doing, hands pressed flat against the
counter, eyes closed, breathing as though she were drowning.

Her boyfriend, Devon, had been patient. But lately she snapped at him over small things—a dish left
in the sink, a question asked too many times. She had thrown a bowl against the wall one night after
he switched on a movie with an explosion scene; she had not known how to explain. He had said her
name, carefully, and she had wanted to hit something. What frightened her most was not the anger
itself, but the fact that she could feel it coming and could not stop it. At work, a senior nurse
had pulled her aside last week and asked if she was okay. Leilani said yes. She could not explain
that she had spent the entire shift scanning the waiting room for threats, that every beeping
monitor sounded like a siren, that when her own heart pounded she felt herself slipping under.

She called the therapist’s office after a night of lying awake, Devon asleep beside her, the memory
of the boy from Nebraska pressing in from all sides. She could feel the rage and the grief coiled
together in her chest, and she could not find any words to describe it. What she hoped for was
simple: she wanted the vigilance to stop, wanted to feel safe in her own skin, wanted to come home
from work without wanting to break something. She was terrified that nothing would help, that she
was broken in a way that words could not reach. But she showed up. She sat in the waiting room with
her hands folded, scanning the corners, and waited for someone to tell her it was possible to live
differently.
