Amara is a 43-year-old IT support specialist living with her husband in a midwestern city. She was
born in a West African country and came to the United States as a young adult, but the events she
carries with her happened long before she left. When she was seven years old, her mother sent her to
live with an aunt in a rural village while she looked for work in the capital. The aunt’s husband, a
man Amara was told to call Uncle, was the one who cared for her during the day while her aunt worked
in the market. He beat her regularly—for crying, for taking too long to fetch water, for being
“mule-headed.” The worst times were when he locked her in a small storage room at the back of the
house, a space barely wider than her shoulders, with no window and a heavy bolt on the outside. She
would press her back against the rough wooden wall and listen to the cicadas screaming from the
trees beyond the compound, knowing he would come back eventually. The room smelled of dried maize
and dust, and she learned to breathe in short, shallow gasps so as not to draw his attention to any
noise she made. She told herself that if she were a better child, a quieter child, a different
child, she would not be there. That belief—that something about her made it happen—settled into her
bones and never left.

Over the decades, Amara built a life that seemed stable enough. She married a patient man who loved
her steadiness, and she took a job at a mid-sized company, handling help-desk tickets from a cubicle
on the second floor. She was good at the work: methodical, reliable, calm. But underneath that calm,
her body never fully let go. She noticed it first in her shoulders—a constant knot just below her
neck that made her feel as though she were bracing for a blow. At her desk, she would find herself
scanning the hallway for no reason, tensing whenever a colleague walked past her cubicle too
quickly. Small sounds—the click of a door latch, the scrape of a chair—made her flinch. She had
trouble following instructions during team meetings; her concentration would fray after a few
minutes, and she would realize she had been watching the way the fluorescent lights trembled instead
of listening. At home, she sometimes snapped at her husband for no reason, then felt a wave of shame
that confirmed what she already believed: that there was something defective in her, a flaw that had
been there from the start.

Her triggers crept up on her in the ordinary geography of daily life. The company’s building had a
small courtyard with a few trees, and Amara avoided it entirely. Walking through a park with her
husband on weekends made her heart race and her throat tighten; she could not explain why, only that
the green and the sound of leaves and the particular quality of the light brought back the heat of
that village, the weight of her uncle’s hand, the terrible memory of being locked in that room. She
also avoided any room with a door that closed all the way. At work, she kept her own cubicle
entrance open, and when a colleague from the next aisle shut her glass door for a private call,
Amara’s breath caught and she had to invent an excuse to walk to the break room until it opened
again. She never talked about the abuse. When acquaintances asked about her childhood, she gave
short, cheerful answers and changed the subject. The thought of describing what happened made her
feel as though she were back in that storage room, waiting.

Avoidance shaped her life in ways she did not fully acknowledge until they started costing her. She
stopped accepting invitations to outdoor company picnics or team-building hikes, which colleagues
noticed and interpreted as standoffishness. Her husband, who loved hiking, gradually stopped
suggesting trips because her refusal always came with a tight smile and no explanation. At work, her
manager expressed concern that she seemed distracted during longer troubleshooting sessions and that
her error rate had crept up—she had missed steps in a software update because she had been too busy
monitoring the door for potential threats that were not there. She had reacted as if in danger more
than once: one afternoon, a co-worker playfully slammed a filing cabinet, and Amara dropped her
coffee cup and half-rose from her chair, heart pounding, before she could stop herself. The co-
worker laughed awkwardly, but Amara could not laugh. She felt the old shame curdling in her chest.

What finally brought her to a therapist’s office was not a single crisis but a slow accumulation of
losses. Her husband had begun to look hurt and confused, and she was tired of carrying the secret of
why she could not go with him into the woods or sit in a quiet room with the door closed. The PCL-5
she filled out in the waiting room came back at 55, but she did not need a number to know she was
struggling. When she sat down for the first session, she said, almost in a whisper, that she needed
to stop believing she was the reason it happened. She did not know if that was possible. But she
could not keep living this way—body rigid, mind fractured, world shrinking to the size of a room she
had left forty years ago but never truly escaped. She hoped, though she was not sure she deserved
it, that someone could help her see the story differently.
