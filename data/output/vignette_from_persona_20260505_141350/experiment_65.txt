Leilani was sixty-seven when she finally walked into a therapist’s office, though she still didn’t
understand why she deserved to be there. For nearly forty years she had been a database
administrator, a job that suited her: quiet, predictable, solitary. She sat in the same cubicle on
the second floor of the county health department, behind a monitor that glowed green in the
afternoons, and she did not look up when colleagues passed. Eye contact had become unbearable. When
someone stopped to ask a question, Leilani would fix her gaze on the keyboard or on a spot just past
their shoulder, her neck stiffening. She knew this made her seem cold, or strange, but she could not
shake the certainty that if she met another person’s eyes, they would see something wrong with
her—or worse, they would see the thing she herself could no longer name.

The abuse had happened inside the small house she had shared with her husband, a man whose love had
always had a sharp edge. He had died six years ago, and she had been relieved, and then ashamed of
her relief. But the body does not forget. Even now, when she saw a man raise his hand to wave—when a
coworker reached out to pat her shoulder in sympathy—her own hand would flinch back, her stomach
tight. She avoided being touched entirely. No handshakes, no hugs, no accidental brushes in the
hallway. She had learned to sidestep, to turn her body away, to keep a distance of at least two
arm’s lengths. At first, people chalked it up to age or fussiness. Over the years, the invitations
stopped. The lunches with colleagues became a thing of the past. Leilani worked through her breaks,
and when she got home she did not turn on the lights.

Sleep was the hardest part. She dreaded the bed, the dark, the stillness that left her alone with
her thoughts. So she stayed up, night after night, watching old television shows with the volume
low, her laptop open to spreadsheets she did not need to update. She would doze off around three in
the morning, in the armchair, waking with a crick in her neck and the sense that she had failed some
basic test of being human. She had come to believe that her reactions—the flinching, the hiding, the
sleeplessness—proved she was a lousy coper. Other women survived worse, she told herself, and they
moved on. She had not moved on. She felt like a stranger to herself, as though the person she had
been before the abuse—a young woman who laughed easily, who sang in the church choir—had been
hollowed out and replaced by someone hollow. There was something wrong with her as a person, broken
at the core, and the only way to keep that brokenness from showing was to keep everyone at arm’s
length.

The trauma itself had not been a single night but a slow, squeezing violence that she had learned to
read in the set of his jaw, the pitch of his voice. She would brace herself when he came home late,
scanning his posture the way she now scanned a spreadsheet for errors. The worst episode—the one
that finally made her leave—happened in the kitchen. He had accused her of being slow, of wasting
his time, and then the back of his hand connected with her cheekbone. She remembered the sound: a
wet crack, then the ringing. She remembered the blood on the white tile floor. And she remembered
thinking, *If I could just be better, he would not have to do this.* That thought had stayed with
her long after the divorce—and later, after his death. She had never told anyone the details. Not
her pastor, not her doctor. The shame was too large.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a notice from her employer: mandatory retirement at
sixty-eight was approaching, and the HR director, a kind woman named Denise, had noticed Leilani’s
isolation. “You seem to be carrying something heavy,” Denise had said. Leilani had blinked, her eyes
fixed on a coffee stain on the carpet. She had felt seen, and the feeling was terrifying. She made
an appointment the following week, not because she believed she could change, but because the
loneliness had grown so thick she could barely breathe. She was afraid of what would happen when the
job ended. Without the cubicle, without the spreadsheets, there would be nothing left to fill the
hours. She was most afraid that she would finally have to sit still in the quiet, and that in the
quiet she would discover she had never really known herself at all.
