Samuel Adebayo, a seventy-nine-year-old man who had spent most of his life working in a warehouse on
the south side of Chicago, had learned to keep moving. He could not afford to stop. Because when he
stopped, when the warehouse floor was quiet and the other men had clocked out, something inside him
would begin to stir — a feeling so old and so raw that it had no image attached to it, only a
sickening drop in his chest, a sudden heat behind his eyes, and a certainty that danger was
approaching even when nothing was there.

He was eight years old when he witnessed his father beat his mother. It happened behind their small
house in rural Alabama, near a patch of woods where he used to play. That evening he had been out
catching fireflies when he heard the shouting. He crept around the side of the house and saw his
father’s hand hit his mother’s face, saw her stumble back against the door frame. The screen door
slammed shut with a crack that seemed to echo through the ground. His father reeked of cheap
whiskey. Samuel stood frozen among the tall grass, the smell of pine and warm earth mixing with the
sour alcohol, and he could not move. He did not scream. He did not run for help. He just stood there
until his father staggered back inside, and then his mother looked up and saw him, and she put a
finger to her lips.

He never told anyone what he saw that night. And over the years the memory itself began to fracture.
When he tried to deliberately recall the details — the color of his mother’s dress, the exact words
his father shouted — he found only a blur, a blank space where the story should have been. What
remained were the pieces: the slam of a door, the smell of whiskey, the feeling of standing in tall
grass with his heart in his throat. And the emotion — a wave of helpless terror that could wash over
him without warning, without any clear image at all, leaving him disoriented and trembling.

Samuel learned early how to outrun that feeling. He worked double shifts at the warehouse, filling
orders and lifting boxes until his arms ached. He made sure he was never alone. He would stay late
to help coworkers, take extra breaks in the break room where people were always talking, and after
work he would go to the local bar and drink until his mind went soft. The drinking became a nightly
ritual — not to remember, but to make sure he did not. The smell of alcohol no longer triggered the
memory the way it once had; by now it was just part of his life, the familiar scent of his own
glass. But the slam of a door could still stop him cold. At the warehouse, whenever the heavy steel
door to the loading dock swung shut with a bang, he would flinch, his hands shaking, and for a split
second he would be back in that Alabama yard, unable to move.

The arguments started somewhere in his thirties. He could not say why, but when the old dread crept
up on him, he would pick a fight with his wife, with a neighbor, with a coworker who had done
nothing wrong. The heat of the argument, the shouting, the rush of adrenaline — it burned away the
numbness. He did not realize he was doing it until after, when the damage was done. His wife left
him after fifteen years. She told him she could not live with a man who seemed to want to fight
someone all the time. Samuel had not disagreed. He understood that he was hard to be around. But he
did not know how to stop.

Now, at seventy-nine, he lived alone in a small apartment. He still kept himself in constant motion.
He volunteered at a community center just to be around people. He drank beer in the evenings. And
the fear had not left him. He could not walk through a park or sit on a bench in a quiet green
space; it made his skin crawl, though he could not say why. The woods, the grass, the stillness — it
all felt dangerous, as if something terrible was about to happen just out of sight.

What finally brought Samuel to the clinic was a new development. The old blankness in his memory had
started to frighten him. He had begun waking in the night, his body flooded with fear, but he could
not remember a nightmare — only the feeling, as if his own mind was punishing him for something he
could not name. His doctor had referred him after a physical turned up nothing. Samuel came
reluctantly. He sat in the chair with his arms crossed and said he did not believe in talking about
the past. But there was something in his eyes — a hope, thin and fragile — that maybe, this time, he
could finally understand what was chasing him.
