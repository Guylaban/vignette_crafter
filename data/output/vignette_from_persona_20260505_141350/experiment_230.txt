Amir is a 54-year-old software engineer who lives alone in a quiet suburban apartment, a place he

and then assaulted him behind a steel door. During the attack, Amir heard sirens outside, growing
then fading, and he remembers silently begging for them to stop and come up the stairs. They didn’t.
Afterwards, he walked home in a daze, showered until the water ran cold, and never told anyone at
work. He was certain he had done something wrong—staying late, agreeing to the check, not fighting
back—and that reporting it would only prove he had invited it. Nothing has been done to the man; as
far as Amir knows, that uniform still patrols the building.

In the months that followed, Amir began to notice that his own body was no longer under his control.
A car backfiring on the street would send him diving toward a wall; the wail of a distant ambulance
would make his mouth go dry and his hands shake. He stopped walking past the office complex
entirely, taking a bus that adds forty minutes to his commute. He also started avoiding any
situation where he might be alone with a man—no more elevators, no more after-hours meetings, no
more one-on-one code reviews. The sight of a police cruiser or a security uniform made him cross the
street, and he began to dread any interaction with authority figures, including his own team lead,
whose firm voice reminded him of the guard’s “just a routine check.” At work, he refused a promotion
because it would have required monthly presentations in a room with a male department head. He told
himself the extra money wasn’t worth the panic, though his bank account began to shrink with the
unpaid leave he took after every close call.

Over time, Amir’s world shrank. He started scanning every doorway, every parked van, every group of
men on a sidewalk. He carried a small folding knife, though he knew it was useless, and he only felt
truly safe inside his own apartment with the blinds down and the door triple-locked. Even there, a
sudden siren from a passing emergency vehicle would make him press his back against the wall, heart
hammering, certain that someone was about to break in. He had long since stopped feeling the normal
emotions he used to take for granted—no real joy when his niece visited, no anger at a rude driver,
only a flat hum of vigilance and exhaustion. He believed he had lost something permanent, that the
man in the uniform had taken not just his safety but his capacity to feel like a person.

What finally brought Amir into therapy was a morning when a fire engine screamed past his building
while he was standing in the kitchen. He dropped his coffee, slid to the floor, and stayed there for
forty minutes, sure that the sirens were coming for him, that the assault was happening again even
though he was alone. Later that day, his sister—who had noticed his withdrawal and his refusal to
visit her family’s home because her husband was there—called and said, calmly, that she missed her
brother. That evening, Amir searched online for a therapist who specialized in trauma, still half-
convinced that talking about it would make it worse, that the shame would swallow him. But what
frightened him most was the idea that if he didn’t try, he would spend the rest of his life
flinching at horns and hiding behind locked doors, certain that no justice existed and that he had
only himself to blame. He wanted to feel something other than fear, even if he no longer believed he
could.
