Arjun was twenty years old when his younger brother Ravi drowned during a family trip to a reservoir
two summers ago. Ravi had waded in past the drop-off and disappeared so quickly that Arjun still
could not believe it when he thinks about the sequence—the splash, the seconds of silence, the cold
panic as he thrashed through the water and clawed at nothing. He held Ravi’s wrist for a moment
before the current pulled the body away; it slipped out of his hand like a fish. Afterwards, when he
closed his eyes at night, that exact sensation of the wrist sliding through his fingers replayed in
full sensory detail, and his stomach would drop as if he were falling himself.

In the months that followed, Arjun stopped being able to sit still. He had always written in coffee
shops or on the fire escape of his apartment, letting the street noise blur into background static,
but now every unexpected sound—a car backfiring, a dropped tray in a café, the rattle of a window
shade—made him flinch so violently that people would stare. He started working from home
exclusively, though the safety of his own desk offered no relief; he lay on the floor at night with
his heart pounding at creaks he knew were harmless, unable to trust the fact that the door was
locked and the radiator was just clicking. Sleep, when it came, was shallow and stained with
fragments of the reservoir, and he would wake up multiple times, drenched and disoriented, checking
his phone for messages that weren’t coming.

He had been a writer of short fiction, a bright young voice in his workshop group, but the sentences
now felt like lies. His characters needed futures, and the idea of planning even a few paragraphs
ahead made his throat tighten. Making any plan for his own life felt worse: scheduling a dentist
appointment, agreeing to meet a friend next week, even thinking about his birthday. Every time he
tried, a voice inside him said, *You don’t know that you’ll still be here. You don’t know that
nothing will happen.* He had come to believe that the world was a dangerous place—not abstractly
dangerous, but actively watching for a moment of careless hope. The same mechanism that had let Ravi
slip under the water could take anything, anyone, anytime. He called his mother three or four times
a day, not to talk but to ask, point-blank, “Are you sure you’re okay? You’d tell me if something
was wrong, right?” She would reassure him, but the reassurance dissolved within minutes, and he
would call again, hating himself for the need.

The grief reminders were everywhere: Ravi’s favorite sneakers still by the door, the Indian takeout
place they always ordered from, a certain radio station that played the songs Ravi hummed. Arjun
stopped taking that bus route, deleted the takeout app, moved the sneakers to a closet he never
opened. But the most insidious reminders were the ones he could not avoid—sunlight on water, the
smell of lake air, a child’s laugh that sounded like Ravi’s. Each one sent him into a spiral of
misery that he could not shake. He used to be a happy person; his friends remembered him as the one
who told stupid jokes and stayed up late trading story ideas. Now he felt hollow, convinced that the
old Arjun had drowned alongside his brother and left this bitter, jumpy shell behind. His beliefs
about how the world worked had been destroyed: he no longer trusted that good things lasted, that
effort mattered, that people died in old age after full lives.

The consequence was slow and concrete. He stopped submitting to journals, stopped responding to his
editor, stopped showing up to workshop. His last piece had been due eight months ago. His parents
flew out from Chicago to see him and found an apartment with unwashed dishes, a laptop covered in
dust, and a son who could not meet their eyes. At their urging, he finally agreed to see a
therapist, mostly because he was too tired to argue. Sitting in the waiting room now, he is
terrified that talking about it will make the memories sharper, but he is also, somewhere underneath
the vigilance, dimly aware that he does not want to spend the rest of his life flinching at a
closing door.
