Alex is thirty-two years old, single, and works as a park ranger in a sprawling national forest in
the Pacific Northwest. His trauma is not something he talks about easily. Two years ago, during a
routine backcountry patrol, he was ambushed by a group of men who had been illegally operating an
off-grid marijuana grow. They held him for three days in a crude plywood shelter, beating him,
burning his legs with a camp stove, and demanding to know when law enforcement would be coming. He
remembers the smell of pine and gasoline, the pressure of rope around his wrists, and one
moment—absurdly clear—when a bird landed on a branch just outside the open door. Other parts, like
how he finally got free or who exactly was there, are blank. He can put the feeling of the rope
burns into words, but not the sequence. When he tries to tell a doctor or a friend what happened,
his sentences fall apart, the thread vanishes, and he stops mid-sentence, shaking his head.

Alex still works as a ranger, but his daily life is now shaped by habits he never had before.
Driving is the hardest. He used to love the hour-long gravel road to the trailhead; now he takes the
bus into town for groceries and asks colleagues for rides to meetings. Being a passenger is worse—no
control over stops or sudden turns—so he avoids it entirely. At the ranger station, he checks the
deadbolt on the back door three times before he can sit down for lunch. If a delivery truck is late,
his chest tightens, and he is certain something terrible has happened—an accident, an ambush. His
body reacts before his mind can catch up: he will be walking to the outhouse and drop into a crouch
because a branch snapped, his heart hammering, no thought at all. He has come to believe that his
body is permanently damaged. There is a dull ache across his ribs that never quite fades, and he is
convinced it means internal harm that doctors missed. He used to trust the seasonal crew, but now he
keeps his distance, certain that if he relied on anyone, they would let him down or turn on him.
This belief feels like a fact, not a fear.

He has learned to numb what he feels. There are hours when he stares at the station’s fireplace and
notices that he does not feel cold or sad or anything. He also rehearses conversations before they
happen—every request, every small talk with a visitor—running the lines in his head so he can
control the outcome. It exhausts him, but the alternative, saying something unguarded, feels too
dangerous. When his supervisor asked if he wanted to lead a new interpretive program, Alex said no
without explaining, then spent the evening going over what he should have said. He is afraid that
his reactions—the shaking, the blankness, the sudden crouching—mean he is going crazy. That he will
eventually lose his grip entirely.

What finally brought him to a therapist was a morning last month. He was supposed to drive a broken-
down utility vehicle back from the east gate, a thirty-minute trip on a service road. He stood by
the driver’s door for fifteen minutes, sweating, then called in sick and walked home. The next day
he found himself in a clinic waiting room, unable to explain why he was there. He hopes, without
quite believing it, that someone can help him stop checking the doors. He is also terrified that the
therapy itself will make him feel more—that the numbing will break and he will drown. He told the
intake therapist, “I can’t describe what happened without losing it, so maybe I shouldn’t try.” He
said it carefully, rehearsed.
