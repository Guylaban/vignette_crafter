Margaret was fifty-one years old, a career civil servant who had spent twenty-three years in the
same state agency, when a routine inspection of a records storage annex became the event that
reshaped her life. A corroded drum of industrial solvent had been leaking for months in a locked
closet, and when she opened the door the chemical smell hit her like a wall. Within hours her eyes
were burning, her throat raw, and she was rushed to the emergency room by coworkers who were
shouting directions she could not follow because everyone seemed to be speaking at once, their words
tumbling over each other into a noise that made no sense. At the hospital she was stripped,
scrubbed, prodded, and questioned by strangers who leaned too close, their gloved hands on her skin,
their voices still that incomprehensible blur. For three days she lay in a bed with a curtain around
it, listening to machines beep and doors slam, never knowing what would happen next.

In the months that followed, Margaret discovered that the memory of that morning did not behave like
a normal story. When her husband asked what had happened, she opened her mouth and the words would
not come out. She could feel the burn in her throat again, see the fluorescent light of the hospital
corridor, hear the beeping, but she could not arrange these sensations into a sentence. The harder
she tried to deliberately retrieve the sequence—first the closet door, then the smell, then the
shouting—the more the pieces slipped away until all that remained were fragments: the cold of the
examination table, the sting of decontamination wipes. She learned to stop trying. When a colleague
mentioned the incident at work, Margaret’s chest tightened and she excused herself to the restroom,
unable to finish her own coffee.

Work became a landscape of hazards. The open-plan office where she had spent years was now full of
unpredictable triggers: a sudden laugh from the next cubicle made her flinch; the clatter of a
dropped stapler sent her heart racing. Strangers in the hallway—new hires, delivery drivers,
visitors—approached her desk and she felt a surge of threat so strong she could not answer them
without first rehearsing the conversation three times in her head. Before any interaction—even
asking a colleague for a file—she practiced the exchange silently, word by word, because if she did
not, she was certain she would lose control of the words and the whole thing would turn into the
same kind of incomprehensible noise that had surrounded her in the emergency room. She stopped
eating lunch in the breakroom because someone might start a conversation she had not prepared for.

The avoidance spread into her marriage. Her husband reached for her hand and she pulled away, unable
to explain why a simple touch felt like a violation. She stopped sitting next to him on the couch.
Physical contact of any kind—a pat on the shoulder, an accidental brush in the kitchen—made her skin
crawl, as if she were still being examined by those strangers. She refused invitations to holiday
gatherings because she could not predict the noise, the crowd, the chance that someone might speak
to her before she was ready. The only place she felt safe was a single armchair in her living room,
positioned so she could see both doors and the window. Every other location—her car, the grocery
store, her own back yard—triggered a state of high alert. Loud or unexpected sounds, especially
sirens or sudden voices, made her drop to a crouch.

Margaret began avoiding medical appointments altogether. When a reminder came for her annual
physical, she threw the letter away. The thought of a doctor’s office, of lying down while a
stranger touched her and asked questions she could not answer coherently, was worse than the
potential health risk. She knew this was irrational, but the fear was a physical feeling, not a
thought.

What finally brought her to seek help was her husband’s quiet, desperate question after yet another
evening spent in separate rooms: “Do you remember when we used to just talk, without you having to
plan every word?” She remembered. She wanted that back. But what she was most afraid of was that the
therapy itself would require her to describe the event, to find words for a memory that had none.
She came because she was hoping, against her own certainty, that someone could teach her how to tell
the story without losing herself in it.
