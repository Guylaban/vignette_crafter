Leilani, a 33-year-old network engineer, first came to therapy nearly two years after the events
that had quietly dismantled her life. She described the traumatic moment with a precision that
suggested she had revisited it countless times: her four-year-old daughter, Aina, had been playing
in the living room when a sudden, violent cough turned into a struggle for air. By the time the
paramedics arrived, Aina’s lips had gone blue from a severe asthma attack that would spiral into
respiratory failure and a twelve-day stay in the pediatric ICU. Leilani remembered the hiss of the
ventilator, the constant beeping of monitors, and the way her own body had locked into a state of
frozen watchfulness that never really ended.

In the months that followed, Leilani found herself unable to tolerate even mild disagreements. A
trivial argument with her ex-husband over insurance paperwork sent her heart racing and her mind
spiraling back to the hospital corridor where they had fought about whether to transfer Aina to a
specialty center. At work, where she managed server security for a large finance firm, she began to
dread team meetings. The smallest raised voice or sharp exchange among coworkers made her feel as
though danger were closing in. She had come to believe that conflict was not just unpleasant but
physically threatening, and she started to avoid any conversation that might turn tense—even
declining to defend her technical decisions, which cost her a promotion.

The people most closely associated with the ICU ordeal became unbearable. Leilani’s mother had been
with her every day at the hospital, holding her hand. Now, when her mother called or visited, the
sight of her face triggered a flood of physical reactions: a racing pulse, shallow breaths, a
tightness across her chest as if the hospital air had filled the room again. She began limiting
contact, making excuses, and eventually stopped answering her mother’s calls entirely. The
loneliness of that decision weighed on her, but the alternative felt worse.

Medical settings of any kind became impossible. When Leilani’s own annual physical came due, she
canceled three times. The thought of sitting in an examination room—of being touched by a doctor, of
lying still while someone checked her body—was enough to make her feel as though she were
suffocating. Even a routine blood draw for a work requirement sent her into a cold sweat, and she
had to leave the clinic before the needle touched her skin. The irony was not lost on her: the very
procedures that had saved Aina now felt like threats.

The hypervigilance that had kept Leilani alert at the hospital followed her home. She slept poorly,
waking at every creak of the house, and found herself scanning the living room for hazards long
after Aina was safely asleep. Unexpected movement—her daughter bouncing into the kitchen, a
colleague stepping suddenly into her cubicle doorway—made her flinch as if she had been struck. Her
body stayed primed for crisis, even though the crisis had passed. She noticed that she was always
braced for the next bad thing, and that this vigilance was wearing her down.

The consequences accumulated quietly. Her divorce, already strained by the financial and emotional
toll of the illness, became final after she could no longer tolerate her ex-husband’s presence—every
conversation felt like a minefield. At work, her startle response and avoidance of conflict left her
isolated. Coworkers had begun to avoid her, interpreting her jumpiness as hostility. She had stopped
going to family dinners, stopped scheduling her own doctor’s visits, stopped inviting friends over.
Her world had shrunk to the small perimeter of her home, and even there she could not relax.

Leilani finally sought help because she could not imagine living this way for another year. What
frightened her most was the thought that her daughter would grow up seeing a mother who was scared
of everything. She hoped—though she could barely say it aloud—that somewhere inside her there was
still the calm, capable person who had talked to doctors and kept the monitors straight during the
worst hours of Aina’s illness. She wanted to be that person again, not just at work, where she still
managed complex networks, but at home, where she had become a stranger to herself.
