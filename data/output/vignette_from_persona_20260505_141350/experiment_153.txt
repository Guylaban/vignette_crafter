Priya is a twenty-two-year-old delivery driver who dispatches parcels six days a week across a
sprawling suburban zone. Her route takes her through strip malls, apartment complexes, and quiet
residential streets she once knew by heart. That changed eleven months ago, on a December evening
just after dusk. She had pulled into the parking lot of a shuttered laundromat to drop off an
envelope at a side door, as the dispatch note instructed. Before she could step out of the car, a
man yanked open her door, pressed something hard into her ribs—she still does not know whether it
was a knife or a gun—and ordered her to hand over her phone, her wallet, and the keys to her
delivery van. She complied without a word, her hands shaking so badly that she dropped the phone
twice. He shoved her onto the passenger seat, drove two blocks, then abandoned the van in a gas
station lot and ran. She sat there for ten minutes, unable to move, before a police officer arrived.
It was the officer’s presence that she remembers more starkly than the mugger’s voice: the lights,
the clipped questions, the way he asked, “Did you get a look at his face?”—as if her failure to do
so might be her fault.

In the weeks that followed, Priya found she could not drive. The thought of sliding into the
driver’s seat made her stomach clench, and on the rare occasions she forced herself to be a
passenger—a cousin’s car, a ride-share to the grocery store—she spent the entire trip scanning every
shadow, every pedestrian who lingered too long near the curb, every car that slowed alongside them.
She began identifying escape routes everywhere she went: which exit in a café led to a street with
foot traffic, which aisle in the supermarket had a back door, which bench in the park gave her a
clear view of all approaches. At night she woke repeatedly, heart pounding, and crept through her
apartment to check that the deadbolt was thrown and the windows were locked, even though she lived
on the fourth floor and had never worried about break-ins before. Her body refused to relax, even at
home; she sat coiled on the couch, listening for footsteps in the hallway, for the creak of the
elevator, for any sound that might precede a demand.

She told herself constantly that the mugging was not that bad. She had not been seriously hurt; she
had only lost a few hundred dollars and a phone. She repeated this like a mantra, as if saying it
often enough would erase the raw, unbidden terror that sometimes flooded her without warning—no
image, no memory, just the full crash of adrenaline and a voice in her chest that screamed, *You are
about to die.* When she did get images, they arrived as freeze-frames: the door of her van opening,
the glint of light on the metal object, her own hands dropping the phone. She heard his voice
repeating the word *now* in a flat, bored tone, and sometimes she heard the police officer’s voice
asking the same questions over and over.

She began smoking marijuana most evenings, because it dulled the edge of that hypervigilance, made
the escape-route planning feel less urgent, allowed her to fall asleep without waking every hour.
But the drug also deepened her sense of isolation; she stopped returning texts from friends and
declined invitations, because she could not explain why she needed to survey every room before she
could sit down, or why the sight of a police car—even one parked harmlessly at a red light—made her
want to disappear. Most of all, she could not forgive herself for doing nothing. She had not fought,
had not screamed, had not run. She had simply handed over everything and sat still. In her mind,
that made her weak, and she was certain that weakness would happen again—that no matter how
carefully she planned her escape routes or how many nights she checked the locks, she could not stop
bad things from coming.

She missed work without calling, then quit outright to avoid the confrontation. Her boss left
voicemails she never answered. Her savings dwindled; her mother called, worried, and Priya lied and
said she had found a better job. She spent her days in her apartment, scrolling delivery apps she
was too afraid to use. What finally brought her to a clinic was a panic attack in a convenience
store, triggered by nothing more than a man in a hoodie who lingered too long by the chip aisle. She
fled, left her groceries on the counter, and stood shaking in the parking lot until a stranger asked
if she was okay. Priya wanted to say no, but the word would not come. She is now sitting in an
intake session, her hands folded in her lap, trying to decide whether she is hoping to feel safe
again or simply hoping to stop feeling anything at all.
