Laura is a thirty-five-year-old married woman who works as a counselor at a community mental health
clinic. Eight months ago, while driving home from a late session, she struck a cyclist who had
swerved into her lane on a poorly lit stretch of road. The man survived, but his injuries were
severe—a traumatic brain injury and multiple fractures—and he has not regained full function. Laura
remembers only fragments of that moment: the thud, the sound of her own screaming, the way her hands
shook as she called 911. She cannot piece together the seconds just before or after. When she tries,
her mind goes blank, and then a wave of grief hits her—so sharp and sudden that she bows her head,
as if mourning someone she loved. She does not know the cyclist’s name, but she feels his life
folding into hers, and the weight is crushing.

In the weeks that followed, Laura stopped driving altogether. Her husband takes her to work, and she
keeps her eyes fixed on the floor of the car. She began avoiding medical settings entirely: if a
colleague mentions a hospital visit, she excuses herself; when a client needs a referral for a
physical exam, she passes the task to another therapist. The sight of an ambulance on the street
makes her stomach clench, and she turns away. At the clinic, she finds herself unable to hold eye
contact with anyone—clients, coworkers, even her husband. Looking into another person’s eyes feels
like exposing herself, like they might see what she did and know she is dangerous. She tries to push
the accident out of her mind, filling her days with busywork and staying late to avoid the quiet of
the evening. But the thoughts surface anyway: in the middle of a session, during a meal, as she lies
awake at three in the morning staring at the ceiling.

Sleep is a battlefield. She dreads the moment she turns off the light, because her mind starts
churning—replaying the few images she does have, or worse, imagining what the cyclist’s family saw
when they got the call. She often wakes with a start, heart pounding, certain she heard the thud
again. During the day, Laura feels jumpy and on guard. Walking to her office, she scans the parking
lot for any sudden movement. If a door slams nearby, she flinches. She has come to believe that the
accident happened because of the way she acted—that she should have seen the cyclist sooner, should
have braked harder, should have been more careful. She also believes she failed to protect him, and
that this failure defines her now. Before, her work as a counselor gave her a sense of purpose; now,
nothing feels meaningful. She sits with clients and hears their pain, but she thinks: *Who am I to

good.
