Duy is a 24-year-old house painter who came to therapy three months after the end of a two-year
relationship with his ex-boyfriend, Marcus. The physical abuse had started slowly—a hard grip on his
arm during an argument, a shove when Duy questioned him—but escalated one night in the apartment
they shared. Duy had come home late from a job, paint still under his nails, and Marcus was waiting
in the dark kitchen. He accused Duy of cheating. Duy tried to explain, but Marcus grabbed him by the
throat and slammed his head against the refrigerator door. Duy remembers the cold metal against his
scalp, the pressure on his windpipe, and then a series of disjointed images: the kitchen floor
tiles, the edge of the counter, Marcus’s face twisted and red. He cannot put the sequence in order.
Some moments—the sound of his own choked breathing, the way the refrigerator hummed—are
excruciatingly clear. Others are completely blank, as if the tape was cut. He remembers Marcus
letting go and walking away, and Duy lying on the floor, gasping, unable to move. That night, for
the first time, he felt that he no longer belonged anywhere—not in that apartment, not in the
relationship, not in his own life.

In the weeks that followed, Duy ended the relationship and moved into a small studio alone. But the
memory did not stay in the past. When he lies in bed at night, the sound of a door closing or a
floorboard creaking will yank him back into that kitchen. For a few seconds he is inside the moment
again—his throat tight, his heart pounding, the helplessness flooding through him as if Marcus is
still there. He has learned to avoid times of day that tend to bring on these episodes: late
evenings, when the light is just like it was that night. He stays up until the early morning,
watching television with the volume low, listening over the dialogue for any sound that might mean
someone is entering his home. He checks the locks obsessively. When a delivery is late or a text
goes unanswered, his mind jumps to catastrophe: something bad is happening, someone is coming for
him.

At work, Duy paints interiors for a small crew. As soon as he enters a new house, he scans every
room for exits before he picks up a brush. On a ladder, if a homeowner walks into the room
unexpectedly, he freezes completely, unable to move or speak until the person leaves. He is certain
that they are watching him, judging him, and that any moment they might turn hostile. The feeling of
being observed makes his hands shake so badly that he has ruined two ceilings this month. His boss
has noticed and warned him about the mistakes. Duy has started turning down jobs in neighborhoods
that remind him of Marcus—the same coffee shops, the same corner stores, the same street where he
used to park. He cannot explain why; he just knows that if he sees a car like Marcus’s, his chest
goes tight and he needs to leave.

Duy blames himself. He has come to believe that the abuse happened to him because of the sort of
person he is—weak, too trusting, inadequate. His current boyfriend, Leo, is gentle and patient, but
Duy cannot shake the feeling that he does not belong in a safe relationship either. He feels like a
fraud, waiting for Leo to see the same flaws Marcus saw. When Leo asks him what is wrong, Duy
freezes again, unable to find the words. At night, he lies still beside Leo, scanning the dark for
threats, listening for footsteps that are not there.

What finally brought Duy to therapy was a panic attack in the middle of a job site. He dropped his
roller, sat down on the drop cloth, and could not stop shaking. His boss called Leo, who drove him
home. That evening, Leo held him and said quietly, "You don't have to carry this alone." Duy is
terrified that if he lets himself feel the memory fully, something will break inside him. But he is
also desperate to stop scanning every room, to stop checking the locks, to stop believing that he
deserved what happened. He does not know how to belong anywhere again, but he has come to this room
hoping that someone can help him find a way back.
