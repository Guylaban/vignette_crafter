Ravi Patel is a 42-year-old tax inspector who lives with his wife and two children in a suburban
town outside Chicago. He came to therapy at his wife's urging, after months of what she called
"disappearing inside himself." Ravi described the incident with a flat, quiet voice, as though he
were reading a report he had already memorized: eighteen months ago, while driving to a rural
business audit, he glanced down to adjust the GPS and struck a motorcyclist who had stopped at a
crossroad. The man—forty-three, a father of three—suffered a traumatic brain injury and spent weeks
in intensive care. Ravi did not know the outcome for certain; he assumed the worst, and he had never
allowed himself to look up the man's name or condition. What he remembered instead were fragments:
the sound of the motorcyclist's helmet scraping across the asphalt, the way the man's hand flopped
open as he lay on the road, the high-pitched ringing that seemed to come from inside Ravi's own
skull afterward. Some moments were impossibly clear—the pattern of the man's denim jacket, the exact
angle of the bicycle's bent wheel—while other parts of the sequence, like the actual impact, were
completely blank. That blankness haunted him more than the clear images. He could not trust his own
memory, and it made him feel as though he had never properly understood what happened, as if he had
escaped into a white fog while someone else's life ended.

In the weeks and months that followed, Ravi developed a set of convictions that he never spoke aloud
but that governed his days. He had come to believe that he was permanently humiliated—not because
anyone had shamed him, but because he knew the truth about himself: he was the kind of person who
could ruin a stranger's family while adjusting a GPS. His belief that the world was basically
orderly and that good people were protected had shattered completely. He had always seen himself as
careful, law-abiding, a public servant who followed the rules. After the accident, he felt like a
fraud. He told himself he deserved to suffer, and he found a grim comfort in that punishment
thinking, because at least it gave him a reason for his constant dread. He also caught himself
thinking, "At least he didn't die," or "It could have been worse—he might have been a child," but
these downward comparisons brought no relief; they only reminded him that he was bargaining with his
own conscience.

Ravi tried not to think about the event. He suppressed the memories with a fierce, silent effort,
but they broke through anyway. Certain sounds triggered auditory recall: a car horn, the screech of
tires, a motorcycle engine idling at a traffic light—he would hear the scrape of the helmet across
the pavement again, sometimes for hours afterward. He avoided driving past the intersection where it
happened, took long detours that added twenty minutes to his commute. When his supervisor scheduled
a team meeting to discuss a case involving a road-rage citation, Ravi claimed a migraine and left
early. Funerals and cemeteries became unbearable. When a coworker's father died and the office sent
a condolences card, Ravi could not bring himself to sign it; he left it on the break room table and
walked away. He knew this was irrational—the dead man in the funeral home was not the
motorcyclist—but the grief in the air felt like an accusation, as if all mourning belonged to him.

His work suffered. A tax inspector must examine receipts, cross-check figures, catch discrepancies.
Ravi, who had once been meticulous, now found himself reading the same line three times without
comprehension. He stopped offering to do fieldwork, citing vague "medical appointments," and stayed
in the office reviewing files that required no travel. His wife noticed his withdrawal. He stopped
attending his daughter's soccer games because the field bordered a cemetery. He stopped driving the
family to weekend trips. His wife asked what was wrong, and he said "Nothing, I'm just tired." He
believed he did not deserve her concern, and he felt that if she knew the full extent of his
humiliation, she would see him as a monster.

What finally brought Ravi to therapy was a morning in late September when he saw a news report about
a car hitting a pedestrian. He stood frozen in his kitchen, the coffee growing cold in his hand, and
heard the motorcyclist's helmet scrape again. His wife found him there, pale and silent, and she
told him he had to get help. Ravi came to the first session with his shoulders bowed, expecting
judgment. He told the therapist he was not sure anything could help, and that the only thing he
hoped for was to stop feeling so afraid of his own mind. He did not say it aloud, but what he feared
most was that the man he had injured had died, and that Ravi's own life would always be measured
against that death—a debt he could never repay.
