Carlos, a 58-year-old forensic analyst who had spent nearly three decades meticulously
reconstructing the scenes of other people's worst moments, now found himself unable to step into a
building he had never seen before. It had been eighteen months since the afternoon the forensic
lab's south wing collapsed. Not an earthquake, not a structural failure anyone could have predicted
— a gas line, severed during renovation, had ignited and torn through the corridor where Carlos had
been reviewing case files. He had heard the rumble, felt the floor pitch, and then the ceiling tiles
had fallen around him like oversized confetti before the fire suppression system drenched everything
in chemical foam. He had walked out with a bruised shoulder and a ringing in his ears that no doctor
could explain. The physical wounds healed. What remained was a body that would not stop listening
for the next disaster.

Carlos’s shoulders stayed tight from the moment he woke until the moment he fell into a restless
sleep. His wife, Elena, had taken to touching his arm before she spoke because he would jump if she
called his name from behind. A car backfiring in the parking lot of the grocery store would send his
heart thudding so hard he had to brace himself against a shelf. He had started to notice that he was
scanning every room he entered — looking for exits, checking the ceiling, calculating how fast he
could reach the door. This vigilance exhausted him, but it felt necessary. He was certain that if he
let his guard down, something terrible would find him. The world, he now believed, was a place where
destruction could arrive without warning, through no fault of anyone's — a broken pipe, a careless
worker, a moment of inattention. He had always thought of himself as someone who could read danger,
who could anticipate what might go wrong at a crime scene. Now he understood that the danger was
everywhere, and he could not read it fast enough.

The most concrete change had been in his work. As a forensic analyst, Carlos had once prided himself
on being able to walk into any environment — a burned-out apartment, a crime scene in an unfamiliar
neighborhood, a high-security evidence locker — and begin his analysis. Now he refused to take
assignments that required him to visit sites he had not already been to at least once. He told his
supervisor that he preferred to work from the main office, processing evidence that others brought
in. He told himself this was a reasonable accommodation. Privately, he understood differently: his
reactions since the event proved he was a lousy coper. A competent forensic analyst did not become
useless because of one explosion. A man who could not walk into a new building without his hands
trembling was no longer capable of doing the things that had defined his career and his identity.
Elena had noticed that he no longer offered to pick up takeout from the new restaurant a few blocks
away. He said it was too much trouble. What he did not say was that the thought of walking into an
unfamiliar place, unable to quickly assess the danger, made his throat tighten.

He finally agreed to see a therapist after a routine fire drill at the office sent him to his knees
in the stairwell. The alarm — shrill, repeating — had triggered such a violent startle response that
he had dropped his coffee and pressed himself against the wall, breathing in shallow gasps. His
colleague had to help him outside. That evening, as he sat at the kitchen table, Elena asked him,
very gently, what he was so afraid of. He could not answer. He was not sure he knew anymore. What he
did know was that he wanted to stop feeling like a stranger in a world that had become nothing but
threat, and he wanted to believe he could be the man he used to be — the one who walked into any
room and saw only evidence, not danger.
