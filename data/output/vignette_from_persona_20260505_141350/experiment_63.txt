Emily is a 38-year-old bartender who has been living with her partner, Jared, for the past three
years. She came to therapy reluctantly, at Jared’s insistence, after months of growing distance
between them. What finally pushed her through the door was a news segment she caught on the bar’s
television two weeks ago, a brief report about a stabbing in a neighboring city. She had frozen mid-
pour, the glass slipping from her hand, and had to retreat to the back room until her shift ended.
That night, for the first time in months, she dreamed of her younger brother’s face.

The event that changed everything happened just over a year ago. Emily and her brother, Leo, had
been walking home from her late shift at a downtown bar. It was a route they had taken dozens of
times, a shortcut through an alley she had always thought was safe enough. Two men came out of
nowhere. Emily remembers the glint of the blade under a streetlight with a clarity that still makes
her flinch, but the seconds that followed are a blank, a black space where the worst thing must have
happened. She remembers screaming, remembers her own hands on Leo’s chest, remembers the warmth and
the wetness, but she cannot recall how she got to the hospital or who called the ambulance. What she
does remember, vividly, is the image of his shirt turning dark, and the way his eyes looked at her,
confused, as if asking why she had brought him there. That question has never left her.

In the months after Leo’s death, Emily began to build a quiet, private fortress around herself. She
stopped watching the news entirely. If a customer at the bar mentioned a violent incident, she would
walk away or change the subject. She told herself she was just being practical—there was no point in
dwelling on things she could not change. But deeper than that, she had come to believe that if she
let even one story in, the images would flood back and she would not be able to stop them. She also
started working double shifts whenever she could, partly to avoid the empty silence of her
apartment, but also because she noticed that when she kept her body moving and her mind on drink
orders, she could almost keep the feelings at bay. She prided herself on being the composed,
unflappable bartender, the one who never let a customer see her sweat. At home with Jared, she had
become quiet, careful not to let her voice tremble or her face betray anything. She was certain that
if she started crying, she would never stop.

The most corrosive change was the way she now saw people. Leo had been kind, trusting, always ready
to give someone the benefit of the doubt. And look where that had gotten him. Emily began to notice
small things—a customer’s too-friendly smile, a neighbor’s offer of help—and she would hear a voice
in her head: *You don’t know what they’re really thinking.* She stopped calling her old friends
back. She stopped asking Jared for support, even when she lay awake at night, staring at the
ceiling, gripped by a certainty that he would eventually let her down, too. She had learned the hard
way that people were not what they seemed. The two men who had taken everything from her had seemed
like just kids, at first. Now she felt she could no longer rely on anyone to be who they said they
were.

Jared had tried to get her to see a therapist months ago, but she had refused. She had a deep,
wordless fear of hospitals and clinics, of anyone in a white coat asking her to open up about
memories she had worked so hard to lock away. She told herself she could handle this alone, that
asking for help would be a sign of weakness, a confirmation that she had indeed fallen apart. But
after the news segment, after the shattered glass and the trembling hands that had not stopped for
two days, she finally said yes. She is here now, sitting across from the therapist, her arms
crossed, her jaw tight. She is afraid of what might come out if she speaks. But she is also, beneath
that, desperately hoping that there is a way to stop seeing Leo’s eyes every time she closes her
own.
