Imani was forty-five when the bomb shattered the Saturday morning service. She had been at the
bimah, midway through the Torah reading, when the blast came from the rear of the sanctuary—a
deafening crack that folded the air and threw her to the floor. For a long moment there was only
ringing and dust. Then the screaming began. She crawled toward the back, toward the broken bodies,
but by the time she reached the doorway the first responders were already pushing her away. In the
weeks that followed, the congregation held her up as a hero for staying calm, for leading the
survivors out. Imani did not feel like a hero. She replayed the moment again and again: the split
second before the explosion when she had heard something—a scrape, a footstep—and had not turned. If
she had turned, if she had shouted, if she had done something, maybe the man with the backpack would
not have made it inside. She told herself this so often that the thought became a kind of prayer,
except there was no comfort in it. She would never forgive herself for not doing more to stop what
happened.

At first the symptoms were easy to explain away. She was tired, she was grieving, she was carrying
the weight of her community. But the sound of a car backfiring on the street outside her study made
her drop a coffee cup. The bang from a collapsing shelf in the supply closet sent her to her knees.
She began to avoid the parts of the synagogue where the damage had been worst, then the entire rear
hallway, then any room without a clear exit. Her husband, David, would find her standing at the
kitchen window at two in the morning, watching the street. She told him she needed to be sure no one
was out there. At night the fear sharpened; the darkness seemed to hide a thousand possible threats,
and she could not make herself believe that the doors were locked, that the house was safe, even
when he showed her each bolt. She started scanning everywhere—parking lots, grocery aisles, the
corridor between her office and the sanctuary. Every face could be the face of someone watching her,
waiting. The sensation of being observed followed her even into her own home. She stopped answering
the phone when the news came on, and when a member of the congregation mentioned a new report about
the attacker’s trial, Imani left the room without excusing herself.

The change that frightened her most was in her marriage. Before the bombing, sex had been a source
of comfort and connection. Now the thought of being that vulnerable—exposed, undressed, unaware—made
her chest tighten. She offered excuses: a headache, late work, exhaustion. David was patient, but
she saw the hurt in his eyes and could not explain that it was not about him. It was about the
feeling that any moment something terrible could happen, and she had to stay ready. She had come to
believe that letting her guard down was a betrayal of the vigilance she owed herself and everyone
around her. Nothing good could happen to her anymore. The future that had once felt full of
possibility now felt like a corridor with no end, only more scanning, more waiting, more guilt.

Her work suffered in quieter ways. She had always been a pastor who visited the sick, sat with the
grieving, led with empathy. Now she found herself hurrying through hospital hallways, her eyes
darting from door to door, unable to focus on the person in the bed. She stopped attending evening
meetings because the drive home after dark felt too dangerous. Her senior staff noticed but
attributed it to trauma, which made Imani feel both grateful and ashamed. She was a Rabbi. She was
supposed to help others make meaning out of suffering, but she could no longer find meaning in her
own life.

What finally brought her to seek help was a small thing. David said, during a quiet argument about
her avoiding yet another family dinner, “You’re not even here when you’re here.” She heard the grief
in his voice, and for a moment she saw herself through his eyes—a woman always scanning, always
bracing, never truly present. She agreed to see a therapist because she was terrified of losing him,
and even more terrified that she had already lost herself. What she hoped for, though she could
barely admit it, was to one day walk into a room without checking the exits first. To feel safe
enough to close her eyes at night. To believe, even for a second, that the world was not waiting to
explode.
