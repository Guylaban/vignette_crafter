Malia, a 75-year-old auditor of Pacific Islander descent, had lived in the United States for nearly
forty years, but the war she fled had followed her. She was a young woman in her thirties when civil
conflict tore through her island nation. She had been working as a government auditor in a
provincial capital when armed fighters overran the town. She and a dozen other civilians hid in the
basement of a crumbling administrative building for three days, pressed together in stifling heat.
The fighters searched the upper floors; Malia could hear their boots, their voices, and the
intermittent gunfire. What she remembered most, what she could still summon without warning, was the
dense smell of sweat and fear—the sour odour of the bodies packed around her, her own skin slick
with panic. In her mind, the memory came as a series of vivid, still images: a crack in the basement
wall, the angle of light through a shattered window, the face of a young mother whose child would
not stop crying. But when she tried to speak about it, the words vanished. She knew what had
happened; she just could not find the language.

After the ceasefire, Malia emigrated with her husband, but the aftermath of those days never left
her. Over time, she developed a series of habits that she came to see as necessary for survival. She
found herself rehearsing conversations before any interaction—checking grocery lists in her head,
planning exactly how she would greet a neighbour—because she was certain that if she said the wrong
thing, people would somehow see what she had been through. She believed that her trauma was written
on her face, that others could look at her and know. This sense of exposure was so acute that she
avoided eye contact and kept her voice flat. She had come to believe that she would never again feel
normal emotions; happiness felt foreign, even dangerous. When she occasionally felt a moment of
relaxation or joy, a wave of dread followed, as if the feeling itself invited disaster. She became
convinced that her life was no longer worth living, though she never acted on the thought.

Malia’s workplace—the local government audit office where she still worked part-time—became a site
of constant vigilance. She insisted on sitting with her back to a wall, scanning the room as
colleagues entered. She frequently looked over her shoulder on the walk from the parking lot,
checking if someone was following her. At home, she moved chairs so that her back was never to a
doorway. Her husband had long ago stopped asking why she needed to sit in the same spot at every
restaurant. What worried him more was her driving. Malia took risks behind the wheel that seemed
reckless for a woman her age—running yellow lights, changing lanes abruptly. She knew it was
dangerous, but part of her felt that speed and unpredictability kept her safe, or perhaps that it
did not matter if she got hurt. She had become numb to the possibility of harm.

The consequences of these strategies were cumulative. Her husband grew distant, exhausted by her
refusal to explain her behaviour. At work, she had been passed over for a supervisory role because
her colleagues found her difficult to approach. She rehearsed her words so carefully that
conversations felt stilted, and she rarely accepted invitations to lunch. The smell of human
sweat—from a crowded elevator, a gym, even her own body after exercise—could trigger a flash of the
basement, the photographic images, the olfactory recall so strong she felt she was back there. She
would then ruminate for hours, going over how the attack could have been prevented, and why it had
happened to her of all people.

What finally brought Malia to seek help was not her own distress—she had learned to tolerate
that—but a confrontation with her husband after a near-miss on the highway. He told her he could not
keep watching her court danger. She came to the clinic reluctantly, sitting with her back to the
wall in the waiting room. When asked what she hoped for, she paused for a long moment. She was not
sure she wanted to reclaim happiness, but she wanted the constant rehearsed vigilance to stop. She
wanted to stop feeling that her life had ended years ago in a basement on the other side of the
world.
