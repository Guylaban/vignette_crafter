over the stern. Bao was coiling a rope near the rail when the deck tilted. Anh saw him slip, saw his
hands grab for nothing, and then the water swallowed him. Anh dove in after him, but the current was
brutal and the visibility near zero. He surfaced once, twice, and then couldn't find Bao again. The
Coast Guard recovered Bao's body three days later. The official report said hypothermia and
drowning; the weather had been unpredictable but forecasted as manageable. Anh never said it aloud,
but he knew the truth: he should have held on tighter, should have made Bao wear a life jacket,
should have turned back sooner. That certainty lived in him like a second skeleton.

In the months that followed, Anh did not stop working, but the work itself became a kind of assault.

grip something solid to keep from falling. He learned to scan every wave as if it might rise up and

the boat owners, not the other men who joked about the sea like it was a pet. He kept his distance

if they knew the full story they would see what he saw: a man who had let his brother die.
