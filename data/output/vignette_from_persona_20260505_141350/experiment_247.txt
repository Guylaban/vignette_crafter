Mariana was twenty-one when the bomb went off. She had been waiting for the downtown bus after her
shift at the warehouse, the evening air still warm, the stop crowded with people heading home. The
sound was not a sound—it was a pressure that folded her inward, and then there was nothing. She
never learned exactly how long she had been unconscious, only that she came to on her back with her
ears ringing and a stranger’s hand on her shoulder, the sky full of dust and sirens. Parts of those
minutes were simply gone. She could remember the feeling of the concrete against her cheek, the
metallic taste in her mouth, but not how she had gotten there from the bus stop. When she tried to
describe it later, the words would not come; she knew what had happened, could see it in flashes,
but her throat would close and her chest would lock and she would shake her head instead. What came
most easily was the rage. Every time the memory surfaced—uninvited, unbidden, triggered by a car
backfiring or a dropped pallet in the warehouse—her hands would clench and her jaw would tighten and
she would want to break something, though she could not say what or who.

In the weeks that followed, Mariana stopped making eye contact. It was not a deliberate decision so
much as a physical aversion: when a coworker spoke to her in the break room, she would look at their
forehead or past their shoulder, because looking into someone’s eyes felt like being seen, and being
seen felt dangerous. Her supervisor noticed and asked if she was all right; she shrugged and said
she was tired. At night she lay awake, listening to the creaks of her apartment building, certain
that the most ordinary noises—a car door slamming, a neighbor’s television—carried a warning she
could not quite decipher. During the day, walking through the aisles of stacked boxes, she scanned
constantly: the corners, the doors, the faces of the men who operated the forklifts. Sudden
movements made her heart pound. A coworker reaching quickly for a box would make her flinch, and she
would pretend she had only been adjusting her footing.

She learned to leave her body on purpose. When the scanning became too exhausting or the rage too
hot, she would let her mind drift upward, out of herself, watching from the ceiling as her hands
moved mechanically across the inventory sheets. It was the only way to keep working. But the
dissociation left her feeling hollow and untethered, as if her body belonged to someone else and she
was just renting it. She had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous, that safety
was an illusion she could no longer afford. And worse, she could not trust her own body anymore: the
way it froze without warning, the way it kept scanning even when her mind told her to stop, the way
it had gone numb during the explosion and never really come back.

Mariana avoided night shifts whenever possible. The warehouse ran a late shift until midnight, and
the first time she worked it after the bombing, she spent the entire four hours with her back to a
wall, jumping at every shadow. She requested a transfer to daytime only, which meant a cut in hours
and a hundred dollars less each week. Her rent became a tighter squeeze. She stopped going out after
dark altogether—no more grocery runs after work, no more meeting friends at the coffee shop. Her
social life shrank to text messages and phone calls, and even those were brief; she could not
explain why she was always too tired or why she kept canceling.

What finally brought Mariana to the clinic was her younger sister’s birthday. She had promised to
take her niece to the park—a Saturday afternoon, sunny, full of families. Ten minutes into the
visit, a child kicked a soccer ball against a metal bench with a sharp clang. Mariana dropped to the
ground, covering her head, her heart slamming against her ribs. Her niece started crying. Her sister
looked at her with a kind of terrified pity that Mariana could not bear. She apologized, made an
excuse, and went home. That night she lay in bed, shaking, and for the first time she admitted to
herself that she could not keep living like this—always on guard, always dissociating, always one
loud noise away from unraveling. She made the appointment the next morning. She did not know if
talking would help, and she was afraid that digging into the memory would make the gaps fill in with
something worse. But she was also afraid of staying the same. So she walked into the therapist’s
office, sat down, and kept her eyes on the floor.
