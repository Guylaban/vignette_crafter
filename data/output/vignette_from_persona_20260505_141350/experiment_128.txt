Fatima was seventy-five when her forty-two-year-old son, Yousef, was diagnosed with an aggressive
form of leukemia. The words came in a small, windowless consultation room at County Medical, where
the antiseptic smell was so thick it clung to her clothes for days. She remembered the doctor’s
mouth moving, the drip stand already set up beside the bed, the needle taped to Yousef’s arm. What
she remembered most acutely, five years later, was the way her own hands had stayed perfectly still
in her lap while she nodded, while she asked the right questions, while she arranged for the first
round of chemotherapy. She had been a social worker for forty years—she knew how to be calm, how to
hold space for other people’s fear. But that calm had felt, even at the time, like a betrayal of
something essential. She should have wept. She should have grabbed his hand. Instead she had
functioned, and that was the moment she later came to believe she had permanently damaged herself.

For months during Yousef’s treatment, Fatima did not allow herself to feel anything fully. She drove
him to appointments, sat through transfusions, cooked meals that he could not keep down. She pushed
every wave of terror or grief to the back of her mind as soon as it surfaced, and for a while it
worked. But after Yousef went into remission, the memories began to return without her permission,
and they returned without images. She would be standing at the kitchen sink and suddenly her chest
would tighten, her throat close, and she would be drowning in the full emotional force of those
hospital afternoons—the fear, the exhaustion, the helplessness—but with no clear picture attached,
no sequence. It came as pure, wordless dread, and then it would leave her shaking, uncertain what
had triggered it. The grief was the worst part. When she did recall a specific moment—the sound of a
ventilator, the sight of a blood bag—it was not just sadness but a crushing, infantile grief, the
kind she had not allowed herself to feel at the actual time. She had failed to grieve then, she
believed, and now the grief came anyway, unbidden, and it came with the certainty that she had
become someone else, someone cold and broken.

Her work as a social worker became a minefield. She had always specialized in supporting families
through medical crises, but now the smell of hand sanitizer in her own office could send her into a
spiral. When a younger colleague mentioned a client who had lost a spouse, Fatima felt a surge of
nausea so intense she had to excuse herself to the restroom. She began to avoid intake meetings that
involved terminal illness. She stopped visiting the hospital to check on her own clients, delegating
those tasks. Over time, she reduced her hours, then took a medical leave. She told herself she was
protecting herself, but she also heard a quiet voice accusing her of abandoning the people who
needed her most—exactly the kind of abandonment she had feared during Yousef’s illness, the kind she
had fought so hard to prevent by staying rigidly, mechanically present.

At home, the avoidance expanded. She stopped going out to the grocery store because the sight of a
bandage on a stranger’s hand could cue the memory of a central line. She avoided the news, avoided
phone calls from friends who might talk about loss, avoided the drawer where she kept Yousef’s old
hospital bracelets. She had come to believe that she was now permanently changed for the worse—that
the woman who had once walked into a crisis with steady hands and a clear head no longer existed.
Worse, she believed that her reaction during Yousef’s illness proved something terrible about her
character: that she had acted in ways that violated her own values. She should have been present.
She should have cried. Instead she had suppressed everything, and that suppression felt, in
retrospect, like a moral failing for which she could not atone.

What finally brought Fatima to seek help, at eighty years old, was a phone call. A former colleague
called to ask if she could cover a single home visit for a hospice client whose social worker had
fallen ill. Fatima heard herself refuse before she could think, and then she heard the
disappointment in her colleague’s voice. That night, she lay awake feeling the familiar wave of
grief without an image, and she understood that she could no longer be a social worker at all—that
she had lost not only her profession but her sense of herself as someone good. She made an
appointment with a therapist not because she believed she could change, but because she was
terrified of what she had become, and she hoped, very faintly, that somewhere underneath all the
damage, the person she used to be might still exist.
