Priya had always taken pride in her body’s reliability. At forty-five, she could still haul pipe
fittings up three flights of stairs, wrangle wrenches in crawl spaces, and hold her breath when a
joint blew. The life-threatening illness came without warning—a crushing chest tightness during a
routine job, then dizziness that buckled her knees, then the terrifying sense that the air had
turned to cement. The paramedics found her gasping on a basement floor, the antiseptic reek of the
hospital corridor still clinging to her memory long after the severe pneumonia had cleared. Her
lungs had filled with fluid; she had nearly drowned on dry land.

What stayed with her was not just the fear of dying, but the discovery that her own body could
betray her so completely. In the months after discharge, Priya began to notice that certain
sensations—a flicker of lightheadedness when she stood too fast, the sound of her own breath
quickening after climbing stairs—sent a current of terror through her chest. She had never paid
attention to these signals before; now she scanned for them constantly. She became convinced that
any physical stress could tip her back into that suffocating darkness. So she started avoiding jobs
that required heavy exertion, and told her dispatcher she could only take “light” calls. Her income
shrank, but worse was the mounting certainty that she could not trust what her body would do next.

The hospital smell—that sharp, clean sting—appeared unexpectedly. A customer had used a bleach-based
drain cleaner, and the moment Priya opened the cabinet under the sink, the odor hit her like a fist.
She stumbled outside, heart racing, and sat on the curb until the dizziness passed. She began
refusing jobs in medical buildings, pharmacies, even homes where she glimpsed prescription bottles.
She told no one why. What was there to say? That a scent could make her feel as though she were back
in the emergency room, tethered to monitors, drowning in air? She had learned not to count on
others; the doctors had been efficient but distant, and her family had not visited—too busy, too
far. So Priya handled things alone, the way she always had.

Over time, her avoidance deepened. She stopped scheduling annual physicals, canceled a dental
cleaning, and ignored a persistent cough because stepping into a clinic would mean facing the
antiseptic smell, the blood pressure cuff, the questions about her health. She even resisted calling
a therapist, though she knew something was wrong. Her sleep grew fragmented; she woke gasping from
dreams of being trapped in a white room. During the day, she kept a hand on her pulse, waiting for
the next sign of failure. She had come to believe that danger was always one misstep away, and that
only constant vigilance kept her alive.

The breaking point came during a routine pipe repair. She was on her knees under a sink, the work
awkward and tight, when she became short of breath from the position—not dangerous, just a normal
physical demand. But the familiar feeling of not getting enough air seized her, and she dropped the
wrench, scrambled out, and hyperventilated in the yard for twenty minutes. The customer called the
company to complain about “unprofessional behavior.” Priya’s supervisor threatened to put her on
leave. That night, sitting alone in her apartment, she understood that she could not keep running
from her own body or from the places that reminded her of how close she had come to dying. She
needed help, but she was terrified of what seeking help would mean—a return to the white walls, the
smell, the vulnerability. Still, she made the call. She told the intake worker she was a plumber who
could not trust her lungs anymore. What she did not say, what she could barely admit to herself, was
that she was more afraid of living like this than she was of the hospital.
