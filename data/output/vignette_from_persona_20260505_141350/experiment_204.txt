Malik Washington was a forty-six-year-old curriculum designer when a routine drive home from a late
meeting split his life into a before and an after. A taillight was out, something he had simply not
noticed, and when the police cruiser’s lights flared in his mirror he pulled over immediately,
turning off the engine, his hands on the wheel exactly as he believed he was supposed to do. What
followed happened very fast and yet felt interminable: the shouting, the door wrenched open, his
body dragged across the asphalt, his wrists locked behind him. He kept asking what he had done but
the questions were lost in the noise. He was processed, held for hours, and released without
charges, but the arbitrariness of it all—the sheer speed with which his agency had been stripped
away—lodged itself deep inside him.

The event itself became a paradox he could not solve. He was entirely unable to put it into words.
When his wife, Nia, asked what had happened, the story collapsed before it reached his lips,
dissolving into a thick, wordless pressure in his chest. Sometimes that pressure arrived on its own,
in the middle of the day, carrying the full emotional intensity of the terror without any clear
image attached—just the sickening plunge, the hot shame, the racing heart. At other times a specific
trigger would drop him directly into a memory, and it would hit him so powerfully that he had to
stop whatever he was doing. He would sit frozen at his desk or excuse himself mid-sentence from a
meeting, locking himself in a bathroom stall until the wave passed.

In his office, the world began to feel permanently dangerous. He found himself scanning every
parking lot before walking to his car, cataloguing exits, noting the position of every patrol car he
passed. A colleague touching his shoulder to get his attention made him jump violently, his body
reacting before his mind could catch up. As a curriculum designer, his work had once involved
regular field testing in schools across the district, but those routes took him past the precinct
where he had been held, and every time he approached that intersection his heart hammered so hard in
his chest that he felt he could not think straight. He quietly volunteered for remote desk projects,
telling his supervisor he preferred the focus of solitary work. The truth was simpler: he could not
bear the physical cost of the commute. He began taking a forty-minute detour that drained him, but
the alternative—that sickening physical flood of recognition—felt unmanageable.

He had come to believe that he was fundamentally changed, cracked in a way that could not be
repaired. His own jumpiness felt like evidence of weakness, a character flaw he could not overcome.
He was constantly on guard, and that vigilance exhausted him. At home, Nia felt the distance
growing. She wanted to understand, but he could not give her the story. She wanted to go to dinner
downtown, but the idea of driving at night, of the dark and the tail lights, left him paralyzed with
an unnamed dread. He grew irritable, snapping at minor surprises, and then hating himself for it.

The moment that finally brought him to the clinic came during a routine team meeting to review a new
unit on community safety. He felt it hit him first as pure, unbidden emotion—terror, humiliation,
helplessness—without any image to anchor it. His throat closed, his hands trembled, and he had to
leave the room. In the parking lot he nearly vomited. He sat in his car staring at the steering
wheel, realizing he could no longer outrun a reaction that was living inside him full-time, waiting
to emerge. He drove straight to the clinic.

He is terrified of what therapy will ask of him—of having to narrate a story he cannot put into
words, of being overwhelmed by the very memories he has spent almost two years avoiding. What
frightened him most was the unpredictability of his own body, the sense that safety could be ripped
away at any moment. He hopes, quietly, for a way to stop living in a state of constant, wordless
alarm. He hopes to feel like himself again.
