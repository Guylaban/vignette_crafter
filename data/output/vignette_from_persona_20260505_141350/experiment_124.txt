Tane was twenty-two when his younger brother, Leilani, was killed. The two were walking home from a
late movie, crossing at the intersection near their apartment, when a pickup truck ran the red
light. Tane felt the rush of air, heard the screech, then the thud. He turned and saw his brother on
the asphalt, and the truck accelerating away. A bystander later said the driver smelled of alcohol.
Tane knelt beside Leilani, but he could not remember much after that, only fragments. He could not
tell a coherent story of what happened even to himself; the details came in flashes—the headlights,
the sound, the way his brother’s hand looked—but the order was jumbled, and when he tried to piece
it together he felt a crushing guilt. If only he had held Leilani back for one more second. If only
he had looked left again. The driver had misbehaved, had left, had shown no remorse, and Tane would
later spend hours stewing in anger at people who broke rules, who took shortcuts, who drank and
drove. But his mind refused to keep the event in a neat timeline. Sometimes, when he tried to recall
the moment of impact, his memory went blank and he felt nothing—just a cold numbness, as if it had
happened to a stranger. Then the guilt would return, sharper.

In the weeks after, his body began to react before his mind could catch up. He would catch a whiff
of something—someone’s cologne, a beer left on a counter, the rubbing alcohol a customer brought to
the pharmacy counter—and his chest would clamp down, his shoulders would lock, his heart would drum.
He did not think about the accident first; his body simply knew danger was near. False alarms became
routine: a car backfiring at the street, a door slammed too hard, a coworker tapping his shoulder
from behind, and Tane would flinch, pulse racing, before he even registered that nothing was wrong.
His body felt constantly tense, coiled like a spring, ready to respond to a threat that never
materialized. Sleep was shallow. He stopped going out after dark.

His work as a pharmacist had once been a source of satisfaction. Now each shift felt like walking
through a minefield. The alcohol smell was everywhere—hand sanitizer, disinfectant wipes, the
occasional patient who smelled of liquor. He learned to breathe through his mouth, to turn his head,
but the tension never left. One afternoon, a patient became argumentative about a prescription
delay, raising his voice and gesturing; Tane felt a surge of heat, his vision narrowed, and he had
to excuse himself to the back room. The patient's misbehavior—loud, entitled—felt like a violation,
a reminder of the driver who had fled. After that, Tane began taking fewer shifts, then calling in
sick. His manager noticed, but Tane had no words to explain.

He had not attended Leilani’s funeral. He could not face it. The idea of a cemetery, of standing
beside a grave, made his stomach turn. His parents went; they brought back a program and a photo.
Tane never looked at them. He avoided driving anywhere near the cemetery. Any grief-related event—a
friend’s memorial, a neighbor’s condolence card—made him feel as though the ground were about to
give way. He had come to believe that his life was destroyed. The loss had cut something essential;
he used to laugh easily, to look forward to the future, but now a permanent misery clung to him.

himself he was beyond recovery, that the happiness he had once taken for granted was gone forever.

What finally brought him to a therapist’s office was a crisis at work. A customer spilled a bottle

left mid-shift and did not return. His supervisor called, concerned, and said he needed a note. Tane
realized he could not keep living like this—constantly on guard, hollow inside, unable to tell his
own story. He was terrified that revisiting the memory would drown him, but he was also desperate to
find something, anything, that still felt real. He hoped, more than he dared admit, that he might
one day look at a photograph of his brother and feel something other than guilt.
