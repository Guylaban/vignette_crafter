Kwame, a 50-year-old plumber who had built his reputation over three decades on steady hands and
reliable judgement, could no longer remember what it felt like to be sure of himself. The assault
had happened eighteen months ago, on a Tuesday afternoon, in the basement boiler room of a low-rise
apartment building he’d serviced for years. A tenant, high on something volatile and furious over a
water shut-off notice, had cornered him by the furnace. Kwame remembered the man’s shadow first,
then the crowbar swinging in an arc he could still see when he closed his eyes—a frozen image,
metallic and stark, without any words attached. He had tried to report it, had filed a police report
and given a statement, but the tenant had a cousin in the district attorney’s office and nothing
ever came of it. No charges, no apology, no acknowledgment that the world had shifted under his
feet. What remained was the picture: the crowbar, the stained concrete floor, the way his own hands
had come up too late.

That image returned at odd moments—while driving, while sitting at the dinner table with his wife,
while trying to fall asleep. It was a silent movie, playing without narration, and Kwame could not
find the language to explain it. When his wife asked what had happened that day, he would say “I was
attacked” and then stop, because the words felt hollow, as if the real event existed only in
pictures that could not be translated. The guilt came with the image. Not guilt about what he had
done—he had done nothing wrong—but a deep, ugly feeling that he should have been faster, stronger,
smarter, that he should have seen it coming. He would lie awake at three in the morning replaying
the moment, trying to rewrite it so that the crowbar missed, so that he ducked, so that the outcome
was different. The sleep that had once come easily now required a careful ritual: checking the locks
twice, sleeping on the side of the bed closest to the door, keeping a heavy wrench under the
mattress. Even then he would startle awake at the sound of a car door or a creaking floorboard,
heart hammering as if the man were standing in the bedroom doorway.

The world had shrunk. Kwame now felt safe only in three places: his own home, his mother’s house in
the next suburb, and the small hardware store where he bought supplies—and even the hardware store
had become uncertain after the day a customer had reached suddenly for a shelf behind him and Kwame
had dropped to a crouch, ready to fight. The customer had laughed, but Kwame had stood up shaking,
his face hot with shame. He started avoiding jobs that required basement work. He told his
dispatcher he could only take residential calls where he could see the front door from his truck.
His income dropped by half. His wife, who had been patient, began to worry openly about the bills.
“You used to fix anything,” she said one evening, not cruelly, but the words sat in him like a
stone. He had come to believe that he was no longer capable of doing the things that defined
him—that the man who had calmly repaired burst pipes and soothed anxious homeowners was gone,
replaced by someone who flinched at shadows and second-guessed every decision. When a leak required
a decision about which valve to turn, Kwame would freeze, terrified of making the wrong choice. The
self-doubt was constant: he could not trust that he would do the right thing, not even in the
smallest repair.

The injustice of it all was a low, persistent hum in his mind. His attacker had faced no
consequences. Kwame had done everything right—stayed calm, cooperated with the police, told the
truth—and nothing had changed. The system that was supposed to protect him had let him down, and now
he was the one who could not work, who could not sleep, who jumped at a sudden motion from his own
wife when she came up behind him in the kitchen. He had stopped going to church, stopped visiting
friends, stopped accepting invites to barbecues because the unpredictable movements of others felt
like threats. What finally brought him to seek help was the morning he had to call his wife to come
home from work because he could not open the front door to leave for a job. His hand had been on the
knob for twenty minutes, the image of the crowbar flickering in his mind, and he had been certain
that if he stepped outside something terrible would happen again. He sat in the hallway waiting for
her, and when she arrived, he saw the fear in her eyes—not fear of him, but fear for him. He was
hoping, now, to find a way back to the man who believed he could fix things. He was terrified that
such a man no longer existed.
