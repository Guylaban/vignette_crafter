Mai was sixty-one years old, a sculptor who had spent decades coaxing form from clay and stone in
the sunlit studio behind her home. The evening that changed everything had started like any
other—she had been walking to the corner market for a bottle of turpentine. She never reached the
store. As she passed the mouth of an alley, she heard footsteps behind her, fast and deliberate, and
then a man’s voice cut off mid-word. She turned. A younger man was on the ground, another man
crouched over him, and the air filled with a sharp, sweet scent of cologne—something floral and
cheap, the kind that lingers. She had not seen the weapon, only the struggle and the stillness that
followed. The attacker ran past her, close enough that the cologne washed over her again, and then
he was gone. Mai stood frozen, unable to scream or move. When she tried to tell the police what she
had seen, the words came out in fragments—she could not say how many men, could not describe the
face, could not place the order of events even inside her own mind. She could only smell that
perfume and hear those footsteps, over and over.

In the weeks that followed, Mai found she could no longer tolerate the unpredictability of ordinary
life. A knock at the door made her drop her tools. Her husband’s late arrival from work sent her
pacing. She began working only during daylight hours because after dusk the house felt fragile, the
shadows too thick, and every creak of the floorboards was a footstep coming nearer. Her studio, once
a sanctuary, became a place of startle and dread. When her assistant walked in quietly behind her,
Mai’s heart slammed against her ribs, and she snapped at the young woman for “sneaking up.” She knew
it was not fair, but the physical reaction—the racing pulse, the trembling hands—felt
uncontrollable, as if her body had a mind of its own and that mind had decided the world was a trap.

She tried to explain what had happened to her husband one night, but the story came out jumbled: she
started with the cologne, then the footsteps, then the man on the ground, then the alley, then the
cologne again. She could see his confusion, and she stopped, ashamed. There was something wrong with
her, she concluded—not just her memory but her very self. How could a person witness something and
not be able to hold it together as a single, tellable story? She had come to believe that she was
broken inside, that other people could see it. She avoided her longtime friends, stopped answering
calls, turned down invitations to gallery openings. She told herself it was because people could not
be trusted—that stranger on the street had proved that—but really she was afraid of the moment
someone would ask, “What happened?” because she knew she would fumble and lose the thread and reveal
how defective she had become.

Her work suffered. She had been commissioned to create a large public sculpture for a city plaza,
but she could no longer bear to go downtown. Every corner felt dangerous, every passerby a potential
threat. She cancelled meetings with the city arts council, made excuses, and eventually lost the
contract. Her husband began to worry not just about her fear but about the way she had shrunk her
life down to the few rooms of their home. She stopped walking their dog in the evening because the
shuffle of leaves behind her felt like pursuit. She started wearing headphones at all times, not for
music but to muffle the footsteps of anyone who might approach from behind.

What finally drove her to seek help was a conversation about their daughter’s wedding, which was to
be held in a large hotel ballroom. Mai said she could not go. Her husband asked why, and she tried
to explain—the crowd, the unpredictability, the scents of hundreds of strangers’ colognes and
perfumes pressing in on her, the footsteps on a dance floor that would sound like the alley. She
could not give a clear reason, only that she was certain something terrible would happen if she
went. He sat with her for a long time and then said, very gently, that he thought she needed to talk
to someone who understood. Mai agreed, but only because she was more afraid of disappointing her
daughter than she was of a therapist’s office. Even so, as she sat in the waiting room, she caught a
faint scent of floral cologne from the hallway, and her hands began to shake.
