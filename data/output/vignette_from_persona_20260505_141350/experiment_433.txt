Samira is a 63-year-old auditor who lives alone in a quiet suburb, a widow by divorce rather than
death, though the loss that shattered her was not of a spouse. As a girl in Detroit’s Middle Eastern
community, she had been close to her younger brother Ali, who still lived nearby when he was killed
twelve years ago. The event itself had unfolded in a way that made every subsequent survival
instinct feel like a betrayal. Ali had been working a weekend shift at a city park maintenance
facility when he was cornered by two men in a stolen van. By the time Samira arrived after frantic
phone calls—she was the one he called first, the one who always knew what to do—he was already
locked inside a small metal shed near the restrooms, bleeding from a stab wound. She had to force
the padlock with a crowbar from her trunk. She remembered the smell of cut grass, the sound of her
own screaming, the way the light fell through the dirty window onto his face. He died in her arms
before the ambulance came. That moment had not ended. It continued, vividly, every time a door
clicked shut behind her in a room without an exit, every time she drove past a patch of trees, every
time she heard a news report about another victim of violence—Ali’s name never spoken, but his face
imprinted on every stranger’s.

In the months afterward, Samira’s life began to reorganise itself around a single certainty: she
could not trust the world to be safe, and she could not trust her own body to stop reacting as
though the attack were still happening. Sleep, when it came at all, was a thin surface broken by
violent starts—a replay of the shed, the crowbar, the weight of Ali’s head, the terror that was not
memory but re-experience, complete with the same metallic taste in her mouth and the same crushing
heat in her chest. She had no warning when it would happen. A glimpse of a locked storage closet at
her office, the smell of damp soil in a landscaping bed by the parking lot, the sound of a key
turning in a lock—any of these could drop her back into that afternoon with full sensory force. She
learned to scan every new environment for exits and threats before she could focus on anything else.
At work as an auditor, she had always been thorough, but now thoroughness became hypervigilance: she
checked the lock on her cubicle door repeatedly, walked the perimeter of any new audit site before
setting foot inside, and eventually refused assignments that required visiting unfamiliar offices.
She told her supervisor she preferred to work from her own cubicle, where she could control the
environment. Over the next years, she stopped accepting any fieldwork entirely, citing health
reasons no one fully questioned. The consequence was quiet but real: she was passed over for a lead
auditor position, and her colleagues began to see her as inflexible, even odd. Samira knew she was
slowing down, but she could not explain that the unfamiliar was, for her, a place where danger lived
invisibly, where she could not assess the risk until it was too late.

Her faith in people, in a benevolent order, had died in that shed. She stopped attending the mosque,
stopped returning calls from Ali’s friends, stopped believing that any precaution mattered because,
ultimately, she could not stop bad things from happening. She had tried. She had arrived and he
still died. Her body, she felt, had betrayed her: even when she was safe in her own locked
apartment, her heart pounded, her muscles tensed, her breath came shallowly, as if the threat were
still immediate. She knew logically she was not in danger, but her body refused to believe it. What
frightened her most was that the helplessness had spread: it was no longer just about that
afternoon. It was about every locked door, every unfamiliar street, every reminder that she could
not protect anyone, least of herself.

She came to therapy because last month, during a routine fire drill at work, the building’s safety
protocol required everyone to remain enclosed in the stairwell for twenty minutes while maintenance
checked the alarm panel. The moment the door clicked shut behind her, Samira was back in the shed,
and she screamed until security officers escorted her out. Her supervisor gently suggested a leave
of absence. Samira, ashamed and exhausted, finally agreed to see a clinician. She hopes, quietly,
that she might be able to sleep without waking in terror, and that she might someday walk through a
park without seeing her brother’s face. She is afraid that nothing will change—that her body and her
mind have learned a lesson too deep to unlearn, and that the locked door of her own history will
never open.
