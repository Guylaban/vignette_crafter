Margaret was seventy-six years old and still read films three mornings a week at a regional hospital
north of Chicago. She had been a radiologist for nearly five decades, but the work that once
absorbed her now felt hollow—a set of mechanical movements performed in a state of constant, low-
grade vigilance. She sat in the darkened reading room with her back to the wall, a habit she could
no longer remember starting. Every time the door opened behind her, her shoulders locked. She told
herself it was normal for a woman her age to be cautious, but she knew better. She had not always
been this way.

The event that split her life into before and after had happened forty-two years earlier, during a
brief stint with a medical relief organization in a city that was at the time under siege. She had
been there for only three weeks when a shell struck the hospital's main entrance during afternoon
visiting hours. Margaret had been in the radiology suite two floors below, and she had felt the
building shake, heard the screaming start, and then—nothing she could put into words. Even now, when
she tried to deliberately recall that afternoon, her mind went blank, as if the memory had been
scraped out. What came instead were fragments: the smell of plaster dust and hot metal, the sight of
a woman’s shoe lying on its side in the corridor, and a feeling of heat on her face that she could
not place. She had never told anyone the whole story because she could not find the words for what
she had seen and done in the hours after the blast. The shame was worse than the fear. She had been
unable to move someone who needed help, had frozen while others carried the wounded past her. The
image of that woman’s shoe—polished brown leather, a low heel—still surfaced without warning, and
when it did, her throat tightened and her hands went cold.

The scanning began gradually. In the months after she returned to the United States, Margaret
started to check every room she entered for exits and places to hide. She learned the layout of
every grocery store she visited, noting where the shelves would provide cover. She avoided
unfamiliar restaurants and theaters because she could not predict where the emergency doors were or
whether strangers might approach her in the parking lot. A man asking for directions on the sidewalk
made her heart race; she would shake her head and walk faster, certain he was a threat. At work, she
began to eat lunch in the radiology suite rather than the cafeteria, where she could not see the
entrance from her seat. Her husband, Tom, noticed that she no longer wanted to try new restaurants
or visit their daughter in the suburbs. She told him she was tired, that at her age it was easier to
stay home.

At home, Margaret lived by routines that protected her from unwelcome feelings. She did not allow
herself to dwell on the past, and she also refused to look forward. When Tom suggested a trip for
their fiftieth anniversary, she changed the subject. She could not imagine making plans months
ahead—what was the point? Instead, she spent hours each evening scrolling through a puzzle app on
her phone, then playing solitaire on the computer until her eyes burned. The games kept her mind
occupied, kept the intrusive fragments at bay. If a quiet moment found her, she felt an impending
dread that she could not name, so she learned to fill every empty pause with activity. She had
stopped watching the news years ago, stopped attending the hospital’s holiday parties, stopped
calling friends who might ask how she was really doing.

What finally brought Margaret to a therapist’s office was a visit from her daughter, who had found
her mother crying in the kitchen over a pair of old shoes she had pulled from the back of the
closet. Margaret could not explain why she was crying—she could not put the sensation into words—but
she had looked at the brown leather and felt the shame rise like hot water, and for once she did not
reach for her phone. Her daughter made the appointment, and Margaret came because she did not know
what else to do. She hoped the therapist might help her find a way to stop watching the door, to sit
in a restaurant without her stomach knotting, to talk about the thing she could not talk about. But
what she feared most was that describing it would make it real, and that she would finally have to
feel what she had been avoiding for forty-two years.
