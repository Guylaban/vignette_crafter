Marcus is twenty-seven years old, single, and drives for a ride-share company in a Midwestern city.
He came to therapy at the urging of his cousin, who found him sitting in his parked car at four in
the morning, staring at the dashboard, after he had refused to take a fare because the passenger was
wearing a wool coat. Marcus could not explain why the sight of that fabric—rough, dark,
familiar—made his chest tighten and his hands go numb. He only knew that he had to get out of the
car.

The memory that haunts him began when he was six years old, living with his father in a small
apartment. His father was a man who believed in discipline delivered with a leather belt, and he had
a specific way of approaching: slow, deliberate footsteps that Marcus learned to recognize from the
kitchen, from the hallway, from anywhere in the house. The steps would stop behind him. Then the
belt would come down across his back, his thighs, his arms. The leather was cold at first—the
temperature of the room—but after a few swings it grew warm from friction and from Marcus’s own skin
heat. He learned to brace for that particular warmth, the way it preceded the sting. He learned to
hate the feel of the belt’s surface and the sound of footsteps that meant his father was done
waiting.

Now, twenty-one years later, Marcus lives inside those sensations. The rough texture of a wool
sleeve, the weight of a footstep too close behind him, the sudden warmth of a summer breeze on his
arm—any of these can drop him straight back into the living room of his childhood. He has become
hypervigilant in ways that exhaust him. While driving, he constantly checks his rearview mirror,
scanning for passengers who might lean forward too fast, for pedestrians who walk too near his door.
He takes routes through high-crime neighborhoods at night not because they are faster, but because
he tells himself he needs to stay sharp. The risky behavior—speeding through yellow lights, driving
with a broken taillight, picking up riders in areas other drivers avoid—feels almost necessary, as
though being reckless keeps him one step ahead of a danger he cannot name. Even when a passenger
tells him, “You’re safe, man, just relax,” Marcus cannot believe it. Safety, for him, is an abstract
word that has never matched what his body knows.

His body does not trust him. It reacts before his mind catches up: sweat on his palms, a racing
heart, a freeze that makes him grip the steering wheel until his knuckles whiten. Marcus has come to
believe that his body is his enemy, that it has permanently changed for the worse, that he is broken
beyond repair. He avoids emotional closeness entirely. The few women he has dated ended things
because he would not let them stay the night, would not let them touch his back, would not let them
walk up behind him. He could not explain why. Instead he uses distraction—loud podcasts, constant
phone scrolling, accepting back-to-back fares that keep him driving for ten hours straight. And when
that fails, he smokes marijuana in his car between rides, telling himself it is the only way to stop
the replay.

The cost of these strategies has been steep. He has lost touch with his mother’s side of the family,
who live in a different state and who used to hold large gatherings every summer. He stopped going
after his uncle accidentally brushed the back of his neck with a cold hand. He has no community, no
church, no sense of belonging to the culture his parents came from. The isolation feels permanent,
like a wall he built so long ago he cannot remember what was on the other side.

What finally brought Marcus to therapy was an accident. Last month, while driving a fare, a
passenger dropped his phone on the floor. The sound—a sharp, close thud that Marcus heard as
footsteps behind his seat—sent him into a flashback. He swerved, hit a curb, and ended up with a
cracked rim and a panic attack that lasted forty minutes. He sat in the parking lot afterward,
trembling, and called his cousin. Now he sits across from me, saying he does not know if talking can
help, but he knows he cannot keep driving. He is most afraid that if he stops the hypervigilance,
the danger will finally catch up. And he is hoping—barely—that maybe his body can learn a different
way to feel safe.
