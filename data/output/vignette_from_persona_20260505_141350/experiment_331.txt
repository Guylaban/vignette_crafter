Marguerite, a seventy-three-year-old painter who had spent four decades turning light and shadow
into landscapes, now spent most of her days in her second-floor apartment with the curtains drawn.
She had moved there six months after the boardwalk collapse, leaving the studio where she had worked
since before her husband died. She hadn’t painted since. The event itself she could still see with a
clarity that terrified her: the weekend arts fair, the wooden boardwalk groaning, then snapping, a
sound she had never heard before and could not forget — not splintering but tearing, like a giant
sheet of canvas ripping in half. She was thrown forward, her hands hitting the planks hard, and in
the next moment people were falling on top of her, crushing her into the slats. Someone’s elbow
pressed against her ribs. A man’s breath, sour with alcohol, was so close to her face she could
taste it. She had lain there for what felt like hours, unable to move, her cheek against the wet
wood, thinking she was going to be buried alive. When rescuers finally pulled her out, their hands
were firm and hurried, and every touch — on her arm, her shoulder, her hip — felt like another
injury, another invasion. She had not broken anything, but something inside her had bent
permanently.

In the weeks afterward, Marguerite found she could not cry, and that worried her. The first time she
did cry was when a news report about a bridge collapse came on the radio in her kitchen. She turned
it off so fast she broke the knob. After that, she stopped listening to the news entirely. She
cancelled her newspaper subscription. When a friend called to talk about current events, Marguerite
made an excuse and hung up quickly. She told herself it was better not to know. But she could not
stop the memories. They arrived without warning, and they arrived as photographs — crisp, silent,
full-frame. She could see the grain of the wood, the blue sneaker of the woman next to her, the
exact angle of the sunlight on the splintered railing. And when the photograph came, her body
responded as though it had never left that position. Her arms would press against her sides, her
cheek would tilt down as if she were still pinned to the boardwalk, and she would feel the guilt
wash over her — a thick, suffocating guilt that she had done nothing, that she had survived while
others did not, that she had not even screamed for help. She was certain her emotions had been
permanently damaged. She could not remember the last time she felt joy, or even simple contentment.
What was the point of painting if you could not feel? She had begun to wonder, in the darker hours
of the night, whether her life was worth living anymore, though she never said it aloud.

Marguerite developed routines to keep the memories at bay. She kept the television off and played
classical music through headphones instead, because silence allowed her mind to drift toward the
disaster. She tried hard not to think about it, pushing the images away the moment they surfaced,
but the effort exhausted her. At night, she lay awake listening. Every creak of the old building was
a footstep. Every clank in the pipes was someone turning a doorknob. She would lie rigid, straining
to hear, certain that someone was trying to enter. She became irritable in ways she had never been
before. When a neighbor knocked on her door to ask about a water leak, Marguerite snapped at her,
told her to call the super, and slammed the door. She felt terrible afterward but did not apologize.
She started avoiding people altogether. She stopped going to the grocery store at peak hours. She
did not answer the phone. The only place she still visited regularly was the corner pharmacy, and
even there she kept her head down and her purchases quick.

Her work as a painter had always been driven by a sense that the world was full of meaning and
order. She believed in composition, in balance, in the way a single stroke could hold the light.
That faith had been destroyed. The world now felt random and dangerous, a place where a perfectly
sunny afternoon could turn into a pile of broken bodies. She had no desire to paint it. Several
months ago, a gallery owner who had represented her for years called to ask if she had any new
pieces. Marguerite said she had nothing, and she could hear the concern in the woman’s voice. But
Marguerite did not know how to explain that she could no longer hold a brush without her hand
starting to shake, without the memory of the boardwalk pressing into her ribs. She had tried mental
distraction — crossword puzzles, sudoku, even online jigsaw puzzles — anything to keep her mind
occupied. It worked for a few minutes, but the moment she stopped, the memories returned.

What finally brought Marguerite to the clinic was a night she spent sitting in her kitchen at three
in the morning, the lights on full blast, her chair pushed against the door, a kitchen knife in her
lap because she was certain she had heard the lock click. No one came. She was trembling and could
not stop. She thought about calling her daughter, who lived in another state, but she did not want
to frighten her. Instead, she called the helpline number from a brochure the pharmacy had left by
the register. She made an appointment the next day. What she hoped for was something small — maybe a
way to sleep without listening, maybe a way to walk past the liquor store on the corner without
catching the smell of whiskey and feeling the weight of the man’s breath on her face. What she was
most afraid of was that she would be told she had to talk about it, to describe the photographs out
loud, because she had not said a single word about that day to anyone since it happened, and she was
not sure she could start.
