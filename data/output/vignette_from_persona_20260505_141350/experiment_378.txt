Robert, a 66-year-old software engineer, had spent most of his career writing code in quiet offices,
but the silence of his home office now felt like a fragile truce. He could not remember a time when
his body was not on alert. The memory that anchored his vigilance came from a winter evening when he
was seven: his father’s hand closing on his shoulder, the cold draft from the open window slicing
across his bare skin a moment before the belt came down. The sensation of that chill on his skin,
the sudden pounding in his chest, had become a permanent recording. Even now, decades later, the
feeling of a particular cool draft against his arm could make him flinch before his mind caught up,
or his own heart thumping after a brisk walk could trigger a visceral flash of that small boy
waiting for the next blow.

He had learned early that safety meant scanning. In any room, his eyes would automatically catalog
objects that could be used as weapons—a pair of scissors on the desk, a heavy glass paperweight, a
kitchen knife left by the sink. He knew it was irrational, but the scanning was automatic, a habit
so ingrained it felt like part of his vision. His body often jumped before his thoughts did. If his
wife touched his shoulder from behind, he would jerk away with a sharp breath, his heart already
racing, his hands raised before he registered it was only her. He hated that reaction almost as much
as the apologetic look that followed.

For years, Robert had managed his life by keeping people at a distance. He had a handful of old
colleagues from his early days at the firm, men he used to have lunch with, but he had stopped
accepting invitations decades ago. Seeing them meant small talk, and small talk meant the risk of a
handshake or a clap on the back. He could not bear physical contact—not a handshake, not a pat, not
even a friendly arm around the shoulder. The sensation of another person’s skin against his felt
like an intrusion that reignited that cold draft. He also avoided looking at his own body in the
mirror, especially his back. His father’s marks had faded, but the scars were still there, and
seeing them brought the memory too close. He showered quickly, in dim light, and dressed without
pausing to inspect.

His wife, Karen, had learned over the years not to startle him, though she sometimes forgot. She had
also learned not to ask too many questions about why he flinched. But recently, she had noticed he
was pulling away even from her. She had suggested couples therapy, then gently said he should see
someone on his own. Robert agreed because he was tired. Tired of the constant scanning, tired of
feeling like an outsider in his own family. At holiday gatherings with Karen’s relatives, he would
stand by the wall, watching everyone laugh and embrace, certain that he did not belong. He had come
to believe that people could not be trusted—not because they were malicious, but because trust
required a vulnerability he could not afford. Being careful had kept him alive as a child; now it
was keeping him isolated.

What finally brought him to therapy was a single incident. A few weeks ago, Karen reached out to
steady him when he stumbled on the stairs, and he had shoved her back with a shout. The look on her
face—hurt, afraid—stayed with him. He was terrified that he might do it again, that his body would
act before his mind could catch up. He wanted to stop living like a soldier still waiting for an
attack that ended sixty years ago, but he had no idea how to lower his guard. He hoped, more than
anything, that someone could show him how to be in a room without first checking the exits.
