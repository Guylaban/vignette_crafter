Amara Obi is an 80-year-old policy analyst, widowed for the past twelve years, who lives alone in a
quiet condominium complex on the outskirts of a Midwestern city. For decades she had been known
among her colleagues as a meticulous researcher who could turn a dense set of legislative proposals
into a clear, actionable brief. But in the months since the collision, she has become a woman who
startles at the sound of a car horn, who avoids entire neighborhoods, and who can no longer bear the
smell of her own cooking if it involves anything that might singe.

The accident occurred on a Tuesday evening in early spring. Amara had been driving home from a
public hearing at city hall, her briefcase on the passenger seat, when a sedan ran a red light and
struck her driver’s-side door at nearly forty miles per hour. The other driver, as the police report
later noted, had a blood alcohol level nearly three times the legal limit. What Amara remembers of
that moment is fragmented and strange: the blinding white of the other car’s headlights, but not the
impact itself; the sound of tearing metal, but not the spinning that must have followed; the sudden,
shocking cold of the shattered window against her cheek, a cold so sharp it felt like a burn. She
remembers the smell of alcohol wafting through the crumpled frame of the other vehicle—a sickly
sweet odor that seemed to fill her own car—and, afterward, the acrid burnt smell of the deployed
airbag, which clung to her clothes and hair for days. But there are entire seconds, perhaps minutes,
that remain blank: how she ended up with her forearm pressed against the dashboard, who opened her
door, when the ambulance arrived. Each time she tries to push past those gaps, her mind slams shut
as if before a locked door, and she finds herself pushing the memory away before she can complete
it.

In the weeks that followed, Amara did not consciously decide to change her life. She simply found
that certain things had become unbearable. The first was temperature in the car: even on a mild
spring day, the air from the vent felt like that first cold shock against her skin, and she began to
take buses instead, though the crowded stops made her chest tighten. Then she noticed that any whiff
of alcohol—a hand sanitizer outside the pharmacy, a glass of wine at a colleague’s farewell
gathering—brought the whole scene back without warning: the headlights, the spinning, the smell. At
those moments she would freeze, her heart pounding, as if the crash were happening again right there
in the doorway of the drugstore. The burnt smell was even worse. She used to enjoy grilling chicken
on her tiny balcony; now she avoids the kitchen when toast is made, and she has stopped visiting the
coffee shop near her old office because the barista sometimes scorches the espresso.

The sudden movements of other people began to terrify her. At the bus stop, if a young man shifted
his weight abruptly or a child darted past, Amara would go rigid, her hands flying up in a defensive
gesture that embarrassed her. She came to believe that other people were dangerous in a way she had
never before considered—that their carelessness, their unexpected motions, could bring harm out of
nowhere. She started to stay at home more, telling herself that the world outside had become too
unpredictable. Her daughter, who lives three states away, called weekly and worried aloud; Amara
assured her she was “just getting old,” but she knew it was more than that. She had not attended a
policy meeting in four months. The last one was held in a conference room near a fire station, and
the faint burnt odor that drifted through the ventilation system had sent her into a full flashback:
she was back in the driver’s seat, the airbag pressed against her face, the alcohol smell mixing
with the hot, chemical scent of the deployed safety device. She had excused herself to the restroom
and never returned.

Now Amara sits in her living room with the curtains drawn, because sunlight through the blinds
sometimes creates a flicker that reminds her of headlights. She rarely leaves the apartment except
for early-morning grocery trips, when the streets are quiet and no one moves suddenly. What finally
brought her to seek help was a moment of recognition: last week, her hand shook so badly while
unlocking her front door that she dropped the keys, and for a long moment she could not bend down to
pick them up, paralyzed by the thought that bending would leave her vulnerable, that someone might
come around the corner and make a sudden movement she could not predict. She called her daughter
that evening and agreed to see a therapist. She is afraid—afraid that talking about the crash will
make the blanks fill in, afraid that the memories she has pushed away will overwhelm her. But she is
also, in a quiet way she does not yet voice aloud, hoping that perhaps she can again walk into a
coffee shop without smelling disaster on the air.
