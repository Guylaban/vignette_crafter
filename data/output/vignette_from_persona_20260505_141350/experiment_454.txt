he had grabbed her—a pain that throbbed for weeks. The police were called, but the man disappeared
before they arrived, and the case eventually stalled for lack of evidence.

fear through her chest, and she would immediately look away, her palms damp, her throat tight. At
work, she dreaded entering homes where she knew the male resident would be present. She started
avoiding houses where she smelled alcohol on a client’s breath or saw empty bottles in recycling
bins; the odour alone made her stomach turn and brought back the feel of being trapped. Physical
pain also became a trigger. The chronic ache in her shoulder from the assault had faded, but any new
pain—a stubbed toe, a pulled muscle—would instantly flood her with the same helpless, trapped
sensation. She had come to believe that pain meant vulnerability, that it signalled a moment when
she could not protect herself.

Priya began to withdraw from her friends. She feared that if she spent time with them, something
terrible would happen—that the man would find her through them, or that they would be hurt because

husband, trying to anticipate every possible threat or misunderstanding. At home, she checked the
locks on the doors and windows repeatedly, sometimes three or four times before she could settle

it, the memory fragmented, and she found herself pushing it away before the full image could form.

rushing back in a sensory wave she could not control.

Her husband noticed the changes. She had stopped inviting friends over, stopped laughing, started
sleeping with a knife under the pillow. He asked what was wrong, but she could only say, “I just
need you to be safe.” She worried obsessively that her husband would be in a car accident, that her
elderly mother would have a fall, that any kindness she received would be followed by tragedy. The
rehearsed conversations grew longer, more elaborate, as if she could script her life into safety.

many times, and one client complained that she seemed “jumpy” and uncomfortable around male family
members. Priya said nothing; she simply avoided that client’s home.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning when she found herself unable to leave the

that one day the taste would stay for good.
