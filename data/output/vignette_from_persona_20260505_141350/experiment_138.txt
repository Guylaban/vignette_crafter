Mai was thirty-one years old, a journalist for a mid-size city paper, and had been divorced for
eighteen months. She had not spoken of her mother’s death to anyone at work, not even when her
editor asked why she kept turning down assignments that involved highway driving or hospital visits.
The truth was that she could not drive past the intersection where it had happened without her hands
turning cold on the wheel, without the image of the overturned sedan flickering behind her eyes like
a frame stuck in a projector. Her mother had been on her way home from a night grocery run, hit by a
driver who ran a red light. Mai had been the one to identify the body, because her father could not
do it. That night, she had stood in the fluorescent morgue corridor in her work slacks, still
holding her press badge, and had seen her mother’s face through a slot of glass. What stayed with
her was not only the sight—the disfigurement that made recognition nearly impossible—but the sound
of her own voice saying yes, that is her, and the way the technician had nodded and slid the drawer
shut. She had not cried then. She had not cried for weeks.

In the months that followed, Mai found that she could not sit still. Even in her own apartment, with
the door locked and the windows closed, her muscles stayed braced as though something were about to
crash through the wall. She had stopped watching television because the sudden volume shifts made
her flinch, and she had stopped listening to podcasts on her commute because she needed to hear
every car engine behind her, every siren in the distance, every screech of brakes. She was certain
that if she stopped scanning, if she let herself relax even once, she would be caught off guard when
the next terrible thing happened—and she was equally certain that the next terrible thing was
coming. At work, she kept her desk angled so she could see the newsroom door. Any time someone
entered abruptly, her stomach dropped and her pulse slammed up into her throat. She apologized for
the jumpiness, laughed it off, but inside she felt a wash of shame that was so sharp it made her
short of breath. She told herself she was being irrational, but that knowledge did nothing. The fear
was a physical fact, as real as the weight of her own bones.

The divorce had happened before the trauma, but afterward Mai’s husband had told her she was “not
the same person.” He meant it as an observation, not a complaint, but she heard the accusation
anyway. She had stopped wanting to go out, stopped wanting to talk about anything that mattered,
stopped being able to sleep next to him without jolting awake every time he turned over. She felt a
constant low-grade anger at him for being alive, for being safe, for not understanding why she now
hated the smell of diesel and the sound of a phone ringing after ten at night. She also felt a bone-
deep guilt—if she had asked her mother to stay longer that evening, if she had offered to pick her
up, if she had not been so absorbed in a deadline—then perhaps the timing would have shifted by
seconds and the car would have passed through the intersection safely. That thought played on a
loop, and she could not stop it. She hated herself for the thought, and she hated the dead driver
for being drunk, and she hated the paramedic who had not been fast enough, and she hated the way her
father now looked at her with a kind of fragile gratitude that only made her feel more like a fraud.

Two weeks before she finally made an appointment at a trauma clinic, Mai had been sent to cover a
city council meeting about road safety. The meeting room was on the second floor of a municipal
building directly across from the intersection where her mother had died. She had stood at the
window during a break, looking down at the traffic lights, watching cars accelerate through the
yellow. Her hands shook. Her jaw tight. The council member next to her asked if she was all right,
and Mai said she was fine, but she was not. She left early, filed a perfunctory article, and then
sat in her car in the parking lot for forty minutes, unable to start the engine because she was too
vigilant to every sound outside. She finally drove home on surface streets, taking a route three
miles longer, and cried in the bathroom when she got there. That was the moment she realized she
could not keep living like this. She was afraid that the fear would never stop, that the guilt would
calcify into something permanent, that she would never again know what it felt like to have her body
be still. She wanted to be able to read a book without her eyes flicking toward the window every few
seconds. She wanted to be able to hear a car backfire and not instantly picture a funeral. She
wanted to stop being pulled back to that glass slot in the morgue every time she closed her eyes.
She did not know if therapy could give her those things, but she had started to think that if she
did not try, the next terrible thing would not be an accident—it would be the slow erasure of
everything she used to be.
