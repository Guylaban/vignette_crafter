Megan was nineteen, but she felt as though the person she had been before had died two years ago, on
a damp October night when a neighbor’s party had spilled out onto the porch and a man she vaguely
knew had cornered her behind a shed. The air had been thick with the smell of beer from his breath
and something metallic from the rusted fence, and the only sound she clearly remembered was the
sudden roar of a motorcycle accelerating down the alley, followed by his voice shouting in Arabic at
someone else before he turned back to her. She had tried to push him away, but her arms had felt
like wet paper. Afterward, she had walked home in a daze and told no one, not even her then-
boyfriend, because she was certain a stronger person would have fought harder, would have screamed
loud enough to make someone come. Somebody else would have stopped it.

The months that followed were a slow, quiet unravelling. Megan stopped going out after dusk, telling
her husband—they had married six months ago, partly because she craved the safety of a locked door
and someone else in the bed—that she just preferred to stay in. She avoided checking her phone on
the anniversary of the assault, which fell on a Friday that year, and instead spent the day in bed
with the blinds drawn. The worst thing, though, was what happened inside her own skin. She found
herself unable to look at her body in the mirror. When she showered, she kept her eyes closed. The
feel of her own hands on her arms made her flinch, because for a split second she would remember the
weight of his palms, and she would stop washing altogether.

Her husband worked nights at a warehouse, and Megan spent those hours sitting rigidly on the couch,
listening. Every creak of the house became a footstep. Every scratch of a tree branch against the
window was someone trying the lock. She had developed a habit of glancing behind her whenever she
walked from the bedroom to the kitchen, even in her own home, and when she ventured outside for
groceries in the late afternoon she would check over her shoulder every few steps, convinced someone
was tracking her. The worst moments came without warning—a sudden shift in a store aisle, the rumble
of a delivery truck, and she would be back in that damp backyard, feeling the cold wall against her
back, smelling the beer and the rust, hearing the harsh Arabic syllables that still rang in her ears
even now, unbidden, when she tried to sleep. She had told her husband about the assault only a month
ago, in fragments, and he had held her and said nothing could hurt her now, but Megan no longer
believed in safety. The world was a place where things happened and no one stopped them.

By the time she came to see a therapist, she was unemployed—she had quit her cashier job after three
panic attacks in one week, triggered each time by a customer who smelled of alcohol—and she was
spending most of her days at home, hypervigilant, replaying the event in sensory flashes she could
not control. She said she felt hollow, as though the assault had scooped out everything that had
once defined her: her easy laugh, her trust in strangers, her belief that bad things happened to
other people. She was terrified that she would never feel capable again, that she was broken in a
way that could not be fixed. But she also said, in a voice so quiet it was almost a whisper, that
she wanted to stop being afraid of her own body, of the dark, of the sound of a motorcycle. She
wanted to believe that the world might one day feel safe again, even though she could not imagine
how that would ever be possible.
