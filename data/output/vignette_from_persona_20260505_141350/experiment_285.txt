Jenna is a 35-year-old actor living in a midsize city, single, with a career built on character work
and stage presence. Two years ago, she was waiting for a late-night bus after a rehearsal when a man
in a dark hoodie opened fire on the crowd at the stop. She was not struck, but she watched a woman
fall beside her, saw the uniformed security guard draw his weapon too late, heard the screams and
the wet sound of bodies hitting concrete. The shooter disappeared into the tunnel; no one was ever
arrested. Jenna remembers fragments: the guard's sidearm, the flash of a police badge on a
bystander's belt, the squeal of the bus brakes as it arrived seconds after the shooting stopped. But
she cannot sequence these pieces into a story she can tell aloud. When she tries to describe what
happened, the words turn into static, or she feels the cold metal of the bench against her back and
the smell of diesel fuel, as if the event is happening again in the room. She has learned to stop
trying.

In the months that followed, Jenna's world contracted. She stopped taking the bus and subway
entirely. Driving became her only option, even when it meant circling for parking or paying for
expensive rideshares. She found herself scanning every uniform—police, security, military—with a
surge of adrenaline that left her hands shaking. At auditions, if a script called for a police
officer or a soldier, she would politely decline, saying the role did not fit. The casting directors
began to see her as difficult. She started choosing seats in restaurants, theaters, even in the
waiting rooms of her own appointments, based on whether she could see all exits and keep her back to
a wall. She told herself it was just being cautious, but she could not sit any other way without a
rising tide of panic.

The feeling of being permanently outside other people's lives grew slowly. Jenna would watch friends
laugh over dinner and think they could not possibly understand what she carried. The shooter was
never held accountable—the investigation went nowhere—and that fact lodged in her chest like a
stone. She became certain that the world was fundamentally unsafe, and that anyone who said
otherwise was naive. To quiet the constant, grinding fear and shame, she began spending impulsively.
A new coat, dinner at an expensive restaurant, online purchases she barely remembered making. The
brief lift of buying something—the click of confirmation, the promise of a package—gave her minutes
of relief before the dread returned, heavier than before.

Her acting work suffered. She could not access the emotional range needed for vulnerable roles
because her own emotions felt like live wires. She turned down a recurring part in a crime drama
because the set would be near a police station. Her agent expressed frustration; Jenna could not
explain. She began to feel that the industry—the world—was moving on while she was stuck in that
bus-stop moment, replaying fragments she could not assemble into a coherent memory, let alone a
life.

What finally brought her to seek help was not the avoidance or the spending, but a night when she
found herself sitting in a corner of her apartment, clutching a throw pillow like a shield, unable
to name what she was afraid of. She had been invited to a friend's birthday at a restaurant near a
transit hub, and she knew she would say no. The isolation was now so complete that she could not
remember the last conversation in which she had spoken freely. Jenna wants to stop being a spectator
in her own life. She is terrified that the therapy will force her to put the event into words—words
she does not have. But she is more afraid of staying this way.
