Leila is a 31-year-old social media manager who lives alone in a one-bedroom apartment in a midsized
city. She came to therapy six months after the accident, though she had been telling herself she was
fine for most of that time. The accident happened in late March, on a Tuesday evening. Leila had
been working late, monitoring engagement metrics on a client’s campaign, and she was rushing home
because her ex-husband was supposed to drop off some of her things. She took a shortcut through a
residential street she rarely used. Her phone buzzed with a notification—a brand alert from the
client—and she glanced down just long enough to read the subject line. When she looked up, a man was
crossing the street in front of her car, not at a crosswalk, but close enough that she should have
seen him. She hit the brake, but too late. The impact was a single, flat thud, and then the terrible
silence of the engine idling while she sat frozen behind the wheel. The man—his name was Marcus, she
would learn later—was thrown onto the hood and then onto the pavement. He survived, but with a
traumatic brain injury that left him with permanent cognitive deficits. Leila’s blood alcohol was
zero, her phone records showed she was not texting, but the police report noted the glancing-at-a-
notification as a contributing factor. She was not charged.

In the weeks that followed, everything changed. Leila could not stop smelling the inside of her car
after the accident: a mix of hot metal, the faint sweetness of spilled coffee from the cup holder,
and something metallic and organic that she later realized might have been blood, though she never
saw any. That smell returned without warning—when she opened a can of soda, when she walked past a
body shop, sometimes for no reason at all. She began to dread being touched unexpectedly. A
colleague patted her shoulder to get her attention and she flinched so hard she knocked a cup of
water across her desk. She started sitting with her back to the wall in meetings, and she avoided
elevators and crowded hallways at work because people pressed too close. At home, she found herself
jumping at the sound of her own cat brushing against her leg.

Her job became a minefield. As a social media manager, she was required to monitor comments, respond
to brand mentions, and schedule posts. But every time her phone buzzed with a notification—the same
sound that had preceded the accident—her heart slammed into her throat and she felt a surge of pure
alarm, even though she knew it was just a client request. She started muting notifications entirely
and checking her phone only at designated times, but that made her miss real-time engagement
opportunities, and her supervisor noticed. More than once, she reacted as if she were about to be
attacked when nothing was wrong: rising from her desk, scanning the room, hands shaking. Her
colleagues began to whisper about how jumpy she had become. She stopped going out for lunch with
them, preferring to eat at her desk with headphones on.

The worst part, she told herself, was how hollow she felt when she tried to remember the accident.
Sometimes she could recall the moment in perfect, sensory detail—the sound of the impact, the smell,
the way Marcus’s body seemed to bounce—but she felt nothing. She described it as watching a movie of
someone else’s life. That scared her more than the guilt, because it made her wonder if she was a
sociopath. Other times, she knew exactly what had happened, but when she tried to tell anyone about
it, the words would not come. She would open her mouth to describe the accident and end up sitting
in silence, her mind blank, as if the language had been erased. She stopped talking about it
altogether.

The divorce had already been finalized before the accident, but Leila had been on reasonably good
terms with her ex-husband. After the accident, she withdrew completely. She cancelled plans with
friends, stopped responding to texts, and spent her evenings in the dark scrolling through the
social media feeds of people who seemed untouched by anything terrible. She believed she could no
longer trust herself to make good decisions—she had looked at a phone for two seconds and ruined a
man’s life. What kind of person did that? The person she used to be—confident, quick to laugh,
decisive—felt like a stranger. She looked in the mirror and saw someone else, someone who had
permanently damaged not only Marcus but also the future she might have had. She was certain she had
changed for the worse, that this was now her real self: a woman who could not be trusted behind a
wheel or a phone, who needed to be careful every second, who could never be forgiven.

What finally brought Leila to therapy was not the guilt or the numbness or the startle, but the
smell. Three nights before her first appointment, she was chopping an onion for dinner and the scent
triggered such a vivid, involuntary flashback that she dropped the knife and stood in the middle of
her kitchen, sobbing, with no idea why. She realised she could not keep living this way, that her
life had become unrecognisable. She hoped, vaguely, that therapy might give her back the version of
herself she lost on that Tuesday in March. But she also feared, privately, that no amount of talking
could undo what she had done, and that the person she had become was all she would ever be.
