Margaret is a 72-year-old network engineer who still consults part-time from home, though she has
not set foot in an office in nearly three years. She is married to Frank, a retired high school
teacher, and they have lived in the same suburban house outside Dallas for four decades. The
traumatic event that reshaped her inner world happened on a Tuesday afternoon in late August 2021,
when she was waiting for a bus on her way to meet a former colleague for lunch. As the bus pulled
up, a man in a dark hoodie walked past her and, without a word, raised a handgun and fired three
shots into the crowd near the bus doors. Margaret was not hit—she was standing five feet to the
side—but she saw a woman in a floral dress fall, saw the blood spread across the pavement before she
turned and ran. The shooter fled; the woman survived after weeks in intensive care. But Margaret has
never been the same.

In the months afterward, she could not bring herself to walk past that bus stop. She took a
different route to the grocery store, a looping detour that added fifteen minutes each way. She
stopped riding the bus altogether, telling herself it was simply more practical to drive, even
though the bus had been her primary mode of transport for years. Crowded places became unbearable: a
Saturday trip to the shopping centre sent her heart pounding so hard she had to sit down on a bench
near the entrance, and she left without buying anything. She began to avoid certain conversation
topics at family dinners—talk about street violence, about news reports of shootings, even about
safety improvements in the city. When her son mentioned a block-watch meeting, she changed the
subject so abruptly that Frank gave her a long, searching look. She told herself she was just being
sensible. “I force myself to think only positive thoughts,” she would repeat internally whenever an
image of that afternoon surfaced. She would deliberately turn her mind to a crossword puzzle or a
recipe, trying to overwrite the memory with something neutral.

But the positive reframing never stuck for long. Margaret had come to believe that the world was
fundamentally dangerous now, in a way she had not understood before. She found herself scanning
every parking lot before she got out of the car, checking the faces of people who walked too close.
She became certain that she had to be especially careful because you never knew what could happen
next. She started to feel like an outsider in her own life—at the weekly bridge club, she sat in
silence while the others joked about their grandchildren and travel plans, because she could not
shake the sense that they were living in a different, safer reality. She was permanently apart from
them now, marked by something they could not see.

Over the next two years, the avoidance hardened into routine. She stopped accepting invitations to
parties or public events, and Frank began going to their daughter’s birthday dinners alone. Margaret
knew this upset him—he had started sleeping in the guest room—but she could not make herself go. The
bus stop she used to pass every day remained a blank spot on her mental map; she would rather drive
forty-five minutes in traffic than risk a glimpse of that corner. She told herself it was simply
good sense.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a minor crisis: her network engineering contract required
