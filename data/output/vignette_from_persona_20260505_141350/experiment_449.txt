Kai is a 27-year-old special education teacher who works in a public elementary school, supporting
children with behavioral and emotional challenges. They live with their partner, Sam, in a small
apartment near the coast. The trauma that fractured their sense of safety began about fourteen
months ago, when a former student’s parent started appearing at the school parking lot, waiting by
Kai’s car. At first it was just a few times, and Kai tried to dismiss it as coincidence. But then
the parent began following them after work—driving slowly behind Kai’s car for several blocks,
pulling over when Kai pulled over, and once, standing at the far end of their street just before
dusk. The worst moment came on a Tuesday evening in late fall. Kai had stayed late to finish lesson
plans. As they walked to the car, they heard footsteps—steady, deliberate, behind them but never
close enough to see a face. They quickened their pace; the footsteps quickened too. When they
finally turned around, the parent was standing motionless under a broken streetlight, just watching.
Kai ran to the car, locked the doors, and drove home shaking. The police were called, a no-contact
order was filed, and the parent eventually stopped appearing. But for Kai, the world never felt the
same.

In the weeks that followed, Kai’s memory of that evening refused to settle into a coherent story.
Some parts were hyper-clear—the sound of gravel underfoot, the orange glow of the streetlight, the
weight of the car key pressed between their fingers. Other parts were gaps, like a film with frames
cut out. They could not remember unlocking the car door, only that they were suddenly inside it.
They could not recall what the parent had been wearing, only the stillness of that silhouette. The
memory came in disconnected fragments that did not fit together, leaving Kai with an incomplete
picture that felt more unsettling than full recall would have been. They began to wonder if they had
missed something, if there was a warning sign they should have caught. Night after night, Kai lay
awake mentally replaying the weeks before the incident, scanning every interaction with the parent
for clues of what they could have done differently—a different route home, a different time to
leave, a different parking spot. This rumination became a nightly ritual, a way of trying to solve a
puzzle that had no solution.

Day by day, Kai’s body remained on alert. At work, in the classroom, they found themselves scanning
the hallway through the door’s small window, checking the faces of every adult who passed. The sound
of footsteps in the corridor, even the brisk steps of a colleague, made Kai’s stomach drop. They had
learned to position their desk so they could see the door at all times, and they left the classroom
blinds open so no movement outside went unseen. At home, they could not relax. Sitting on the couch
felt dangerous; closing their eyes in the shower felt like inviting a threat. Kai had come to
believe that if they let their guard down for even a moment, something bad would happen again—and
next time, they might not get away. So they kept moving, kept checking, kept listening. They stopped
taking walks after dark, stopped going to the grocery store alone, and began taking a longer, more
open route to work even though it cost them twenty extra minutes. Their partner, Sam, noticed that
Kai flinched whenever Sam came up behind them or made a sudden movement in the kitchen. Kai knew
this was straining their relationship, but they could not stop themselves from reacting; the surge
of adrenaline was automatic, and it felt necessary.

The classroom itself became the most difficult setting. Kai was known for being calm and patient
with their students, but now they found themselves distracted during lessons, their attention pulled
toward the door or the windows. They had started carrying a small pocket-sized whistle on their
keychain, an object of preparation they told no one about. They never used it, but its presence
allowed them to feel slightly less exposed. Last month, a delivery person knocked on the classroom
door and then stepped inside without waiting for a response. The sudden movement—a person entering
the space Kai had been trying so hard to control—made Kai shout, startling the children. They later
apologized, but the incident left them shaken and ashamed. It was that moment—seeing the worried
faces of their students, feeling the loss of composure in the one place they had always felt
competent—that finally pushed Kai to seek help. They made an appointment with a therapist who
specialized in trauma. Now, sitting in the waiting room, Kai is afraid that talking about the
stalking will make the fragments of memory worse, that digging into the gaps will open a hole they
cannot climb out of. But they are also afraid of staying this way: always scanning, always
listening, never able to let go of the feeling that someone is still behind them, just out of sight.
