Minh was forty-six years old, a firefighter in a midwestern city, but for two years in his late
thirties he had worked as a civilian relief coordinator in a conflict zone that had no name he could
say aloud anymore. The memory that held him was not a single story—it was a jumble of images he
could not arrange in order no matter how many times he tried. He knew what had happened, but when he
sat in the therapist’s office and opened his mouth, the words would not come. What he could feel was
a sharp, familiar pain in his left shin, exactly where a piece of shrapnel had caught him as he ran
toward a collapsed school. The pain returned every time the memory surfaced, as if his body were
still there.

The day that undid him began with an explosion. He had been distributing food from the back of a
truck when a mortar round hit a nearby market. The bang—a sound like the world cracking open—sent
him diving behind crates. In the silence that followed, he heard shouts, then something worse: men
laughing. When he peered out, he saw two armed men pulling a woman from a doorway by her hair, while
others rummaged through the supplies he had just unloaded. Later, at the makeshift clinic, a medic
made a crude comment about what had happened to the woman, and Minh had said nothing. He did not
stop them. He did not intervene. He just stood there, holding a bag of rice.

Back home, the world refused to feel safe. Silence itself became a threat: the quiet between calls
at the fire station made his chest tighten, because silence in the war zone had always meant
something terrible was about to follow. He had come to believe that his life was destroyed—not just
damaged, but erased. The man he had been before, the one who believed in helping others, had done
something that went against everything he valued: he had been a witness and done nothing. He now
belonged nowhere. Not with the men at the station, who told jokes he could not laugh at. Not with
his wife, who eventually left after he stopped being able to speak to her at all. Not even with
himself.

To manage the feelings that surged without warning—shame, guilt, a hot anger that could erupt over a
misplaced tool or a rookie’s careless attitude—he developed habits that made sense only to him. When
the images pressed in, he would retreat to the station bathroom and press his knuckles into the wall
until the pain pushed the memory aside. Physical hurting became a release, a way to anchor himself
in the present. At night, lying alone in his apartment, he performed silent mental rituals: counting
backward from a hundred in three languages, repeating the same phrase until the tension in his jaw
eased, because some part of him believed that if he did not do these things, another explosion would
come—not in the world, but inside his own mind. He tried hard not to think about the trauma at all,
but the thoughts slipped through like smoke.

At work, triggers followed him. A firework backfiring at a Fourth of July parade sent him to his
knees behind the engine, heart pounding. A colleague’s offhand joke about “getting raped by the
schedule” made him walk off the rig without a word, trembling. And when he saw people misbehaving—a
driver yelling at a paramedic, a captain bending a rule—the moral violation he felt was so acute it
was physical, as if his own integrity were being ripped open again. The irritability cost him. He
had been on the job for six years, but recently his crew had started giving him space, a careful
distance that only confirmed his belief that he no longer fit.

He came in for help after an incident three weeks ago. A minor argument over who should restock the
hose—a small thing, trivial—and he had slammed a locker door so hard the hinge broke, then locked
himself in the bunk room and cut his forearm with a razor he kept in his bag. The chief told him he
had to see someone, and Minh agreed, not because he thought therapy could fix him—nothing could fix
him—but because he was tired of the silence. What frightened him most was the possibility that the
therapist would ask him to describe what happened, and he would sit there, mouth open, pain in his
leg, and find no words at all. He hoped, barely, that maybe if someone else could speak the story
for him, he might finally understand the order it would not give him on its own.
