Mateo Ramirez was twenty-six when his younger brother Javier was killed, caught in a stray bullet
outside a bodega two blocks from the house they had grown up in. Mateo had been working a double
shift at the county detention center that night, and he learned about it from his wife Elena’s
voicemail at four in the morning, her voice cracking as she told him to come home. He drove there
with his hands shaking on the wheel, certain that if he had not been working, if he had just
answered Javier’s last call an hour earlier, he could have kept him inside, could have been there to
push him out of the way. That thought had not faded. It had only calcified.

In the months afterward, Mateo found himself moving through his days as though he were watching
himself from a distance. During a shift in the housing unit, he would suddenly notice that he could
not feel the floor under his boots, or that the inmates’ voices seemed to come from the far end of a
tunnel. The feeling was worse when he saw a news report about a shooting or heard the word “loss”
spoken by anyone, whether a coworker talking about a family death or a chaplain leading a grief
group inside the prison. He would freeze, his chest tightening, and then he would press his palms
against his face, covering his eyes and mouth until the moment passed. A couple of times he did it
in the control booth, and the officer next to him asked if he was okay. Mateo said he was just
tired.

He had come to believe that happiness was a thing he used to have, like a pair of keys he had
misplaced and would never find again. At home, Elena would try to draw him into a conversation about
the weekend, and he would feel a surge of irritation that had nothing to do with her. He would pick
a fight about something small—the way she left dishes in the sink, the volume of the television—and
then let the argument escalate until she walked away. The heat of the conflict, the sharp back-and-
forth, gave him a few minutes of relief. He could focus on the anger instead of the image of Javier
slumped against the brick wall. But afterward, the emptiness was deeper, and he hated himself for
it.

Anything that reminded him of his brother had become poison. He removed Javier’s photo from his
phone and asked Elena to put away the framed picture from their parents’ house. He stopped driving
past the bodega. When his mother called to talk about Javier’s birthday, Mateo hung up without
saying goodbye and then ignored her calls for a week. The avoidance had a cost: his mother stopped
calling as often, and Elena had started sleeping on the couch three nights out of seven, telling him
she could not keep walking on eggshells. Mateo did not argue. He was certain he had no future, that
he would always be this hollow version of himself, and that he deserved it. He should have protected
Javier. That failure had defined him.

What finally brought Mateo to the clinic was an incident at work. During a routine cell search, an
inmate began weeping after receiving a letter about a family member’s death. Mateo stood in the
doorway and felt the world tilt. He walked out of the unit, sat in the break room with his hands
over his face, and did not return for twenty minutes. His supervisor told him he needed to “sort
himself out” or take leave. Elena had said the same thing, with more tears. Mateo agreed to come
because he was tired of feeling like a stranger in his own life, and because he was afraid that if
he did not change something, he would lose the only person still willing to sit beside him. He did
not believe he could feel happy again, but he was hoping, against the weight of everything, that he
might at least stop feeling like a failure every single day.
