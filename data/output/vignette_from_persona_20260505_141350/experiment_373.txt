Raj was fifty-eight, a fisherman who had worked the waters off the coast for nearly forty years, but
he could no longer remember what it felt like to trust the world. The accident had happened three
years ago, on a rainy December evening. He had been driving his pickup truck home from the harbor,
his wife beside him, when a sedan ran a red light and slammed into the driver's side. The impact
spun the truck into a guardrail. Raj remembered the screech of metal, the shattering of glass, and
then a silence so complete it seemed to swallow everything. He had been pinned in the wreckage,
conscious, watching the dashboard crumple toward his chest, certain he was about to die. His wife
was killed instantly. He survived with a broken pelvis, three cracked ribs, and a deep, unnameable
sense that the world had become a place of hidden traps.

In the months afterward, the silence that had filled the car that night began to follow him. He
noticed it first in his own home, when the refrigerator compressor clicked off and the house went
still. His heart would pound, his palms would sweat, and he would find himself standing in the
kitchen doorway, scanning the room for something he could not name. He came to believe that unusual
quiet meant something dangerous was about to happen—that the calm before the ambush was real, and he
needed to be ready. So he kept the television on all day, even when he was not watching. He played
the radio in the truck, in the workshop, in the bedroom. Noise became his shield.

But no amount of noise could quiet the memory. When he tried to tell his daughter about the crash,
the words would not come. He knew what had happened—the light, the impact, the moment he turned and
saw his wife's face—but his mind went blank, and his mouth filled with pressure, as if language
itself had abandoned him. And when he did not try to talk, the memory came on its own, not as a
story he could observe but as something that overtook him. He would be standing at the counter
filleting fish, and suddenly the truck was there, the impact was happening now, and the terror was
exactly as fresh as it had been three years ago. His hands would shake. He would have to sit down.
He learned to avoid any conversation that mentioned loss—friends talking about someone who had died,
a radio program about grieving families. He would walk away, or change the subject, or simply freeze
until the moment passed.

His work became a minefield. The harbor was full of strangers: dockhands, buyers, other fishermen
whose faces he could not read. He had once been sociable, joking with the crews, but now every
unfamiliar person felt like a potential threat. People are not what they seem, the thought would
come, unbidden, and he would avoid their eyes, keep his answers short, retreat to his boat. On the
boat, he scanned constantly—the horizon, the other vessels, the sky. He felt alert even at anchor,
even inside the small cabin. There was no safe place. He had come to believe that his own body had
betrayed him: it had not protected him during the crash, and now it could not be trusted. Every ache
or flutter made him imagine another catastrophe.

He no longer recognized the man he used to be. The Raj who had laughed easily, who had taken
shortcuts without fear, who had believed that hard work kept you safe—that man was gone. In his
place was someone who flinched at silence, who distrusted everyone, who could not find words for the
most important thing that had ever happened to him. He stopped going to the fish market himself; he
let his son handle the sales. He stopped driving on the road where the accident had occurred, taking
a longer, winding route that added twenty minutes to every trip. The avoidance cost him time and
money, but it felt necessary. His neighbors noticed he had grown distant. His daughter worried that
he was depressed. He told her he was fine.

Last month, during a routine physical, his doctor noticed his blood pressure was dangerously high
and asked about stress. Raj found himself unable to explain. He sat in the examination room, the
words piling up behind his teeth, and finally whispered, "I'm scared all the time." The doctor
referred him to a clinic, and Raj agreed to come, not because he believed talking would help, but
because his daughter had begged him, and because he was tired of feeling like a stranger to himself.
He hoped—though he could not say it aloud—that someone might help him find the words, or at least
make the silence less terrifying.
