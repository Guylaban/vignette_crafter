Raj is a 39-year-old interior designer, married for seven years, living in a suburban home that he
and his wife Neha had spent months renovating. Three years ago, his younger brother Arun died in a
car accident on an interstate highway—a collision that Raj learned about through a phone call from
his brother’s phone, a stranger’s voice asking if he could identify the body. He drove to the
hospital in a state of frozen disbelief, and in the morgue he saw Arun’s face, intact except for a
thin line of dried blood along his hairline, the stillness of his features utterly unlike the
brother who had called him the night before to argue about a movie. That image—the cold fluorescent
light, the way the sheet was pulled back, the silence—became a fixture in Raj’s mind, replaying
without warning.

In the months that followed, Raj tried to resume his life. He threw himself into work, taking on
more residential projects than he could reasonably manage. But the flashbacks would come: a sudden
sense of being back in that room, the smell of antiseptic, the hollow weight in his chest, his hands
going numb as if he were still gripping the hospital railing. He could be standing in a client’s
kitchen, discussing tile grout, and in a fraction of a second the world would tilt and he would feel
the terror all over again, his body reacting before his mind understood why—his heart hammering, his
palms slick, a wave of heat climbing his neck. He learned to excuse himself to the bathroom,
splashing cold water on his face, but the irritability that followed was harder to hide. A client’s
indecision over cabinet handles could set off a sharp, sarcastic remark that left everyone in the
room uncomfortable; Neha’s innocent question about dinner plans could trigger a snapping response
that he regretted immediately.

He began to avoid anything that might remind him of Arun. The anniversary of the accident—the third
one was approaching—became a date he would mentally block out well in advance, scheduling two full
days of back-to-back site visits so he would be too exhausted to think. He stopped attending
funerals, even for the fathers of close friends, making excuses about work conflicts until people
stopped inviting him. When a colleague mentioned her mother’s recent death, Raj felt his throat
close and he changed the subject abruptly, feigning a phone call. He removed all the photos of Arun
from his phone and from their apartment; he told Neha it was time to redecorate the living room,
replacing the family portraits with abstract prints. He stopped visiting the park where he and Arun
used to jog, and he drove an extra fifteen minutes to avoid the street where his brother had lived.

But the avoidance came at a cost. Raj had developed a compulsive ritual of mentally rehearsing the
worst-case scenario for any situation. Before every client meeting, he would imagine the project
failing, the client suing, his reputation collapsing. Before Neha left for work, he would picture
her car rolling on that same interstate, and he would stand at the door listening until he heard the
engine fade, only then allowing himself to breathe. He was certain that if he stopped preparing for
the worst, the worst would happen—that his vigilance was the only thing keeping disaster at bay. Yet
the preparation never made him feel prepared; it only left him wired and exhausted, sleeping poorly
and waking with his jaw clenched.

At work, his reputation for fast, creative solutions had eroded. He had declined three commissions
in the past year because the clients mentioned designing spaces for a deceased loved one—a memorial
garden, a reliquary wall, a bedroom to be kept exactly as it was. The last one had sent him into a
panic attack in his car. His boss had started to question his reliability. Neha had begun to
withdraw, hurt by his mood swings and by his refusal to talk about Arun at all. She had suggested
they attend a Diwali celebration at her sister’s house; Raj had snapped that he didn’t want to see
“all those happy families,” and she had left the room in tears.

Three weeks ago, during a home consultation in a client’s sunlit living room, a detail—a particular
shade of blue on the wall, the same color as Arun’s favorite T-shirt—triggered a flashback so vivid
that Raj sat down on the floor, gasping, convinced for a moment that his brother was about to walk
through the door. The client called Neha, and when Raj came home that evening, shattered and
ashamed, she told him she could not keep living this way. He agreed to seek help because he was
afraid of losing her, but what frightened him most, he admitted to the therapist in his first
session, was the idea that the grief would never stop intruding—that he would always be one stray
memory away from falling apart. He hoped, without quite believing it possible, that he could someday
look at a photograph of Arun and feel only love, not the cold rush of the morgue.
