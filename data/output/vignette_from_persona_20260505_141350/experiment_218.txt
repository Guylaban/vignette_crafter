Maya was thirty-six years old, an urban planner who spent her days in a sunlit office studying
zoning maps and pedestrian flow diagrams, but the work that had once felt purposeful now seemed
hollow. Two years earlier she had been a relief worker in a conflict zone, assigned to a field team
that coordinated food distribution in a rural region where front lines shifted unpredictably. For
months the danger was abstract—until the afternoon her convoy was caught in an ambush. Shouting
erupted from all sides, gunfire tore through the vehicle, and she lay pressed into the dirt beside a
collapsed irrigation ditch, the smell of wet earth and smoke filling her lungs while bodies lay
still around her. She remembers fragments: a photograph of a child she had been trying to help, torn
and lying in the mud; the way the sky looked greenish through the haze of burning fields. She never
finished remembering the rest. When she tries to describe what happened to her partner now, the
words scatter. She will start and then stop, her throat closing, the sequence collapsing into
confusion. The memory does not feel like hers. Sometimes she wonders if it happened to someone else,
a woman in a documentary she once watched, and she has no right to claim it.

Back home, Maya threw herself into work, filling every hour with meetings, design reviews, late-
night drafting sessions—anything to keep her mind occupied. But the triggers found her anyway. Old
photographs, even harmless ones from her childhood, set her heart racing. She stopped scrolling
through social media and deleted the albums on her phone. Natural environments were worse. The city
had built a new park along a creek, and during a site visit she saw tall grass and a ditch lined
with reeds; she could not breathe. She now takes the long way around to avoid that route. Shouting
is the most immediate. A raised voice in the next cubicle, a driver honking in traffic—her body
reacts before her mind catches up. She flinches, her hands clench, and for a terrifying second she
is back in the ditch, waiting for the next round.

At night, sleep is a problem. She does not fall asleep easily because she needs to stay alert,
listening for footsteps in the hallway, the creak of the bedroom door. Even when her partner is
beside her, she lies rigid, scanning the dark. Any sudden movement from him—a shift, a stretch—makes
her startle so violently that she once pushed him away without meaning to. She knows he is not a
threat, but knowing does not stop the response. The flashbacks come without warning, triggered by a
sound or a smell, and for a few seconds she is not in their apartment but in the war zone, the fear
so real she can taste the grit in her mouth.

She has tried to push the memories out of her mind, to bury them under spreadsheets and traffic
simulations. It works during the day, but the rage waits. When a memory does break through, she
feels an overwhelming, burning fury—at the people who ambushed them, at herself for surviving, at
the world for continuing as if nothing happened. The anger is so immense that she has begun to hurt
herself physically to release it. A few times she has dug her nails into her forearms until they
bled, or struck her thigh with her fist, because the pressure inside her chest was unendurable. She
keeps these marks hidden under long sleeves, tells her partner she bumped into furniture. He has
started to ask questions she cannot answer.

Maya’s work has suffered. She avoids site visits in natural areas, so she delegates them to
colleagues, who are beginning to notice. She cannot explain; she cannot finish a sentence about any
of it. Her relationship feels fragile. She loves her partner, but she is always on guard, always
waiting for something to go wrong. What finally brought her to therapy was a morning when she woke
up on the bathroom floor, her wrist bloody, and could not remember how she got there. She is afraid
of losing control completely. She is also, against all odds, hoping that someone can help her make
the memory feel real again—or at least quiet enough that she can sleep through the night without her
heart pounding against her ribs.
