Minh was sixty-nine years old, a plumber by trade, married for over forty years to a woman he had
met after arriving in the United States as a young refugee. He could not have said exactly what had
finally pushed him to call the clinic. Perhaps it was the job three weeks ago, when he had agreed to
fix a leak under a sink in a small house on the outskirts of town. The homeowner was a man in his
fifties, alone, and as Minh followed him down the narrow basement stairs he had felt his chest
tighten and his stomach turn. He had tried to tell himself it was just a job, but when the man
gestured toward the crawlspace and said, “You’ll have to go in there,” Minh had frozen. The nausea
came first—sudden, rising, familiar—and then the ache in his neck, a sharp band of tension that had
been with him for decades but always seemed to flare at the worst moments. He had mumbled an excuse
and left, and he had not taken another job since.

The event that had shaped Minh’s life had happened when he was not much more than a boy. He had been
taken from his village by soldiers and held in a camp for what he later estimated to be eight
months. He did not speak of it easily, and when he tried, the words seemed to scatter. He knew what
had happened—the beatings, the hunger, the small locked room where he had been kept alone for days
at a time—but when he opened his mouth to tell someone, he could not find the language. The
sentences broke apart. His wife had long since stopped asking. She knew only that he had been in a
war, that he came to her country with nothing, that he was a good man who sometimes screamed in his
sleep.

What Minh carried instead of a story was a film. Inside his mind, the memories played over and over,
always the same scenes: the rough hands dragging him from his family, the metal door slamming shut,
the cold concrete floor. He could see it as clearly as if it were happening again, but he could not
speak it. So he had learned to detach himself. When the film started, he would push it away, force
his attention elsewhere, go numb. He had done that for so many years that it felt automatic now, a
reflex. But the detachment never lasted, and the film always returned.

His life had become a series of precautions. He never entered a room without noting the exits, the
windows, the possible paths to the door. Even in his own home—his home, where he had lived for
thirty years—he was on guard. He would stand in the kitchen with his back to the wall, watching his
wife move around the stove, and he would feel the alertness in his shoulders, the readiness for
something that never came. Sudden movements from her or from anyone else made him flinch. When his
grown son came to visit and walked briskly around a corner, Minh’s heart would pound, and his hand
would go to his neck, where the old pain lived.

He avoided dates he could not forget. The anniversary of his capture, the day he was released, the
month of his escape—these were times when the film played louder, when he could not sleep and could
not eat, when the nausea was constant. He would tell his wife he was sick, and he would stay in bed,
waiting for the days to pass. She had learned not to challenge him.

The consequences had accumulated quietly. His work had narrowed over the years. He would not take
jobs that required him to be alone with a male client, and he avoided basements and attics whenever
possible. He had turned down more work than he had accepted, and his income had shrunk. His wife had
noticed and had asked, gently, if he might consider retirement. He had not known how to explain that
retirement meant staying home, and home did not feel safe either.

What finally brought him to the therapist’s office was not the lost jobs or the sleepless nights,
but a moment of confusion. He had been sitting in his living room, watching a film on
television—nothing violent, a comedy—and suddenly the images on the screen had dissolved, and he was
back in that room, the metal door, the silence. He had sat there, heart hammering, unable to move,
and when it passed he had found himself on the floor, his neck stiff, his body shaking. He had
looked at his hands and realized he could not remember the last time he had felt anything but fear.
He wanted that to change. He was afraid of what would happen if he talked about the memories, afraid
he would lose the thread of himself entirely. But he was also afraid of staying the same.
