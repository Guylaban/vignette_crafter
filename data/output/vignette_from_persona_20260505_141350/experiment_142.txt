Mark was thirty-four when he finally admitted that something was wrong, though he had known it for
months. As a firefighter, he had seen his share of horror, but it was the six months he spent as a
relief worker in a war zone that had broken something inside him. He had volunteered for a
humanitarian mission in a conflict region, delivering medical supplies to a frontline hospital. The
memory that returned most often was the afternoon a mortar struck the compound's entrance. He had
been standing twenty feet from the blast, his ears ringing, his body pelted with debris, and for a
long moment he had watched a stranger — a young aid worker he barely knew — lie motionless in the
rubble. Mark had pulled him to cover, but the man died in his arms. That moment, the dust and the
silence afterward, had become the center around which everything else now revolved.

Back home, Mark found that familiar spaces had turned threatening. He felt on guard even inside his
own house, his shoulders tight as he moved from room to room. He could not sit with his back to a
door; he had to always stay near an exit, even at the dinner table with his wife, Sarah. Every time
he walked into a room — the fire station's common area, the grocery store, his own living room — he
would scan for exits before he could focus on anything else. Strangers approaching him, even a
neighbor asking about the weather, made his heart race. He had become certain that anyone he did not
know could be a threat, that the ordinary world was a place where danger could appear without
warning.

The strategies he used to manage this feeling only narrowed his life further. He stopped exercising,
even though he had always run five miles a day. The gym felt too enclosed, too far from an exit, and
the pounding of his own heartbeat on the treadmill reminded him of the adrenaline surge he now
associated with danger. He pushed away positive emotions because they felt unsafe — laughter with
Sarah, a good meal, a joke from a colleague. Joy seemed like a trap, a moment of vulnerability where
something terrible could slip in. He had come to believe that if he let himself feel good, he would
lower his guard, and then the next mortar would find him.

At work, his crew noticed the change. He was still competent, still brave when the alarms rang, but
he no longer lingered after a call to debrief or share a beer. He left the station as soon as his
shift ended, always taking the route that kept him closest to the wall. His captain said nothing,
but Mark saw the concern in his eyes. At home, Sarah had begun to sleep in the guest room because he
woke three or four times a night, certain he had heard something. She asked him to talk, but he
could not find the words. What frightened him most was the thought that this vigilance would never
end, that he would always be scanning, always ready to flee, always pushing away the good moments
before they could be taken from him.

He came to therapy because Sarah had made an appointment for him, and because the night before, he
had found himself pacing the kitchen at 3 a.m., checking the locks for the fifth time, and he could
not remember what peace felt like. He was afraid of what he might become, and he was afraid of what
it would cost his marriage. But he was also afraid, somewhere beneath the vigilance, that perhaps
this was the only way to stay alive.
