Mark had always prided himself on his composure under pressure. As a combat pilot, he had trained to
remain calm in the cockpit during maneuvers that would make most people panic. But that composure
had shattered one night two years ago, after a unit celebration, when a senior officer he had
trusted implicitly cornered him in an empty briefing room. Mark had frozen—not from cowardice, he
now told himself, but from disbelief. This was the man who had mentored him, praised his flying,
recommended him for promotion. The assault itself was over quickly, but the aftermath had never
stopped.

In the months that followed, Mark found himself scanning every room he entered for exits, for people
who stood too close, for voices that dropped to a whisper as he approached. He had come to believe
that his reactions since that night proved something fundamental about his character: he was a lousy
coper. Other pilots had served in combat zones, seen things, and still functioned. He couldn’t even
walk through the squadron lounge without his heart hammering at the sight of two colleagues leaning
together, talking low. He was certain they were discussing him, maybe planning something. He had
lost his faith in people entirely—and in life itself, in any higher power that would allow a man to
betray someone who had trusted him completely.

At home, a small apartment near the base, Mark slept with a knife under his pillow and a chair
wedged under the doorknob. Every creak of the building’s old pipes made him jerk upright, listening
for footsteps. Ordinary sounds—a car backfiring, a dropped pan in a neighbor’s kitchen—sent him
diving for cover, heart racing, shame flooding in afterward. He was on guard even inside his own
home, the one place he should have felt safe. He had stopped inviting anyone over. When friends
asked why, he said he was busy with training.

On the base, Mark requested only solo flight time. He avoided the briefing room before missions,
claiming he needed to review data alone. But even in the cockpit, unpredictable situations—a sudden
change in air traffic control instructions, an unexpected gust of wind—sent his mind spiraling back
to that night, to the feeling of being trapped and powerless. He would startle at the crackle of the
radio, his hands shaking on the throttle. He knew he was becoming a liability, but he could not stop
listening for sounds that might mean someone was entering his airspace—or his home. He felt watched,
vulnerable, never safe.

The consequence accumulated slowly. His performance reviews dropped from excellent to average. His
commanding officer noted a “lack of engagement” and recommended him for a desk evaluation. Mark’s
social life had evaporated. He stopped going to the gym, stopped answering calls from his family,
stopped dating. He was terrified that anyone who got close would see the weakness—or worse, become a
threat. The only time he felt briefly calm was when he was alone in the air, miles above the ground,
where no one could touch him.

What finally brought Mark to seek help was a nightmare so vivid that he woke up screaming, convinced
the assault was happening again. The dream residue clung to him all day—the smell of sweat and cheap
cologne, the feel of a hand over his mouth. At work, when a colleague placed a friendly hand on his
shoulder, Mark spun around and shoved him against a wall. No one said anything, but the look on that
man’s face—betrayal, confusion—echoed his own from two years ago.

Mark came to therapy because he was afraid he would lose his career. But what he was most afraid of
was never feeling safe again. What he hoped for, though he could barely admit it, was to stop seeing
a threat in every shadow and to believe, just once, that someone might be trustworthy.
