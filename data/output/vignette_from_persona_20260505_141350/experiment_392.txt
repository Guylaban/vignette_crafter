Ravi was sixty-four years old, a warehouse worker who had spent most of his life stacking pallets
and driving forklifts in a sprawling distribution center on the outskirts of Chicago. He was married
to his wife, Sunita, for thirty-eight years, and they had raised two children who were now grown and
living on the East Coast. But the man who came to the clinic was barely recognizable as the same
person Sunita had known for decades. He had not been the same since the three days he spent held
captive in a shipping container behind an abandoned truck stop outside of Delhi during a visit to
his homeland.

The kidnapping happened at 2 a.m. on a sweltering July night. Ravi had been waiting for a bus back
to his village when a van pulled up, and two men yanked him inside. They blindfolded him and drove
for hours before shoving him into a dark, metal container. For three days, he was kept in total
blackness, the heat suffocating, and the only sound was the rustle of something slithering across
the floor. He never saw the snake, but he heard it at night, and he felt a cold, scaled body brush
against his ankle on the second night. He had been certain he would die either from the snake or
from the heat or from the men who occasionally slid water under the door but never spoke. When he
was finally released—left on a roadside with nothing but his soiled clothes—he had not told his
family the full story. He said he had been robbed and lost his wallet. But the truth of that
container stayed locked inside him, and it began to leak out in ways he could not control.

Back in Chicago, Ravi tried to return to work, but the warehouse became a minefield. The night
shift, which he had worked for years, now terrified him. He started requesting day shifts, but even
then, the dimly lit aisles of stacked boxes made his heart pound. He found himself constantly
looking over his shoulder while walking the warehouse floor, convinced that someone was following
him—though he knew logically no one was. The most puzzling trigger was snakes. A coworker once left
a book on reptiles in the break room, and Ravi had a full-blown panic attack. He could not explain
why, but the image of a snake’s coil sent him back into that container. He began avoiding any
conversation about animals, and if someone so much as mentioned a snake, he would walk away.

Nighttime became unbearable. He could not bring himself to go to sleep at the same hour the
kidnapping had occurred—2 a.m.—so he stayed awake until exhaustion forced him to collapse. Even
then, he could only sleep with the lights on. The dark felt like a physical presence, pressing on
his chest, and he would wake up drenched in sweat, gasping. Sunita found herself waking repeatedly
to his restless thrashing and his repeated questions: “Am I okay? Am I safe? Check my ankles—is
there a mark?” She would inspect his skin, find nothing, and tell him he was fine, but he would ask
again an hour later. He checked his own body constantly throughout the day—running his hands over
his arms, his legs, his stomach—searching for any sting or bite or bruise that might signal that
danger had finally found him.

The consequences began to pile up. He missed work three times in one month due to exhaustion, and
his supervisor warned him that he was on probation. Sunita grew short with him, snapping that she
could not keep telling him he was safe when nothing was actually wrong. He stopped going out after
sunset altogether, which meant no dinners with friends, no evening walks, no visits to his
grandson’s school plays. He had come to believe that he was fundamentally helpless—that no matter
what he did, bad things would happen to him. Some nights, lying rigidly in bed, he thought that
maybe life was not worth living anymore. He never said it aloud, but the thought sat in his chest
like a stone. He also avoided thinking about the event at all, because he was certain that if he let
his mind go there, he would fall apart completely and never recover.

What finally brought Ravi to the clinic was a confrontation with Sunita. She had found him at 3 a.m.
standing in the kitchen, staring at the knife block, and she demanded that he get help or she would
leave. He made an appointment the next morning. In the waiting room, he sat with his back to the
wall, scanning the room for exits. He did not know if therapy could help—he was afraid that talking
about the event would destroy him—but he was also afraid of losing Sunita, of losing his job, of
losing himself. He hoped, more than anything, that someone could tell him he was safe and that he
could finally trust it.
