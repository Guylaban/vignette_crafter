Preeti was forty-seven when she finally walked into a therapist’s office, but the woman who sat down
across from the intake clinician was not the pastor her congregation had known for years. That
version of Preeti—the one who could counsel a grieving widow at noon and lead a potluck supper by
six, the one who believed brokenness could be redeemed—had been disappearing for months, and she had
not known how to stop it.

The torture happened seventeen years ago, in a basement in the city where she had been born. She had
been a lay preacher then, arrested for distributing tracts that the authorities considered
seditious. There were four men. They wanted names. They did not believe her when she said she had no
names to give. She remembered the fluorescent light overhead—that was clear, so clear she could
still feel the buzz behind her eyes. But the rest came in pieces: the wet rope around her wrists,
the smell of sweat and something metallic, the taste of something thick and salty that they forced
into her mouth—she had never told anyone it tasted like a copper coin left on the tongue. She could
not remember their faces, only the sound of one man’s belt buckle scraping against the cement floor.
And then: blank. Hours missing. A different room. A woman’s voice saying something in a language
Preeti did not understand. She had never been able to piece together how she got out.

For years she managed. She came to America as a refugee, completed seminary, took a pastorate in a
midsized city. She worked hard. She did not talk about the basement. When well-meaning parishioners
asked about her past, she smiled and said God had given her a second chance. She thought that was
enough.

But lately, her body had begun to remember things her mind refused to. At the church’s monthly
fellowship dinner, a volunteer served a lentil stew that tasted faintly of turmeric and something
else—something metallic. Preeti did not even register the taste before her stomach clenched, her
mouth went dry, and she was on her feet, excusing herself to the restroom, hands shaking. She stood
over the sink and told herself she was safe. *You are in Ohio. The door is unlocked. The women’s
auxiliary is two tables away.* But her body had already reacted: her heart pounding, her skin slick
with cold sweat, before her mind had even caught up. She could not make herself believe the safety.
Even when her associate pastor told her, gently, that there was nothing to fear, Preeti heard the
words and felt nothing—no relief, only the certainty that the danger was still there, just out of
sight.

She started scanning. Every new face in the sanctuary became a potential threat. A man in a dark
coat sitting in the back row made her pulse jump. She found herself memorizing exits during sermons.
She began to avoid the fellowship hall entirely, eating lunch alone in her office, chewing the same
bland crackers every day, controlling every bite because the alternative—being caught off guard by a
flavor—felt unbearable.

She tried not to think about the basement. When a memory surfaced—the belt buckle scraping, the
metallic taste—she shoved it down with such force that she felt dizzy. She told herself she could
not handle what was there. If she opened the door, she would fall apart and never put herself back
together. She became good at suppressing. But the fragments leaked through anyway: a clear image of
the fluorescent light, then nothing; guilt so heavy it sat on her chest like a stone. She did not
understand what she had done wrong—she had not given any names—but the guilt was there, hot and
persistent, as if she had failed someone she could not name.

The congregation noticed. Preeti had stopped visiting the sick. She had stopped accepting
invitations to dinner. She said she was tired, but the truth was that she no longer believed she was
capable of doing the things that had once defined her. She watched herself from a distance, a woman
who used to preach hope, and she saw no future at all. Only the same days ahead, each one requiring
her to be hypervigilant, to taste metal in her mouth, to press down the guilt and the fragments and
the fear that she would never be safe again.

She came to therapy because her deacon—a kind, persistent woman—had found her crying in the church
kitchen after a funeral reception, and would not let her deflect. Preeti sat in the waiting room
with her hands folded, afraid of what talking would do, but also, somewhere beneath the
hopelessness, hoping that maybe the pieces could be made to fit together without destroying her.
