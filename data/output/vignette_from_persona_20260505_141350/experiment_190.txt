Sione is a 74-year-old editor who has spent the last four decades of his career in a small
publishing house, proofreading manuscripts and fact-checking articles. He is a large man, broad-
shouldered and soft-spoken, the kind of person colleagues describe as “gentle.” But they also find
him distant. He rarely joins office lunches, never argues about editorial decisions, and if someone
suggests a change to his work, he becomes very still and says nothing for a long moment before
quietly agreeing. What his colleagues do not know is that the moment someone corrects him, the world
splits into slides—a tight close-up of a woman’s hand, then a flash of light over the kitchen floor,
then a sound he cannot name. He sees these pictures, but they do not feel like his own memory. They
feel as though they belong to a different man, a man who was weak enough to let that happen.

The event that seeded this came thirty-five years ago, in the house he shared with his wife, a
gifted violinist named Leilani. Their marriage had been passionate and volatile, and Sione had long
believed that his own temperament—too placid, too accommodating—was what kept provoking her. When
she struck him, he told himself it was his fault for being the sort of person who made her that
angry. He told himself he must have deserved it. He became an object in those moments, something to
be acted upon, and after she left—she left him, finally, for a cellist who matched her fire—he
carried that objecthood inside him. He could not feel anything properly after that. Joy felt like a
photograph someone else had taken. Grief was a distant hum. Rage, though, was real. When he
remembered her hand, or the sound of a door slamming, a hot wave of fury would arrive unbidden. But
even that rage felt foreign, as though it belonged to the other man, the one who should have
defended himself and never did.

For years Sione managed by staying busy. Editing was perfect for this: every sentence needed his
attention, every comma a small decision. He could lose himself in someone else’s story. He learned
to stop reading the sections of manuscripts that described physical conflict—he would hand those
pages to a junior editor without explanation. He stopped watching films with violent scenes. He
stopped going to concerts, because the sight of a violinist on stage would tighten his throat. And
most of all, he stopped letting himself feel anything strong. When a colleague praised his work, he
nodded and changed the subject. When he felt loneliness at night, he turned on the television and
watched cooking shows until his eyes blurred. Strong emotions were doorways to the snapshots, and he
refused to walk through them.

The price of this was a slow erosion of his life outside work. His divorce had been
inevitable—Leilani had left years before, but Sione had never pursued another relationship. He told
himself he did not have the emotional capacity for it. His friendships became arrangements of shared
activities: a weekly chess game with an old neighbor, but no talk of the past. By the time he was
seventy, his social world had shrunk to his desk and the quiet evenings in his apartment. He was not
unhappy, exactly. He was empty, and emptiness had become comfortable.

What finally brought him to a clinic was a single sentence from a new colleague, a young woman who
handed back his edit of her article and said, “This whole paragraph is a mess—what were you
thinking?” The words hit him like a slap. He saw the snapshots: a kitchen floor, a shattered cup,
Leilani’s face above him. He felt the rage, hot and impossible, and then he felt his hand gripping
the edge of the desk so hard his knuckles went white. He did not say a word. But that night, alone
in his apartment, he admitted to himself that he had been holding his breath for thirty-five years.
He wanted to know if he could breathe again, if he could feel normal emotions without being swamped
by pictures that did not even feel like his own. He came to therapy because he was afraid that the
alternative—continuing to live as an object, a body that edited manuscripts and avoided
feelings—would leave him emptier still.
