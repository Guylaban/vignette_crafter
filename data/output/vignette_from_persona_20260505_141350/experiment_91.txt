Robert, a 56-year-old combat pilot, had spent decades mastering the controlled chaos of aerial
warfare. He was a man who trusted his instincts, his aircraft, and the precision of his own body
under extreme G-forces. That trust shattered two years ago during a routine training exercise over
the desert. A catastrophic engine failure produced a deafening explosion that seemed to come from
inside his own skull. The cockpit filled with acrid smoke, the plane pitched violently, and Robert
felt a sudden wave of dizziness so severe he could barely read his instruments. His vision blurred,
nausea surged, and he knew—with a cold certainty that still visits him at night—that he was about to
die. He ejected, but the memory of that helpless, spinning disorientation, ears ringing, stomach
heaving, has never left him.

In the weeks after the crash, Robert was hospitalized for minor fractures and a concussion. The
doctors praised his survival, but every beeping monitor and antiseptic smell made him want to flee.
Physical examinations felt like another kind of assault. He started canceling follow-ups, then
refused all medical appointments outright. When his wife Sarah asked him about a persistent cough,
he snapped that there was nothing wrong. He had come to believe that any doctor’s office, any
ambulance siren, even the sight of a bandage could pull him back into that moment of pure
vulnerability.

The crash also robbed him of the things he once loved. Robert, an avid fly fisherman and weekend
golfer, gradually dropped those activities. He told himself he just wasn’t interested anymore, but
the truth was that every pleasant activity felt hollow, like he was watching someone else’s life. To
regain some sense of control, he began restricting his eating—only small meals at exact times, no
spontaneous snacks, no foods that made him feel full or queasy. His body, he reasoned, had betrayed
him once; he would not let it surprise him again.

At home, Robert lives in a state of perpetual readiness. His body feels wired, as if the crash is
still happening just around the corner. When Sarah walks into a room unexpectedly, he jumps, heart
slamming, fists clenching. A sudden movement from a dog or a child in the park makes him flinch as
though under fire. He cannot walk through a parking lot without scanning every car, every shadow. He
is certain the world is now a dangerous place—not just in combat zones, but everywhere. The beliefs
that once anchored him—that he was competent, that the universe was orderly—have been destroyed. He
told his wife last month, “I feel dead inside. There’s nothing left.”

At work, Robert is no longer flying. He was reassigned to a desk role after the accident, ostensibly
for medical reasons, though he never finished the required physical evaluation. The office is
supposed to be safe, but even the hum of a photocopier or the bang of a filing cabinet sends him
into a cold sweat. His colleagues notice his reluctance to leave his cubicle, his refusal to attend
unit briefings near the hangar where aircraft take off. He has started avoiding the base cafeteria
entirely—the noise, the crowds, the chance of hearing a sudden clatter—and eats his bland sandwich
alone at his desk.

What finally brought Robert to seek help was a morning three weeks ago when he became dizzy simply
standing up from a chair. The sensation was mild, only a few seconds, but it triggered a full panic
attack: racing heart, hyperventilating, the conviction that he was back in the spinning cockpit.
Sarah found him on the bathroom floor, sobbing and shaking. She made an appointment at a trauma
clinic, and Robert came—not because he believed it would help, but because he was terrified of his
own body, and even more terrified of losing his wife. He is most afraid of being told he will never
feel normal again. But beneath that, in a voice he can barely acknowledge, he hopes that someone
might finally give him a way to stop the world from feeling like it is always about to explode.
