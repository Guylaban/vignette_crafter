Minh Nguyen was a thirty-four-year-old chaplain at a large urban hospital, a role he had held for
nearly six years after his wife’s death from cancer had reshaped his sense of vocation. The mugging
happened on a Tuesday evening in late October, as he cut through a parking garage on his way to the
bus stop. A man came up behind him, shoved him against a concrete pillar, pressed something hard
into his ribs—Minh never saw a weapon, but the pressure was enough—and demanded his wallet, his
phone, his watch. Minh complied. The man’s breathing was loud and ragged, inches from Minh’s ear,
and the smell of stale cigarette smoke and cheap cologne clung to him. The whole thing lasted
perhaps ninety seconds, but in the months afterward those seconds became the only things that felt
real.

When Minh thought about the event now, it was almost always in pictures—a series of frozen images:
the gray pillar, the glint of a streetlamp on the man’s jacket, the blur of his own hand handing
over his wallet. Sometimes the smell would return without warning, acrid and close, and he would
find himself scanning a hospital corridor for a man who was not there. What surprised him most was
the rage. He had not felt angry during the mugging—only afraid, compliant, numb—but now when the
memory surfaced a hot, wordless fury would rise in his chest, directed at the man, at himself, at
the sheer randomness of it. He had come to believe the event happened because of the sort of person
he was: someone too trusting, too soft, too oblivious to danger. Somebody else—anybody else—would
have seen it coming, would have stayed alert, would not have walked through that garage at that
hour. He was certain that bad things would keep finding him, that no amount of caution could change
the kind of person he was.

The triggers were everywhere. Crowded hospital hallways during shift changes made his shoulders knot
and his breath shorten; he found himself taking longer, emptier routes to the chapel. Any heavy
breathing—a patient on oxygen, a nurse hurrying past, even his own breath after climbing
stairs—brought him back to that garage, to the sound against his ear. He began to avoid the
emergency department and the respiratory therapy wing, inventing reasons why his visits could wait.
People who reminded him of the mugger were harder to avoid: men of a certain build, wearing a
certain jacket, standing too close. He would cross the street to avoid someone in a parking lot, or
leave a waiting room if a new patient sat down near him. He told himself it was sensible, just good
boundaries, but his supervisor had started to notice the gaps in his coverage—the units he never
volunteered for, the on-call shifts he traded away. Minh missed one staff meeting after another,
then stopped attending entirely; he told his colleagues he was busy, but the truth was that the room
felt too small, too full of people, and he could feel the rage building under his skin for no reason
he could name.

At home, in the apartment he had shared with his wife before she died, Minh spent most of his
evenings sitting in the dark. He had stopped watching television because certain scenes—sudden
violence, heavy breathing in a chase—would send him reeling. He slept poorly, woke often from dreams
that were vivid pictures and nothing else, and found himself checking the locks three or four times
before bed. The helplessness he felt was absolute: he could not stop a bad thing from happening to
him, could not trust his own judgment, could not rely on his own body to stay calm. The loss of his
wife had taught him that the world was fragile; the mugging had taught him that he was too weak to
survive it.

What finally brought Minh to the clinic was a morning in the hospital chapel. A young man had come
in, crying, after learning his father had died. Minh sat with him, spoke the quiet words he had
spoken a hundred times, and then the man took a deep, shuddering breath—loud, ragged, exactly the
sound. Minh felt his hands clench into fists, felt a surge of pure rage at a grieving stranger, and
had to excuse himself to the bathroom where he stood gripping the sink until his knuckles went
white. After that he called the employee assistance number and asked for help. He did not know if he
could ever go back to the work that had once felt like his calling. He was afraid he had become
someone who could not help anyone, not even himself.
