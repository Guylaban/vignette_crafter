Mai is a 37-year-old refugee aid worker, married, living in a Midwestern city. She came to the
United States from Vietnam as a child, but she returned to the region for several years with an
international humanitarian organization. The trauma happened during a distribution of supplies at a
camp near a border crossing. Local police arrived in force to disperse what they called an
unauthorized gathering. Mai was pinned against a shipping container as officers pushed into the
crowd with batons. A young man on a bicycle tried to weave through the chaos; an officer swung at
him, the bicycle collapsed, and Mai watched him fall under the wheels of a police truck. She tried
to reach him but another officer shoved her to the ground, his boot on her back while she screamed.
She could hear the cyclist’s weeping stop. No one was held accountable. The incident was classified
as a riot dispersal with minimal injuries.

In the months that followed, Mai found that she could not tolerate being in crowded spaces. The
press of bodies in a supermarket aisle made her chest tighten, and she would leave her cart and walk
out. She also began to notice police presence everywhere—a patrol car idling at a red light, an
officer in the coffee shop—and each sighting sent a jolt of cold alertness through her. Strangely,
even seeing someone cycling on a quiet residential street could undo her: the rhythm of pedals, the
posture of the rider, sometimes just the soft whir of wheels. She had come to believe that any of
these moments could pull her back into that day with the same visceral terror, the same grief that
washed over her when the memory returned unbidden. At work, she avoided the field sites where police
or crowds were likely, and she turned down invitations to team dinners or celebrations. Anything
that might have made her feel happy or hopeful felt dangerous, as if permitting herself joy would
leave her unprotected.

She tried not to think about what had happened. When images surfaced, she would press her palms
against her eyes or start a mental list of grocery items. At night, she lay awake for hours,
replaying the sound of the bicycle frame bending, the officer’s boot pressing into her spine. Her
husband had begun to sleep in the guest room because her restlessness woke him. She found herself
snapping at him for small things—leaving a cup on the counter, humming a tune—and she knew the anger
was not about the cup, but she could not stop herself. She started gambling online, a few dollars at
first, then larger sums, because the pull of the spin gave her something else to feel besides the
heaviness in her chest. The losses mounted, and yet the momentary rush when the reels stopped was
the only time she felt anything other than numb or terrified.

What frightened Mai most was that she no longer recognized herself. She had been someone who ran
toward danger to help, who believed in order and fairness. Now she saw only injustice: what happened
to her was deeply unfair, and no one had apologized, investigated, or even acknowledged it. She was
certain that someone else—a different person, a stronger person—would never have gotten into that
situation in the first place. That thought hollowed her out. She began to take risks that felt
