Tane was 24 years old when the helicopter took fire. He remembers the exact moment—6:47 in the
morning, the sun just beginning to break through the haze over the jungle canopy. The first round
hit the fuselage and he was thrown sideways, his hand slamming against the shredded fabric of his
seat, the coarse threads digging into his palm as he scrambled to brace himself. The sound of the
rotors faltered, then screamed, then fell silent. He does not recall the crash itself, only the
terror that arrived before impact and the texture of that seat fabric, which he can still feel
against his skin if he lets himself think about it. For decades after, he told himself the memory
belonged to someone else—a younger man, a different life. But the terror that accompanies recall is
unmistakably his own, and it comes without warning. He will be standing in his kitchen or walking
through an airport terminal and something in his surroundings—a rough fabric on a chair, the
particular quality of early morning light, or the distant thrum of helicopter blades—brings the full
memory crashing back, and he is back in that seat, waiting to die.

Tane returned home from the war and resumed his career as an airline pilot, a job he had loved since
childhood. In the cockpit he was competent, focused, in control. But outside it, the world had
become a different place. He noticed it first in small ways: rising before dawn to prepare for a
flight, he would find himself checking the locks on the front door twice, then three times, unable
to shake the feeling that something was waiting for him in the dark. He began to dread the night
shift rotations, not because of the flying but because of the hours before and after, when the house
was quiet and his wife slept and he lay awake, straining to hear sounds that did not belong. He came
to believe that he was not safe at night, that darkness carried a threat he could not name. During
the day, in public spaces, he found himself scanning every room he entered for the exits—mapping the
doors, the windows, the emergency routes before he could pay attention to anything else. If someone
moved suddenly beside him at the grocery store or in the airport lounge, his body would lock into a
posture of readiness before his mind caught up. He knew it was irrational, but the feeling was
undeniable: the world was a dangerous place, and he was the only one who seemed to see it.

The texture trigger was the hardest to escape. The seat fabric in his commercial aircraft was
smooth, synthetic, nothing like the shredded canvas of the helicopter. But every time he ran his
hand over the armrest during preflight checks, he had to suppress the urge to pull his hand back. He

the bristled surface of a new carpet. At home, his wife noticed that he had replaced all the throw

but the truth was more complicated. He could not explain that the feel of a particular weave
transported him to a moment of such terror that his heart would race and his palms would sweat, and
he would have to excuse himself to the bathroom to breathe.

Over the years, the avoidance hardened into habit. He stopped flying early morning routes whenever
possible, trading shifts with younger pilots to avoid the hour that had fractured his life. He told
himself it was about the light, the glare, but really it was about the feeling of that 6:47 sky. He
stopped attending gatherings where helicopters might be discussed or, worse, seen. He stopped
watching films with combat scenes. He stopped talking about the war at all. Yet the memory did not
stop arriving. It came unbidden, sometimes daily, and with it the certainty that something
fundamental was wrong with him. He used to be a happy man—his wife reminded him of that, showed him
old photos, described the way he used to laugh. But he could no longer access that person. He felt
broken, defective, as though the man who survived the crash had not returned home but had been
replaced by someone hollowed out and wary.

His marriage suffered. His wife grew tired of his silences, his hypervigilance, the way he would

because it was true, but he also could not stop. The memory felt alien—like a story he had been told
about someone else—yet the terror it produced was immediate and physical, a betrayal of his own
body. He had come to believe that he was beyond repair. The world was dangerous, and he was proof of
it.

stayed with him. He told his wife that night, and she said nothing, but she held his hand. The next

way, haunted by a memory that does not feel like his own, in a world that does not feel safe,
carrying a terror he cannot explain.
