her university’s science building. It was late, just before closing time, and she needed to restock
a few reagents for the organic chemistry lab she taught as a graduate assistant. The overhead
fluorescent flickered and then went out, leaving only the faint glow from the hallway. She reached
for the wall switch, but her hand brushed against a metal drum that had been left precariously near
the edge of a shelf. It tipped. The cap wasn’t sealed. A colorless, acrid liquid splashed across her
arms and chest, and the fumes hit her throat like a wall of fire. She gasped, stumbled backward, and
her vision blurred. The next thing she knew, she was on the concrete floor, her lungs seizing, her
skin burning. A janitor found her minutes later, but those minutes felt like hours of suffocation.

The paramedics arrived, and she remembers the bright lights of the ambulance, then the antiseptic
smell of the emergency room, the sting of oxygen masks, the cold of a hospital bed. Her arms were
treated for chemical burns, and she spent two days in observation for inhalation injuries.
Medically, she recovered. But the sense of her body as something that had turned on her, that had
nearly killed her, never left.

In the months afterward, Mariana found herself unable to explain what had happened. When friends
asked, she could picture the moment: the drum, the darkness, the burn. But words stuck in her
throat. She knew the story but could not arrange it in order—was the smell first or the pain? Did
she scream? The sequence refused to line up, and when she tried to force it, a raw, burning
sensation pulsed in her chest and along her forearms, as if the chemical were still eating into her
skin. She stopped trying to talk about it. Instead, she tried to erase it entirely, pushing the
images away so hard that she felt exhausted by the effort. She stopped going near the science
building, found a longer route to her apartment that avoided that part of campus entirely.

But the world kept reminding her. At night, she would wake in complete darkness, her heart slamming,
certain the air was turning toxic. She began sleeping with a lamp on, but even then, the moment she
opened her eyes in the small hours, the panic was already there. During the day, she found herself
scanning every room she entered—her classroom, the library, the grocery store—looking for spills,
leaks, anything out of place. If a classmate touched her shoulder unexpectedly, she jerked away with
a sharp gasp, which embarrassed her and made people think she was on edge. Her body stayed coiled,
her shoulders hunched, her jaw tight, as if waiting for the next chemical attack. She told herself
she was overreacting, but the physical reactions happened anyway: her hands would tremble, her
breath would shorten, whenever she caught a whiff of hand sanitizer or passed a hospital.

Mariana had always thought of herself as capable and independent. Now she felt fundamentally broken.
She had come to believe there was something wrong with her as a person—not just that she’d been in
an accident, but that her own body had betrayed her. It kept reacting as if she were still in that
storage room, as if the danger were still there, even when she knew rationally that she was safe.
She no longer trusted her own lungs, her own skin. Every twinge or ache felt like the start of
another poisoning.

The consequences crept into her daily life. She had taken a leave of absence from her graduate
program because she could not enter the science building, and her supervisor did not understand why
she would not switch to a different lab on another floor. Her social circle shrank; she declined
invitations to movie nights because the dark theater would trigger her, and she avoided restaurants
with strong cleaning smells. She felt isolated, ashamed, and convinced that she was failing at being
a normal twenty-six-year-old.

What finally brought Mariana to the clinic was a panic attack during a routine doctor’s appointment.
The antiseptic odor hit her the moment she walked in, and within seconds she was shaking, unable to
speak, her memory dissolving into fragments of that Friday night. The physician referred her for
psychological help. Mariana is terrified that nothing will make the body memories stop. But she is
also, somewhere beneath the fear, hoping that maybe she can learn to sleep with the lights off
again—and to trust that her own body is not her enemy.
