Amir is a 41-year-old fast food worker, single and living alone in a small apartment near the
restaurant where he has worked for the past seven years. The event that changed everything happened
fifteen months ago, on a Tuesday evening during the closing shift. A customer who had been waiting
in line for several minutes suddenly began shouting at the cashier, a young woman named Leila. Amir
was at the fry station, and he remembers the sound of the argument escalating—the customer’s voice
rising, the manager’s attempt to intervene. Then he heard a loud crack, a sound he later learned was
a gunshot. He saw Leila fall; he saw the customer drop the weapon and flee. Amir did nothing—he
stood frozen behind the counter, his hands still gripping a metal basket, his ears ringing. He did
not run, did not call for help until someone else did. In the months that followed, that moment
replayed in his mind not as a coherent story but as a jumble of sensations: the heat of the fry oil,
the ringing silence after the shot, the way Leila’s eyes went wide. He had come to believe that his
immobility meant he was cowardly, that he had failed her. He was certain that if he had stepped
forward, he might have prevented it, even though he knew, logically, that he was unarmed.

The first trigger he noticed was the time of day. The incident happened at 9:15 PM, during the last
hour before the restaurant closed. Now, every evening as the clock nears nine, Amir feels a physical
tightening in his chest. He has changed his schedule to work only morning and early afternoon
shifts, so that he can leave before that hour arrives. His manager has complained, but Amir has
offered no real explanation. Being at the restaurant at all is difficult, but the morning is
bearable. The real trouble began with being criticised or corrected. When a customer complains about
his order, or when the shift supervisor tells him he is moving too slowly, Amir’s heart hammers and
he feels a wave of dread—as though the criticism is a prelude to something worse. He has been told
he overreacts to simple feedback; once, when a manager asked him to re-clean a counter, Amir walked
off the floor and locked himself in the supply closet for ten minutes. He knew it made no sense, but
in that moment he was certain that being singled out would lead to another violent confrontation.
Similarly, when something takes longer than expected—a delivery truck that is late, a customer who
cannot decide on an order—he finds himself scanning the room for exits, assuming that a delay means
danger is approaching. He monitors every unexpected sound: the slam of a refrigerator door, the hiss
of a pressure fryer, a dropped tray. He is on high alert for any loud noise, and his startle
response has become so intense that coworkers now avoid surprising him. He has lost count of how
many times he has dropped a tray himself because a sudden bang made him flinch.

The only places Amir feels safe are his apartment and, oddly, the restaurant’s walk-in cooler—a
small, windowless room where the hum of compressors drowns out other sounds. He has started taking
his breaks there, sitting on a crate of lettuce. His occupational life has narrowed: he no longer
works the register, avoids the front counter, and will not go near the spot where Leila fell. His
supervisor has written him up twice for unprofessional behaviour, and a third incident would likely
lead to termination. Amir knows he is close to losing his job, but the idea of finding another
position feels impossible if the same fears follow him.

He finally sought help after a night when a car backfired outside his building and he spent three
hours huddled in his bathroom, certain that someone was coming for him. What he fears most is that
he will never feel normal again—that he is stuck inside a mind that turns every ordinary moment into
a threat. What he hopes for, though he can barely say it aloud, is that talking about what he saw
might loosen its grip, that he might one day walk through the restaurant without feeling the ghost
of that gunshot in his ears.
