Emily is a 28-year-old police officer who lives with her partner in a mid-sized American city. For
the past fourteen months, she has been haunted by an event she cannot fully claim as her own. It
happened late on a night shift when she was still a probationary officer, riding with her training
officer—a man she had trusted to teach her the job, someone she had come to see as a mentor. During
what should have been a routine vehicle stop, the driver became combative, and her training officer,
instead of backing her up, turned on her. He pinned her against the patrol car, slammed her head
against the window, and later told internal affairs that she had escalated the situation. No one
questioned him. The official report made it look like she had panicked and that he had restrained
her. She was reassigned to a quieter precinct, but the damage was done. What Emily remembers most
vividly is the shock of his betrayal—the moment she realized the person meant to protect her was the
one causing harm. Her mind stores that instant in full, overwhelming sensory detail: the cold
pressure of the glass against her cheek, the smell of his cheap cologne and coffee breath, the
grinding sound of her radio as it was crushed beneath his weight. When the memory comes back, and it
does constantly, it feels completely real across every sense at once. She has lost count of the
mornings she has woken up disoriented, convinced she is still on that street corner. And yet, even
as the memory feels that real, it also feels like it belongs to someone else—a stranger she is
watching from far away. That hollow contradiction terrifies her more than the violence itself.

Over the months that followed, Emily’s body began to act as if it had its own agenda. Her heart
pounds at a distant siren, at a raised voice on a radio call, at the sight of a stationary patrol
car with its doors open. She has come to believe that her body is a traitor, forever reacting as if
she is still pinned against that window, even when she is standing safely in her own kitchen. Every
physiological surge feels like proof that danger is imminent, and that belief has spread outward:
the world now seems saturated with threat. She scans every driver she pulls over for concealed
anger, every fellow officer for signs of hidden malice. The station house, once a place of
camaraderie, now feels like a minefield. She no longer eats lunch in the breakroom because she
cannot shake the certainty that someone is watching her, waiting for her to slip. She has stopped
socializing with colleagues entirely, and her relationship with her partner has become strained—she
flinches when he touches her from behind, she cannot explain why, and she has started sleeping in
the guest room because the nightmares jolt her awake screaming.

Emily’s avoidance has become a full-time project. She requested permanent day shifts so she would
not have to drive past the intersection where it happened. She deleted the training officer’s
contact, unfriended everyone who might mention him, and she has not stepped into the precinct that
originally housed her patrol unit in more than a year. The consequences have accumulated quietly:
her supervisors have noted her repeated refusal to partner with certain veteran officers, and her
performance reviews now include remarks about “difficulty trusting team members.” She knows she is
seen as difficult, but the alternative—being vulnerable again—feels unthinkable.

What finally brought Emily to seek help was a moment at work last week. A new officer, young and
eager, asked her for advice during a traffic stop. His posture was relaxed, his voice friendly, and
Emily suddenly saw her former training officer’s face superimposed on his. She froze, her finger
hovering over her radio, drenched in sweat, unable to speak. The officer asked if she was okay, and
she stammered an excuse and walked away. That evening, she told her partner she could not keep
living this way. She is terrified that her body will betray her at a critical moment—that she will
freeze when someone needs her help. She hopes, more than anything, that she can learn to trust her
own senses again, and to believe that the danger is not still happening right now.
