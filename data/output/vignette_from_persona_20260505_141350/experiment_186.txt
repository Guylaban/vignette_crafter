Kwame, a twenty-five-year-old journalist, arrived at the clinic with a quiet, watchful posture that
seemed to take up as little space as possible. He sat at the edge of the chair, his hands resting on
his knees, dressed in a loose black hoodie despite the warm spring afternoon. He had come because
his girlfriend, Maya, had finally convinced him that he could not keep going the way he was. For
months he had been deteriorating, and she was afraid of what might happen if he did not talk to
someone.

The event that changed everything had happened just over eight months ago. Kwame had been assigned
to cover a peaceful protest downtown, one of many that summer. He had his press credentials around
his neck and his camera in hand, moving through the crowd to get shots of the speeches. He
remembered the sound of a song playing from a nearby car—something upbeat, he could not recall the
name now without his stomach tightening—and then the sudden surge of police officers pushing into
the crowd. He had tried to step back, to hold his press ID higher, but a baton caught him across the
ribs and he went down. Another officer grabbed his camera and smashed it on the pavement. Kwame had
curled up, shielding his head, and heard footsteps all around him, boots landing hard and fast. When
it was over, he saw that a colleague, a young photographer named Amara, was not getting up. She had
been hit in the head. She died later that night in the hospital.

In the weeks that followed, Kwame’s world shrank. He stopped going out after dark. He began wearing
baggy, concealing clothing—hoodies, dark jackets, sunglasses even on cloudy days—because he had come
to believe that standing out in any way made him a target. He was certain that if he looked
nondescript enough, no one would notice him, no one would single him out. He avoided unfamiliar
neighborhoods entirely, reasoning that he could not predict the dangers in a place he did not know.
Every time he walked down a street, he found himself glancing over his shoulder, checking behind
him, scanning for footsteps that might be closing in. He could not concentrate at work. Assignments
that used to take him an hour now stretched into three because his mind would snag on a sound—a car
door slamming, a raised voice—and he would lose the thread of his own thoughts. His editor noticed
and pulled him from field assignments, telling him to focus on desk work. Kwame had always been a
reporter who lived for the story; now he felt he was no longer capable of the work that had defined
him.

His memory of that day was strange. When he tried to tell Maya what had happened, the words came out
in fragments. He would start to describe the protest, then the song, then the boots, and then he
would lose the thread entirely, unable to finish a single sentence. But the memory itself did not
feel fragmented; it felt more real and vivid than ordinary memories. Sometimes, when he was alone in
his apartment, he would hear the sound of the police officer’s radio crackling, or the music from
that car, playing inside his head as clearly as if it were happening again. He could not stand
silence because silence seemed to invite those sounds.

Kwame had once been a cheerful, energetic person who loved discovering new parts of the city and
meeting strangers. Now he described himself as miserable, certain that his life had been destroyed.
He avoided anything that reminded him of Amara—her name in an email, a photograph of the two of them
at a previous protest, a news story about police accountability. He also avoided feeling anything at
all, positive or negative, because emotions felt dangerous. He kept himself flat, neutral,
unresponsive. Maya told him she missed the man she had fallen in love with. He knew she was right,
but he did not know how to find him again.

What finally brought Kwame to the clinic was a moment of panic in a grocery store. A customer behind
him dropped a jar, and the sharp sound of glass hitting the floor sent Kwame ducking and shaking. He
fled the store without buying anything. Later that night, Maya held his hand and said he could not
live like this anymore. He agreed, because he was terrified that if he did not change, he would lose
her, and he would lose himself for good. He hoped, more than anything, that someone could help him
feel safe in his own skin again. But he was also afraid that the man he used to be—the journalist
who walked into any crowd without hesitation—was gone forever, and that no amount of therapy would
bring him back.
