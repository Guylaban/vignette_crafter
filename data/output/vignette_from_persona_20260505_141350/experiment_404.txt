choke in her throat. She managed to stand and stumble to the front, but by the time she reached her
stop, she felt as though something essential inside her had been ripped away.

In the weeks that followed, every bus that passed her stop made her stomach clench. She started
driving herself to work—a twenty-mile detour that cost her in gas money and time—because the very
thought of that narrow seat, the close heat of another passenger, sent her heart into her throat.
But the bus was only the first of many traps. A conversation with a neighbor about safety on the
roads, a mention of “stranger danger” on the evening news, even a casual remark from Tom about her
being late—each of these could drop her, without warning, back into that afternoon. She began to
notice a pattern: before any situation she had to face, she would run through worst-case
possibilities in her head. If she had to go to the grocery store, she imagined someone grabbing her
in the parking lot. If Tom suggested they visit his sister, she pictured being cornered at the
dinner table. It exhausted her, but she could not stop. The rehearsals felt like the only way to be
ready.

during breaks. She came to believe that she could not rely on herself anymore—that she had not been
able to stop the assault, and therefore she could not trust her own judgment or strength to keep

her own kitchen, with Tom reading the paper at the table, she felt set apart, as though a pane of
glass separated her from everyone else.

hypervigilance wore grooves into her days. Tom noticed her flinch when he touched her shoulder; he
