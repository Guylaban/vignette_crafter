Keoni was seventy-one and had painted his whole life—houses, storefronts, the occasional mural. He
worked alone, a ladder and a bucket his only company, and he liked it that way. Then came the
morning of the bombing. He had been on the sidewalk outside a municipal building, touching up trim
along the second floor, when a pressure cooker packed with nails detonated a block away. The shock
wave hit him mid-swing. He lost his grip on the brush, his feet slipped from the rung, and he landed
hard on his left side, knees drawn toward his chest, arms wrapping his head. He stayed like that for
what felt like minutes, ears ringing, feeling the heat gust over him, listening to the wet slap of
debris against cars. Someone was screaming. He could not move. He could not make himself move.

In the months afterward, Keoni did not sleep through a single night. The memory did not come as a
story he told himself but as a physical takeover. He would be standing at the sink washing a brush
and suddenly his body would contract—his knees pulling up, arms folding, ribs pressing against his
thighs—as though the pavement had caught him again. He could feel the exact pressure on his hip, the
gravel digging into his palm, the smell of burned rubber and dust. The memory was bright and quick,
and it forced its way in without warning. He learned to hate silence, because silence gave it room.

He began to read the world differently. The street that had once felt like a familiar path now
seemed lined with threats: a parked van with its engine running, a discarded backpack, a man in a
heavy coat on a warm day. Other people, he decided, could not be trusted to keep him safe, because
no one had kept him safe that morning. Worse, he could not trust himself. He had frozen. He had lain
there curled like a child while the air filled with noise. He had not run. He had not helped. If a
man could not count on his own legs to carry him, what could he count on?

The easiest solution was to stop going out. Keoni turned down every job that required him to leave
his apartment. He had a small stash of savings and a few indoor canvases he could touch up, but he
stopped answering calls from clients. When his nephew stopped by to check on him, Keoni would ask
the same question two or three times: *I’m okay, right? No, I mean, really—I’m okay?* His nephew
would say yes, and Keoni would feel a moment of relief, then doubt, then ask again. He tried to keep
his feelings locked away, because once they started they did not stop. He would paint for hours
without music, without the radio, because any sound could pull the memory back. He painted the same
wall over and over, covering it in a flat white that meant nothing.

The consequence was quiet and cumulative. His sister stopped visiting as often. His nephew stopped
calling. His savings thinned. He had not stepped outside in eleven days by the time he finally
called a clinic someone had mentioned. He was terrified of the session itself—terrified of the
street he would have to cross, the stranger he would have to talk to—but he was more afraid of the
feeling that he was shrinking inside his own home, becoming a smaller and smaller man in a smaller
and smaller room. He did not know if talking would make the memories stop forcing their way in, but
he knew that staying still was no longer working.
