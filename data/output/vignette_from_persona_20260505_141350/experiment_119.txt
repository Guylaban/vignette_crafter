Kwame, a 33-year-old social media manager, had spent two years as a relief worker in a conflict zone
in the Horn of Africa. The event that shattered him happened on a Tuesday afternoon. He was riding
in a clearly marked UN supply convoy when it was ambushed by militia fighters. The attack was swift
and methodical—gunfire erupted from both sides, the lead vehicle exploded, and for nearly twenty
minutes Kwame lay pinned beneath a flipped truck, listening to the screams of colleagues he had
shared meals with that morning. When the firing stopped, he was the only survivor from his vehicle.
The militia had moved on, leaving the dead where they fell. No one ever claimed responsibility. No
investigation followed. The aid organization evacuated him within seventy-two hours, and he was back
in the United States before the bodies had been identified.

For the first few weeks, Kwame told himself he was fine. He returned to work, resumed his routines,
and said nothing about the ambush to anyone except his wife, Nia. But the images would not stay
buried. He learned to push them away by detaching—he would stare at a wall until his mind went
blank, or he would mentally recite the names of every social media platform he managed until the
scenes faded. He refused to let himself think about the ambush deliberately; if a memory surfaced,
he would shake his head and find something else to do. He also began scanning every room he entered,
checking exit points, watching strangers’ hands, noting anyone who moved too quickly. At his desk,
he kept his chair facing the door. In restaurants, he sat with his back to a wall. He was certain
that danger could come from anywhere, that the world was full of people who would hurt him without
reason and face no consequences—because he had seen it happen.

The hypervigilance became exhausting. He found himself startling at the sound of a car backfiring,
at a colleague tapping his shoulder, at the ding of a notification on his phone if the tone was too
sharp. His concentration suffered: he would read a client’s brief three times and still miss key
details, or he would spend an hour staring at a blinking cursor while his mind raced through
contingency plans for an attack that would never come. Nia noticed that he had stopped watching the
news with her. When she asked why, he said it was because everything felt staged and pointless. What
he did not say was that he could not bear to see reports from the region—any image of a dusty road
or a military vehicle pulled him straight back under the truck.

His sense of injustice had calcified into something bitter and immovable. The men who had killed his
colleagues were still out there, probably doing the same thing to other convoys. No one had been
held accountable. No one had even tried. And Kwame had begun to believe that he could not rely on
himself anymore. He had frozen during the attack, had lain there useless while people died around
him. If he could not trust his own instincts and reactions in that moment, how could he trust them
now? This thought gnawed at him whenever he had to make a quick decision at work—which post to
approve, how to respond to a PR crisis—and he found himself second-guessing every choice, then
blaming himself for the hesitation.

The consequences accumulated slowly. At the office, his manager had begun to wonder why Kwame, once
the most creative person on the team, now produced flat, repetitive content and missed deadlines. He
had started avoiding meetings where the topic might touch on international affairs, claiming
scheduling conflicts. He had also deleted the company’s news-tracking app from his phone after a
headline about aid workers triggered an hour-long panic. His marriage had grown strained: Nia felt
he was living in a fortress she could not enter, and she had stopped asking about his day because he
would either deflect or sit in silence.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was a small moment. Last week, while scrolling through
Instagram for campaign ideas, he saw a photo of a red dust trail in a landscape that resembled the
convoy route. He immediately closed the app, left his desk, and walked three blocks before he
realized he was not wearing his shoes. He called a therapist from the sidewalk. Now, in her office,
he is terrified that talking about the ambush will shatter the fragile compartment he has built
around it. But he is more terrified of losing his marriage and his job—and of spending the rest of
his life watching for threats that never come, certain that no one will answer for what happened,
and unsure whether he can ever trust himself again.
