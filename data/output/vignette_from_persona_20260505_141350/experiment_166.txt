clatter of a coffee mug hitting the breakroom counter sending a jolt through his chest. His heart
hammered, his palms damp, and for a moment the fluorescent lights seemed to dim—not because they
actually did, but because his mind had dropped him somewhere else, somewhere he could not put into
words. He was thirty-six years old, divorced, living alone in a suburban apartment that felt more
like a bunker than a home. He had come to therapy at his boss’s insistence after that morning’s
incident—a colleague shouting across the floor had triggered something so visceral that Ravi had
ended up in the bathroom, hands braced against the sink, gasping for air. He could not explain what
had happened. He could not explain any of it.

The event that had shaped him happened when he was eight. He had been sitting at the top of the
stairs in his family’s house in New Jersey, listening to his father’s voice rise and then the sharp
crack of a hand against flesh, his mother’s cry. He had frozen, unable to move, unable to call out.
The memory never came back as a story he could tell; it came as a picture—the pattern on the stair
runner, the smell of curry and sweat, the thud of a body hitting a wall—and with it the same terror,
undimmed by decades. He could not find words for it. When his therapist asked what he remembered,
Ravi shook his head and said, “I just see it. I feel it.” That was all.

In the years that followed, Ravi learned to keep watch. At work, he chose a desk with his back to
the wall, his eyes scanning the corridor every time someone passed. He flinched at raised voices in
meetings and felt his body lock into a protective tension that never fully released. He was
constantly aware of exits, of sudden movements, of the pitch of a conversation shifting toward
anger. His colleagues thought he was just intense, maybe anxious. They did not know that every small
reminder—the slam of a door, the scrape of a chair, a man’s voice booming in laughter—could
instantaneously, without warning, drop him back onto those stairs. He could not talk about any of
it. The words felt stuck.

His marriage had ended two years ago. His wife had described him as emotionally unreachable, always
on guard, never able to relax. Ravi had not argued. He knew she was right, but he also knew that his
body did not give him a choice. He had come to believe that bad things simply happened, and that he
could do nothing to stop them—not then, not now. That helplessness had seeped into everything: his
job, his relationships, his sense of himself. He used to be a happy person, he remembered that
dimly; now misery felt like his baseline, a gray wash over every day. And what gnawed at him most
was the injustice—the fact that his father had faced no consequences, had never been held
accountable. That thought simmered constantly, an ember of anger that he could not put out.

Avoidance had become a quiet habit. He stopped attending family gatherings where his father might
appear. He changed the subject whenever conversations turned to childhood. He avoided any movie or
show with domestic conflict. The consequence was a narrowing life—fewer friends, longer silences, a
growing dread that he was failing at being human. His divorce had been the first major loss; now his
job performance was slipping. He could not concentrate, could not sit still, was always scanning,
always bracing.

What finally brought Ravi to the office of a clinical psychologist was that morning’s attack: the
shout from across the floor, the rush of adrenaline, the complete loss of control. His boss had

the card numbly. Now, sitting in the therapist’s chair, he was afraid—afraid that he would never
feel safe, that the happiness he once knew was gone for good, that he would always be this coiled,
watchful person. But he was also, beneath that fear, searching for something: a way to stop the
triggers from hijacking him, to make sense of the wordless terror, to believe, just once, that the
next bad thing did not have to happen.
