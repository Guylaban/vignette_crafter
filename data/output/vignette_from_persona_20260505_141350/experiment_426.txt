was necessary. Before the deployment, he had enjoyed the open-plan office, the background hum of
conversation, the rhythm of colleagues moving past his desk. That felt like another life. The one
that mattered now began on a reconnaissance mission in a dusty, indistinct valley, when the vehicle
ahead of him hit an improvised explosive device. He was thrown from his own Humvee, the blast
tearing away most of his gear and shredding the upper half of his uniform. Dazed, half-blind with
dust, he found himself sprawled on the ground, his torso exposed to the sun and to whatever else
might be coming. Then he heard it: the high-pitched, sputtering drone of a motorcycle engine
approaching from the east, growing louder. He could smell diesel and something acrid—burning rubber,
or maybe flesh—and he lay there, unable to move, convinced that the next moments would be his last.
He was certain that being half-naked, without even a weapon within reach, made him nothing more than
a target. The motorcycle did not stop; it passed on some parallel track, but the sound stayed inside
his head like a splinter.

For months after returning home, Minh told no one about those seconds. He could not find the words
to describe what had happened—when his wife, Leanne, asked how he was, he said “fine” and turned
away, because the event itself refused to be shaped into sentences. It came back to him as a loop, a
film that played without his permission: the blast, the sky tumbling, the exposed skin on his chest,
and then that engine noise, always the same, always building toward something that never came but
might next time. He began to notice that certain smells—gasoline, charred food, even the faint
metallic scent of a freshly opened can—could make him stop mid-sentence, his heart thudding against
his ribs. He stopped watching television altogether, because any programme that showed combat or
even a news report about an overseas conflict would bring the loop roaring back. He unsubscribed
from streaming services, then stopped checking the news online. When Leanne wanted to watch a drama
set in a war zone, he left the room and sat in the dark of the garage until she finished.

The avoidance spread. Going out became a calculation of risk. He avoided unfamiliar places because,
as he told himself, he could not assess the danger without knowing every exit, every possible hiding
spot, every source of a sudden sound. Even a trip to a new grocery store felt like walking into an
ambush. When strangers approached him—a cashier who stepped too close, a jogger who called out from
behind—he felt a spike of alertness that was hard to control. He began to work from home, citing a
need for focus, but the truth was that commuting meant passing construction sites with loud
machinery, and the office itself had become unbearable: the open layout left too many approaches,
too many people he could not see coming. He also stopped exercising. Running had once been his
outlet, but now the pounding of his own feet, the heavy breathing, and the sensation of his heart
racing all felt too close to the panic of that day. He used to run on a trail near his house, but
after a cyclist passed him from behind without warning, he quit entirely.

At home he remained on guard. He sat with his back to a wall, checked the locks repeatedly, and woke
at every creak of the house settling. He knew, rationally, that the suburbs were quiet, but
rationality had little to do with what his body did. He would catch himself listening for a
motorcycle engine that wasn’t there, then hearing it anyway in the static of his own memory. His
marriage was strained: Leanne wanted to go out to dinner, visit friends in a neighbouring town, take
a weekend trip—none of which he could tolerate. He saw the disappointment in her eyes and understood
that he was failing her, but the thought of an unfamiliar restaurant, unpredictable smells,
strangers at nearby tables, and the long drive home after dark was enough to flood him with dread.

What finally brought Minh to seek help was a Tuesday evening when he could not bring himself to open
the front door to retrieve a package. He stood in the hallway, hands trembling, the cardboard box
only ten feet away, while a neighbour walked a dog past the driveway. Minh could not make his legs
move. After ten minutes he asked Leanne to get it, and she did, silently. That night she told him
she loved him but she could not watch him shrink any further. Minh agreed to come to therapy, though
he was terrified that talking about what happened would only make the film play louder. What he
hoped for, still too fragile to say aloud, was a single day when the smell of diesel might not feel
like the start of an ending.
