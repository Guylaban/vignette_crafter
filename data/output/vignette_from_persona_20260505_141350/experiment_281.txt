Marta, a seventy-four-year-old physical therapist, had spent decades helping others rebuild strength
after injury, but she could not rebuild her own sense of safety. She had been seven years old when
her family fled Hungary in 1956, during the Soviet invasion. She remembered the soldiers’ boots on
the cobblestones, the sharp, acrid smell of burning buildings that clung to everything, and the
heavy, sweet stench of cheap alcohol on the breath of a man who had grabbed her mother’s arm. Those
smells had not stayed in the past. They lived in her nose, ready to ambush her at any moment.

Decades later, Marta worked at a suburban rehabilitation clinic, guiding elderly patients through
gentle exercises. She was good at her job—patients appreciated her patience—but she had secret
rituals that kept her alert. Every time the clinic door opened, she would scan the waiting room,
noting each face, each exit. If a new patient approached too quickly, her chest would tighten and
she would step back, her hand finding the wall. She knew this was not rational; these were frail,
elderly people, not soldiers. But her body did not care about rationality. When a man with a worn
leather jacket and a faint smell of smoke walked into her office, she had to excuse herself to the
bathroom, where she splashed water on her face and told herself she was safe.

At home, Marta would listen. She would sit in the dark living room after her husband had gone to
bed, straining to hear footsteps in the driveway, the click of a lock being forced. She had
memorized the creak of every floorboard. Her husband had long stopped asking why she always checked
the deadbolt twice, then three times. He knew better than to comment on the half-full bottles of
whiskey she kept in the kitchen cabinet, the ones she told herself were for cooking. Some nights,
after a particularly vivid flash—the crack of a gunshot, a child’s scream that was not hers but felt
like it—she would pour herself a drink, not to celebrate, but to quiet the noise. She had come to
believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous, that danger could appear anywhere, disguised as
an ordinary moment.

The worst moments were when the memory did not feel like a memory at all. Lying in bed, she would
hear the thud of boots and the clatter of a tank’s tracks, sounds so clear she would jerk upright,
her heart pounding, convinced the invasion was happening again. She would see the grey sky above the
burning street, the flames licking the corners of her vision. Her husband would find her standing in
the hallway, drenched in sweat, insisting she had heard something. He would gently guide her back to
bed, but Marta knew he did not understand. For her, the past was not over. It was always waiting.

She had tried not to talk about it. If a colleague asked how she had come to the United States, she
would change the subject. She could not bear to describe the boat, the uncertainty, the way her
mother had held her so tightly it hurt. But the avoidance had consequences. At work, she had begun
to refuse certain referrals—patients with burn injuries, or anyone who smelled strongly of smoke.
She made excuses, saying she was too busy, but her supervisor had started to notice. At home, her
husband had grown distant, tired of her vigilance, her drinking, her silences. She felt herself
shrinking, becoming a stranger to the woman she had been before—the girl who had run through
sunflower fields, unafraid.

What finally brought Marta to seek help was a Tuesday afternoon in the clinic. A patient’s family
member arrived wearing a heavy cologne that smelled, to her, exactly like the alcohol on that
soldier’s breath. Marta froze. She could not speak. She excused herself and locked herself in the
staff bathroom, trembling, unable to stop the images. When she came out, she called a number her
primary care doctor had given her months ago. She was terrified—terrified that talking about the
event would make it worse, terrified that she was losing control for good. But she was also hopeful,
for the first time in years, that maybe it did not have to always feel like 1956.
