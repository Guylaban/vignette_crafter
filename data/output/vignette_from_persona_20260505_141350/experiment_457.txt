Megan, a 41-year-old firefighter with fourteen years on the job, had always taken pride in her
physical resilience. Then came the meningitis. What began as a blinding headache and stiff neck
spiraled into septic shock, a ten‑day ICU stay, and a series of lumbar punctures and central lines
that she could never bring herself to describe aloud. When she tried, the words dissolved into a
surge of heat, a tightness in her chest, and an involuntary stillness—her body locking into the same
prone, helpless position she had occupied on that hospital bed, watching the fluorescent lights
flicker above while a nurse adjusted the IV drip. The terror that washed over her then, raw and
wordless, returned intact each time a memory surfaced, as if no time had passed at all.

Returning to work six months later, Megan found that the station itself had become a minefield. The
bleach-based cleaner used to disinfect the bunker gear carried a faint, hospital-like odor that made
her stomach clench and her palms sweat whenever she walked past the decontamination bay. She began
to scan her environment constantly—for the sharp gleam of a needle, for the sight of an ambulance
pulling into the bay, for any sound that might signal a medical call. Her body stayed wired, muscles
braced, ready to react, even during quiet nights at the station. Every routine health log reading or
minor cough among her crew sharpened her vigilance; she was certain, on some level, that any sign of
physical compromise could herald another collapse.

She started to avoid emotional intensity entirely. When a fellow firefighter shared a difficult
story from a call, Megan would nod and excuse herself, finding a task in the apparatus bay. At home
with her husband, she deflected conversations about her recovery—redirecting her mind to the next
meal, the schedule for the week, anything to block the images before they fully formed. She also
avoided medical cues with a discipline that had begun to erode her professional standing. On medical
emergency dispatches, she would offer to swap with a colleague for a fire run, or she’d linger in
the engine bay until the ambulance left without her. Twice she was reprimanded for not taking her
turn on paramedic training; she explained it away as “bad timing,” but her captain noticed the
pattern. The avoidance extended to her own health: she skipped routine physicals and ignored a
lingering fatigue, rationalizing that the hospital waiting room would trigger a panic she could not
afford.

The most difficult consequence was the growing distance from her husband. He had been her constant
support during the illness, but now Megan pulled away from any conversation that risked strong
emotion. She preferred quiet evenings with the television on, her mind trained to drift elsewhere.
He tried to ask about how she was feeling; she gave short answers and moved to another room. She
knew he was hurt, but the alternative—letting herself feel the fear that hummed beneath her
skin—felt far more dangerous.

What finally brought Megan to the department’s employee assistance program was a routine training
drill. A mannequin was set up with an IV line and a simulated wound. The moment Megan saw the
tubing, the antiseptic smell of the training room hit her, and her body snapped into that same
hospital‑bed stillness. She could not move or speak; she only felt her heart hammering against her
ribs, the old terror flooding back. The training officer had to guide her outside. Her captain told
her gently that she needed to talk to someone.

Now Megan sits in the clinician’s office, unable to put her experience into words. She is afraid
that the simple act of speaking will break something open inside her, yet she is equally afraid of
continuing to live this way—always braced, always scanning, always pulling away from the people she
loves most. She wants to feel safe again, even if she is not sure what safety looks like anymore.
