Amir was a dancer before the war, and he remained a dancer afterward, though the word no longer
meant the same thing. He had been a civilian in a city that became a battlefield—not a soldier, not
a target, just a man who happened to be home when the shelling started. What he remembered most
clearly was not the blast itself but the seconds that followed: the dust settling, the smell of
overturned earth and burned stone filling his nostrils, and then the name—a neighbor’s name, called
out in panic—repeated over and over until it became meaningless sound. That smell, that name, the
particular quality of a sudden percussive noise—these were the details that stayed, not as memories
but as raw sensations that could surface anywhere.

In the months after he left the war zone and resettled in the United States, Amir tried to rebuild
his life. He found work with a small contemporary dance company, teaching classes and performing.
The studio should have felt safe. Instead, it became a place where triggers arrived without warning.
A dancer dropping a heavy prop onto the floor—a sound like a mortar round—would make him freeze mid-
movement, his heart hammering, waiting for something that never came. The smell of wet soil after a
rainstorm, drifting in through an open window during rehearsal, would transport him back to that
moment of crouching in rubble. And hearing someone speak a name that resembled the
neighbor’s—*Samir*, *Jamal*, anything with two syllables and a falling pitch—would make his stomach
turn as if the name itself carried shrapnel.

Amir had come to believe that his trauma was written on his face. He was convinced that other
dancers, his wife, even strangers on the street could look at him and know exactly what he had been
through. He caught himself avoiding eye contact during warm-ups, standing at the back of the studio,
speaking only when necessary. His wife noticed that he no longer talked about his day, no longer
reached for her at night. When she asked what was wrong, he said he felt dead inside—and meant it.
There was a space inside him where feeling used to live, and now it was empty, like a bombed-out
building. He had lost faith in people, in the idea that life was fair, in any higher power that
might have watched what happened and done nothing.

Over the weeks and months, avoidance became a habit. He stopped walking through the park because the
soil smell was too strong. He turned off the news because the sound of a helicopter or a distant
siren made his chest tighten. He stopped using his phone when his wife was nearby, afraid she might
hear a voice message from someone with a triggering name. The consequence was a slow erosion of
connection. His wife began to sleep in the guest room. At work, his artistic director commented that
his performances had become mechanical, that he seemed to be going through the motions without
emotion. Amir did not argue. Emotion was precisely what he was missing.

What finally brought him to seek help was a panic attack during a rehearsal. A door slammed—not
loud, just the ordinary heavy sound of a fire door—and he found himself on the floor, shaking,
unable to speak, while his colleagues stared. He saw their faces and was certain they could see
everything: the rubble, the dust, the name. Afterward, he called a therapist. He was afraid of what
therapy might uncover—afraid that if he let himself feel, the numbness might break and he would
drown. But he was also afraid of staying where he was: a dancer who could not move through a simple
sound, a husband who could not touch his wife, a man who carried a war he could not put down.
