of his cry and the sight of his leg twisted beneath him, but the seconds just before the contact are
missing. No matter how hard she tries, she cannot bring back the image of him stepping into her
path. The first thing she recalls clearly is the flashlight beam trembling in her hand as she knelt
beside him, blood seeping through his pant leg.

In the weeks that followed, while the man underwent two surgeries to repair his shattered tibia,
Leilani’s mind refused to leave the alley. She found herself replaying the same sequence over and
over, trying to pinpoint the exact moment she should have seen him, the fraction of a second where
she could have braked harder, turned sooner. Why had she not used the high-beam setting? Why had she
taken that route at all? She would lie awake at night, her thoughts circling through every detail
she could still access: the cart’s speed, the angle of the headlights, the pattern of shadows. She
tried to make sense of the event, to find a reason it had happened to her, as if understanding might
lift the weight from her chest. But the more she examined the memory, the stranger it became.
Sometimes, when she described the accident to her supervisor, she heard her own voice as if from a
distance, flat and detached, recounting facts that felt as though they belonged to a stranger. At
other moments, the whole scene surfaced with a jolt of numbness rather than terror—she could recall
the impact, the shouting, the blood, but felt nothing, as if she were watching a movie about someone
else’s mistake.

transfer to the front gate, where her primary task was checking visitor IDs and waving trucks

she sat in a small booth, her hands idle, watching other guards handle the patrol routes. The
silence during her shifts gave her too much time to herself, and her thoughts would drift back to
the alley, and she would start again the slow, painful process of searching her mind for the missing
moments. She began to avoid the break room, worried that she might overhear colleagues discussing
the accident or exchanging awkward glances. She ate lunch alone in her booth, her sandwich
untouched.

kept conversations short. She stopped accepting invitations to after-shift coffee. At home, her
apartment felt emptier than before. She had never married, and her few close friends had drifted
away over the years, so there was no one to tell about the nightmares that had started to creep into
her sleep—fragments of the accident replayed in distorted sequences, ending with her waking in a

missing seconds.

What finally brought Leilani through the clinic door was a weekend shift where a delivery driver

real—hers—so she could finally begin to live with it. But she was also terrified that if she truly
looked at what she had done, she would find herself unforgivable.
