Mateo is twenty-five years old, a data scientist at a mid-sized analytics firm, and the single
father of a four-year-old daughter named Sofia. He comes to therapy because he can no longer pretend
that the world feels safe. The trouble began eighteen months ago, when Sofia was rushed to the
emergency room with septic shock secondary to a severe respiratory infection. Those first hours
remain vivid in a fractured, unhinged way: the ambulance siren screaming outside his apartment, the
paramedics’ bodies close to his, the sour smell of sweat and antiseptic rising off their uniforms as
they worked over his daughter. He remembers crying—his own sobs, a stranger’s—but he cannot put the
sequence in order. The memory feels more like a nightmare than something that actually happened to
him, even though he knows it did. What terrifies him most is that when he lets himself recall that
night, the terror is not past; it is present, as if he is still standing in that bright, sterile
hallway.

In the months since Sofia’s full recovery, Mateo has built a careful, invisible structure around his
life to keep the memory at a distance. He never lets himself feel anger. When a colleague snaps at
him during a meeting or when his mother criticises his parenting, he feels a hot pressure behind his
ribs and immediately pushes it down—anger is too close to the panic he felt in the hospital, and he
is convinced that if he lets it out, he will lose control completely. He also tries hard not to
think about the trauma at all. He changes the subject when friends mention illness or hospitals. He
avoids driving past the hospital where Sofia was treated, taking a longer route to work even if it
costs him twenty minutes. At his desk, surrounded by screens and data, he can usually keep his mind
on code, but triggers break through anyway. The wail of a siren from the street outside his office
building makes him drop his coffee. A coworker’s sudden crying on a phone call in the next cubicle
sends his heart slamming against his ribs; he pulls on his headphones and clenches his jaw until it
passes. The scent of sweat in the elevator after someone’s morning run makes him nauseous and dizzy.

His body is on permanent alert. Ordinary sounds—a chair scraping, the beep of a printer, a car
horn—make him flinch hard enough that people have noticed and asked if he is okay. He has trouble
falling asleep because his ears strain for any noise from Sofia’s room, as though he must stay
watchful for the first sign of distress. When something does remind him of the hospital, his hands
shake and he breaks into a cold sweat, even if the reminder is just a phrase in a TV show. He has
become expert at avoiding medical cues. He skipped Sofia’s last two well-child checkups because the
waiting room smelled like the same antiseptic and the sight of a blood pressure cuff made his throat
close. He told himself it was not a big deal, that she was healthy, but he knows deep down that he
is failing to take care of her properly.

What finally brought him to therapy was an incident two weeks ago. He was walking Sofia to daycare
when an ambulance screamed past. Mateo froze in the middle of the sidewalk, his daughter’s hand in
his, unable to move or speak for nearly a minute while she tugged at his arm and asked why he was
stopping. He managed to get her to daycare and then sat in his car, shaking, the terror so fresh it
was as though the hospital night had never ended. He realised that he was now afraid of the very
things that were supposed to keep his child safe. He is terrified that his vigilance will never turn
off, and that he will always be one sound away from falling apart. What he hopes for, though he
barely dares to say it aloud, is that he might one day hear a siren and not believe it is coming for
his daughter.
