Harold had been a widower for eleven months, though it felt to him as though he had been suspended
somewhere outside of time since the moment the call came. His wife, Linda, had been admitted to the
hospital for what they both thought was a routine pneumonia; within forty-eight hours she was on a
ventilator, and by the third morning she was gone. The doctor’s words on the phone—“we did
everything we could”—still arrived in Harold’s mind as a complete, unbidden sentence, often in the
middle of a spreadsheet or during a client lunch. He had not chosen to replay that call; the memory
simply appeared, and when it did, his chest would tighten and his hands would grow damp against the
keyboard.

He worked as a financial analyst at a mid-sized firm, spending most of his day in a cubicle
reviewing portfolios and preparing quarterly reports. The work itself was a relief—numbers required
no emotion—but the environment was not safe from intrusion. A colleague might mention, in passing,
that a relative had lost a spouse, and Harold would feel his face go hot and his throat close. He
had learned to excuse himself to the restroom, where he would stand with his palms on the cool sink,
breathing slowly until the feeling passed. Twice he had snapped at a junior analyst who asked too
many questions about a client’s retirement timeline, the anger flaring out of nowhere and leaving
him ashamed. He could not explain why the mere word “loss” made him want to punch a wall.

At home, Harold had developed a careful rhythm. He had moved Linda’s photograph from the living room
mantel to a drawer in the study, because catching sight of it unexpectedly would trigger a cascade
of physical reactions—his heart racing, a knot in his stomach, a sense of being underwater. He no
longer watched television shows that involved hospitals or funerals, and he had stopped attending
the monthly neighborhood gatherings where someone always brought up a deceased relative. When a
friend from church called to invite him to a memorial service for a mutual acquaintance, Harold said
he was busy, then felt a wash of guilt that he quickly suppressed by turning his attention to tax
forms. He had become expert at steering his thoughts away from the event itself. But the effort was
constant, and it left him exhausted.

The most concrete consequence had emerged at work. His annual review noted that he had become
“brusque with colleagues” and “less collaborative on team projects.” His supervisor had politely
suggested he take a few days off. Harold had not told anyone the reason for his irritability; he was
certain that if he admitted how much the memories still invaded his day, people would think he was
weak or, worse, that he should have been over this by now. So he said nothing, and he continued to
suppress, continued to avoid, continued to feel the tightness in his chest whenever a conversation
drifted toward grief.

What finally brought him to seek help was a Friday afternoon at a client’s office. The client, a
seventy-two-year-old widow, began to describe the process of settling her late husband’s estate.
Harold felt the familiar wave of physical reactivity—his face flushed, his palms sweated, his jaw
clenched—and before he could stop himself, he cut her off sharply, saying, “I don’t need the
details,” in a tone that made the client blink and fall silent. He apologized later, but the
incident stayed with him. Driving home, he realized he could not continue this way. He was afraid of
what he might do or say next, afraid of losing the professional reputation he had built over four

remember her. He wanted the intrusive memories to stop, but he also wanted, he admitted to himself
for the first time, to be able to think of her without terror. That hope—small and fragile—had led
him to call a therapist. He sat in the waiting room with his hands folded, uncertain what he would
say, but certain that the way he was living now was unbearable.
