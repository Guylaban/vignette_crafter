Arun Patel was thirty-two years old and had worked as a housekeeper at a mid-sized hotel in Chicago
for nearly six years. It was steady work, cleaning guest rooms and public areas, a job that required
him to be invisible, efficient, and alone for long stretches. That aloneness had always felt
neutral, even comfortable. That changed on a November night after his shift ended at eleven. He was
walking through the employee parking lot, a gravel expanse behind the hotel’s kitchen, when a man
stepped out from between two parked vans. The man was young, maybe early twenties, and he moved
fast. Before Arun could react, the man grabbed Arun’s left arm, twisting it behind his back, and
pressed something hard and sharp into his ribs on the right side. A knife. Arun could feel the point
through his uniform shirt, just below his ribcage. The man said something, but Arun’s mind went
white. He fumbled for his wallet, handed it over. Then the man punched him hard in the stomach,
doubling him over, and ran. Arun sat on the cold gravel for what felt like a long time, one hand
pressed to his ribs, the other clutching his empty pocket.

In the months that followed, Arun found that the memory of that night returned with a clarity that
felt almost like reliving it. He could see the man’s jacket, the pattern of the gravel, the exact
angle of the streetlight. But the clearest part was the physical sensation. Whenever something
reminded him—a sudden movement in his peripheral vision, the sound of footsteps behind him, even the
smell of the hotel’s kitchen grease—he would feel a sharp, stabbing pain in his right side, exactly
where the knife had pressed. The left arm would ache, as if still twisted. His stomach would clench.
He knew what had happened, could replay it in his mind in perfect detail, but when his wife, Priya,
asked him to describe it, he found himself unable. The words would not come. He would open his mouth
and nothing would form, as if the memory was locked behind a door that had no handle.

Arun had always been a fairly trusting person. He believed in giving people the benefit of the
doubt. After the mugging, that belief crumbled. He began to see threat everywhere. The world felt
like a stage where anyone—a guest checking in, a delivery driver, a colleague he’d known for
years—could turn dangerous without warning. People were not what they seemed, he decided. They were
hiding intentions, waiting for an opportunity. He trusted no one, least of all strangers. This
conviction grew stronger each time he had to enter a hotel room to clean. Every closed door felt
like an ambush. He started knocking and waiting longer before entering, listening for footsteps. He
began to avoid certain tasks: cleaning the parking lot, taking out trash at night, working the late
shift. He traded shifts with a coworker, which cost him a small favor and ate into his sleep because
he now had to work early mornings instead. His supervisor noticed he was slower, more hesitant, but
Arun could not explain why. When Priya asked why he seemed so jumpy, he said nothing. He could not
find the words, and even if he could, he was not sure she would believe him. He had begun to feel
that everyone, including her, was capable of turning on him.

The avoidance spread. He stopped walking to the drugstore after dark. He stopped taking the train if
there were fewer than three other people in the car. He began to check the locks on their apartment
three times before bed. Priya grew frustrated. She said he was becoming a different person, distant
and irritable. He knew she was right, but he could not stop. What frightened him most was not the
memory itself but the certainty that the next attack was only a matter of time. He scanned every
face, every shadow. The world was a dangerous place, and he was not strong enough to survive again.

It was Priya who finally convinced him to see a doctor, after he flinched violently when she touched
his arm from behind. He agreed mostly because he was exhausted. He had not had a full night’s sleep
in weeks. The pain in his side came and went unpredictably. He hoped someone could tell him how to
stop feeling like a target. He was afraid of never being able to trust anyone again—and more than
that, he was afraid of what that mistrust was doing to his marriage, his job, his life. He came to
the clinic not sure if talking would help, but certain that staying silent was no longer possible.
