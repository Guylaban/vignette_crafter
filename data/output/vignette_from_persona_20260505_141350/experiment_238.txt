Michael is a 49-year-old city council member who has been married for over twenty years. He had
served two tours of duty in Iraq, and the memory he carries most vividly is also the memory he
cannot hold together. It comes from his second deployment, when a roadside detonation tore through
the lead vehicle of his convoy. He remembers the heat, the ringing, the shouting—but the seconds
just before and just after are a smear of black static. He can see a hand, someone’s hand, reaching
for him through the smoke, and he knows that hand belonged to a man who did not make it, but he
cannot say for sure if he reached back. When he tries to tell his wife about it, he loses the thread
mid-sentence, his mouth going dry, and he shakes his head and walks away. The terror that seizes him
in those moments is the same terror he felt in the dust and noise—it has never dimmed or faded.

At home, Michael keeps the television on almost constantly, volume high enough to fill the rooms. He
does this because quiet leaves space for his own thoughts, and his own thoughts frighten him. He has
come to believe that if he does not monitor what passes through his mind, he might drift into
something uncontrollable—a panic, a breakdown, perhaps even a violent outburst. He checks himself
all day, scanning for any thought that feels unhinged, any flicker of anger or disorientation that
could be a sign he is going crazy. At night, sleep rarely comes easily, and when it does it is
broken; often he lies awake staring at the ceiling, trying to keep his mind blank.

The world outside his front door feels like a threat he cannot lower his guard against. Even when
his wife tells him the neighborhood is safe, that the city is quiet, he finds it impossible to
believe her. Danger feels latent, everywhere, and he has to be vigilant at all times. His body seems
to know this before his mind does: if his wife touches him from behind at the kitchen counter, he
flinches violently, his heart slamming. He has started avoiding all physical contact with others. At
city council meetings, he positions himself so that no one can approach him from the back. He no
longer shakes hands with constituents, offering instead a tight nod and a step back. Some people
have noticed, have asked if he is feeling unwell, and he makes vague excuses about a back injury.

The consequences have crept up slowly. He used to enjoy the public forums and town halls, the work
of listening to people’s concerns. Now he dreads them. The crowded rooms, the sudden brushes of
shoulders, the unexpected hand on his arm from a well-meaning supporter—each sends a jolt through
him that takes minutes to settle. He has delegated two committee meetings to a deputy this month,
and last week he left a school board joint session early because the auditorium was too packed. His
wife has grown quiet around him, careful not to startle him, and he can feel the distance widening
between them though he does not know how to close it.

What finally brought Michael to seek help was a moment at a veteran’s day ceremony. He was standing
at the back of the hall, trying to keep his composure, when an elderly man in a service cap clapped
him on the shoulder without warning. Michael spun around, his arm raised in a defensive motion, and
for a terrible second he saw the man’s face register shock and fear. He apologized profusely, but
the shame stayed with him all day. That night, he told his wife he needed to talk to someone. He is
terrified of what he might discover—that he is damaged beyond repair, that his mind is truly
slipping—but he is also tired of living in a world that feels like a war zone, and he hopes, more
than anything, that someone can show him how to feel safe again.
