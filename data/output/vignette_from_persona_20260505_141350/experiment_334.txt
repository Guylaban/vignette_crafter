council member. She had been standing near the stage at a community unity rally in a park she had

police officer grabbed her arm and pulled her toward a gap in the fence. She ran, but she could not
stop looking over her shoulder. In the weeks that followed, the city council held closed sessions
about the attack—someone had planted a device, a man-made disaster born of political extremism.
Priya attended every meeting, but she sat with her back to the wall, her eyes fixed on the door.

In the first months afterward, she began to notice small changes that she did not connect to the
explosion. She stopped going to the gym because the feeling of her heart pounding during a run
reminded her too much of that night; she had come to believe that any physical stress—sweating,
breathlessness—made her vulnerable, as though her own body might betray her into helplessness. She
started scanning every room before she could focus on a conversation. At council meetings, she would
trace the exits with her gaze before sitting down, and if the only available seat was far from a
door, she would stand instead, even as colleagues motioned for her to take a chair. She became
vigilant toward people, especially men in crowds, watching their hands, their coats, the way they
stood too still. What frightened her most was nightfall. She had come to feel that darkness
collapsed the distance between herself and danger, that the world became something she could not see
clearly enough to trust. She stopped attending evening events—town halls block parties, the annual
lantern festival—and began leaving board meetings early, always before the parking lot emptied. Her
staff noticed that she would circle the block twice before pulling into her own driveway, checking
for anything out of place.

knew what could happen next. She planned escape routes everywhere she went—the grocery store, the

altogether, insisting that council meetings be held only at the main municipal building, where she

being inflexible; Priya did not explain that her heart rate spiked just thinking about walking into
a room she had never seen. Over time, her refusal to attend night events or drive to unfamiliar
neighborhoods cost her relationships with constituents and fellow council members. One senior

constant surveillance of every shadow. She was not sure it was possible, but she called a therapist
the next morning because she could no longer bear being the only one who was watching.
