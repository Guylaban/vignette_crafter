Kofi is a 34-year-old parole officer who came to therapy at his partner’s urging after a colleague
noticed he had started flinching whenever a male parolee entered his office. The trouble had been
building for two years, ever since the night his former boyfriend, Marcus, beat him so badly that
Kofi ended up in the emergency room with a fractured orbital bone and three cracked ribs. He had
gone to the hospital alone, too ashamed to call anyone, and the antiseptic smell of the ER—bleach
and iodine and hand sanitizer—had stayed lodged in his sinuses. Eighteen months later, that same
smell could still ambush him: a whiff of disinfectant in a public restroom, the sting of hand
sanitizer on a coworker’s desk, and suddenly he was back in that trauma bay, the fluorescent lights
too bright, the crunch of his own ribs audible beneath the nurse’s gloved hands. He would taste the
metallic tang of blood in his mouth, the same taste he had had that night, and for a few seconds the
memory did not feel like a memory at all—it felt as though the attack was happening right now, in
the present, and he would have to grip the edge of his desk to remind himself where he was.

At work, Kofi’s body stayed on permanent alert. His shoulders were drawn up, his jaw tight, his eyes
constantly moving—scanning the waiting room, watching the hands of every man who walked into his
cubicle. He avoided eye contact because he had learned, the hard way, that looking directly at
someone felt like an invitation. When Marcus had looked at him that way—a certain slant of
appraisal, a sexualized scrutiny—violence had followed. Now Kofi did not want anyone to look at him
like that again, so he kept his gaze down, his face neutral, his body small. He also avoided any
situation that smelled like confrontation. If a parolee arrived angry or demanding, Kofi would agree
to whatever the man wanted just to end the interaction, even if it meant bending the rules. He had
stopped calling violations. He had stopped pushing back. His supervisor had started to notice that
his caseload was oddly quiet, and that some of his clients were showing up with infractions that
should have been documented but were not. Kofi told himself it was not worth the risk—disagreements
led to raised voices, and raised voices led to the floor coming up at him.

At home, Kofi had come to believe that his life was fundamentally broken. He would say to his
partner, “What happened to me was unfair, and nothing was ever done about it.” Marcus had never been
charged; Kofi had never filed a report. The injustice sat inside him like a stone. He had begun to
push away positive emotions—when his partner reached for him in bed, when a friend made him laugh,
when he felt a flicker of happiness—because those moments felt dangerous. They were the kind of
moment that preceded the attack: a good evening, a shared joke, and then the switch. So he learned
to stay flat. He learned not to feel too much. Some nights he lay awake and thought that maybe his
life was not worth living anymore, though he never said it aloud and never made a plan. It was just
a thought that drifted in and stayed.

Two weeks before his first session, a new male parolee was assigned to Kofi. The man was tall,
broad-shouldered, and when he sat down across from Kofi he leaned forward and held eye contact a
beat too long. The smell of cheap deodorant and stale sweat hit Kofi’s nose. He heard Marcus’s voice
in his head—a specific growl that meant the beating was coming—and Kofi’s hands began to shake. He
excused himself, walked to the bathroom, and stood with his forehead against the cold tile until the
taste of blood left his mouth. He called his partner that evening and said, finally, “I think I need
help.” He was terrified that if he let himself feel any hope about therapy, hope itself would become
another thing to be punished for. But he was more terrified of the moment when the memory stopped
feeling like a flashback and started making him believe that the attack was still happening, that it
would never be over.
