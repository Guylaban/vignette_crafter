Anh was fifty years old, a divorced musician who had spent most of his adult life in the quiet
rhythm of practice rooms and small venues—spaces he could control, where the acoustics were known
and the faces familiar. That rhythm ended two years ago, when a fan began to appear at every show,
then outside his apartment, then inside his building’s stairwell. The man never threatened him
directly, but he left notes, stood too close, and once—Anh remembered this with a sickening clarity
that still slammed into him without warning—pressed a single sheet of paper against Anh’s mailbox
glass: a photograph of Anh leaving a grocery store, taken from across the street. The police were
called, but they said there was no crime, no clear threat, nothing they could act on. Anh changed
his locks, stopped playing late shows, and eventually moved to a different neighborhood. The
stalking stopped, but something else had already started.

There are parts of those months that Anh cannot retrieve. He knows he went to a rehearsal in early
November, but the drive there, the faces of the bandmates, the set list—all of it is gone. What
comes back instead arrives in pieces: the smell of cigarette smoke in the stairwell, the fluorescent
hum of the hallway, a sudden cold sensation on the back of his neck as if someone were breathing
there. When these fragments hit, they feel absolutely real, not memory but presence—the dingy light
of the lobby, the specific weight of his keys in his hand, the faint sound of footsteps behind him.
He finds himself standing in his kitchen, heart pounding, for no reason he can name. The pieces
vanish as quickly as they came, leaving him disoriented and hollow.

That hollowness has settled into something permanent. Anh describes it as feeling dead inside, as
though the part of him that used to care about music, about people, about anything, has been scooped
out. He is certain that he is weak—that a stronger person would have confronted the man, would have
stood his ground, would not have let a scrap of paper unravel his life. The police and the apartment
management had dismissed him; he had come to believe that the people who should have protected him
had failed, and that this meant he was alone in a world where danger was unacknowledged and
unavoidable.

Now, Anh cannot go to unfamiliar places. If a friend invites him to a new café, he declines without
explanation; the thought of stepping into a space where he cannot see all exits or assess every
person makes his throat close. He watches other people constantly—on the street, in the grocery
line, from his window—scanning for the small signs that might betray hostile intent: a lingering
glance, a hand in a pocket, someone walking too slowly. Night is worse. He has always felt safer at
night, the soft quiet that used to inspire his compositions, but now darkness amplifies every creak
and shadow. He sleeps with lights on and wakes three or four times to check the lock, the window,
the closet.

As a musician, Anh had earned his living in clubs and concert halls; now he only accepts daytime
gigs at venues he has played for years, and he arrives two hours early to inspect the space. His
booker has stopped calling. His ex-wife, who still lives in the same city, left him a voicemail six
months ago asking if he wanted to try again. He never called back. The numbness made it impossible
to feel the loss; he simply forgot to reply. What finally brought him to therapy was a session of
his own—a solo piano piece he had been composing for a friend’s wedding. He sat at the keyboard, the
same instrument he had played for thirty years, and could not bring his hands to the keys. The music
felt meaningless; the room felt unsafe; he felt nothing at all. That scared him more than the
stalking ever had. He hopes, though he does not say it out loud, that therapy might give him back
the capacity to feel something—even if it hurts. But what he fears most is that the deadness is
permanent, and that the man who followed him is still out there, or someone like him, and that no
amount of watching or avoiding will ever make him safe.
