Amara Osei was eighty years old, a midwife who had delivered hundreds of babies over four decades,
mostly in the tight, windowless delivery rooms of the small community hospital where she worked. The
accident happened on a rainy December night when she was riding as a passenger in a colleague’s car,
rushing to a home birth. A driver ran a red light and T-boned them at the intersection. The impact
crushed the passenger door, and Amara was pinned inside the crumpled metal, her left leg twisted
under the dashboard, the sirens of emergency vehicles already wailing in the distance as she tried
to stay conscious, staring at the shattered windshield and the other car—the one that had been
hit—spinning into a lamppost. It took fire crews forty minutes to cut her out. She did not deliver
that baby; someone else did.

In the weeks that followed, Amara’s physical injuries healed slowly—a fractured femur, three broken
ribs, deep bruising—but the doctor said she would walk again. She tried to believe him. But what she
could not shake was the conviction that her body had been permanently damaged by what happened, that
some essential soundness had been taken away. She felt older than her eighty years, fragile in a way
she had never known. She began to notice that she could not bear being in small rooms with the door
closed. The delivery suite at the hospital, once her sanctuary, now felt like a trap. When a fellow
midwife asked her to cover a shift, Amara made an excuse: her back hurt, she was too tired. What she
did not say was that the thought of being locked in that room for hours made her throat close up.

At home, she started sleeping in the living room armchair, because the bedroom felt too enclosed and
lying down made her feel pinned again, as though the wreckage were still pressing on her chest. Her
husband, Kofi, would find her there at dawn, wide-eyed and trembling at the sound of a distant siren
passing three blocks away. She jumped at the kettle’s whistle, at the front door slamming, at a
dropped pan in the kitchen—ordinary sounds that would not bother most people, but that sent her
heart crashing into her ribs. She became hypervigilant during her daily walks, scanning every
intersection for cars that might run a red, watching drivers’ faces for signs of distraction. She
had come to believe that somebody else—any other midwife, anyone with better judgment—would not have
gotten into that car that night. It was her fault for agreeing to go, for not insisting on the
hospital van with its reinforced doors. This thought circled through her mind, especially at three
in the morning when she lay rigid in the armchair, unable to sleep, replaying the crash frame by
frame.

The triggers were everywhere. A locked bathroom door at a friend’s house made her forget why she had
entered. The wail of an ambulance on the television—even in a drama—sent her into a cold sweat, her
mind filling with the image of the other car spinning, the screech of metal. She began avoiding the
street where the accident happened, taking long detours that added twenty minutes to her errands.
Then she stopped driving altogether. Kofi did the grocery shopping. She stopped visiting her
daughter in the city because the highway went past the intersection. Her world shrank to the house
and the one-block radius she could walk without seeing a crash scene.

The breaking point came two months after the accident, when a neighbor’s car backfired on the street
outside. Amara dropped to the floor, her hands over her head, certain she was back inside the car,
hearing the sirens, smelling the gasoline. Kofi found her there, weeping, and for the first time she
told him what she had been thinking for weeks: that her life had been destroyed by the trauma, that
she was no longer the midwife who could walk into any delivery room, calm and capable. She was
afraid she would never be that person again. What frightened her most was the thought that the
accident had taken not just her mobility but her purpose—that she would spend the rest of her years
hiding from locked doors and sirens, waiting for the next sound that would bring her back to that
intersection. She agreed to see a therapist because Kofi asked her to, but also because a small part
of her hoped that someone might help her find a way back into a delivery room, door open or closed,
just one more time.
