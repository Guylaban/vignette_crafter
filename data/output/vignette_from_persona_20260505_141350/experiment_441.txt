Ravi Patel, a 41-year-old architect, sat in the intake office still unsure why he had come. He had
told his wife Priya he was “just tired,” but the truth was more jagged. Six years ago, when he was
living with Meera—a woman he had loved and trusted—the physical abuse began quietly. At first it was
a shove during an argument after she had been drinking. The smell of cheap whiskey on her breath,
the incomprehensible chatter of her rage in a mix of Hindi and English that left him unable to
follow, the sudden lurch of his body hitting the kitchen counter. Over eighteen months, the episodes
grew; she would slap him, grab his wrists, once she threw a ceramic bowl that shattered inches from
his head. He never fought back. He was certain that if he did, he would become the monster she
accused him of being.

The night it ended, she had come home reeking of alcohol, and before he could step away, her fist
connected with his cheek. He remembered that moment as a single frozen photograph: the pattern of
the linoleum, the tilting of his vision, the metallic taste. Another snapshot: her mouth open in a
word he could not decode, the sound a blur of syllables. He left that night, but the photographs
stayed.

Now, years later, Ravi’s body seemed to know danger before he had a chance to think. At his drafting
table, a colleague’s sudden laugh across the room made him flinch, his pen skidding across
blueprints. In meetings, when two clients spoke over each other in rapid bursts, he lost his place,
his chest tightening as if he were being backed into a corner. He had come to believe that he could
not trust his own judgment—if he had misread Meera so badly, how could he know when he was making
the right decision at work or at home? The thought lodged deeper: he had failed in his most
important role in life. A man protects his partner. He had not. And he had been betrayed by someone
he had let into his heart.

At night, Priya would settle into sleep beside him, but Ravi lay rigid, straining to hear any change
in the house—a creak, a whisper from the street. He needed to stay alert. He told himself it was
just insomnia, but his jaw ached from clenching, and his startle response was so sharp that once he
knocked a full cup of coffee off his desk when the office printer jammed and beeped. The smell of
alcohol remained the hardest. A coworker’s breath after a lunchtime beer, the whiff of hand
sanitizer on the train—each time his throat tightened and the snapshots returned: the linoleum tile,
the ceramic shard, Meera’s unrecognizable face.

He had built careful avoidances. He stopped attending happy hours, claiming deadlines. He worked
late to avoid crowded elevators where someone’s loud, incomprehensible phone call might spike his
heart rate. When he felt physically unwell—a headache, a queasy stomach—he became convinced
something bad was about to happen, and he would cancel plans, stay home, pace. The fatigue and
missed social events had begun to worry Priya. She noticed he flinched when she touched his back
unexpectedly. Last week, at a family dinner, an uncle’s slurred speech from a few drinks had sent
Ravi to the bathroom for twenty minutes. Priya found him gripping the sink. She asked gently, “What
is happening to you?” That night, he finally admitted he needed help.

What Ravi most feared now was that he was irreparably broken—that the photos in his mind would never
fade, that his body would always react before his mind could catch up, and that he would fail in his
marriage as he believed he had failed before. But he was also hoping, against the hard edges of his
memories, that someone might show him how to stop the trembling before it started.
