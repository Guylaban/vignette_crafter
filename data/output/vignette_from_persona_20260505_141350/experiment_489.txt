to keep a dozen moving parts in sync—budgets, timelines, subcontractors, all humming under her
quiet, steady hand. That was before the illness. Two years ago, a routine cut on her hand turned
into a cascading sepsis that landed her in the intensive care unit for eighteen days. She remembered
the blur of fevers and the cold, insistent presence of needles: the arterial line threaded into her
wrist, the central line snaking toward her heart, the daily blood draws that left her arm a
patchwork of bruises. Most vividly, she remembered the pulmonologist pressing a stethoscope to her
chest and saying, “We’re not sure you’re going to make it, Leilani.” She had felt her body
surrender, becoming nothing more than a surface upon which machines and hands acted. The shame of
that helplessness had settled into her bones alongside the infection.

In the months after discharge, Leilani tried to resume her old life. But she found herself flinching
violently when her husband, Mark, touched her shoulder from behind, her heart hammering as though
she were back in that bed being prodded without warning. She began to snap at him for the smallest
things—leaving a dish in the sink, humming off-key—and then felt a deeper wave of shame for her own
irritability. At work, she struggled to hold a thread of concentration through a single project
meeting; numbers swam on the whiteboard, and she would catch herself staring at the clock, counting
the minutes until she could escape. When a junior team member casually mentioned his father’s
colonoscopy during a coffee break, Leilani felt the floor tilt. The word “scope” pressed against her
chest like a physical weight. She excused herself, locked herself in the bathroom, and pressed her
palm to the cold tile until the wave passed.

The memories came not as words but as sensations. Lying in bed at night, she would suddenly feel her
body contort into the same rigid, curled posture she had held in the ICU—knees drawn up, arms
crossed over her chest, jaw clenched. The smell of antiseptic would flood her nose, and the phantom
pinch of a needle would register in her forearm. She could not describe these episodes to Mark; when
she opened her mouth, the only words that came were “I can’t,” and then silence. The event itself
had become something that could not be shaped into language, only re-experienced in full color and
terror.

Leilani had come to believe that the trauma had hollowed out her life. The woman who had once run
marathons and managed $10 million projects now saw her body as a traitorous vessel that could betray
her without warning. She felt a deep, private shame about what had happened to her body—the scars,
the dependence, the way strangers had seen her naked and inert. She could not trust her own
heartbeat: every palpitation felt like the beginning of another collapse. She avoided doctor’s
appointments, refused to look at medical equipment in TV shows, and changed the subject whenever
anyone mentioned an illness. At her annual performance review, her supervisor noted that she had
become “difficult to work with” and that her distraction was affecting deadlines. Leilani knew she
was losing grip on the career that had given her identity.

What finally brought her to a therapist’s office was a morning when Mark asked if she wanted to talk
about the anniversary of her discharge, and she screamed at him so viciously that he stepped

would never feel safe in her own skin again, that the irritability would cost her the marriage she
loved, and that the memories would keep returning, unbidden, like a tide she could not outrun. But
beneath the fear was a thin thread of hope: that someone might help her find a way to put what had
happened into words, and that doing so might loosen the body’s grip on the past.
