Amara Okafor can still hear her uncle’s footsteps. She was eight years old, sitting at the dining
table in her family’s home in Newark, when she heard the heavy, deliberate tread on the hardwood
floor. Then came his voice—a deep, calm baritone that could shift to a roar without warning.
“Amara.” He only used her full name when she was in trouble. She had left a single dish unwashed.
The sound of his belt unbuckling, that specific metallic click, is a sound she has been trying to
outrun for thirty-nine years.

The abuse was not random; it was a pedagogy of pain delivered with terrible consistency. Her uncle,
Daniel, who raised her after her parents died, believed that breaking a child’s will was the only
way to save her soul. He told her, repeatedly, that the beatings were her fault. “You make me do
this because of who you are.” She believed him. That belief calcified into a core conviction that
followed her into every corner of her adult life: the event happened because of the sort of person
she is. There is something fundamentally wrong with her.

As a plumber, Amara is skilled and meticulous, but her work life has become a minefield of auditory
threats. She has built her schedule around avoidance. She refuses to work in homes where a male
client’s voice resonates in the same low frequencies as her uncle’s. A single deep-voiced phone call
is enough for her to pass the job to a colleague, costing her thousands of dollars a year. The name
“Daniel” sends a jolt of ice through her chest; she scans every work order for that name before
accepting a job. Last spring, she walked off a commercial assignment at a school because the
principal—a tall man with a deliberate stride—called out to someone in the hallway. The tone, the
authority, the weight of his footsteps on the linoleum: she stood frozen in the boiler room, her
heart pounding against her ribs, until she fled without explanation. Her supervisor has started to
notice, and she fears she is becoming unreliable in a trade that demands steadiness.

At home, her marriage to Marcus is strained. He sees her flinch at unexpected sounds, watches her
scan rooms for exits, and endures her long silences. He doesn’t understand why she spends so much
time stuck in the past, replaying what her uncle did to her. What he doesn’t see is the rumination
that runs on a loop in her mind—the other-blame that burns in her chest as she mentally rehearses
the injustice, the conversations she will never have, the apology she will never receive. But even
as she blames her uncle, the older, deeper voice inside her whispers that he was right. She keeps
Marcus at arm’s length, partly out of shame, partly out of a sense that a defective person does not
deserve to be truly known. Nothing in her life feels meaningful anymore; she goes through the
motions of work and marriage, but the color has drained out of both.

What finally brought Amara to the clinic was a complete failure of her elaborate avoidance system.
Last week, a client’s elderly father shuffled down a hallway. The sound of his slippers on the floor
was wrong—it was not a threat—but then the man called out for his son, “Daniel!” and a door slammed
behind him. Amara dropped her wrench. Her vision narrowed. She felt eight years old again, small and
worthless and waiting for the blow. She hyperventilated in the client’s bathroom for twenty minutes
before making an excuse and leaving the job unfinished. That night, for the first time, she looked
up the number for a trauma specialist.

She walks into the office with her shoulders tight, monitoring the therapist’s voice, the scrape of
their chair. She is terrified of what therapy might unearth: if she stops blaming herself, if she
stops monitoring every sound for danger, what will be left? She hopes, cautiously, that maybe there
is another way to carry the past. But a deeper, louder part of her is still certain that the problem
is not just what happened *to* her, but what is wrong *with* her—and that is the fear she cannot
bring herself to say out loud.
