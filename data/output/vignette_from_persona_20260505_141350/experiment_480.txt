behind the wheel for nearly a decade without incident. That changed on a rainy November afternoon
when he was running behind schedule, trying to squeeze in one more drop before rush hour. He
approached a poorly lit intersection, glanced at his dispatch screen for the next address, and felt
a thud—heavy and final, nothing like the potholes he usually hit. He slammed the brakes and saw a
man lying in the crosswalk, his bicycle crumpled a few feet away. Arjun jumped out, his hands
shaking, and called 911, but by the time the ambulance arrived the man was unconscious with a head
injury that later required emergency surgery. The police report said the victim would survive but
would likely have permanent cognitive deficits. Arjun was not charged—the light was yellow, the
victim had darted out—but he never stopped replaying the split second he looked away from the road.

In the weeks that followed, Arjun became convinced that he could have prevented the whole thing if
he had been more alert, if he had left earlier, if he had honked, if he had braked a second sooner.
He told himself over and over that he should have protected that man—it was his job as a driver to
watch for pedestrians, and he had failed. The thought looped through his mind during every shift:
*If I had just been paying attention, he would be fine.* He also carried a crushing sense of
inaction guilt. He had gotten out of the truck, yes, but he felt he had not done enough. He had not
known first aid; he had just stood there, frozen, while the man bled. He started to say to his wife,
Meera, that he would never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened, for not somehow
intervening before the collision. And the self-blame was specific: the event happened because of the
way he acted—rushing, checking his phone, not pausing at the intersection. He began to see himself
as reckless, careless, a danger to others.

Driving, once automatic, became a minefield. Every intersection made his palms sweat; every
crosswalk he approached he slowed to a crawl, scanning for figures that might appear. He started
taking longer routes to avoid three particular crossings near delivery zones—one where the accident
had occurred, and two others that looked too similar. His manager noticed his delivery times
slipping and warned him about efficiency. Arjun began dreading the start of his shift, but he could
not afford to quit—the family needed his income, and Meera was pregnant with their first child. At

about traffic, unable to tell her the truth because saying it aloud would make it real again. He
stopped watching movies that showed car crashes, avoided news reports about traffic accidents, and
found himself unable to drive past the hospital where the victim had been treated, even though it
was not on his route.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment six months after the accident. He was driving
home on a quiet residential street when he saw a child's soccer ball bounce into the road. He
slammed the brakes so hard that the driver behind him nearly rear-ended him. The child ran out,
grabbed the ball, and waved. Arjun sat in his truck, his heart hammering, and wept. He realized that
his constant vigilance had not made him safer—it had made him terrified of every shadow. That night,
he told Meera everything, and she encouraged him to see a therapist. Now he sits in the clinic,
afraid that if he talks about it he will be swallowed by the guilt, but also quietly hoping that
someone can help him trust his own hands on the wheel again.
