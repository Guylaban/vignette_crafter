freelance photographer, he had spent years traveling to remote regions, documenting stories that
others preferred not to see. But nothing had prepared him for what happened during an assignment
three years ago in a small coastal town in the Pacific. He had been on his own, staying with a local
contact, when a group of men broke into the house at dawn. They held him for eleven days in a
concrete storage room with no windows. The details of what they did—the threats, the beatings, the
uncertainty of whether he would ever leave—had become a kind of script that played in his head at
unpredictable moments. What stayed with him most vividly was the sound of footsteps approaching, the
way his captors would sometimes appear without warning, their sudden movements triggering a cascade
of terror that he could not control.

After he was released and returned home to his wife, Elena, Manaia initially believed he would
recover with time. But the world outside felt fundamentally different. He had come to believe that
danger could materialize from anywhere—a man walking too quickly behind him in a supermarket, a car
slowing down as he crossed the street, the way a stranger’s arm moved abruptly to reach for a
product on a shelf. Every sudden motion sent a jolt through his body. His muscles remained taut for
hours after such moments, as though his body was perpetually waiting to fight or flee. He found
himself scanning every face in a crowd, looking for the specific kind of cold watchfulness he
remembered from his captors. If someone’s gaze lingered too long, his heart would pound and he would
instinctively calculate the nearest exit.

These habits bled into his work. As a photographer, he once thrived on street portraiture and candid
shots in crowded public spaces. Now he could not bring himself to enter a busy marketplace or a
festival without first circling the perimeter to locate every possible exit. Before he could lift
his camera, he needed to find a seat or a spot—usually with his back to a wall—where he felt less
exposed. Even then, he remained hyperaware of the people around him, more focused on their movements
than on the images he was trying to capture. His portfolio began to shrink to static landscapes and
commissioned shoots in controlled indoor settings. Elena noticed he had started asking her
repeatedly, sometimes three or four times in an evening, “I’m okay, right? Nothing bad is going to
happen tonight?” She would reassure him, but the relief never lasted more than a few minutes.

The family gatherings he used to enjoy became exhausting. At every restaurant or relative’s home,
Manaia needed to sit facing the door, with no one behind him. If someone got up suddenly from a
table nearby, he would flinch and grip the edge of his chair. His younger brother once playfully
clapped him on the shoulder from behind, and Manaia reacted by shoving him away before he could stop
himself. The embarrassment and shame that followed were acute, but they did not change his
vigilance. He began declining invitations, telling Elena he was tired or busy, because the constant
state of alertness was itself a kind of exhaustion.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment in his own studio. A client arrived for a
portrait session, and as she stepped briskly toward him to shake his hand, Manaia felt a surge of
panic so intense that he stumbled backward and knocked over a lighting stand. He apologized, made an
excuse, but he could see the confusion on her face. That evening, with Elena’s gentle insistence, he
agreed to see someone. The thought of talking about the concrete room, the footsteps, the
uncertainty of those eleven days, terrified him more than he could articulate. But living this
way—always planning an escape route, always bracing for danger—had become its own kind of captivity,
and he was no longer sure he could break free on his own.
