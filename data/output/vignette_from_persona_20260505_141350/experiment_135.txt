Kwame Osei is a sixty-five-year-old retail worker who has spent the last four decades stocking
shelves and assisting customers at a discount department store in a midsized Midwestern town.
Married for thirty-eight years, he lives with his wife in a quiet neighborhood that once felt safe.
That changed on a humid June afternoon three years ago, when an EF‑3 tornado tore through the strip
mall where he worked. The storm hit without warning. He was in the back room when the sirens blared;
he ran toward the front, but the glass doors exploded inward and the ceiling began to peel away. He
pressed himself flat behind an overturned display rack, debris hammering the metal, and for what
felt like minutes he was certain the building would collapse on him. He remembers the roar and the
pressure in his ears, the way the air turned black with dust. When it was over, he crawled out
through a gap in the wall, unharmed but shaking. He has not felt safe since.

In the weeks that followed, the memory of that afternoon refused to settle. It returned not as a
recollection but as a full sensory re-experience: he would be folding shirts and suddenly he could
hear the wind, smell the wet plaster, feel the weight of the rack against his spine. The memory
played like a film he could not stop, always the same sequence of shattering glass and buckling
ceiling, and each time it felt as if the tornado was happening right then, in the present, not
something that had finished. He began to avoid the back of the store, and he stopped walking past
the front doors during his break. More than that, he started to scan every room he entered for an
exit: the break room, the restroom, even his own living room. As soon as he crossed a threshold, his
eyes found the door and he calculated the shortest path to it. He had to know he could get out.

Kwame came to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous, that a catastrophe could arrive
without warning at any moment. He told himself that he had to be on guard all the time, that if he
relaxed even for a second the next disaster would catch him. He began to see danger in ordinary
sights: a dark cloud on the horizon, a sudden gust of wind, the hum of an air conditioner that
sounded like the storm’s approach. At work, he positioned himself near the emergency exit at the
back of the sales floor so that he could slip out quickly. When customers asked for help in the
front aisles, he felt a tightness in his chest and often sent them toward a younger colleague. He
started carrying a small radio to check the weather, and he would turn it on whenever he felt the
air pressure shift, distracting himself with the forecast instead of the panic rising in his throat.

At night, sleep became a battle. He lay awake, listening, alert for the first hint of wind or a
distant rumble. His wife would find him sitting up at three in the morning, staring at the window.
He told her he needed to stay watchful, that something could happen any minute. She grew frustrated
and worried; their evenings turned silent. He felt he had changed permanently for the worse, that
the man who used to joke with customers and sleep through thunderstorms was gone, and he could not
find a way back. His hypervigilance exhausted him, but when he tried to stop checking his
surroundings, the anxiety spiked so sharply that he could not think.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was a moment in the break room six weeks ago. A coworker
slammed a metal cabinet door, and Kwame dropped to the floor, heart hammering, certain that the roof
was coming down. The coworker helped him up, but the shame was crushing. His wife told him that she
could not keep watching him suffer, that he deserved to have his life back. He agreed to come to
therapy, though he does not believe much can change. He is terrified that the memories will never
stop feeling so real, and he is equally terrified that letting his guard down will invite another
disaster. He hopes, quietly, that maybe he can learn to rest again. He does not know how.
