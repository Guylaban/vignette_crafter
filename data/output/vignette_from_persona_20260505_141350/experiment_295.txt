Kwame had never liked bars much, but that night a friend had dragged him out to celebrate a
promotion. The streets were loud, and the air carried a mix of cheap beer and cologne. He didn’t
remember exactly how it started—just a figure stepping out of a doorway, a voice demanding his
wallet. He remembered the smell, warm and sour, and then a heavy object—a bottle?—striking his
shoulder before he could react. The man’s face twisted with anger, shouting words Kwame’s brain
didn’t process. After that, only a handful of moments stayed with him: the asphalt against his
cheek, the screech of a car, a pair of boots landing nearby. Then nothing.

Those moments had become snapshots, frozen images that came without warning. He would be sitting at
his desk in the municipal planning office, reviewing zoning maps, and suddenly he saw the fragment
of a gray sidewalk, a broken piece of glass. The emotion hit him so fast and so hard that he had to
drop everything—push back his chair, press his palms against his thighs, wait for the wave to pass.
His heart pounded. Sometimes his breath got stuck. When his supervisor asked what was wrong, Kwame
could only shake his head. He knew the story: the assault, the police report that went nowhere, the
officer who said there was little to go on. But he could not find the words to describe what he
actually felt. The pieces wouldn't line up in his mouth.

In the months afterward, he started to avoid anything that felt like confrontation. At work, when a
colleague pushed back on a traffic-flow proposal, Kwame would nod quickly and say nothing, even if
he disagreed. He took on solo projects, stayed quiet in meetings. Any expression of anger—even a
raised eyebrow or a firm tone—sent a chill through his chest. Once, a coworker slammed a drawer shut
and Kwame flinched so visibly that the person apologized. Part of him believed that if he stopped an
argument from starting, he could stop something worse. He could not trust safety. Even when his boss
assured him the office was a good environment, Kwame felt like something bad was always about to
happen.

The smell of alcohol became unbearable. Passing a bar on his walk home, he would cross the street,
sometimes walking six blocks out of his way. If a colleague returned from lunch with beer on his
breath, Kwame would invent a reason to leave the room. He knew he was overreacting, but the smell
seemed to pull him straight back to that doorway, to the sour heat of a stranger’s rage.

At night he could not stop turning the assault over in his head. He dwelled on what he had done
wrong: Why hadn’t he just handed over his wallet immediately? Why had he argued back? Why had he
walked down that street at all? He told himself he was stupid, irresponsible, weak. And then, to
quiet the spiral, he started gambling. Small bets at first—online poker, sports pools. The rush of a
win replaced the hollow feeling for an hour or two. He lost more than he won, but the distraction
worked. On bad nights he would stay up until 3 a.m. chasing that numbness.

Everyday sounds set him off. The slam of a car door. A dropped clipboard. A sudden laugh in the
hallway. His body reacted before his mind caught up—a violent jump, shoulders tensed, pulse racing.
Unexpected movements, even harmless ones like someone reaching for a stapler, made him feel as
though he were under attack. He knew rationally that he was safe. He could hear himself repeat the
reassurance. But it did not stick. The terror came from somewhere deeper, and he had no authority
over it.

What had finally brought Kwame to the clinic was a moment in a weekly planning meeting. A senior
planner, frustrated with a budget delay, tossed a pen across the table. It clattered and bounced.
Kwame gasped, stood up, and walked out without a word. He sat in the bathroom stall for twenty
minutes, shaking, convinced he could not handle anything. The institution he had trusted—the police,
his own office—had not protected him before, and he saw no reason to believe they would now. He felt
helpless. Even a tiny upset felt like collapse.

Now he sat across from the intake worker, trying to put all of this into a sentence. He managed to
say he was afraid of losing his job. But what he really wanted, what he could not yet express aloud,
was to find a way to believe that the next ordinary sound, the next raised voice, the next whiff of
alcohol, would not destroy him again.
