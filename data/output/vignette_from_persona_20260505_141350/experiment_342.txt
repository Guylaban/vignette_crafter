Leila was eight years old the first time she saw her father’s hand move that way—fast, flat, and
final—across her mother’s face. She remembers the crack of impact and the way her mother’s body
folded sideways, but if anyone asks her what happened, the words disappear. The memory is all heat
and pressure behind her eyes, a chest that locks, a throat that closes. She cannot narrate it. She
can only feel it, fresh and suffocating, as if no time has passed.

Now thirty, Leila works behind a bar in a downtown lounge. The job demands constant motion—shaking
cocktails, reaching for bottles, wiping counters—and constant exposure to people. That is where the
trouble lives. A customer reaches suddenly for a napkin and Leila’s body locks. Shoulders rise,
breath stops. She sees the movement before she registers the intention, and everything in her
screams that something bad is about to happen. She has learned to fade backward, to keep the bar
between herself and anyone who gestures too quickly. It feels less like a choice than a reflex, a
muscle memory she never asked for.

She has also learned to prepare. Before every shift, she runs the worst outcomes through her mind: a
drunk man grabs her wrist, a fight breaks out, the door won’t lock. She rehearses her response—what
she would say, where she would run, how she would protect herself—so that nothing ever catches her
off guard. The rehearsal helps, but it also exhausts her. She is always two steps ahead, and the
cost of staying ready is that she is never really present.

At home, Leila checks the front door three times before she can sit down. She checks the windows.
She checks them again before she lies down. She knows it is excessive—she lives on the third floor,
the locks are new—but the ritual quiets something in her chest, so she does it. She has come to
believe that safety exists only in a very short list of places: her apartment, the back corner of
the bar near the exit, her car with all doors locked. Anywhere else feels too exposed. Open spaces
especially—parking lots, wide sidewalks, the dining room of a restaurant—make her feel as though she
is being watched from every angle. She avoids them.

She avoids a lot, actually. She avoids emotional conversations because she learned long ago that
feelings lead to scenes, and scenes lead to harm. She keeps her voice flat at work. She does not
date. She does not let herself get angry, because she is not sure she could stop it if she started.
She has come to doubt every decision she makes, second-guessing whether she locked the door right,
whether she read a customer’s mood correctly, whether she should have stayed home. She tells herself
she cannot rely on herself. She tells herself people cannot be trusted, and the world has proved her
right.

Her marriage ended two years ago. Her ex-husband was not abusive—he was patient, even kind—but Leila
could not stop scanning his movements, could not shake the certainty that he would one day raise his
hand. She checked the locks when he was home. She rehearsed what she would do if he turned. He asked
her why she flinched when he touched her shoulder, and she could not explain. She had no words. They
parted with a quiet divorce, and she told herself it was for the best.

What finally brought her into the clinic was an incident two weeks ago. A man at the bar lunged for
a falling glass. Leila saw the lunge and dropped to the floor, covered her head, and did not come up
until a coworker touched her arm. She was humiliated. She was terrified. The manager asked if she
needed to go home. She said no, finished her shift, but the shaking did not stop. That night she
could not sleep. She checked the locks fourteen times. She rehearsed what she would do if the bar
was robbed, if she was attacked on the street, if she lost her job because she could not hold
herself together.

Now she sits in the therapist’s office with her hands folded in her lap. She does not know how to
say what happened to her as a child. She does not know if saying it will make it worse. But she
knows she cannot keep living this way—split between a past she cannot speak and a present she cannot
trust. She is afraid that she will never feel safe, and afraid that asking for help is just another
thing she will do wrong. But she is here. That, at least, is something.
