revealed elevated blood pressure and a sleep study showed severe insomnia. What Ravi told the

recognized the possibility of posttraumatic stress disorder and referred him for a psychological
evaluation.

The trauma that shaped Ravi’s life occurred in 1971, during the Bangladesh Liberation War, when he
served as a young infantry soldier in the Pakistani army. He was stationed near the border when his
unit was ambushed during a night patrol. He remembers the sudden explosion of a land mine that
killed two men beside him, the heat and the smell of burning flesh, and the frantic, disorienting
firefight that followed. For three days he lay pinned under a collapsed bunker, unable to move his
legs, listening to the sounds of battle and the cries of wounded men. He was eventually rescued, but
the terror of being trapped, helpless, and expecting death at any moment never left him. What Ravi
recalls most vividly is the utter silence that fell after the firing stopped—the sound of his own
heartbeat and the certainty that he would be discovered by enemy soldiers and killed. He was not,
but that certainty became a permanent part of his inner world.

In the years that followed, Ravi emigrated to the United States, worked in warehouses, married, and
raised children. But the war lived inside him. He avoided any situation that felt confined or that
reminded him of that night: he could not ride an elevator, refused to attend crowded weddings or
funerals, and would not let his wife close the bedroom curtains all the way. At work, he insisted on
taking the stairs even when his knees ached, and he always positioned himself with his back to a
wall, watching the entrances. His co-workers thought he was unfriendly; they did not know that he
was scanning every face for threat. He never spoke about the war, not even to his wife, because he
believed that if he started talking he would lose control completely, and that would prove he was
weak or crazy.

staying up watching television until he collapsed from exhaustion. When he did sleep, he kept a
small flashlight under his pillow, ready to illuminate the room at the first sign of
darkness—because darkness, for Ravi, was not just an absence of light; it was the moment when the
enemy could move unseen. His wife eventually could not tolerate his rigid routines, his sudden
irritability, and his refusal to explain why he recoiled from her touch when she startled him. She
left after twenty years, telling him he was a stranger. He was not surprised; he had been certain
that no one could truly understand what he carried, and that to let anyone close would be to burden
them with his shame.

there is still a person who can feel something other than dread.
