Ravi was thirty-seven years old, a clinical psychologist living in a Midwestern suburb with his
wife, Priya. Eighteen months earlier, he had been two blocks from a downtown holiday market when a
lone attacker opened fire with a rifle. The shots came in a sudden, tearing sequence—pop-pop-
pop—just as he was stepping out of his office building. He had no time to think, only to drop to the
ground behind a concrete planter, his heart slamming against his ribs while bodies around him
scrambled and fell. For what felt like an eternity, he lay there with his hands pressed over his
ears, utterly certain that the next sound would be the one that ended him. He did not see the
attacker, but he felt the absolute dissolution of any control he had ever imagined having over his
own safety. When the shooting stopped and police swarmed the area, he walked back to his car on legs
that would not stop trembling, and he drove home in silence.

In the weeks that followed, Ravi told himself he was fine. He had treated trauma survivors for
years; he knew the signs intellectually. But his own experience refused to stay on the page. At
night, he woke in the dark with a jolt, his hands already reaching for something, his breath
shallow, convinced that someone was inside the house. He could not fall back asleep unless he had a
drink—at first one, then two, then three. Alcohol became the only thing that quieted the
hypervigilance, the constant scanning for danger that had taken up residence behind his ribs. He
started to avoid anything that felt even remotely stressful. At work, he stopped taking new clients
who had histories of violence or sudden loss. He steered every intake toward what he called “low-
acuity” cases, knowing full well that he was shrinking his practice, his professional identity, by
the week. His colleagues noticed, and he made excuses—burnout, a need to simplify his caseload. What
he could not tell them was that the sight of a client in tears now sent him into a cold sweat,
because it reminded him of that afternoon when he had been equally powerless, and powerlessness now
felt like the only truth that mattered.

His marriage began to fray. Priya would reach for him at night and find him rigid, staring at the
ceiling. She would ask what was wrong, and he would say nothing, because how could he explain that
he had come to believe the world was a place where catastrophe could arrive without warning, that he
could not stop it, and that the person lying next to her, the psychologist who used to offer calm
and reason, had been replaced by someone he barely recognised? He had started drifting off into
elaborate daydreams during the afternoon, constructing whole alternate lives in his head—a quiet
cottage by the sea where no one could find him, where no sounds ever startled. It was the only time
he felt any relief, but it also meant that Priya had to repeat things to him three times before he
heard her. Once, she asked if he even wanted to be with her anymore. He had no answer that made
sense.

What finally brought Ravi to this office—his own therapist’s office, something he had put off for
nearly a year—was a single moment two weeks ago. He was walking to his car after an evening session
when a teenager in the parking lot accidentally slammed a car door. The sound tore through him like
the first shot. He dropped his briefcase, his knees buckled, and for ten seconds he stood frozen in
the dark, gasping, unsure where he was. A security guard came over and asked if he was all right.
Ravi said yes, but he was not. He got in his car and drove home and poured himself a drink with
shaking hands, and that night he told Priya he needed help. He is sitting here now, across from a
stranger, terrified that talking about it will make it worse, and equally terrified that not talking
about it will cost him everything he still has left.
