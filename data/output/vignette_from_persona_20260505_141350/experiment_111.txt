steady, quiet confidence—managing portfolios, advising clients, raising a family alongside their
late spouse. But the rape, which happened three years ago, shattered that. The details were never
fully accessible. When Rangi tried to recall what exactly happened that evening after a client
dinner, the story wouldn’t form. Instead, fragments surfaced: the metallic taste of blood from a
bitten lip; the sound of the perpetrator’s belt buckle clinking, a sound that now appeared without
warning in their head, crisp as the original moment, day or night. They could taste the blood again,
feel the salt, smell the faint cologne. But the sequence of how they had gotten from the restaurant
to that hotel room, or how they had left—those pieces were gone, a black gap in the timeline. What
remained was a conviction: people could not be trusted to behave decently. The perpetrator had worn
a suit, had smiled, had been a colleague’s associate. In Rangi’s mind, the event proved that beneath
any polished surface, people were capable of profound moral violation.

Weeks after, Rangi noticed a creeping dread in places that had never bothered them before. The sight
of a police cruiser idling at an intersection made their chest tighten and their palms sweat. Once,
nearing a precinct to file a routine financial report, they turned around and walked ten blocks the
other way. They did not know why police felt dangerous—they had never interacted with them that
night—but the uniform, the authority, the possibility of being questioned or not believed felt like
a direct threat. At work, a supervisor’s gentle correction on a spreadsheet model sent a spike of
heat through Rangi’s body. Being criticized now meant something more than a professional remark; it
felt like an accusation, a sign that their own judgment was fundamentally flawed. Rangi began to
avoid meetings where their work might be reviewed. They stopped speaking up during team discussions.

colleagues. The isolation grew, but it felt safer.

pride in mentoring junior analysts, the quiet joy of gardening after a long week—had drained away.
Nothing felt meaningful. Not the portfolio they had managed for thirty years, not the house now
filled only with their collection of seashells and photographs of a spouse who had died a decade
before the assault. Rangi had, for years, been the steady one. Now they could not rely on
themselves. They would second-guess a grocery list, replay a conversation obsessively, worry that
they had missed some crucial sign of danger. The reactions themselves—the sudden taste of blood in
the checkout line, the sound of a belt buckle during a quiet evening—frightened Rangi most of all.

acknowledgment that living this way—fragmented, afraid of criticism, mistrusting every stranger and

beneath the fear, was to find even one piece of a coherent story that they could hold onto without
