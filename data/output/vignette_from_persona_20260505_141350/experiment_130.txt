Minh Pham, a 52-year-old HVAC technician, had spent thirty years handling refrigerants without
serious incident. That changed one humid August afternoon when he was working alone in the
mechanical room of an apartment complex. A corroded valve on a chiller unit gave way without
warning, releasing a pressurized cloud of R-22 gas directly into his face. The chemical hit his
throat and lungs like a wall of ice and fire at the same time. He stumbled backward, coughing,
gasping, unable to draw a full breath. By the time emergency responders arrived, he had collapsed on
the concrete floor, his chest burning and his vision streaked with black spots. He spent three days
in intensive care with chemical pneumonitis, and the doctors told him he was lucky—the exposure had
been sublethal, but only just.

What Minh came to understand in the months that followed was that luck had nothing to do with
safety. Back at work, he found himself unable to trust the equipment he had maintained for decades.
He started checking and rechecking gauges, then moved on to checking the locks on his truck cab,
then the deadbolts on his front door, then the windows. Even with his wife, Lan, sitting beside him
in the living room, Minh could not convince himself that the air around him was safe. When she told
him the house was fine, that the carbon monoxide detector was working, that no tank had sprung a
leak, he heard her words but felt nothing—no shift, no relief. His body remained coiled, his
shoulders up and his jaw tight, as though some invisible chemical agent were only seconds away from
bursting into the space around him.

He began to avoid situations where his guard might drop. After work, he had always liked to sit on
the back porch with a cup of tea and watch the birds. Now that felt impossible—relaxing felt like
voluntarily putting down a shield, like waiting to be attacked. He stopped sitting anywhere without
a clear path to the door. Lan would find him standing in the kitchen, eating his dinner over the
counter, unwilling to settle into a chair. He avoided being alone in a room or a building
altogether, following Lan from room to room, or keeping the television and radio on so the silence
would not trick him into believing something was wrong. He also began to dodge anything that might
involve stress: he turned down new jobs that required working with unfamiliar chemicals, stopped
taking calls from anxious clients, and eventually asked his supervisor to assign him only the
simplest residential tune-ups. The result was a steady drop in income; he took a pay cut and lost
the respect of his younger colleagues, who whispered that he had lost his nerve.

The avoidance extended to his body itself. Minh was constantly scanning the inside of his own chest
for tightness, for any sensation that might be the beginning of another chemical burn. He could not
walk into a hardware store without checking the ceiling for compressed gas canisters, and several
times he left a work site early because a faint smell reminded him of refrigerant. At home, he began
checking the doors and windows three and four times before bed, often waking Lan with his movements.
She tried to reassure him, but Minh’s reasoning had become fixed: if he allowed himself to feel
safe, he would stop looking for danger, and the moment he stopped looking would be the moment danger
returned.

What finally brought him to seek help was a panic attack in a big-box store. He had walked past a
display of aerosol cans, and his chest seized, his vision tunneled, and a voice inside him said,
*This is it, it’s happening again.* He ended up crouched between two shelves, gasping, until
security escorted him outside. That evening, Lan told him she could not keep living this
way—watching him suffer, watching him shrink his life. Minh agreed to see a clinician, though what
he feared most was being told he would never feel safe again, and what he hoped for, in a quiet part
of himself he barely acknowledged, was that someone might finally show him how to let the air back
in.
