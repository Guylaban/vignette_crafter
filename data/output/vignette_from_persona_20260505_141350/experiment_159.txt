line of sight to the nearest exit, and that she would abruptly end one-on-one meetings to grab a
coffee for herself and her visitor—an excuse to keep the office door open and to ensure she was
never truly alone with another person.

The roots of this vigilance lay in a childhood she had never spoken about in full. Her father, a
stern and devout man, had disciplined her with his hands and a leather belt, often in the small back
room of their apartment. She remembered the way the room would darken as he closed the door, the
smell of dust and his sweat. But when she tried to describe it now, words failed her. She could only
feel the memory in her body: sometimes, without warning, her arms would curl protectively over her
head, her knees would pull up to her chest, and she would find herself in a tight ball on the floor
of her own kitchen, her heart pounding as if she were still a frightened child in that back room.

ashamed afterward. She couldn't explain to anyone why she sometimes screamed at her own empty
apartment, or why she had to fight the urge to hit the wall.

to dread even the thought of work. When she did go, her eyes darted to the corners of the room, and
she frequently looked behind her as she walked from her car to the building, certain someone was
following. She knew no one was there—logically, she knew—but the feeling was as real as the pain in
her shoulders. Minor surprises, like a door slamming in the hallway, sent her into a full panic, her
body bracing for a blow that never came.

church secretary, she nearly drove away her closest friend. People began to walk carefully around
her, which only confirmed her belief that she was dangerous, that she could not be trusted around

never let anyone get close. Being alone with another person felt like an invisible cage closing in;
the exit was not just a door but a symbol of her only chance at escape.

afraid of continuing to live in a world where every closed door felt like a trap, and where the only
safety she could imagine was to never stop running.
