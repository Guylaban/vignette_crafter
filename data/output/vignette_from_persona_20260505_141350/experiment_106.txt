Leilani, a 57-year-old architect of Samoan descent, could still summon the antiseptic smell of the
hospital room with startling clarity. It had been three years since her twenty-four-year-old
daughter, Talia, had died there from a sudden asthma attack, but the olfactory memory arrived
without warning—sharp and chemical, flooding her with a helplessness as fresh as that evening. In
the emergency room, the doctors and nurses had spoken rapid Spanish to one another, and Leilani, who
only knew a few phrases, had caught fragments she could not piece together. “No entiendo,” she had
shouted, but they just kept talking, incomprehensible chatter that burrowed into her memory. The
physical location of that room—the linoleum floor, the curtain track, the sink in the corner—had
become a trigger that seemed to follow her. Walking into any building with similar institutional
tile, or even passing a building that reminded her of the hospital’s layout, could crack the present
open, dropping her back into that night. The divorce had come two years later, partly because her
husband had wanted to move forward and she could not; he had said she was “a ghost” of herself, and
she had not argued.

In the months following Talia’s death, Leilani found it harder and harder to meet friends for coffee
or dinner. She was certain that the moment they looked at her, they saw everything—the panic, the
failure, the daughter she could not save. She had come to believe that her face was a kind of
billboard: “bereaved mother, broken woman.” So she stopped calling back, declined invitations, and
slowly the circle that had once sustained her thinned to nothing. She had lost faith not only in the
medical system that had failed Talia but in people more broadly, and in any higher power that would
allow such a thing. Nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore; her work as an architect, designing
office towers and shopping centers, struck her as absurdly trivial.

What Leilani did instead was run. Every morning before dawn, she laced up and pushed herself through
a ten-mile route along the coastal trail, regardless of rain or pain. Compulsive exercise became the
only thing that calmed the noise inside her head—a way to exhaust herself back into a numb quiet.
But she ran through injuries, ignoring the ache in her left knee until it swelled to the point where
she could barely climb the stairs to her apartment. She avoided hospitals, ambulances, and physical
examinations with a near-phobic determination; when a routine dental checkup required an x-ray, she
canceled three times. As an architect, she now refused every project that involved a medical
facility, claiming she was “just not interested” in the aesthetic. Her boss had grown frustrated,
and she sensed her reputation was shifting from reliable to difficult. Yet the very idea of stepping
into a hospital—by choice or by need—felt like walking into the room where Talia had died, with the
antiseptic smell rising to meet her.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was a convergence of unbearable facts. Her knee had grown
so painful that she could no longer run, and without that outlet her anxiety spiraled into a
constant, trembling vigilance. Her boss had sent her an email suggesting she take a leave of
absence; a colleague had mentioned noticing her flinch at the sound of a janitor’s mop bucket
sloshing with disinfectant. And last week, passing a bench on the coastal trail where she used to
stop with Talia, she had heard two tourists speaking a language she did not understand and had
broken down sobbing, right there in public. Leilani finally called a therapist because she was
terrified that if she did not, she would never find a way to live without the running, without the
avoidance, without the belief that everyone around her could read her loss in her face. She was
hoping—though she could barely admit it—that someone might help her feel that she was not forever
trapped in the moment she had been unable to change.
