Ethan was twenty-three when it happened, a fisherman out of Gloucester whose work had always meant
early mornings alone on the water. One night he was ambushed on his own boat by four men who had
come for his catch and the cash he kept in a waterproof box. They didn’t just take the money. They
bound his wrists behind his back with zip ties, forced him to kneel on the deck, and spent hours
methodically breaking him with a tire iron and a knife. The pain was sharpest in his ribs and across
his palms where they had pressed the blade flat, showing him they could do worse. Through it all,
one of the men had a baby monitor clipped to his belt, and from its speaker came the thin, pulsing
cry of an infant—a sound that cut through the ocean noise and lodged itself in Ethan’s memory. When
they finally left him, bleeding and tied to the mast, he had to wait until dawn before another boat
spotted him.

In the three years since, Ethan has learned to live around the memory, but the memory has not
learned to live quietly inside him. The rage that comes when he thinks of those men is a hot,
shaking thing—he once punched a hole in his kitchen drywall just from replaying their laughter. But
worse than the rage is the way his body betrays him. When a memory surfaces, he feels his arms
wrenched behind his back, his knees pressed hard against a cold surface, even if he is standing in
his own living room. The same ribs ache, the same palms burn, and no amount of stretching or
painkillers makes it stop. He has come to believe that his body is still in that moment, and that
the men might still be in the world, maybe even looking for him again.

He now sleeps with his back pressed against the headboard so he can see the bedroom door. He has
stopped taking the bus into town because the closed space and the strangers’ shoulders trigger the
sense of being trapped, and he has to get off before his next stop, heart pounding, convinced he
hears the squeal of the baby monitor. The first time he heard a real baby cry in a grocery store, he
dropped a jar of pickles and walked out without paying. He does not tell his wife, Maria, the full
reason for these behaviors. When she asks why he stiffens when she touches his shoulder from behind,
he says he’s just jumpy. But the truth is that feeling emotionally vulnerable—letting her see his
fear or his grief—feels as dangerous as kneeling on that deck. He is certain that if he lets down
his guard, something terrible will follow.

His vigilance has become a habit he cannot shake. In any room, he scans for potential weapons: a
fire extinguisher, a heavy lamp, a pair of scissors left on the counter. He listens for footsteps
when Maria is at work, tuning in to creaks in the hallway, the click of a lock, anything that might
mean someone is coming inside. He has told himself this is just being prepared, but he knows it is a
lie. He is waiting for the next attack. His marriage has begun to fray—Maria feels locked out, and
she has stopped asking him to go to dinner with friends because he insists on sitting with his back
to the wall and will not explain why.

What finally brings Ethan to the clinic is a morning on his own boat, the only place he still feels
somewhat safe. He was hauling traps when a buoy line tangled, and for a few seconds his arm was
pinned. The sudden restraint, the pressure on his wrist, sent him into a full reenactment: he
collapsed onto the deck, his body rigid in the same kneeling position, his ribs aching, and he heard
the baby crying from nowhere. He lay there for five minutes, trembling with rage and pain, before he
could stand. That night, he told Maria he needed help. He is afraid of what his anger might make him
do, but more than that, he is afraid that the men are still out there, and that he will never stop
listening for a sound that ended years ago. He hopes, quietly, that talking about it might release
his body from the position it has held for so long.
