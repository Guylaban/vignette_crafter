hallway, listening. Her husband’s breathing was even behind the door. She had already checked the
front door twice and the kitchen window once, but the click of the lock had not sat right in her
chest. She padded back to the entryway, placed her hand on the deadbolt, and pressed—once,
twice—until she felt the metal seat fully into the frame. Only then did her shoulders lower a
fraction of an inch. She would be up again before dawn, because her body no longer trusted the night
to stay closed around her.

The stalking had begun eighteen months earlier, during a closing shift at the burger chain where she
had worked for eleven years. A middle-aged man in a baseball cap had lingered at the counter after
the last customer left, asking about the fryer oil, the schedule, whether she worked alone. Samira
had smiled politely, the way she smiled at all customers, and told him she needed to lock up. He
left, but the next evening he was there again, this time in the parking lot, leaning against a
sedan. He waved. She waved back, uncertain. Over the following weeks he appeared on her bus route,
then near her apartment building, once holding a takeout bag from her restaurant as if it were a
joke only he understood. She never called the police. She told herself she was overreacting. But she
began watching faces the way she had once watched for oil spills on the kitchen floor—automatically,
scanning for something off. Every customer who lingered too long, every man who held her gaze an
extra second, became a potential signal. At work she checked the back door between orders, wedging a
chair under the handle even though the lock worked fine. Her manager noticed and asked if she was
okay. Samira said she was just tired.

She did not tell anyone about the smells. When the memory surfaced unbidden—the grainy texture of
the parking lot asphalt, the way the streetlight buzzed—she sometimes caught a phantom scent of the
man’s cheap cologne, sharp and powdery, as if he had just walked past her in the break room. The
olfactory flash would hit without warning, and she would freeze, her hand gripping the fry basket
until the oil burned through her glove. Worse were the times when the memory came and she felt
nothing at all—a cold, hollow numbness, as though the story had happened to a stranger in a movie
she had once watched. She could recite the facts (he followed me for four months, he left notes on
my car, he was arrested when a coworker called 911), but the words felt borrowed. Sometimes she
looked at her own hands and thought, *this cannot be real*. The whole thing was too extreme, too
lurid, to have happened to a woman who spent her days wiping counters and her nights beside a
husband who snored softly. She had become someone she did not recognize: a woman who checked her own
reflection in the window to see if he might be standing behind her.

The person she used to be—the one who chatted with regulars, who walked to the bus stop without
scanning every car—had disappeared so gradually that Samira only noticed the absence when her
daughter called and asked why she never visited. Samira said she was busy. The truth was she could
not tolerate the subway, the press of bodies, the way a stranger’s sleeve might brush her arm. She
had stopped taking alternate routes home because every new street felt like a trap. At home she had
begun to distrust even her husband’s assurances. *People can’t be trusted*, she told herself, *not
really*. He meant well, but he had not been there when the man stood outside the restaurant window
tapping his watch. She did not say this aloud. She kept the thought coiled inside, along with the
belief that if she ever let herself think about the stalking in full—if she let the memory unspool
from beginning to end—she would not be able to handle it. She would shatter. So she pushed the
images away, but the vigilance remained: her body stayed tensed for a threat that never quite
arrived, and the checking continued.

What finally brought Samira to a therapist’s office was not her own decision. Her husband found her
standing at the front door at four in the morning, still in her work uniform from the day before,
turning the deadbolt back and forth. He told her gently that she needed help. Samira nodded, but
inside she was terrified—not of the memory, but of what might happen if she stopped watching. She
wanted to stop, desperately. She wanted to feel the night as a time for rest, not surveillance. She
wanted to walk into her own kitchen without smelling cologne. But the thought of letting go of the
vigilance felt like standing at the edge of a dark hole. She was afraid of what she would find if
she looked down. And she was afraid, too, that the person she had become was the only person left.
