Sofia Martinez was seven years old the first time she tasted copper. Not from a penny or a piece of
metal, but from her own lip where she had bitten down so hard trying not to scream that she drew
blood. She was crouched behind the kitchen counter, a half-eaten mango slice still sticky in her
hand, while her father’s voice rose and fell like a storm and her mother’s body made a sound against
the wall that Sofia would never be able to describe but could always taste. The mango and the blood
and the kitchen floor—those pieces were sharp and clear, but the order of what came next was a
tangle. She remembered the door slamming, remembered her mother’s hand on her shoulder, remembered
being told to go to her room, but she could never line them up the right way. Sometimes when the
memory returned it was as if she were still seven, still crouched, still tasting fruit and salt, and
the rest of her life had not happened yet.

Now Sofia was nineteen, and she drove trains through the dark for a living. She had taken the job
because it paid well and required no small talk, but the irony was not lost on her. Every shift was
a battle with the very things that stalked her. The tunnel ahead, black and absolute, made her hands
seize up on the throttle. The sudden blast of the horn—hers or another train’s—sent her heart
slamming into her ribs, and then that pounding in her chest became its own trigger. Her own
heartbeat, fast and frightened, summoned the same helplessness she had felt behind the counter: I
can’t stop bad things from happening to me.

She had tried to outrun the feeling by filling every hour. She worked double shifts whenever she
could, and when she was not on the rails she was cleaning her apartment, organizing her closet,
scrolling through her phone until her eyes burned. Busyness was the only thing that made the
remembering stop, but it left her hollow and thin. She had started skipping meals months ago, not
because she planned to, but because the sight of food sometimes brought back that mango taste—the
sweet and the metallic together—and her stomach would turn. She had lost weight she did not have to
lose, and her supervisor had asked if she was okay. Sofia said she was fine. She was not fine.

At home, she checked the locks on her door and windows three times before she could sit down, and
then again before she could sleep. But she rarely slept. Night was a wide open fear. Every creak was
a footstep, every distant train horn a threat. She had grown certain that darkness itself was
dangerous—not just the absence of light, but a presence that waited for her to let her guard down.
She had begun keeping a small lamp on all night, but even then she lay rigid, listening.

What she thought about most often was how things might have been different. If she had called out,
if she had run to the phone, if she had been braver. She had been seven, but that did not matter in
the story she told herself. She had failed to protect, and the event had happened because of the way
she acted—because she had stayed frozen, because she had said nothing. The counterfactual thinking
looped endlessly: If I had done something, my mother would not have been hurt. If I had done
something, we would have left sooner. If I had done something, I would not be this way now.

The night she finally sought help, she had been on a late shift. A loud bang from a coupling error
sent her into a panic so complete that she locked herself in the break room, unable to stop shaking.
Her supervisor found her there, knees to her chest, repeating that she could not go back out. She
was terrified of losing her job, but she was more terrified of another night like that. She wanted
to stop being afraid. She wanted to eat a meal without tasting blood. She wanted to lie down in the
dark and believe she was safe. She did not know if that was possible, but she had come to find out.
