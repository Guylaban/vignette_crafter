Mai was forty years old when her body turned on her. A routine kidney infection escalated into
septic shock within hours, and she found herself in an ICU bed, surrounded by the hiss of
ventilators and the sharp, chemical smell of antiseptic that seemed to coat everything—the sheets,
her skin, the air itself. She remembered the cold: the way the ice packs pressed against her armpits
and groin to bring her fever down, the shock of refrigerated IV fluids entering her arm, the way she
couldn’t stop shivering under the thin hospital blanket. What she did not remember were entire
days—gaps where the calendar jumped forward without her consent. But other moments were violently
clear: the needle sliding into her wrist, the doctor’s face looming over her as he inserted a
central line, the drip. The drip especially—the slow, rhythmic fall of medication into the tubing, a
sound and sight that now seemed to be burned onto the back of her eyelids.

For months after she was discharged, Mai told herself it was over. She had survived. But the memory
did not behave like a normal memory. Sometimes she would be standing at the kitchen counter,
chopping vegetables for her daughter’s dinner, and a wave of terror would crash over her—her heart
hammering, her breath short—with no image attached. Just the emotion, raw and inexplicable, as if
her body remembered what her mind had erased. At other times, the whole experience felt like a story
that had happened to someone else. She would catch herself thinking, *That wasn’t real*, even as she
knew it was. The gaps in her memory, the extreme clarity of a few moments, the floating sense of
unreality—all of it made her wonder if she was losing her grip on reality.

Terrified of that possibility, Mai began monitoring her own thoughts. She scanned her mind
constantly for signs that she was going crazy—for intrusive images, for strange beliefs, for any
hint that her mind was unraveling. The moment a thought about the hospital surfaced, she would
actively push it away, trying to erase the memory entirely. But the effort itself became exhausting.
She came to believe that her life had been destroyed by what happened. She was no longer the person
who could run a project team, manage deadlines, or even trust her own body. Her body, she was
certain, had been permanently damaged. Even though her doctors said she had made a full physical
recovery, she felt fragile—a paper-thin shell that could tear open at any moment.

As a project manager at a tech firm, Mai had once thrived on the fast pace. Now she took elaborate
detours to avoid driving past the hospital where she had been treated. She cancelled her annual
physical, then her dental checkup, then any appointment that involved a waiting room. When her
husband suggested they watch a medical drama, she left the room. At work, she avoided the break room
near the elevator because it had a faint antiseptic smell—the cleaning crew used a brand similar to
the hospital’s. One winter morning, stepping out of her car, a blast of cold air hit her face, and
she froze. She could not move. Her chest tightened, and she felt the same icy panic she had felt in
the ICU, but she could not summon the image of the room. It was just cold, and she was terrified.

Her most important role in life, she believed, was as a mother to her nine-year-old daughter, Layla.
Mai had always prided herself on being present, reliable, the one who never missed a school event.
But after the trauma, she found herself pulling away. She could not bring herself to take Layla to
the pediatrician for a routine vaccination—she asked her husband to go instead. When Layla came home
with a scraped knee, Mai’s stomach turned; she looked away, unable to handle the sight of blood and
bandages. She felt like a failure. The shame was immense: she had failed in her most important role.
She was supposed to protect her child, and now she could not even stand near a first-aid kit.

What finally brought Mai to therapy was Layla’s soccer game. It was a chilly autumn afternoon, and
Mai was sitting on the bleachers, cheering. A gust of wind hit her bare arms. Without warning, her
entire body went into crisis—pounding heart, tunnel vision, a sense of imminent doom. She had no
image of the ICU, no flashback. Just pure, senseless terror. She excused herself and sat in the car,
shaking, and for the first time she said aloud to her husband, “I think I’m going crazy.” He held
her hand and said, “You need help.” She agreed.

Now, sitting in the therapist’s office, Mai is afraid of two things: that talking about the event
will make it real again, and that she will discover she is, in fact, permanently broken. But she is
also hoping—quietly, almost against her will—that someone can help her become the mother and the
person she used to be before her body became a minefield of smells, cold, and needles.
