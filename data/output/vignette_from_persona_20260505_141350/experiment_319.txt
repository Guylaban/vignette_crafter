Leilani still remembers the taste. It was the only thing that stayed sharp while everything else
dissolved into fragments—the metallic tang of dust and blood, blended with the sickly sweetness of
the millet porridge she’d been spooning into plastic bowls when the first shots cracked. She had
been a relief worker for a small NGO in a conflict zone, her third month in a camp outside the city.
That day, a group of armed fighters had stormed the distribution point. She remembered the
screaming, the crush of bodies, and then a mother shoving an infant into her arms before the woman
was pulled away. She had held the child, crouched behind an overturned table, and she remembered
tasting the dust and something else—the baby’s own blood on her cheek, or maybe just the salt of her
own tears. What she could not remember, no matter how hard she tried, was how she got out. There
were minutes—maybe hours—that were simply gone. Whenever she tried to deliberately recall what
happened next, her mind would slip sideways, and before she reached the end of the memory she would
shove it away, not ready to find out what she might have missed or done wrong.

Back home, after the evacuation and the debriefings, Leilani had tried to return to ordinary life.
She was already trained as a parole officer, and after the divorce—her ex-husband had said she was
“a different person” after she came back—she threw herself into the work. But the triggers found her
there. The taste would hit without warning: a sip of coffee, the metallic tang of a paperclip in her
mouth, and suddenly she was back behind that table, her heart slamming. More often, it was the
anger. Parole officers dealt with frustration daily: men and women who were angry at the system, at
their circumstances, at her. A client raising his voice in a routine check-in would send a cold
spike through her chest. She would see the fight erupting in the camp, the same fury in a stranger’s
eyes, and she would freeze. She had learned to keep her own face neutral, but inside she was
running.

Worst were the victim memories. Her caseload included violent offenders, and every time she read a
victim impact statement or visited a crime scene, she saw the mother and the child. She would lie
awake replaying a fragment she could not hold: the infant’s fingers, the mother’s scream. She had
come to believe that she had failed them both, that if she could only remember what she did in those
missing minutes she might know for sure. But trying to remember only made the memory slide further
away, like a dream dissolving at dawn.

At work, she had begun avoiding certain tasks. She would ask colleagues to handle angry clients. She
would take the long way past the break room where the coffee machine sat, because the taste of stale
brew made her nauseous. Her supervisor had noticed she was less reliable, more withdrawn. She had
started calling in sick on days when she knew a particularly volatile parolee was due. The
consequences had accumulated: a formal warning, a strained friendship with a coworker who said she
“wasn’t herself.” Leilani knew she wasn’t. She felt hollowed out, controlled by the taste and the
rage and the faces.

What finally brought her to therapy was a new case: a young man recently released for armed robbery,
whose anger during their first meeting had been quiet but intense. He reminded her of the fighter
who had shouted orders in the camp. That night, she had a nightmare she could not recall upon
waking, only the taste of porridge and blood thick in her throat. She knew she could not go on this
way. She was afraid—terrified of what she might discover if she ever fully remembered—but more
afraid of losing her job and whatever remained of her life. She hoped that someone could help her
hold the memory still, just long enough to see it clearly, so that it might stop owning her.
