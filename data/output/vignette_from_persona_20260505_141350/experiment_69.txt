Kwame was fifty-eight when the call came in about a suspicious package in a downtown parking garage.
After thirty-four years as a bomb disposal technician, he had learned to read the subtle signs—the
geometry of a circuit, the faint smell of homemade accelerant—but nothing had prepared him for the
tear-gas canister rigged to release a chemical agent. In the moment of detonation, there was a
deafening crack that seemed to split the air itself, and then a fine, burning mist enveloped him. He
felt his throat seize, his eyes stream, and when he finally crawled out of the garage, his hands
shaking, an ambulance was already waiting. The antiseptic sting of alcohol wipes and the sharp,
clean smell of the hospital corridor would become the scent of his worst memory.

For the first few weeks after the exposure, Kwame told himself he was fine. He returned to work as
soon as the doctors cleared him, saying nothing about the way his heart hammered when a colleague
slammed a locker door. He began to notice that he could not sit still in the break room; his eyes
would drift from the door to the windows to the ceiling vents, cataloging every possible way out
before he could even think about coffee. It became automatic, almost a second job: scanning the room
for danger the moment he entered any indoor space. At home, his wife Miriam would place a hand on
his shoulder to get his attention, and he would flinch so hard he nearly knocked over a table. He
started sleeping with the bedroom door open, even though he had always liked the privacy, because
being alone in a closed room felt like being trapped in the garage again.

The triggers multiplied quietly. A sudden bang from a car backfiring on his street would send him
into a cold sweat, his mind replaying the crack of the canister. A routine physical at the clinic,
with its bleached sheets and the faint chemical scent of disinfectant, made him feel so lightheaded
that he left before the doctor could even take his blood pressure. He began avoiding hospitals
altogether, and when Miriam asked him to come for her own appointment, he made an excuse about
overtime. That was true, in a way: Kwame had started working twelve-hour shifts, then fourteen, then
sixteen. He told himself he was dedicated, that the unit needed his experience, but what he could
not say aloud was that when he was focused on a dismantled fuse or a schematic, he could not hear
the echoes of the garage in his head. Work became a container, a place where he could be safe
because he was never alone with his thoughts. Over the months, the overtime became a habit, and
Miriam began sleeping alone more often than not.

What frightened Kwame most was the sense that danger was still there, even when he knew it was not.
He would stand at the entrance to a supermarket and identify three exit routes before stepping
inside. In a restaurant, he sat facing the door and made sure he could see the kitchen exit. He had
come to believe that if he let his guard down for even a moment, something would find him—a gas
leak, a stray sound, a touch from behind that he had not anticipated. The startle response became a
constant humiliation; a colleague once tapped his shoulder to ask a question, and Kwame spun around
with a shout, his hands up in a defensive stance. After that, he tried to stand with his back to a
wall whenever possible.

The cost of all this vigilance was a kind of quiet erosion. His marriage began to feel like a
distant thing, built more on avoidance than on connection. Miriam stopped asking why he did not want
to go to the movies or to family dinners; she just arranged her own plans. Kwame noticed that he
could no longer tolerate being home alone on his day off—the silence was too loud, too likely to be
broken by the wrong sound—so he went to work even when he was not scheduled. His boss commented that
he was burning out, and for the first time, Kwame admitted to himself that he might be breaking
down.

What brought him into the clinic, finally, was a simple moment: he was driving home from a double
shift, and a truck backfired in the lane next to him. He jerked the wheel, nearly hitting a curb,
and pulled over, shaking. He sat there for twenty minutes, unable to move, while the antiseptic
smell of his own sweat reminded him of that hospital gurney. When he got home, Miriam was waiting
with a phone number. She did not say much, just handed it to him. He took it, and now he sits in a
therapist’s waiting room, terrified of what might come next but more afraid of what will happen if
he keeps running. He hopes, without quite believing it, that somewhere past the scanning and the
startle and the constant planning for escape, there might be a version of himself who can just walk
into a room.
