happened next was a blur of running, his wife’s hand in his, the acrid smell of burning rubber, and
a terrible weight on his chest that made it impossible to draw breath. He had led his family into a
drainage ditch, but the soldiers had followed, and afterward, when he crawled out, there was only
silence and a sky that felt too wide. Eighty-nine civilians had died. Sok survived because he lay
still under a body that was not his. He had not spoken of that day in forty-seven years.

The first year after the war, Sok noticed that his memory of the event was incomplete. He could
recall the trucks, the dust, the feeling of suffocation, but the faces of the people next to him
were gone. The memory felt as though it belonged to a stranger, not to the man who now stood at a
welding table. He would sometimes tell himself that the story could not have happened—it was too
extreme, too unreal—but then a car backfired on the street outside and his chest would tighten. The

deeply unfair, that the soldiers who had done it had never been held accountable, and that his own
actions—running to the ditch instead of the forest—had caused the deaths. He could not shake the
conviction that if he had acted differently, his neighbors would have lived.

Over the decades, Sok had built a careful life around avoidance. He never talked about the war, not

at family gatherings, he would redirect his thoughts, forcing himself to count the rivets on a steel
beam or to replay a baseball game from 1984. He avoided open spaces—parks, parking lots, the
backyard—preferring the cramped intimacy of his shop. He had difficulty concentrating; his son had

alert. His wife had long stopped asking why he slept in his clothes.
