Leilani was seven years old the first time her father’s hand connected with her face hard enough to
leave a red wel that lasted for days. By the time she was twelve, she had learned to read the
temperature of a room by the set of his jaw, the clench of his fist at his side, the way his voice

them blurred together into a single, long ache—but certain moments were carved in high definition:
once, he grabbed her by the hair and slammed her head against the bathroom door frame while
screaming about a spill on the kitchen floor. The sound of his voice that day, the way the wood
splintered against her skull, the sudden quiet after—those fragments were crystalline. Everything
before and after was fog, a gap she could not fill. Sometimes she wondered if the memory even
belonged to her. It felt like a story she had been told about someone else, or like a scene from a
movie she had walked in on halfway through. She could never decide whether the blank spaces meant
she had locked them away deliberately, or whether her mind simply refused to hold them.

As an adult, Leilani became a plumber. The work suited her: it was concrete, mechanical, and kept
her hands busy. But the job also placed her in homes and job sites where she could not control what
happened around her. A customer raising his voice over a leaky pipe sent a jolt through her chest
that she could not hide—her shoulders would hitch, her hands would freeze on the wrench, and she
would have to look away until her vision cleared. A homeowner standing behind her, watching her
work, made the back of her neck prickle with the certainty that she was about to be struck. She had
learned to position herself so that her back was never fully turned. Once, a foreman on a large job
expressed mild frustration about a delay—his tone calm, his face neutral—and Leilani felt her mouth
go dry, her pulse hammering, as though she were back in that bathroom doorway. She excused herself
and sat in her truck for fifteen minutes, telling herself she was just tired.

To manage the constant sense of danger, Leilani kept her mind occupied every waking moment. She
listened to podcasts through earbuds while cutting pipe, answered work calls before they could ring
twice, and invented small mental puzzles—counting threads on a bolt, memorizing sequences of serial
numbers—to block out intrusive fragments. The effort left her exhausted, and she often struggled to
focus on simple instructions. At the end of a shift, her body remained tensed, as if waiting for
something to strike. A sudden movement in her peripheral vision—a colleague walking around a corner,
a bird darting past a window—made her flinch as though it were an attack. She had come to believe
that vigilance was the only thing keeping her safe.

Food had become another battleground. Leilani found that eating made her feel exposed: the act of
chewing, of sitting still, left her feeling vulnerable to watching eyes. Over the months, she had
begun skipping meals deliberately, especially when she was on job sites where strangers might
observe her. She told herself she was simply not hungry, but the truth was that eating felt like
letting her guard down. Her husband noticed the weight loss and the way she would push her plate
aside at dinner, but she brushed off his concern. He was a gentle man, patient and soft-spoken, yet
even his occasional frustration—a raised voice over a missed appointment or a bill—sent her
retreating into a cold, silent place. She could see the hurt in his eyes, but she could not stop
herself from stepping back when his tone rose even slightly.

What finally drove Leilani to seek help was not the flashbacks or the tension she carried like a
second skin. It was the growing sense that the memory had never really been hers—that the girl who
had been hurt was not the woman now installing a toilet in a suburban bathroom. But the fear was
unmistakably her own, and it was ruining her marriage, her appetite, her ability to do a job she had
once loved. She came to the clinic hoping to understand why the past felt like a stranger's story,
and why that stranger’s flinch still lived in her own muscles. She was afraid that if she let
herself talk about it openly, she would fall apart—but she was more afraid of spending the rest of
her life watching doors and counting breaths.
