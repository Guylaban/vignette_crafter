Priya was twenty-two when it happened, and the details of those three days still came to her not as
a story she told but as a sudden, unwelcome arrival. The basement, the ropes, the questions she
couldn't answer—the men who didn't believe her. She had been a newlywed then, visiting her husband's
home village in South India for the first time, and she had been taken from the market by men who
thought her husband's family owed them money. They did not hurt her the way the movies showed. They
burned her with cigarettes, one on each hand, and they kept her in a room with no windows until the
debt was settled. She never saw their faces, only their boots and the gleam of the metal they used.
When she was released, she walked out into the sunlight and never told anyone the full story, not
even her husband. She told him she had been held in a shed, and that was true enough.

In the six years since, Priya had built a life around not telling. She worked as a tailor from a
small room in her Queens apartment, altering dresses and stitching curtains for neighbors who
brought their fabric to her door. The work was good because it kept her hands busy and her mind on
the hemline, the seam allowance, the sharp line of a stitch. She took jobs only from women she knew,
never from strangers, and she never let a customer stand between her and the door. Every room she
entered—the bank, the grocery, the hallway of her own building—she found herself counting steps to
the nearest exit, noting whether the door pushed outward or inward, whether the lock turned easily.
It was not a choice; it was a reflex she could not stop, like blinking in a bright light.

The triggers were everywhere, though most people did not see them. A sudden knock at the door sent
her pulse racing and her hands shaking for hours afterward. The smell of cigarette smoke on a
neighbor's jacket made her stomach clench and her throat tighten until she could not swallow. She
had stopped going to the doctor three years ago after a routine gynecological exam left her
hyperventilating on the table, convinced that the stirrups were ropes and the doctor's gloves were
the hands of men she could not name. She had a persistent cough now, a rasp that came and went, but
she told herself it was nothing. Her husband begged her to see a physician; she said she would think
about it, and then she did not. She avoided not only medical care but any situation that made her
feel a strong emotion—tears, anger, even joy—because she had learned that feeling too much meant
feeling everything, and once the feelings began, they became the basement again. She could not cry
without losing control, so she did not cry. She could not laugh without the laughter turning into a
gasp, so she did not laugh.

The worst part, the part that exhausted her most, was the vigilance it took not to relax. Priya
never sat with her back to a room. She never closed her eyes in a car. She did not lie down to rest
during the day, because every time she tried, her body would jerk awake with the certainty that
something was coming for her. So she stayed upright, stayed busy, stayed working at her sewing
machine until her shoulders ached and her eyes burned. She told herself it was diligence, but it was
fear wearing the mask of industry.

She came to therapy because of a flashback in the subway last month. She had been standing on the
platform, scanning for exits as always, when a man behind her dropped a metal tool from his bag. The
clang hit the concrete and Priya was no longer in the station. She was on the floor of a room with
no windows, and she could feel the burn on her hand again, and she screamed before she could stop
herself. Two strangers helped her to a bench. She told them she was fine. She was not fine. Her
husband found her crying in the bathroom that night—the first time she had cried in years—and he
made an appointment for her without asking. She went because she was too tired to fight, and because
somewhere underneath the exhaustion there was a thread of hope that she might one day walk into a
room and not think about the exits first. She was terrified of what the therapy would ask of her—the
talking, the remembering, the feeling—but she was also terrified of another day spent bracing for a
clang that had already happened.
