Amir was eleven years old the first time he learned that the safest place in a room was the wall.
His father’s hand had come across the dinner table without warning—not a slap, but a hard, flat
strike to the side of Amir’s head that sent his vision white for a moment. The argument had been
about nothing: a grade, a forgotten chore, a tone of voice Amir didn’t even remember using. What he
remembered was the shock of being touched, the way his mother’s face crumpled as she tried not to
cry, and the certainty that any moment of disagreement could turn into a blow. Over the years that
followed, the pattern became as predictable as gravity. A raised voice meant flinch. A sudden hand
near his shoulder meant freeze. And crying—whether his mother’s silent tears after an incident, or
later, anyone else’s—meant something was terribly, dangerously wrong.

Now nineteen, married for just over a year, and working as an environmental scientist for a small
consulting firm, Amir had built a life that looked steady from the outside. But the internal
architecture of that life was entirely defensive. In the office he chose his desk second, after
watching where his coworkers sat—always the corner desk with his back to the wall, his line of sight
open to the door and every face that entered. When he met with clients, he arrived early to seat
himself at the far end of the conference table, or positioned himself against a bookshelf. He had
come to believe that he could read danger in a furrowed brow, a too-quick exhale, a gaze that
lingered a half-second too long. This scanning had become automatic, almost exhausting, but letting
it go felt like taking off a helmet in a war zone.

At home, his wife Leila had begun to notice that he left the room whenever she started to
cry—whether from a sad movie or a stressful phone call. He never said why; he only knew that the
sound of it made his chest tighten and his feet move toward the door. Arguments were worse. When he
felt a disagreement coming, even about something small like whose turn it was to do the grocery
shopping, Amir would go silent, agree too quickly, or physically leave the house. He had never told
Leila that the last time he’d argued with his father, he’d ended up with a cracked rib. She only
knew that her husband seemed to disappear whenever things got hard.

Avoiding people was simpler. He had no close friends at work, no one he met for coffee. He took his
lunch at his desk or in his car, and he scheduled field sampling days so he could be alone in the
wetlands he monitored, away from colleagues. But that solitude brought its own terror: being alone
in an empty building or a quiet stretch of marsh made his skin prickle. He was certain that in an
open, unguarded space, something could come at him from any direction. So he kept his phone on, kept
his back to a tree when he stopped, and never stayed late at the office if he could help it.

The cost of these habits had become visible. His supervisor had noted that he seemed “hard to
collaborate with” and had put him on solo projects. Leila had started sleeping on the far side of
the bed after waking one night to find Amir rigid, his eyes open, his arm locked across his chest as
though bracing for a blow. She had been reaching out to touch his shoulder—something he’d told her
he hated, being touched without warning—and he had jerked away so violently he hit his head on the
headboard.

What finally brought Amir to a therapist’s office was not a single crisis but a slow erosion. He was
tired of scanning faces, of feeling safe only in corners, of watching his wife’s confusion turn into
frustration. He wanted to believe that a disagreement could end without harm, that crying didn’t
mean someone was about to be hurt, that he could sit in the middle of a room and still breathe. He
came because he was afraid that if he didn’t learn a different way to live, he would lose the
marriage he had fought to build—and because, for the first time in years, he was willing to let
someone see the fear he carried.
