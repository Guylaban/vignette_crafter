At seventy-seven, Dr. Anela Kealoha had spent more than four decades in operating rooms, her hands
steady as she repaired torn vessels and resected malignancies. She had been the one who delivered
the news to hundreds of families, never flinching. Then, two years ago, she became the patient. A
routine physical revealed a pancreatic mass, and the world tilted. She remembered the radiologist’s
voice, the way the light caught the edge of the film, and then—nothing. There were hours, maybe
days, that existed only as fragments: the cold of an IV line, a nurse’s hand on her shoulder, the
beeping of a monitor that seemed to come from very far away. Some moments were searingly clear—the
face of the anesthesiologist as she counted backward—but the spaces between them were blank, like
pages torn from a book. She could not recall the conversation where her surgeon explained the
prognosis, nor the moment her daughter arrived at the hospital. These gaps troubled her, and in the
quiet of her recovery, she began to turn them over, searching for meaning. Why had this happened to
her? She forced herself to think only positive thoughts, repeating affirmations about gratitude and
resilience, but the blanks in her memory seemed to mock her efforts. The harder she tried to fill
them with light, the more they yawned open, and the more she felt that the person she had become was
unrecognizable. The woman who had once commanded an OR, who had been certain of her place in the
world, now felt like a stranger in her own skin. She had lost her faith in the order of things—in
medicine, in fairness, in the God she had never questioned. The fragmented quality of her memories
seemed to confirm that she was broken, that the coherence of her life had been shattered along with
her pancreas.

In the months following her recovery, Anela developed a careful set of routines. She retired from
surgery, telling colleagues she was ready for a quieter life. But the truth was more specific: she
could not bear the sight of a hospital. The beeping of a heart monitor on a television show sent her
heart racing. She changed her primary care doctor to one whose office was in a standalone building,
far from the hospital campus. When her daughter suggested a routine mammogram, Anela refused,
inventing a scheduling conflict. She told herself she was being sensible, protecting her peace, but
the avoidance fed something darker. Each time she turned away from a medical cue, she felt a flicker
of relief, followed by a deeper conviction that she was no longer the person who could face such
things. The memory gaps, which she tried to suppress with determined positivity, only worsened. She
would find herself staring at the refrigerator, unable to recall the drive home from the grocery
store, and she would think: see, it is getting worse. The person I am now cannot trust her own mind.

The cycle tightened. The more she avoided, the more her memories felt like disconnected shards, and
the more she believed she had lost herself entirely. She began to isolate from old friends,
canceling dinner plans because the conversation might turn to health, to aging, to the body’s
betrayals. She had come to believe that the woman who had once been a surgeon was gone, replaced by
someone fragile, someone who could not even look at a stethoscope without feeling the floor drop
away. What frightened her most was the recognition that her own mind had become unreliable—that the
gaps in her memory were not just absences but evidence of a fundamental unraveling. She could not
remember the details of her own survival, and so she could not trust that she would survive again.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment of clarity that felt like a betrayal. She was
walking past a fire station when the siren wailed, and instead of panic, she felt only a dull,
familiar ache. She realized she had not felt anything sharp in months—only the gray fog of avoidance
and the hollow certainty that she was no longer herself. She wanted, she told the therapist in her
first session, to know who she was now, and whether that person could live without constant
vigilance. She was most afraid that the answer was no.
