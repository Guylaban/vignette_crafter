traffic stop had seemed mundane until it wasn’t. The driver of the sedan had been drunk, and when

twelve feet away, frozen in the back seat, the door locked. He remembers the taste of copper in his
mouth, the way his vision narrowed to a tunnel, and the certainty, absolute and cold, that he was
going to die in that moment. He didn’t. The officer survived, though with a shattered femur. Makai
walked away physically unharmed.

That was three years ago. Now, every time the first officer steps onto the flight deck and the cabin
door seals behind him, Makai feels a flicker of the same tunnel vision. It’s not the flying that
gets him—the air is empty, the sky is vast, and there are no strangers leaning into his space. It’s
the delays. A late fuel truck, a hold on the runway, a message from the tower that their slot has
been pushed back twenty minutes—each one feels like the moment before something terrible happens. He
finds himself gripping the yoke too hard, his jaw tight, scanning the horizon for something he
cannot name. He has come to believe that when something takes longer than expected, it is because
the universe is lining up a catastrophe. His co-pilots have noticed. They ask if he’s okay. He says
he’s fine. He isn’t.

The memories come back with a force that surprises him every time. They are more real than ordinary
memories—the smell of the patrol car’s vinyl seat, the exact angle of the sun through the
windshield, the sound of the officer’s voice saying “stay in the car.” When these memories surface,
they do not feel like the past. They feel like the present, like he is back in that back seat, and
the terror is as fresh as it was then. Sometimes, the memory is so extreme that he has trouble
believing it actually happened to him, as though he is recalling a scene from a movie he once
watched. But that disbelief only makes the next intrusion more disorienting. The memories are
triggered by ordinary things: the smell of gasoline, the weight of a seatbelt across his chest, the
sensation of his stomach dropping during turbulence. Lately, even nausea from a bad meal can send
him spiraling, because his body remembers the sick, hollow feeling of watching a man raise a gun.

He has started avoiding driving with people. When his ex-wife asked for a ride to the airport last
month for their daughter’s flight, he told her to take a taxi. He could not explain why the thought
of another person in the passenger seat made his palms sweat. He knows it is irrational, but he is
certain that if he is not alone, something bad will happen—that the proximity of another body will
somehow invite the same violence. At work, he has become hypervigilant in ways that are hard to
hide. He startles at the clatter of a coffee cup in the crew lounge, at the sudden hiss of the cabin
pressurization system, at the sound of footsteps approaching from behind. When strangers—passengers,
gate agents, even the ramp crew—walk toward him in the terminal, he feels a spike of adrenaline, a
readiness to defend himself that makes no sense in a brightly lit airport. He knows this, and he is
embarrassed by it, but the feeling does not listen to reason.

The loop is tight now. A delay triggers a threat response, which triggers a memory, which makes him
feel vulnerable again, which makes the next delay feel even more dangerous. He has started arriving
at the airport two hours before his check-in just to have a buffer, but even that does not help,
because the waiting itself is the problem. His performance reviews remain strong—he is a good
pilot—but he has stopped volunteering for overtime, and his captain has asked if everything is all
right at home. Makai said yes, because he could not say that home is where he sits in the dark,
replaying a three-minute sequence of events that will not let him go. He finally sought help after a
passenger with a loud cough made him flinch so badly that he nearly dropped his flight bag in front
of the gate agent. He is afraid that if he does not fix this, he will lose the only job he has ever
loved. He is afraid that the memory will always feel more real than the life he is living now.
