Raymond is a seventy-one-year-old security guard who has been married for forty-three years. The
trauma that shattered his sense of competence occurred two years ago, during a late-night shift at a
municipal parking structure. Two men approached his booth, and before he could react, one pressed a
handgun against the glass and demanded the cash drawer. Raymond froze. He handed over the money,
hands trembling, while the second man laughed and said, “You’re supposed to protect people, old
man.” They left. Raymond called the police, but the men were never caught. What haunts him most is
not the gun, but the laughter—and the certainty that he should have done something, anything, to
stop them.

In the months that followed, Raymond found he could not shake the vivid, detailed images of the
booth, the gun, the men’s faces. The memory plays in his mind like a film he cannot pause. Yet
paradoxically, when he tries to tell his wife what happened, the story comes out in fragments; he
cannot arrange the sequence in his head, and sometimes he recites the facts while feeling completely
numb, as if the event happened to a stranger. He has come to believe that another person—someone
younger, sharper—would never have let himself be cornered like that. This thought has calcified into
a deeper appraisal: he has failed in his most important role in life. He was supposed to be the one
who kept others safe, and he could not even protect himself. Because he now sees himself as
fundamentally unable to cope, even the smallest upset—a car backfiring, a raised voice at the
grocery store—floods him with dread. He tells his therapist, “I can’t handle anything anymore. I’m
useless.”

This sense of helplessness drives a rigid set of habits. Every time Raymond enters a room—his own
living room, the break room at work, a restaurant—he must sit with his back against a wall so he can
see the entire space. He scans faces constantly, vigilant for anyone who might be dangerous. The
behavior makes him feel briefly in control, but it also keeps the memory alive: each time he checks
a corner or studies a stranger’s hands, the parking structure booth flashes back into his mind. The
hypervigilance itself reinforces his belief that he is still vulnerable, still failing. He has also
begun to ruminate obsessively about revenge—imagining what he would do if he ever saw the assailants
again, running through scenarios of getting even. These fantasies give him a fleeting sense of
power, but they also pull him deeper into the memory, and afterward he feels more ashamed and more
convinced that he is broken.

The cycle has tightened over the past year. The more Raymond scans and replans, the more vivid the
memory grows; the more vivid it grows, the more he believes he cannot cope. His wife has noticed
that he no longer joins her for Sunday brunch because he cannot tolerate sitting in the middle of
the diner. He has asked his supervisor to move him to a back-office security post, away from the
public entrance. At first, the avoidance felt like relief. Now he feels trapped by his own
precautions, and the rumination about revenge has become almost constant. What finally brought him
to therapy was a moment last week when his granddaughter hugged him and he felt nothing—just
numbness, as if he were watching someone else’s life. He is afraid the numbness will spread, that he
will lose the last thing he has left: his family. He hopes, quietly, that someone can show him how
to stop the film in his head.
