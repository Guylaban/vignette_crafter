throat. It happened in the kitchen of their small apartment in Dearborn, Michigan—a place that had
always smelled like cumin and garlic, but that night smelled like fear. She remembered the way her

doorway, unable to scream, unable to run, unable to do anything except watch. She never told anyone.

without words, moments that returned as pure sensation: the heat of the kitchen, the thud of a body
against a cabinet, the silence afterward. Even now, at thirty, Layla could not describe what she had
seen. When a therapist once asked her to talk about it, she opened her mouth and nothing came
out—the words simply would not form, as if the memory itself had stolen her voice.

That inability to find language for what happened became a quiet certainty that she could not trust
herself. If she could not even speak about the most defining moment of her childhood, how could she
rely on her own judgment in anything? She began to doubt every decision she made—what to order at a
restaurant, whether to cross the street, whether she had locked the door behind her. And because she
could not trust herself, she could not trust anyone else either. People, she had learned, were
capable of anything. That lesson had been carved into her when she was small, and it had never
faded.

The doubt and mistrust bled into how she saw the world. Every sudden movement from a customer at the

through her chest. She would freeze, her eyes tracking the person’s hands, her body braced for
something she could not name. She only felt safe in a handful of places: her apartment, the bar’s
back office, the small corner of the break room where she could see both exits. Everywhere else felt
exposed, open, dangerous. She checked the locks on her doors and windows three times before bed,
sometimes four, and still she would lie awake rehearsing worst-case scenarios—what if someone had a
knife, what if a fight broke out at the bar, what if she froze and did nothing, what if she did the
wrong thing and someone got hurt. Running through those possibilities in her mind made her feel
prepared, but it also kept the memories close. The more she rehearsed disaster, the more the old
images surfaced: her mother’s face, the kitchen light, the sound of a chair tipping over.

you cannot even handle your own mind. How can you handle anything? That thought loop tightened over
the months. She tried to control her emotions, to keep everything flat and even, but the effort
