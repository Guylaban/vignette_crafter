silence of his home was often shattered by the violent arguments between his parents. The traumatic
event that has shaped his life was not a single explosion of violence, but the recurring,

heavy glass ashtray hitting the wall, the sharp, gasping sobs from his mother, and the way his
father’s shadow would loom large and distorted in the hallway light. The most vivid memory, the one
that feels as fresh now as it did then, is of a night when he was nine. He had hidden behind the
living room sofa, his small body trembling so hard the springs creaked, listening to his father’s
voice escalate from a low grumble to a roar. He couldn’t see the blows, but he heard the sickening
thud of a fist connecting with flesh. He felt a terror that was pure and physical, a cold paralysis
that filled his chest and throat. Even now, as a 36-year-old man, remembering that night brings back
the same visceral terror, a feeling so raw it feels like he is back behind that sofa, a helpless
child again.

Rajan has never been able to fully describe the event to anyone. When he tries, the words feel
hollow and wrong, unable to capture the specific quality of the fear or the smell of the dust from
the old sofa cushions. This inability to put the frightening event into words has left him feeling
isolated, as if the most defining experience of his life is locked inside him, a secret he can’t
share. In the years since, this sense of isolation has hardened into a deep-seated belief that the
people responsible—his father, the neighbors who never called the police, the system that failed to
intervene—have faced no consequences. This belief has curdled into a broader, more pervasive
helplessness. He is certain, on a level that feels like bone-deep truth, that he can’t stop bad
things from happening to him. He used to be a happy person, but now he is always miserable, and he
has come to believe that this misery is simply the natural state of things, the price of being alive
in a world where chaos can erupt without warning.

This worldview directly feeds his constant state of threat. Because he believes he is helpless and
that the world is fundamentally unjust, his body feels constantly tense and ready to react to
danger. He is superalert and watchful, scanning every room he enters for exits and potential

full physical reaction: his heart pounds, his palms sweat, and he feels a surge of adrenaline that
leaves him shaky for hours. As an accountant, he works in a quiet open-plan office, but the very
silence that should be calming is a source of anxiety. The sound of a raised voice from a colleague
on the phone can instantly bring back the full memory of his father’s yelling, without any warning.
This is the core of his suffering: a trigger in his environment—a tone of voice, a slammed door—will
flood him with the nonverbal, sensory memory of the trauma, which in turn spikes his terror and
reinforces his conviction that the world is a dangerous, unpredictable place. The more he feels
helpless, the more he scans for danger; the more he scans, the more he is triggered; and each
triggered memory proves to him that he is, indeed, helpless.

He has tried to manage this by avoiding people. He divorced two years ago, in part because his ex-
wife’s frustration with his constant vigilance and his inability to explain his past felt like
another form of blame. At work, he keeps to himself, eating lunch at his desk with his back to the
wall, refusing invitations to after-work drinks. He has stopped watching movies or TV shows that
contain any hint of conflict, because the sight of a man raising his hand in an argument on screen
is enough to send him into a state of hypervigilance that lasts for the rest of the day. His life
has become a series of narrow, controlled channels, designed to prevent any unexpected noise or
confrontation. But the effort of maintaining this control is exhausting, and he has come to feel
that he is not living, merely surviving. What finally brought him to seek help was a moment in a
grocery store, when a child dropped a glass jar. The shattering sound was so sudden that Rajan

entirely, that the next trigger will break him completely. More than anything, he is hoping that
