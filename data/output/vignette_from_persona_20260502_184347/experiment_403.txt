Layla, a 45-year-old auditor of Middle Eastern descent, had been divorced for several years, living
alone in a quiet apartment. Her trauma occurred during a humanitarian mission in a conflict zone, a
detail she rarely spoke of. The event itself was a sudden, violent explosion that ripped through a
market where she was standing. She remembered the deafening bang, the heat, and the chaos, but the
clearest, most visceral memory was the smell—a specific, sweet cologne worn by a man who had been
standing next to her, who was killed instantly. She could not recall his face, only the scent that
now haunted her.

In the months that followed, Layla returned to her work as an auditor, a job that required
meticulous focus. Yet, the world felt permanently unsafe. The most potent triggers were the
olfactory ones. The faintest whiff of a similar cologne in an elevator or a department store would
instantly transport her back. A loud bang, like a car backfiring or a door slamming, would make her
flinch, her heart hammering. Waking from a nightmare left her disoriented, the dream residue
clinging to her for hours. These triggers directly intensified her maladaptive strategies. She began
to avoid open, exposed spaces, like the plaza near her office, feeling a raw vulnerability. She
constantly scanned her environment for signs of threat, a habit that was both exhausting and
reflexive. The triggers also fractured her memory further; the bang of a car door would bring back
the sound of the explosion, but the image of the man's face remained a blank, frustrating gap. She
was certain that her body's reaction to these triggers was proof of her own weakness. She believed
the event happened to her because of the sort of person she was, and that she had failed to protect
the others around her. This negative appraisal of herself as a character fundamentally flawed made
her feel that she deserved this ongoing torment, and it made the triggers feel like just punishment.

Layla’s sense of safety had eroded to almost nothing. She only felt safe in her own apartment, and
even then, she would check the locks multiple times. Everywhere else, she was on high alert. She
frequently looked behind her to see if someone was following, and before she could feel comfortable
in any new space, she would automatically identify all possible escape routes. This constant threat
monitoring fed directly back into her maladaptive strategies, making her scanning behavior more
intense. It also warped her memory; the feeling of being hunted made the gaps in her memory feel
like deliberate, dangerous secrets she was keeping from herself. The threat state also reinforced
her negative appraisals, confirming her belief that her body was an enemy that kept reacting as if
she were still in danger, even when she knew she was safe. The threat itself became a trigger; the
mere anticipation of feeling unsafe was enough to send her into a spiral of hypervigilance.

Layla’s negative appraisals were a central engine of her suffering. She engaged in counterfactual
thinking, endlessly replaying how her life would have been different if she had not been assigned to
that mission. This rumination deepened her conviction that she was a bad person, which in turn made
her threat monitoring more desperate. She would think, "I failed to protect them, so I must be
vigilant now, or I will fail again." This cycle of thought made the triggers more frequent and more
potent, as her mind was primed to find evidence of her own failure and danger everywhere. Her
maladaptive strategies, in turn, reinforced her negative appraisals. Her constant checking and
avoidance felt like a confirmation of her fundamental brokenness. She would think, "Normal people
don't have to plan escape routes. I am not normal. I am the sort of person this happens to."

The memories themselves were a source of profound shame. They came in fragmented pieces that did not
fit together—a flash of heat, a scream, the smell of cologne, but no coherent narrative. These gaps
and the incomplete picture made her feel like she was going crazy. This fragmented memory quality
directly fed her threat perception; if she couldn't even trust her own mind to tell her a complete
story, how could she trust the world to be safe? The memory gaps also fueled her negative
appraisals, as she wondered what terrible thing she had done that her mind was protecting her from
remembering. Her maladaptive strategies, like the constant scanning, actually made the memory worse,
because her brain was too busy searching for threats to process the past, keeping the fragments
scattered.

Layla finally sought help on the advice of her supervisor, who had noticed her becoming increasingly
isolated and jumpy at work. She had begun to avoid the office cafeteria because of the mix of
smells, and had taken to eating lunch alone at her desk. What brought her through the door was a
combination of exhaustion and a faint, desperate hope. She was most afraid that she would never feel
truly safe again, and that her life would be a permanent state of managing a threat she could not
name. She hoped, more than anything, to be able to walk into a room without first planning how to
escape it.
