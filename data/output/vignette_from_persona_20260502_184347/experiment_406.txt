Leila, a 46-year-old pharmacist of Middle Eastern descent, still remembers the exact sound of the IV
pump. Her son, then four, had been admitted with a sudden, severe infection that spiraled into
sepsis. For twelve days she sat by his bed, watching the numbers on the monitor, listening to the
beep that meant he was still alive. He survived. But something in Leila did not. She returned to
work at the pharmacy, but the smell of antiseptic would catch in her throat, and she would find
herself holding her breath. She began to believe that the experience had fundamentally broken her.
What kind of mother, she thought, could not protect her child from something so simple as a fever?
This thought—that she was defective, that there was something wrong with her—took root.

In the months that followed, Leila’s belief that she was a weak person became a lens through which
she saw everything. She started to avoid all medical environments. If a customer at the pharmacy
described a hospital visit, she would excuse herself to the back room, her heart pounding. She began
to think that nothing good could happen to her anymore, that her son’s survival had used up all her
luck. This sense of a spoiled future made her feel constantly on edge. She found herself unable to
relax, even at home, where she knew she was safe. She would jump at ordinary sounds—the clatter of a
dropped bottle, the hiss of the espresso machine—as if her body were bracing for another
catastrophe. She was certain that her own weakness had caused the trauma, and this certainty made
the world feel permanently unsafe.

Leila tried hard not to think about the hospital. She would push the memory away when it surfaced,
telling herself to focus on work, on the prescriptions she needed to fill. But the suppression only
made her more vigilant. She began to have trouble falling asleep, lying awake listening for any
sound that might signal an emergency. Her inability to relax convinced her that she was indeed a
broken person; other people could move on, but she could not. She started to ruminate on why the
event had happened to her, searching for a reason that would make it make sense, but the searching
only deepened her conviction that she was somehow at fault. The more she tried not to think about
the trauma, the more her body reminded her of it—through the startle response, through the
insomnia—and the more she felt that there was something wrong with her as a person.

At work, Leila became efficient but withdrawn. She avoided the small talk about weekend plans,
because any mention of a doctor’s visit or a sick child sent her into a spiral. She began to see her
own vigilance as further proof of her defectiveness. What kind of pharmacist, she thought, was
afraid of medicine? Her avoidance of medical cues had become a daily habit: she would ask a
colleague to handle any customer who mentioned a hospital, and she would take her breaks in the
stockroom, away from the posters about flu shots and blood pressure screenings. Each act of
avoidance reinforced her belief that she could not handle normal life. She was unable to relax
because she was always scanning for danger, and the scanning made her more convinced that danger was
everywhere. The cycle tightened.

After nearly two years, Leila’s son is healthy and thriving. But she still cannot sleep through the
night. She still jumps when a car backfires. She has stopped attending family gatherings where
someone might ask about the hospitalization. What finally brought her to seek help was a moment at
the pharmacy counter: a mother came in with a prescription for her child, and Leila felt her chest
seize so badly that she had to sit down on the floor behind the counter. She realized she could no
longer do her job without fear. She is afraid that she will never feel safe again, but she is also
afraid that the therapist will confirm what she already believes—that there is something
fundamentally wrong with her.
