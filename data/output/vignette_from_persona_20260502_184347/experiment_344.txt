The night it happened, Mei was on her second week of a three-week field training exercise at Fort
Irwin. She had been on perimeter security duty, standing alone in the dark near a dry wash, when a
fellow soldier from a different platoon approached her from behind. He grabbed her by the shoulders,
spun her around, and slammed her against the hood of a Humvee before she could even shout. The
assault lasted less than two minutes, but in Mei’s mind it stretched into something
endless—something that, even now, months later, she could only access in disjointed flashes. She
remembered the grit of the hood against her cheek, the smell of diesel and dust, and the sound of
her own voice cracking as she tried to yell. But when she tried to tell the story to her husband
later, it came out flat and fragmented, like she was describing a scene from a movie she had once
watched. The memory felt like it belonged to someone else—someone weaker, someone who hadn’t been a
soldier. She found it almost impossible to believe it had actually happened to her, because the
person in those images was not the person she had been before.

As the weeks passed, Mei began to notice that the images came most vividly when she was alone. She
would be lying in bed, and suddenly the flash of headlights on her bedroom wall would send her back
to the Humvee’s halogen beams cutting through the desert dark. To push the images away, she started
redirecting her thoughts—she would force herself to mentally rehearse her daily schedule, or count
backward from a hundred in Vietnamese, her grandmother’s language. She also began eating more than
usual after these episodes, telling herself that a full stomach would settle her nerves and help her
fall asleep. But the redirection and the overeating only seemed to make the memories feel more
alien. The more she tried to shove the images out of her mind, the more they returned at unexpected
moments, and the less they felt like her own experience. She would think, *Somebody else would have
stopped this from happening. Somebody else would have fought back.* That thought would send her back
into the images, and then she would start overanalysing—going over every detail of the assault,
trying to find the moment where she could have acted differently, trying to make sense of why she
had frozen.

The overanalysing became a nightly ritual. Mei would lie next to her sleeping husband and replay the
event in her mind, searching for a version of herself who had been faster or stronger. Each time she
did this, she felt more convinced that the person she had become was unrecognisable to her. She had
always thought of herself as capable, as someone who belonged in the Army. Now she felt cut off from
her own unit, from the camaraderie she had once relied on. She had lost her connection to her
community and her culture—she no longer attended the monthly Vietnamese-American soldier gatherings
she had once looked forward to, because she was certain that if they knew what had happened, they
would see her as weak. The more she withdrew, the more she turned to her maladaptive strategies: she
would redirect her thoughts during the day by burying herself in paperwork at her desk in the
battalion headquarters, and she would overeat in the evenings, telling herself it was the only thing
that helped. But each time she used these strategies, she felt a little more estranged from herself,
and the memories felt a little less like hers.

At work, the triggers were everywhere. The sound of a Humvee engine starting in the motor pool could
send her heart racing. The smell of diesel fuel from a passing convoy would bring the images rushing
back, and she would have to excuse herself to the bathroom to redirect her thoughts. She had started
avoiding the motor pool entirely, taking long routes to her office to steer clear of the smell. Her
squad leader had noticed her absences from the morning physical training runs—she had been calling
in sick, saying she had a stomach bug—but she could not explain that the real reason was that she
could not bear to stand in formation in the dark, because the dark was where it had happened. What
frightened her most was that she could no longer trust her own mind. The memories felt like someone
else’s, and yet they controlled her. She finally made an appointment at the behavioral health clinic
on post because she was terrified that if she kept going like this, she would lose everything she
had worked for—her place in the Army, her marriage, her sense of who she was. She did not know if
she could get better, but she knew she could not keep living inside the loop she was in.
