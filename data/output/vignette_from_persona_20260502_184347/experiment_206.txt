María Elena had been a pharmacist for forty-three years, the kind who remembered every regular
customer’s blood pressure medication and asked after their grandchildren. That was before the night
her husband, Roberto, came home from his brother’s funeral and turned on her with a fury she had
never seen in thirty-eight years of marriage. He shoved her against the kitchen counter, then pinned
her to the floor with his knee on her chest, his hand clamped over her mouth as she struggled to
breathe. She remembered it not as a story but as a series of frozen images: the crack of her head
against the cabinet, the pattern of the linoleum tiles pressed against her cheek, the way his
wedding ring dug into her lips. Whenever the memory surfaced, her body would lock into that same
position—shoulders hunched, jaw clenched, arms pinned at her sides—as though she were still there on
the cold floor. The shame that followed was immediate and crushing: she was a pharmacist, a woman
who had raised three children and managed a household, and yet she had not fought back, had not
screamed, had not even called the police afterward.

Now, at seventy-four, María Elena still worked three days a week at the same pharmacy, but she had
come to believe she was no longer capable of the things that had once defined her. When a customer
made an offhand comment about family—a joke about a difficult mother-in-law, a complaint about a
sibling—she felt a surge of heat in her chest and a ringing in her ears, her body tensing as though
Roberto might appear from behind the vitamin aisle. She had lost her faith in people entirely; it
seemed to her that anyone could turn violent without warning, that kindness was merely a mask. The
weakness she felt was so complete that she could not stand to look at her own body in the mirror,
because when she did, she saw only the woman who had lain helpless on the kitchen floor.

The door at the pharmacy was heavy and pneumatic, but whenever it slammed shut—a delivery driver, a
teenager in a hurry—María Elena’s heart would race and her gaze would dart to the entrance, scanning
for threat. She found herself listening constantly for sounds that might mean someone was entering
her home, even during the day when Roberto was at work. Her body felt perpetually tense, as though
bracing for a blow that had not yet landed. This hypervigilance made her avoid other people
altogether outside of work; she stopped attending church, declined invitations from her daughters,
and told herself she was simply tired. When she did feel a strong emotion—anger at a rude customer,
sadness during a commercial about an anniversary—she immediately pushed it away, because she had
come to believe that feeling anything deeply was dangerous, that it would crack her open and reveal
the pathetic person she had become.

The shame and the belief in her own weakness made the memories more vivid and more frequent. A door
slamming at the pharmacy would trigger not just a racing heart but a full sensory flashback: the
smell of floor cleaner, the pressure of Roberto’s knee, the shame of her own stillness. And each
time she relived it, she felt more convinced that she was no longer capable, that she had lost
herself completely. This cycle tightened over the months. The more she avoided her feelings and her
own body, the more she confirmed to herself that she was weak, and the more she scanned for danger.
She watched for signs in Roberto’s voice, in the way he walked through the door, in the silence of
the house. She caught herself listening for footsteps in the hallway at night, even though he had
never hurt her again after that single night.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning at the pharmacy when a young woman came to the
counter, crying, and asked for a referral to a counselor. María Elena had filled the prescription
without a word, her hands shaking, and had spent the rest of the day avoiding the memory of her own
helplessness. That evening, alone in the bathroom, she caught a glimpse of her face in the mirror
and did not recognize the woman staring back. She was terrified that she would never feel safe
again, and she was desperate to believe she could.
