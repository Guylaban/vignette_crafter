Marta, a 74-year-old physical therapist of European descent, had lived in the United States for over
four decades, but the life she had built felt fragile, a thin veneer over a night she could not
outrun. The traumatic event was not a single moment but a crescendo of terror during the war in her
home country. She had been hiding in a cramped cellar with her young son as soldiers swept through
her village. The smell of smoke from burning buildings had seeped through the cracks, mingling with
the sharp, suffocating odor of alcohol on the breath of a soldier who had kicked the cellar door
open. She had pressed her hand over her son’s mouth, feeling his small body tremble, listening to
the soldier’s boots on the stone steps. The memory was not a story she told; it was a series of
images and sounds that ambushed her—the flicker of firelight, the creak of the door, and the sound
of a man’s voice, low and laughing, that she could still hear in her head as if it were happening
now.

In her work as a physical therapist, Marta helped elderly patients regain their strength, but the
clinic had become a minefield of triggers. A colleague’s hand sanitizer, with its sharp alcohol
smell, would hit her nostrils and instantly the cellar was around her again. The memory would surge
forward—visual, auditory, feeling present—and she would feel her heart race, her palms dampen, as if
the soldier were standing behind her. The burnt smell from the clinic’s toaster in the break room
had the same effect, yanking her back into that smoke-filled night. These triggers directly fed her
sense of threat; she had come to believe that any ordinary situation could turn dangerous, that the
past was not over but waiting to repeat itself. She found herself listening for sounds that might
mean someone was entering her home, and she often reacted as if she were in danger even when nothing
was wrong, the false alarms leaving her exhausted and ashamed.

The memories themselves drove Marta’s maladaptive strategies. When a visual memory of the cellar
door swinging open would flash in her mind, she felt compelled to check her surroundings, scanning
the clinic’s hallways for signs of threat, certain that danger was approaching. This constant
scanning, in turn, would stir up more memories—the soldier’s face, the texture of the stone
wall—tightening the loop. She had also started drinking alcohol in the evenings, a glass of wine
that became two, then three, because the numb haze it provided was the only thing that softened the
edges of the images that felt so present. But the alcohol use, she noticed, made her more vigilant
the next day, her threat system on high alert, and she would find herself dwelling on how she used
to be before the event—a woman who had not flinched at a stranger’s approach.

The threat she carried with her was a constant engine for these cycles. Feeling threatened by
strangers approaching her even in ordinary situations, like at the grocery store, would trigger a
rush of memory—the soldier’s boots, the smell of alcohol—and she would then scan the environment
more frantically, her mind replaying the cellar scene. This threat-driven scanning made the memories
more vivid, more present, and less like the past. She had started avoiding conversations about the
war, and when a patient mentioned a news report about refugees, Marta’s mind would be hijacked by
the auditory recall of that low laugh, and she would excuse herself to the bathroom, where she would
stand shaking, listening for sounds that were not there.

The consequences had accumulated. Her husband had noticed her drinking, her withdrawal from social
events, and the way she jumped at the sound of the doorbell. She had stopped attending family
gatherings where someone might bring up the past, and at work, she had begun to dread the break
room, avoiding it entirely when she smelled toast. What finally brought Marta to seek help was a
moment in the clinic when a patient’s son—a tall man with a certain gait—walked toward her, and she
had frozen, convinced she was back in the cellar, the memory so present she could not speak. She had
excused herself and wept in the supply closet, terrified that her mind was breaking. What frightened
her most was the thought that she would never feel safe again, that the past had not only happened
but was still happening, and that she was losing the life she had fought so hard to build. She
hoped, quietly, that someone could help her find a way to let the memory be over.
