Raj was thirty-seven years old, a clinical psychologist in a busy community mental health clinic,
and for three years he had been telling himself that he was managing fine. The truth was more
complicated, and it began on a warm October evening when he and his wife, Priya, had stopped for
dinner at a crowded outdoor plaza downtown. A single gunshot—sharp, flat, and utterly without
warning—had cracked through the noise of conversation. He remembered the split second of silence
that followed, the way the world seemed to hold its breath, and then the screaming. He had pulled
Priya to the ground, his body covering hers, his heart slamming against his ribs like a trapped
animal. The shooter was blocks away, never a direct threat to them, but Raj had not known that at
the time. What he had known, in that moment, was that he was completely helpless. He could not see
the source of the danger, could not move, could not protect her any more than he could stop the next
bullet from finding them. The feeling of that powerlessness had seared itself into him.

In the weeks that followed, Raj began to notice that his body reacted before his mind could catch
up. The sound of a car backfiring on his commute would send a jolt through his chest, leaving his
hands trembling on the steering wheel. Waking in the dark at three in the morning—a common enough
occurrence for any tired professional—became a small terror, his breath catching as his brain
flooded with the certainty that something terrible was about to happen. And it was not just the
sudden noises or the nighttime stillness; it was the broader sense that he could not control his own
life. A cancelled appointment, an unexpected email from his supervisor, a traffic jam that made him
late—each small disruption felt like evidence that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that he was
at its mercy. He had come to believe that danger lurked in the loss of control itself, that the only
way to stay safe was to keep everything predictable.

Raj started to avoid anything that could be stressful. He stopped taking on new clients with complex
trauma histories, citing a full caseload. He declined invitations to social gatherings, telling
Priya he was too tired. He began drinking a glass of whiskey—sometimes two—every evening, telling
himself it helped him unwind, though he knew, somewhere beneath the rationalization, that it dulled
the edge of his hypervigilance. More quietly, he found himself drifting off into a world of his own
during the day. In the middle of a supervision meeting or while driving home, his attention would
slip away, and he would imagine a different life—one where he was not a psychologist at all, but a
painter living by the sea, where nothing unexpected ever happened. These fantasy escapes were brief,
but they were becoming more frequent, and they left him feeling hollow when he returned.

The more he drank and avoided and drifted, the more he felt like a stranger to himself. The person
he had become was unrecognizable. He had built his career on being calm, rational, someone who could
sit with distress and help others make sense of it. Now he was a man who flinched at a loud noise,
who organized his evenings around the guarantee of a quiet house, who needed alcohol to feel
settled. He began to believe, with a certainty that felt like truth, that he could not stop bad
things from happening. The world was a dangerous place, and he was not equipped to handle it. This
belief, in turn, drove him deeper into his strategies: he drank more, avoided more, retreated
further into his daydreams, each loop tightening the knot.

His work environment became a minefield. As a psychologist, he was surrounded by people in distress,
by stories of violence and loss, by the very unpredictability he now dreaded. He had started to
dread his own office. The ringing phone, the knock on the door, the client who arrived in
crisis—each felt like a trigger, a reminder that he could not control what came next. He began to
cancel supervision sessions. He stopped returning calls from colleagues. He was certain that if he
let his guard down, something would happen. And the more he withdrew, the more isolated he became,
and the more the world outside his small, controlled bubble seemed hostile and overwhelming.

What finally brought him to seek help was not a crisis, but a quiet moment of recognition. He had
been sitting in his car outside his clinic, a full ten minutes before his first appointment, and he
realized he was dreading the walk to the door. He was afraid of what the day might bring—a client's
story, a sudden noise, a change in the schedule. He was afraid of himself, of the man he had become.
He had come to believe that he could not stop bad things from happening, and that belief was no
longer just a thought; it was the shape of his life. He was hoping, though he could barely admit it,
that someone might show him how to find his way back.
