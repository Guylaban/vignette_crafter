was blood on the windshield, blood on her hands as she tried to find a pulse she never found. She
could still smell it, sometimes—copper and diesel and wet clay—as if it were happening all over
again. That smell would hit her without warning, and the memory would surface, but it felt like it
belonged to someone else, not to her. She would push it away before she could fully recall what the
man’s face looked like, but the pushing itself took effort, and she had come to believe that the
effort was a sign of something wrong in her.

In the weeks that followed, Leila began to avoid anything that might bring the event to mind. When a
coworker mentioned the accident at a team meeting, she excused herself and walked out. She started
staying up until two or three in the morning, delaying sleep because she dreaded what her mind might
replay in the dark. What frightened her most was the certainty that she had failed—that she should
have done more, should have climbed down the embankment faster, should have known first aid well
enough to save him. She had acted in ways that went against who she thought she was; she had frozen,

and that misery felt like proof that she had become someone else entirely.

Her work suffered. As a forester, she spent her days surrounded by the sights and sounds of the
woods, but now she was constantly scanning the environment for signs of threat—a distant engine, a
flash of metal between the trees. Every car she saw on the logging road was a car that could crash.
Every blood-red berry on a bush made her stomach turn. The scanning was exhausting, but she was
certain that if she stopped paying attention, something terrible would happen again, and it would be
her fault. She had come to believe that her vigilance was the only thing keeping the world from
falling apart, even as it kept her from sleeping, from concentrating, from feeling like herself.
