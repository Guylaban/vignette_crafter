Marcus, a 53-year-old photographer of Oceania heritage, had spent most of his career capturing
landscapes and portraits, his work defined by a quiet patience and an eye for the unguarded moment.
That changed three years ago, when he was taken captive during an assignment in a remote region. For
six days, he was held in a concrete room, bound and blindfolded, with no sense of time and no
certainty he would survive. His captors spoke in a language he did not understand, but their
movements were unmistakable—the scrape of boots, the sudden shift of a chair, the weight of a hand
on his shoulder. He learned to read threat in every sound, every change in pressure against his
skin. When he was finally released, he returned home physically unharmed, but the world had become a
different place.

In the months that followed, Marcus found himself unable to sit with his back to a door. In
restaurants, he would scan the room before his eyes adjusted to the light, choosing a seat against a
wall where he could see every entrance. His body remained on alert, his shoulders hunched, his jaw
tight, as though bracing for a blow that never came. He began to notice things he had never
registered before—the way a stranger’s hand moved too quickly into a jacket pocket, the way a man’s
eyes lingered on him in a grocery aisle. Each of these moments sent a spike of adrenaline through
him, and he would find himself mapping the nearest exit, rehearsing the steps he would take if he
needed to flee. He had come to believe that safety was something he had to earn through constant
vigilance, and that relaxing even for a moment would leave him vulnerable.

This vigilance bled into his work. As a photographer, Marcus had always moved through crowds,
blending in, waiting for the right shot. Now, he avoided assignments that required him to enter
unfamiliar buildings or work in dense urban settings. He took only portrait sessions in controlled
environments—his studio, a client’s home—but even there, he found himself watching the door, noting
the position of windows, calculating the distance to the nearest exit. His wife, Lena, noticed that
he no longer wanted to go to the movies or to dinner with friends. When she asked why, he told her
he just felt tired. What he did not say was that every public space felt like a trap, and that he
had begun to ask her, several times a week, whether he seemed okay, whether he was acting strangely,
whether she thought he would ever feel normal again. She would reassure him, and for a few hours the
anxiety would lift, but soon enough another trigger would surface—a sudden movement from a passerby,
a door slamming in a hallway—and his body would lock back into that state of readiness, and he would
need to ask again.

The cycle tightened over time. The more he scanned for danger, the more he found it, and the more he
found it, the more he needed to check with Lena that he was not losing his grip on reality. He had
come to believe that his own perception was unreliable—that he could no longer trust his instincts
to distinguish between a real threat and a false alarm. So he relied on her to confirm that the
world was safe, but her reassurance never lasted. Within hours, sometimes minutes, he would feel the
tension return, and he would begin planning his escape routes again, his eyes darting to the corners
of the room, his body coiled and waiting. His work had slowed to a near halt. He had turned down
three assignments in the past month, citing health reasons, but the truth was that he could no
longer bear the weight of being in a space he could not control.

It was Lena who finally convinced him to seek help. She had found him one evening in the living
room, seated in the chair with the best sightline to the front door, his hands gripping the
armrests, his breathing shallow. When she asked what was wrong, he said he did not know—he just felt
that something was about to happen. He was certain that if he let his guard down, even for a moment,
he would not survive it. What he hoped for, more than anything, was to sit in a room without
watching the door. What he feared was that he would never be able to.
