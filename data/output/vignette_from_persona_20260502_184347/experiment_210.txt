Leila is a 34-year-old agricultural worker who came to the United States from Syria seven years ago,
following her husband at the time. She is now divorced and lives alone in a small apartment near the
fields where she works. The event that changed everything happened three years ago, during the grape
harvest. She was walking back to the crew bus at dusk when she saw two men dragging a third man into
a shed. She heard a single gunshot, then silence. She did not see the body, but she saw the blood
seeping under the door. She ran. She did not call anyone. She told no one for weeks, afraid that
speaking it aloud would make her part of it.

In the months that followed, Leila found she could no longer tolerate certain sounds. Babies crying
became unbearable—not because of the noise itself, but because the high, thin pitch reminded her of
the sound the man made before the shot. She also began to notice people acting suspiciously
everywhere. A worker glancing over his shoulder, a foreman speaking in a low voice to a
supervisor—she was certain these were signals of something hidden, something dangerous. She stopped
joining the other women for tea after shifts, claiming exhaustion. Certain conversation topics,
especially about politics or immigration status, made her chest tighten so sharply she would excuse
herself to the bathroom and stay there until her hands stopped shaking.

Leila responded to these triggers by trying to control what she could. She started cleaning her
apartment compulsively—scrubbing the kitchen counters three times before bed, rearranging the cans
in her pantry by height and label. She told herself that if everything in her home was orderly,
nothing bad could happen there. She also began forcing herself to think only positive thoughts. When
an image of the blood under the door surfaced, she would silently repeat, *I am safe now. I am safe
now.* until her jaw ached. And when that failed, she tried to push the memories out of her mind
entirely, like slamming a door on a draft.

But the cleaning and the positive reframing did not make her feel safer. Over time, they deepened
her sense that she no longer belonged anywhere. She had lost her faith in people—she was certain now
that everyone was capable of cruelty, that the men she worked beside every day could turn on her
without warning. She began to believe that people were not what they seemed, that kindness was a
mask. And the more she isolated herself, the more she felt like a ghost in her own life, a woman who
belonged to no country and no community. The compulsive cleaning, meant to restore order, only
reminded her that the world outside her door was chaotic and untrustworthy. The positive reframing,
meant to calm her, felt like lying to herself, which made her distrust her own mind.

This cycle tightened over the course of two years. Leila stopped speaking to her sister, who had
asked once too often why she never visited. She stopped going to the mosque, because the sound of a
baby crying during prayer would send her heart racing. At work, she began arriving early just to
avoid the crew gathering, and she took her lunch alone behind the irrigation shed. She was certain
that if she let her guard down, something terrible would happen again—and she was equally certain
that she was already broken, that she no longer belonged among people who could laugh and talk and
trust.

What finally brought Leila to a clinic was a panic attack in the grocery store. A baby in the next
aisle began to cry, and she dropped her basket and fled, leaving her cart full of food. She sat in
her car for forty minutes, unable to start the engine. The next day, she called a number her sister
had left on her voicemail months ago. She does not know if she can be helped. She is terrified that
talking about the memory will make it real again. But she is also tired of cleaning counters she has
already cleaned, and she is tired of being alone.
