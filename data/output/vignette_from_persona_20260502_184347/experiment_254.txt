shattered one afternoon three years ago, when a lone attacker drove a truck into a crowded outdoor
market in the city where Kwame and his wife had gone to buy produce for a family gathering. Kwame
was thrown against a concrete pillar, fracturing his ribs, but what he remembers most vividly—and
what replays in his mind like a film he cannot shut off—is the sight of the driver being pulled from
the cab and beaten by a mob. The attacker was dead within minutes, but the crowd did not stop. Kwame
watched people kick and stomp a body that no longer moved, and the memory is terrifyingly clear and
detailed: the wet sound of boots on flesh, the roar of voices, the smell of dust and blood. He had
always believed in the basic decency of people, and that belief was annihilated in a single
afternoon.

Since that day, something in his surroundings can instantly bring back the full memory without
warning. A sudden shout in the archive, the sight of a crowd gathering near the campus gates, even
the smell of overripe fruit at a farmer’s stand—these moments hijack his mind, and he is back in the
market, watching the mob. The anniversary of the event, which falls in late summer, has become a
period of dread that begins weeks in advance, and he has learned to avoid certain conversation
topics, especially any discussion of justice, punishment, or what people are capable of when they
are angry. He is certain that people are fundamentally corrupt, that the veneer of civilization is
paper-thin, and that anyone, anywhere, might suddenly reveal a capacity for cruelty. What frightens
him most is not the memory of the attack itself, but the memory of what the crowd did—the moral
violation of it, the proof that people misbehave not as an exception but as a revelation of their
true nature.

To manage this, Kwame has developed a pattern of strategies that have gradually shrunk his world. He
avoids crowded places such as shopping centres or public events, and he has stopped attending family
gatherings, because being surrounded by people now feels like being trapped in a room with a ticking
bomb. He also avoids situations that might make him feel happy or hopeful, because he has come to
believe that hope is a dangerous illusion that leaves you unprepared for the worst. When the
memories press in too hard, he drifts off into a world of his own, a fantasy escape where he is
alone in a vast, silent library with no other people, no sounds, no unpredictability. This escape
provides temporary relief, but it also means that when he returns to the present, the memories feel
even more vivid and unmanageable, as if his mind has been rehearsing them in the background. His
wife has noticed that he no longer laughs, no longer plans for the future, and has started to
withdraw from her as well.

The cycle has tightened over the past year. His avoidance of crowds and positive events has led to
fewer and fewer external triggers, but his internal world has become saturated with the memory. The
more he escapes into fantasy, the more the memory intrudes when he is not prepared, and the more he
feels the need to control his environment by avoiding anything that might feel good or hopeful. At
the archive, he has started taking his breaks in a locked storage room, and he has asked to work
only with collections that require no interaction with patrons. His supervisor has expressed
concern, but Kwame cannot explain why the sight of people laughing in the hallway makes him feel
sick.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment last week when his wife found him standing in the
kitchen, staring at a knife he had taken from the drawer, not with any intention of harm but with a
sudden, overwhelming certainty that the people around him were capable of anything. He could not
shake the image of the mob, and he could not convince himself that his wife, his colleagues, the
students on campus were any different. He is afraid that he is losing his grip on reality, but what
he is most afraid of is that he is not—that his view of people is simply the truth, and that hope is
the lie he can no longer afford to believe.
