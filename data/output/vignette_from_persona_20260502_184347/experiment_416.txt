pounded on the wood. She remembers the sound of his breathing on the other side, the smell of dust
and mothballs, and the certainty that no one would come. What haunts her most is the betrayal—she
had trusted him, and her mother had done nothing.

Decades later, Dorothy’s life is still shaped by that closet. She describes her memory of the event
as photographic, as if she can flip through still images in her mind: the crack of light under the
door, the pattern on the wallpaper, the feel of her own tears on her cheeks. Yet sometimes, when she
tries to tell someone what happened, the words vanish. She knows what occurred but cannot find
language to describe it, and in those moments she feels completely numb, as if it happened to a
stranger. This emotional distance is a relief, but it also fuels a deeper shame: she believes she
will never forgive herself for not doing more to stop what happened, for being too small and too
scared to fight back.

not fully calm her. The causal loop is tightening: each trigger brings a surge of threat, which
strengthens her belief that she is helpless, which makes the memory of the closet feel more vivid
