event that had fractured him occurred during a routine supply convoy. An IED detonated beneath the
lead vehicle, and in the ensuing chaos, a helicopter had been called in for a medevac. Kwame had
worked on three men, two of whom did not survive. For years after, he had managed, but then the
sounds of the forest began to betray him. A distant aircraft, even a commercial plane, would cause
his stomach to drop. The crack of a dry branch under a hiker’s boot would make him freeze. He had
come to believe that these sounds were not just noises but signals, warnings that he was about to be
ambushed again.

What frightened Kwame most was the way his own mind seemed to have turned against him. When he heard
a helicopter—and he heard them often, as they flew over the park for fire patrols or rescue
missions—he did not simply feel startled. He felt a surge of threat so immediate and physical that
he would drop into a crouch, scanning the treeline for muzzle flashes. The sound triggered a
cascade: he would then become convinced that he had to be on guard all the time, that he had
permanently changed for the worse, and that he would never forgive himself for not doing more to
stop what had happened. In those moments, his memories of the event felt more real and vivid than
the trail in front of him. He could smell the thick, oily smoke and the sickly-sweet scent of blood,
as though he were still kneeling in the mud beside the burning vehicle.

The memory itself reinforced his negative appraisals. Every time he recalled the event with that
unbearable clarity—the way the images were sharper than the present—he would think, *I have
permanently changed for the worse.* This thought would then make him more sensitive to triggers. He
began to watch other people closely for signs that they might harm him, even the families picnicking
by the lake. He would scan the parking lots for anything out of place, his heart rate climbing
before he had even identified a threat. The hypervigilance was exhausting, but he was certain that
letting his guard down would lead to disaster. He had come to believe that the world was
fundamentally dangerous and that he was the only one who could keep himself safe, even though he
also believed he was broken beyond repair.

The cycle tightened over the months. A trigger—say, the distant thrum of rotor blades—would spike
his threat level, and that state of high alert would drag the memory back into his mind with full
sensory force. Then the memory would confirm his negative self-view, which in turn made him more
reactive to the next sound. He avoided the busier parts of the park, rerouting his patrols to areas
where aircraft were less common. His wife noticed he no longer wanted to go to the grocery store,
because the hum of the refrigerators reminded him of the engine of the helicopter. He had begun to
feel detached, unreal, as though he were watching his own life from a great distance.

It was his wife who finally insisted he seek help, after he had spent an entire afternoon hiding in
the maintenance shed because a search-and-rescue helicopter had been circling for hours. Kwame sat
in the clinic, his hands resting on his knees, and said that what he was most afraid of was that he
would never feel normal again, that the permanent damage was already done, and that he was only
going through the motions of a life he no longer believed he deserved.
