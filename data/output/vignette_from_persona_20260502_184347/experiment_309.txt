Arthur, then still a state senator, was driving to a committee hearing when a pickup truck drifted
across the median and struck his sedan head-on at highway speed. He remembers the impact not as a
continuous event but as a series of frozen frames—the grille of the truck growing impossibly large,

minutes, and the smell of gasoline mixed with spilled bourbon from the other driver’s cab has never
left him. When he tries to tell the story now, at his wife’s request or in a half-hearted attempt to
explain his absences to colleagues, he finds himself losing the thread mid-sentence, unable to
connect one picture to the next, and he trails off into silence.

Arthur is sixty-eight, a career politician who has spent thirty years in public life, and he had
always prided himself on his composure. But in the months after the crash, he began to notice that
the smell of alcohol at fundraisers or receptions—a volunteer’s wine glass, a lobbyist’s
Scotch—would send a jolt through him, and he would find himself suddenly back in that frozen moment,
the ache in his ribs flaring without warning. He had come to believe that the accident happened
because of the sort of person he is: someone who was too careless, too distracted, too arrogant to
see the danger coming. This conviction grew stronger each time a trigger caught him off guard. A car
stopped oddly at an intersection, the sight of a damaged vehicle on the evening news, even the feel
of his own sedan accelerating onto the highway—each one seemed to confirm that he was fundamentally
exposed, that others could see what had happened to him just by looking at his face.

Because he was certain that danger could appear at any moment, Arthur began to wake during the night
to check whether he was safe, lying still in the dark and listening for the wrong kind of silence.
An unusual quiet in the house now felt to him like the moment before a disaster, and he would get
up, walk through each room, verify that the doors were locked. This vigilance bled into his days. He
had difficulty concentrating during meetings, his attention snagged by a car alarm outside or a
sudden pause in conversation, and he found himself repeatedly blaming himself for the crash—dwelling

somehow punish himself into preparedness. The more he blamed himself, the more he felt that others
could read the shame on him, and the more he scanned his surroundings for the next threat.

His driving became impossible. He avoided being behind the wheel entirely, and even as a passenger
he felt the old pain in his ribs tighten with every turn. His wife drove him to appointments and
events, but Arthur made excuses to cancel most of them. The rumination followed him everywhere. Each
time he blamed himself, the memory returned with sharper physical force—the ache in his ribs, the
smell of bourbon—and each time the memory surfaced, he blamed himself more. He started to believe
that the only way to stay safe was to remain watchful, but the watchfulness itself brought back the
frozen images, and the images brought back the shame, and the shame made him feel that he deserved
whatever came next.

The cycle tightened over the course of a year. Arthur stopped attending committee hearings that
required him to drive or to sit near anyone drinking. He told his staff he was working from home,
but he spent most of his time in his study, staring at the wall, trying to hold the pictures of the
crash at bay. His wife found him one night standing in the hallway at 3 a.m., listening to the
silence. He could not explain what he was waiting for. What finally brought him to seek help was a
single moment at a constituent dinner: a waiter uncorked a bottle of red wine near his table, and
Arthur felt his ribs seize, his vision narrowing to a single frame of the truck’s grille, and he
stood up and walked out without a word. His wife drove him home in silence. The next morning, she
made an appointment with a therapist who specialized in trauma. Arthur is afraid of what he will
find if he stops watching, but he is more afraid of what he has already lost—the career, the
presence, the sense that he was ever in control.
