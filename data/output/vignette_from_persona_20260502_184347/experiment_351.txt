Elena was 43, a cybersecurity analyst in a downtown office, and a widow for six years. The trauma
that had reshaped her life happened on an ordinary Tuesday evening. She had been driving home from
work, tired and distracted by a phone call from her sister about their mother’s health. At a four-
way stop, she glanced down to silence the ringer, and when she looked up, her car was already moving
through the intersection. A man on a bicycle—a father, she would later learn, on his way home from
his job as a mechanic—crossed her path. She hit him at twenty-five miles per hour. He survived, but
with a traumatic brain injury that left him with permanent cognitive and physical impairments. Elena
remembered the impact, the sound of metal and bone, and then nothing else for several seconds. There
were parts of the event she simply could not recall no matter how hard she tried—the moment right
before the collision, the exact position of the bicycle, the man’s face as he came into view. What
she did remember, with excruciating clarity, was the sight of his mangled bicycle frame and the way
he lay motionless on the pavement, and the paramedics telling her she could not leave until the
police arrived.

In the months that followed, Elena found herself increasingly unable to shake the event. She worked
in a high-security building where security guards occasionally carried visible sidearms, and the
sight of a weapon—any object that could be used as a weapon, even a heavy flashlight or a metal pipe
near a construction site—would send her heart racing. When she saw such an object, the memory of the
accident would surge back unbidden: the bicycle frame twisted like a piece of wire, the man’s
stillness, the paramedic’s voice. The memory was so vivid, so immediate, that she would feel the
steering wheel under her hands and the sickening jolt of impact all over again. Sometimes, without
any clear image or memory, she would be struck by the full emotional intensity of the event—a wave
of grief so overwhelming that she would have to excuse herself from a meeting and sit in the
bathroom stall, trembling and tearful, unsure what had triggered it.

The connection between these triggers and the memories became a loop she could not break. A
confrontation at work—a tense exchange with a colleague about a security protocol—would make her
blood run cold, and then the memory would return, and then the memory itself would make her
hypervigilant, scanning her environment for anything that could be a weapon or a threat. She had
come to believe that she was fundamentally dangerous, that her carelessness had revealed a truth
about her character she could not outrun. She began to avoid driving altogether, taking the train to
work, and she avoided the street where the accident had happened, even though it added twenty
minutes to her commute. She stopped going to her son’s soccer games because the parking lot was too
close to a busy intersection. At work, she avoided the break room where people sometimes argued
about politics, because raised voices made her feel as though she were about to be blamed for
something catastrophic.

Her grief was not for the man she had injured, though she felt that too. It was a deeper, more
private grief—a sense that she had lost the person she used to be. She had been a careful, competent
woman, a mother who planned birthday parties and a widow who had rebuilt her life. Now she was
someone who could not look at a bicycle without feeling her chest tighten. The memory gaps troubled
her most of all; she would lie awake at night, trying to reconstruct those missing seconds, as
though if she could just remember, she could somehow prove it had not been her fault. But the memory
would not come, and the grief would wash over her again, and the next morning she would find herself
scanning the faces of her colleagues, looking for signs of judgment.

What finally brought Elena to seek help was a performance review at work. Her manager had noted that
she had become withdrawn, that she avoided collaborative projects, that her usually sharp attention
to detail had slipped. Elena was afraid she would lose her job. But what she was most afraid of, she
told the therapist in her first session, was that the man on the bicycle might forgive her—because
if he did, she would have to forgive herself, and she did not know how to do that.
