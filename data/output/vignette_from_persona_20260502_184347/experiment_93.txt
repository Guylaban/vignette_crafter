The night the pneumonia took hold, Maya remembered the precise sound of her own breathing—a wet,
ragged rasp that seemed to come from somewhere outside her body. She was fifty-one then, three years
divorced, working as an electrician for a commercial contractor in Portland. The illness had
escalated fast: a fever that spiked at 104, the ambulance ride with its antiseptic smell clinging to
the paramedic’s jacket, the ICU where machines breathed for her for six days. She survived, but
something in her chest never fully settled. Now, at fifty-four, Maya could not hear heavy
breathing—not even her own after climbing a ladder—without her stomach dropping. The anniversary of
her discharge, the first of November, arrived each year like a door she had to walk through. And
hospital smells, that sharp chemical overlay of bleach and hand sanitizer, could stop her mid-
sentence in a hardware store aisle.

Those triggers did not just unsettle her; they demanded something from her. When a coworker on a job
site coughed hard and then panted, Maya found herself stepping back, her mind sliding sideways. She
had developed a trick for it: she pretended it happened to someone else. She would picture a
stranger in that hospital bed, a woman she did not know, and the story became less hers. This helped
in the moment, but it also left her feeling hollow afterward, as if she had misplaced her own
history. She also spent long hours imagining how life would have been different if she had never
gotten sick—she would not have lost her rhythm at work, would not have stopped dating, would not
have learned to scan every room for exits. But that counterfactual thinking looped back on itself;
the more she imagined an alternate life, the more she noticed the sounds and smells that proved she
was still in this one. The triggers became sharper, more frequent, as if her own mind were
sharpening them.

Her body had learned to react before she could think. Maya would be running cable in a basement,
focused on the junction box, and then suddenly her heart would hammer, her palms would sweat, and
she would be halfway to the stairs before she understood why: a nurse in the building had passed by,
trailing that hospital smell. Her body felt constantly tense, coiled like a wire under tension,
ready to snap at nothing. She had come to believe that her own physical responses were the real
danger—that if she let herself feel the fear fully, she might stop breathing again. So when the
tension spiked, she dissociated. She told herself the trembling hands belonged to someone else. This
worked briefly, but it also meant she never checked whether the threat was real. Her body stayed on
alert, waiting for the next trigger, and the cycle tightened.

Over the months, Maya began avoiding anything medical. She cancelled her annual physical three
times. She drove an extra twenty minutes to avoid a street with a hospital on it. When a friend
suggested they meet for coffee near a clinic, Maya said she was too busy. The avoidance cost her:
she lost touch with that friend, and her supervisor noticed she turned down jobs near the medical
district. But what frightened her most was the way her own mind had become a trigger. Thinking about
how things might have been different—imagining the life she could have had—always circled back to
the sound of her own breathing in that ICU bed. She was certain now that her body would never stop
being ready for danger, and that certainty kept her scanning, kept her tensing, kept her pretending
it was someone else’s story.

She came to therapy because she had walked off a job site last week. A heavy breather in the
elevator. She had left without telling anyone, and her boss said if it happened again, she was done.
Maya sat in the chair with her hands clasped, her jaw tight, and said she just wanted to stop
feeling like her own body was a trap.
