The event itself was something Camila still could not fully describe. She had been working late in
the chemistry lab, a routine titration running behind schedule, when a corroded valve on a waste
solvent container gave way. There was no explosion, no fire—just a thin, sweet-smelling vapor that
rose silently and filled her side of the room before the ventilation system kicked in. She coughed,
her eyes burned, and within minutes she felt a deep ache in her chest and a metallic taste that
would not fade. She walked herself to the campus health center, but the memory of that walk was
already jumbled—she could not say whether she had passed the fountain first or the library steps, or
whether the security guard had spoken to her. The sequence was gone, and what remained was only the
pain: a tight, searing sensation across her ribs that returned every time she tried to think back to
that afternoon.

In the weeks that followed, Camila began to notice that her own body felt foreign and unreliable.
Her heart would race at odd moments, her muscles stayed braced as if she were still standing in that
chemical haze, and she found herself scanning every room she entered for the source of any smell—a
cleaning product, a hand sanitizer, the antiseptic sting of the campus clinic. She had come to
believe that there was something fundamentally wrong with her, that her body had betrayed her by
reacting so violently to what others called a minor exposure. She no longer trusted her own lungs to
tell her the truth about the air she was breathing. This conviction colored everything: the moment
she caught a whiff of bleach in the hallway, she was certain she could taste that same metallic
edge, and her chest would tighten as though the vapor were still there.

Darkness became another kind of threat. Camila had always slept with the lights off, but now the
moment the room went black, she could not find the boundaries of her own body. The absence of visual
cues left her disoriented, and the physical pain in her ribs would flare without warning, as though
the chemical burn were happening again in the dark. She began leaving a lamp on all night, but even
that was not enough. When she woke in the small hours to find the bulb had burned out, she would lie
rigid, her breath shallow, listening for sounds that were not there, her body already bracing for
contact. Once, her roommate came home late and touched her shoulder to say goodnight; Camila jolted
upright with a cry, her heart hammering so hard she thought she might be sick. After that, she
started sleeping with her back to the wall and a chair wedged under the doorknob.

She tried to erase the memory altogether. She deleted the photos on her phone from that week, she
stopped walking past the science building, and she took a different bus route that added forty
minutes to her commute. When the image of the corroded valve surfaced anyway—usually at night, when
she could not distract herself—she would force her mind to go blank, repeating a phrase or a number
until the picture dissolved. But the more she pushed the memory away, the more her body stayed on
alert. The scanning became automatic: every room she entered, she noted the exits, the air vents,
the smell of the air. She could not concentrate in lectures because she was too busy monitoring the
temperature and the scent of the room. Her grades slipped, and she started skipping classes
altogether, telling herself she would catch up later.

The worst moments came when she could not find the words. She knew what had happened—she could feel
it in her ribs, she could taste it—but when her advisor asked her to describe the incident for a
safety report, her mind went empty. She sat in the office with her hands clenched, unable to produce
a single coherent sentence about the order of events. That failure confirmed what she already
suspected: that she was broken, that her body and her mind were no longer under her control. The
silence in the room felt like an accusation, and afterward she avoided her advisor entirely, ducking
into bathrooms when she saw him in the hallway.

chosen because there was no chemical smell. She had stopped cooking, eating only cold food from

clinic was a night when the power went out and she found herself crouched in the corner, her hands
over her mouth, waiting for a pain that did not come. She was afraid of what she might do if the
lights stayed off any longer.
