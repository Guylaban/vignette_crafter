when he was detained and tortured by police during a business trip to a country where he had once
lived. For twelve hours, he was held in a concrete room, beaten, and subjected to mock executions
while officers shouted at him in a language he only half understood. The incomprehensible chatter of
their voices, the dizzying spins of vertigo from a blow to the head, and the distant wail of sirens
outside the building had all fused into a single, unbearable memory. He had never spoken of it to
anyone, not even his wife, because he was certain that nothing good could come of reopening that
night. He believed, with a quiet, unshakable conviction, that there was something fundamentally
wrong with him—something that had made the officers choose him, something that made him deserve what
happened. That belief had hardened into a general mistrust of everyone around him, and he now
assumed that people could not be trusted, that their kindness was always a prelude to betrayal.

Because he felt intrinsically flawed and saw no future worth hoping for, Raj had begun to avoid
anything that could possibly be stressful. He stopped attending team meetings where disagreements
might arise, delegated every difficult decision to a junior colleague, and declined invitations to
social gatherings. He also started monitoring his body constantly, scanning for any sign of
dizziness or lightheadedness, which he had come to associate with the onset of a panic attack or,
worse, a repeat of the night he could not forget. This body monitoring, in turn, reinforced his
sense that something was wrong with him, that his own body was a source of danger. When he saw a
police car or a person in uniform on the street, he would cross to the other side or duck into a
shop until they passed. These avoidance strategies, though they gave him momentary relief, deepened
his conviction that he was broken and that no good could ever come his way again.

His constant vigilance made it impossible to relax. Even at home, in his own living room, he sat
with his back to the wall, his shoulders tight, his eyes scanning the room. He woke several times
each night to check the locks on the doors and windows, certain that someone had entered. And when
his wife touched his shoulder unexpectedly, he would flinch violently, his heart hammering, his
breath catching. This startle response was so ingrained that he had begun to sleep on the edge of
the bed, facing the door, to avoid being caught off guard. His hypervigilance also made him more
sensitive to triggers. The sound of sirens from a passing ambulance would send him straight back to
that concrete room, and he would feel the dizziness wash over him again. The incomprehensible
chatter of strangers in a coffee shop—just two people speaking another language—would make his palms
sweat and his vision narrow. He had come to see these triggers as proof that his body was still in
danger, which only made him monitor himself more closely.

The cycle tightened over months. The more he avoided, the more he believed he was fundamentally
flawed. The more he scanned for threats, the more triggers he found. The more he found triggers, the
more he avoided. His wife began to complain that he was distant, that he no longer laughed or
planned for the future. At work, his performance reviews slipped, and he was placed on a performance
improvement plan. What finally brought him to seek help was a morning when, after a night of broken
sleep, he stood in the kitchen and felt a wave of dizziness so strong he had to grip the counter. He
was certain he was about to collapse, and for a moment he thought the police had found him again. He
told himself he could not go on like this, but even as he said it, he was not sure he believed that
anything could change. What frightened him most was the possibility that the therapy would confirm
what he already knew: that he was too broken to be fixed.
