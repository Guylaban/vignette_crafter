Amina is a forty-two-year-old train conductor who was tortured during a period of political
detention in her home country before immigrating to the United States a decade ago. The torture had
been systematic and sensory: she was kept in a concrete room where guards would suddenly fall
silent, then beat her without warning; she remembers the smell of her own burning skin from a heated
metal rod pressed against her thigh, and the sight of blood pooling around another prisoner's head.
These moments have never become a coherent story in her mind. When she tries to recall what
happened, she cannot tell a clear sequence—instead, she is flooded with vivid, disjointed images:
the glint of the metal rod, the silence before a blow, the smoke rising from her own flesh. The
memories are not memories she can narrate; they are pictures that crash into her present, and when
they arrive, she feels as though the torture is happening all over again. The reliving is so
complete that she loses track of where she is, and afterwards she often finds herself lighting a
cigarette, the act of smoking the only thing that seems to tether her back to now.

Amina works long shifts on the commuter rail, a job that keeps her in constant motion but also
exposes her to her most potent triggers. A passenger with a visible injury—a bandaged arm, a bruised
face—can send her heart racing, and she has learned to look away quickly, but the image already
burns into her mind. Worse is the silence. Sometimes, between stations, the train goes quiet for a
few seconds, and in that stillness she hears the concrete room again; her chest tightens, and she
finds herself mentally rehearsing worst-case scenarios—what she would do if someone attacked her in
the aisle, how she would escape, where she would hide. She has come to believe that this mental
rehearsal keeps her prepared, that if she imagines every possible danger, she might survive it. But
the rehearsals themselves feed back into her memories: after a shift, she cannot stop seeing the
images, and she often stays up late into the night, smoking one cigarette after another, checking
her body for any new bruise or cut, convinced that something must be wrong.

At home, Amina is unable to relax. Even with her husband asleep beside her, she lies rigid,
listening for sounds that might mean someone is entering the house. A creak in the hallway, a
floorboard settling—each noise sharpens her attention, and she feels her body go tight, ready to
fight or flee. This hypervigilance keeps her memory alive: the more she scans for threat, the more
the images of the torture surface, and the more she relives the terror of being trapped. She has
started to avoid sudden movements by others; when her husband reaches quickly for something on a
shelf, she flinches, and the flinch itself reminds her of the guard who lunged without warning. In
response, she smokes more, and she checks her body compulsively—running her hands over her arms, her
ribs, her thighs—looking for any sign of injury that might confirm the danger she feels is always
near. This body monitoring, she believes, is the only way to stay safe, but it also keeps her locked
in the past, because every check reminds her of the wounds she once had.

The cycle has become relentless. The triggers—silence, smoke, visible injuries—pull her directly
into reliving, and the reliving makes her more vigilant, and the vigilance demands more rehearsal,
more checking, more smoking. She has lost weight; her husband has started to sleep in the other room
because she wakes him with her pacing. What finally brought Amina to seek help was an incident on
the train: a passenger collapsed, and someone shouted for a medic, and the shout was followed by a
sudden, terrible silence as everyone waited. Amina froze at the controls, her hands shaking,
convinced she could smell smoke. She could not move or speak for nearly a minute. Afterward, she
understood that something was breaking inside her. She is afraid that if she does not get help, she
will lose her job, and she is equally afraid that talking about the torture will make the reliving
worse. But she is more afraid of what will happen if she keeps going as she is.
