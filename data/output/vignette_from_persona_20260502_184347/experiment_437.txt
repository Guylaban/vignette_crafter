Frank is a 77-year-old delivery driver who has been married to his wife, Eleanor, for fifty-two
years. The assault happened six months ago, on a Tuesday afternoon in late October. He had just
pulled his van into the lot behind a grocery store on his usual route, stepping out to hand a stack
of boxes to the receiving clerk, when a man came around the side of the building. Frank never saw
him coming. The first blow caught him on the side of the head, just above the ear, and he went down
hard on the asphalt. The man kicked him twice in the ribs before grabbing the cash envelope from
Frank’s jacket pocket and running. Frank lay there for what felt like a long time, staring at the
wet leaves stuck to the pavement, waiting for someone to come. No one did for almost ten minutes.
The clerk finally found him when he came out for a smoke break.

In the weeks that followed, Frank could not stop thinking about the moment he hit the ground. The
image came to him at odd times—while he was eating breakfast, while he was driving, while Eleanor
was talking to him. He would feel the cold of the pavement against his cheek and the weight of the
man’s foot against his ribs, and his chest would tighten so hard he could barely breathe. He started
to believe that he should have seen it coming. A man his age, working alone in a back lot—what had
he been thinking? He had always prided himself on being careful, on reading people and situations,
but he had missed every sign. That realization hollowed him out. He began to feel that his instincts
could no longer be trusted, that his judgment had eroded without him noticing. What else had he
missed over the years? What other dangers had he walked past, blind and stupid, while Eleanor
trusted him to keep them safe?

The more he doubted himself, the more he felt that if he let himself think about the event—really
think about it, the way it kept trying to force its way into his mind—he would not be able to come
back from it. He was certain that dwelling on the assault would break something inside him that was
already cracked. So he did everything he could to keep the thoughts at bay. He started eating more,
especially at night, sitting in front of the television with a plate of whatever was in the fridge,
chewing mechanically until the tightness in his chest loosened just a little. He also threw himself
into busyness with a kind of grim determination. He volunteered for extra shifts, offered to cover
routes that other drivers hated, and came home exhausted and sore, his ribs still aching from the
fall. He told Eleanor it was good to stay active, that sitting around would only make him feel old.
What he did not tell her was that the moment he stopped moving, the moment he sat still, the image
of that wet pavement came rushing back, and he could feel himself falling again.

The busyness worked, sort of. As long as he was driving, or lifting, or walking into a warehouse, he
could keep the memories at a distance. But the cost was mounting. His appetite had grown so much
that his clothes no longer fit properly, and Eleanor had started making comments about his health.
He avoided her worried looks by leaving earlier for work and staying later, telling himself it was
just the season, the holiday rush. He had come to believe that if he let himself rest, if he let his
guard down for even one evening, the thoughts would flood in and he would not survive them. So he
kept moving. He kept eating. He kept his mind fixed on the road ahead, on the next delivery, on
anything except what had happened in that parking lot.

What finally brought Frank to seek help was a morning last week when he could not get out of bed.
His body would not move. He lay there staring at the ceiling, his heart hammering, while Eleanor
stood in the doorway asking him what was wrong. He could not tell her. He could not say that he was
afraid to get up, afraid to go back to that lot, afraid that his own mind had become a trap he could
not escape. He is most afraid that if he stops running, he will fall apart completely. But he is
also afraid, somewhere underneath that, that he has already fallen apart and just has not noticed
yet.
