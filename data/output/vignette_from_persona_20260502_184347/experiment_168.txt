The attack happened three years ago, in the walk-in cooler of the restaurant where she had worked
for a decade. A new sous-chef, a man she had trained herself, had cornered her among the hanging
sides of beef. He had been joking, then he was not. He had pressed her against the cold metal
shelving, his hand clamped over her mouth, his other hand gripping her wrist so hard the bones
ground together. She had frozen, certain he would kill her. He did not. He let her go, laughed, and
walked out. She never reported it. She told herself it was nothing, that she had overreacted. But
her body remembered what her mind tried to bury.

Elena could not put the event into words. When she tried to tell her husband, the sentences
dissolved into fragments—the cold, the pressure, the smell of raw beef—and she would stop, shaking,
unable to finish. The memories came in disconnected pieces: the feel of the metal shelf digging into
her spine, the sound of his belt buckle clinking, the sudden silence after he laughed. Sometimes,
standing at her stove at work, she would feel her body lock into that same frozen posture, her
shoulders drawn up, her breath shallow, as though she were still pinned against that cooler wall.

She had come to believe that you can never know who will harm you. The man she had trusted, whom she
had trained, had betrayed that trust utterly. Now, she was certain that people cannot be
trusted—especially men. This belief hardened into a constant watchfulness. When a male customer
leaned over the pass to compliment her food, she felt her chest tighten. When a stranger approached
her in the grocery store parking lot, her pulse would spike before her mind could register that the
man was just asking for directions. Unexpected movement nearby—a colleague reaching for a pan, a
waiter stepping suddenly into her peripheral vision—made her flinch as if she were under attack.

The triggers were everywhere, and they fed the cycle. Certain conversation topics—talk of betrayal,
of workplace politics, of men and women working together—would send her mind spiraling back to the
cooler. She would feel the cold, hear the clink of his belt, and the memory would flood in, vivid
and wordless. Each time this happened, her mistrust deepened. She began to believe that every man
who showed her kindness was a potential threat. When a new male line cook tried to compliment her
knife skills, she snapped at him to focus on his station. She avoided being alone with any man at
work, even the elderly dishwasher. She would wait until another woman was in the kitchen before she
entered the walk-in.

This hypervigilance exhausted her. She felt threatened by strangers approaching her in ordinary
situations—a man asking for the time on the street, a delivery driver at the back door of the
restaurant. Her startle response was so sharp that a colleague had asked if she was okay. She had
laughed it off, but inside she was certain: the world was full of people who would hurt her if given
the chance. The more she watched for danger, the more she saw it. The more she saw it, the more she
believed her fear was justified. Each unexpected movement, each stranger's approach, each casual
conversation that brushed against the topic of trust—all of it looped back to the memory, and the
memory looped back to the belief that she could not trust anyone.

Her marriage began to fray. She avoided emotional closeness with her husband, pulling away when he
tried to talk about what had happened. She told him it was over, that she had moved on, but she
could not let him hold her at night. She was not afraid of him, exactly, but she was afraid of the
vulnerability that closeness required. She had come to believe that being emotionally close to
someone was the first step toward being betrayed.

What finally brought her to seek help was a simple thing: a male customer had smiled at her and
said, "Thank you, chef." She had frozen, her hand gripping the plate so hard her knuckles went
white, and she had seen the cooler again, felt the cold, heard the silence. She had excused herself
to the bathroom and cried for ten minutes. She was afraid she was losing her mind. But more than
that, she was afraid of what she might do if she did not find a way to stop the cycle—afraid she
would lose her job, her marriage, her self. She came to therapy because she could not live like this
anymore, and because somewhere, beneath the fear, she still hoped that trust was possible.
