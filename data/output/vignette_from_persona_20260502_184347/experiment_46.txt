pressed in, and the sound of the other vehicle's engine, still running and whining, played over and
over in his ears.

In the months that followed, Letao found he could not shake the feeling that the event was still
happening. A sudden car sound on the street—a backfire, a horn—would instantly return the full
memory without warning: the smell of gasoline, the sensation of the car still spinning, the auditory
replay of that grinding engine noise in his head. The memory felt present, not past, as though he
were again trapped in the dark. He began to notice that his body reacted before his mind had time to
think; his shoulders would tense, his breathing would quicken, and he would be scanning for exits
the moment he entered any room, even his own kitchen. He woke during the night to check whether he
was safe, lying still and listening for sounds that did not belong.

his mind dwell on the crash, even for a moment, he would not be able to handle it—the fear would
overwhelm him completely. So he avoided thinking about it, but the vigilance itself became
exhausting. Because he was certain that you never knew what could happen next, he started driving
only on familiar roads during daylight, and he began to leave every meeting early so he could be

street to avoid it, and on days when the wind carried it toward him, he felt the old dread surge.

switch before stepping inside, his heart already pounding.

belief that the world was unsafe. He became convinced that he needed to be especially careful, which
only heightened his bodily alertness. The loop tightened over weeks and months: he would wake alert,
checking for threats, and the silence of the house would somehow remind him of the quiet after the
crash, bringing the memory flooding back. At work, his concentration frayed. He began to avoid the
windowed conference room because its street view let him hear traffic; he moved his desk to an

broken.
