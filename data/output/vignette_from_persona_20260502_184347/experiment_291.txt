sometimes felt nothing at all, as if it had happened to a stranger. But other times, the guilt would
surge up, a sickening wave: he should have done the homework earlier, should have been better,
should have made his father proud.

That guilt had become a habit. He dwelled on what he had done wrong, replaying the night in his
head, searching for the moment he could have avoided it. But he also dwelled on what his father had
done, spinning the same angry loop: the injustice, the cruelty, the way his father had never
apologized. This rumination was not a choice anymore; it was a groove in his mind. He had come to
believe that the abuse had permanently damaged him, that he had changed for the worse, and that
people could not be trusted. These beliefs felt like facts. Because he was broken, he told himself,
he deserved to be alone. Because people were untrustworthy, he kept them at arm’s length. His
divorce had only confirmed this. His ex-wife had said he was cold, unreachable—and she was right. He
was certain that his reactions since the abuse meant he was going crazy, and that thought only drove
him deeper into the same mental ruts.

even inside his own home, a loft he had designed to feel open and safe, but it never did. When

more vivid, more real.

fragile, that he had to be careful, that he was permanently damaged. The rumination, the avoidance,

apart. But what frightened him most was the possibility that nothing could change, that the damage
was permanent, and that he would spend the rest of his life jumping at shadows in his own home.
