could still summon the smell of it. Not the image—that remained fractured, a slide deck of
disconnected moments: the housekeeper’s panicked knock, the acrid yellow haze seeping under the

unbidden, a metallic sweetness that flooded his chest with terror before his mind could even
register what was happening. He had been supervising a routine pipe repair in the basement when a
corroded valve burst, releasing a cloud of industrial solvent into the sealed room. He got everyone
out, but not before he had inhaled deeply. In the weeks that followed, he could not trust his own
body. It kept reacting as if he were still in that basement—heart hammering, skin prickling, breath
short—even when he was standing in his quiet kitchen. He had come to believe that his body was no
longer his ally, that it would betray him at any moment, and that he was fundamentally helpless to
stop bad things from happening.

The helplessness bled into everything. Harold began to feel that the world was shot through with
hidden threats, and he found himself scanning his environment constantly. At home—the only place
that felt even remotely safe—he was still on guard, flinching at the rumble of the garbage truck or
the sudden slam of a neighbor’s door. He had reduced his safe places to three: his apartment, the
small park bench by the river where he could see all approaches, and the hotel lobby during the
quiet morning shift. Everywhere else felt like a potential trap. The sense of threat was so
pervasive that it began to feed back into his memories; the more vigilant he became, the more often
the disconnected fragments of that day intruded, often triggered by nothing more than an unexpected
sound. A dropped pan in the hotel kitchen could send him right back to the hiss of the ruptured
valve, his throat closing, the metallic smell ghosting through his sinuses even though nothing was
there.

To manage the intrusions, Harold had developed a set of strategies that made sense to him but that
he knew, in quieter moments, were not really working. He kept the television or a radio playing
loudly at all times in his apartment, drowning out the silence that his mind would otherwise fill
with the sound of that hissing pipe. He also monitored his own thoughts constantly, scanning for any
sign that he was losing control, that the terror might break through and he would finally go crazy.
The effort was exhausting, and it only reinforced his sense that his body was unreliable—that he had
to police himself because he could not trust his own mind. He had also stopped going anywhere near
the hotel basement, taking the long way around the building to reach the main office, and he had
instructed his assistant to handle any maintenance issues personally. This avoidance had begun to
strain his working relationships; his staff noticed he was jumpy, less available, and he had started
delegating tasks he had once prided himself on handling directly.

The cycle tightened. The more he avoided and monitored, the more convinced he became that he could
not cope, that his helplessness was a permanent fact. And the more helpless he felt, the more on
guard he became, scanning for danger even in the safety of his own home. His sleep was poor, his
appetite diminished, and he had lost touch with most of his friends, who had grown tired of his
refusal to go to restaurants or social gatherings. What finally brought Harold to seek help was a
moment in the hotel lobby last Tuesday, when a delivery driver dropped a metal dolly on the marble
floor. The crack sent Harold into a full panic attack in front of the front desk staff. He could not
breathe, could not speak, and for several minutes he was certain he was dying. Afterward, he sat in
his office with the door locked, trembling, and realized that he could not keep living this way. He
was most afraid that the therapy would fail, that he would be told he was too old to change, and
that the rest of his life would be spent trapped in that basement, over and over.
