Meena is an 80-year-old divorced South Asian woman who works part-time as a dentist. She had built a
quiet life around her practice, her grown children, and the small garden behind her home in a
suburban neighborhood. That life fractured in a single afternoon three years ago, when a car bomb
detonated two blocks from her dental office. She had been walking back from a late lunch, and the
blast threw her against a brick wall. Smoke and dust filled the air, sirens screamed from every
direction, and for what felt like an eternity, Meena lay on the ground, unable to move, pinned by
debris and the weight of her own shock. A stranger grabbed her arm and pulled her into a doorway,
but in that moment, she had no control over her body or her fate. She was utterly at someone else's
mercy.

In the weeks that followed, Meena returned to work, but the world no longer felt safe. The first
time she heard a siren outside her office, her hands began to shake, and she had to excuse herself
from a patient's chair. She became acutely aware of how vulnerable she was during a procedure —
someone else was always in control, whether it was the patient reclining in her chair or the
assistant handing her instruments. This sense of power imbalance, of being trapped in a situation
where someone else held authority over her safety, began to surface whenever she felt she could not
leave. Smoke from a neighbor's grill or the faint smell of burning leaves from a nearby yard would
send her heart racing, and she would find herself scanning the room for exits. Over time, Meena
developed a set of habits she never would have imagined needing. She stopped walking past the block
where the bombing occurred, taking a longer route to work instead. She began checking her
surroundings constantly — every time she entered a room, she catalogued the doors, the windows, the
people, the shadows. She made sure to sit or stand near an exit in every waiting room, every
restaurant, every hallway. These strategies worked, in a way: they gave her a sense of control. But
they also meant that her attention was never fully on her patients or on her own thoughts. She was
always watching, always listening.

The more she scanned and checked, the more she noticed. A distant siren became not just a sound but
a signal that something was about to happen. The smell of smoke from a passing car became a warning.
And the more she noticed these triggers, the more her vigilance intensified. She began to believe
that if she let her guard down for even a moment, she would be caught off guard again — that the
only way to stay safe was to never stop watching. This loop tightened over months. She would avoid a
certain street, then find herself checking the exits in a patient's room, then hear a siren and feel
her chest tighten, and then scan again. Each strategy fed the next trigger. Each trigger demanded a
new strategy.

The consequences crept into her life. Her children noticed that she no longer joined them for
outdoor dinners at the local park, where the smell of charcoal grills was too close to the smoke she
remembered. She told them she was tired. She told herself she was being careful. But what frightened
her most was the quiet erosion of her confidence. She had always been a steady dentist, calm under
pressure. Now, during a root canal, she would catch herself glancing at the door rather than the
tooth. She worried she might make a mistake. She worried she might not hear the sirens in time.

What finally brought Meena to seek help was a single moment in her office last month. A fire drill
was announced, and the building's alarm — a high, pulsing wail — sent her into a panic so severe
that she locked herself in the supply closet. When her assistant found her, Meena was trembling,
unable to speak. She knew then that her strategies were no longer keeping her safe. She was tired of
living on alert. She wanted to sit in a room without checking the exits. She wanted to hear a siren
and feel nothing but the sound. She was afraid she might never get there, but she was even more
afraid of staying where she was.
