that followed, she could not stop replaying it.

Now, that voice haunts her daily. She hears it in the courthouse hallway when a colleague raises
their voice in debate, or in the coffee shop when a stranger speaks on their phone. Each time, her
body reacts before her mind can catch up. Her heart pounds, her breath goes shallow, and she feels a
cold certainty that someone is following her, watching her from a doorway or a parked car. This is
not a distant memory; it is a present danger. The trigger—the voice, the feeling of being
tracked—immediately pulls her into a state of high alert. She becomes hypervigilant, scanning every
face, every shadow, her muscles coiled for flight. She wakes in the middle of the night, her ears
straining for the sound of footsteps outside, needing to check that the apartment door is locked,
that her husband is still breathing beside her. She has come to believe that safety is an illusion,
that it exists only in a very limited number of places: her own bedroom, with the curtains drawn, or
the locked bathroom at work.

This constant state of threat has slowly poisoned how Layla sees herself and the world. She used to
be a happy person, quick to laugh, confident in her ability to handle a difficult case or a family

to eat lunch alone in her office, to avoid any public space where a voice might rise unexpectedly.

begun delegating her most demanding cases to junior colleagues, inventing excuses about needing to

again, to walk through a hallway without her heart racing, to hear a voice and know it is just a
