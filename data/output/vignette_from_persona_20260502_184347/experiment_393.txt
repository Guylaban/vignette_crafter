war zone, a life she had sealed away behind a wall of silence for decades. The event that had
shattered her was a single afternoon, years ago, in a bombed-out school she had been using as a
distribution point. A group of armed men had arrived, not in uniform but in a chaotic mix of
military jackets and civilian clothes, and she had been certain she was about to die. She could
still hear the specific, metallic scrape of a boot on concrete, the low, guttural order in a
language she only half-understood, and the taste of dust and copper in her mouth as she pressed
herself into a corner, waiting for the bullet that never came. They had only taken the supplies and
left her shaking, but for Therese, the world had never felt safe again.

In the decades since, she had built a life of rigid order. Her courtroom was a sanctuary of
procedure and predictability, where the chaos of the outside world could be held at bay by rules and
robes. But the past had a way of finding her. The anniversary of that afternoon would arrive without
warning, and she would find herself unable to sleep for a week, the metallic taste returning to her
tongue. A uniformed police officer testifying in her court could trigger a full sensory reliving of
that moment—the smell of dust, the scrape of a boot, the terror so absolute it felt like her heart
would stop. She had learned to avoid these moments. She would schedule her vacations around the
anniversary, and she had quietly asked her clerk to ensure that uniformed officers submitted their
testimony in writing whenever possible, claiming a preference for efficiency. She tried hard to
control her emotions, believing that if she ever let the fear show, the whole fragile structure of
her life would collapse.

Therese had come to believe that others could see what had happened to her just by looking at her.
When a colleague gave her a curious glance in the hallway, she was certain they could read the
terror on her face, that they knew she was not the composed figure she worked so hard to project.
This feeling of being exposed only deepened her sense of isolation. She felt set apart from
everyone, even from her own family, who had long since stopped asking why she never came to holiday
dinners. The world, she was convinced, was a dangerous place, and every unexpected sound—a door
slamming, a raised voice in the hall—was proof of it. These beliefs made the memories worse. When
she caught a glimpse of a military jacket on the street, the negative appraisal of the world as
dangerous would prime her memory, and the full sensory reliving would crash over her like a wave.

The memories themselves, vivid and terrifying, only reinforced her negative appraisals. After a
particularly bad flashback, she would feel even more certain that others could see her fragility,
and more convinced that the world was a minefield. This created a self-reinforcing cycle: the more
the memories came, the more dangerous the world felt, and the more dangerous the world felt, the
more the memories were triggered. She had begun to avoid more and more circumstances—any dark room,
any place that smelled of dust, any gathering where a stranger might wear a uniform. She suppressed
the thoughts during the day, but they would surface at night, the auditory recall of that guttural
voice so clear she would sit bolt upright in bed, her heart pounding.

face, had worn a dark green jacket to court, and Therese had frozen mid-sentence, the courtroom
swimming before her eyes. She had excused herself, retreated to her chambers, and sat in the dark
for an hour, unable to stop the full sensory reliving. She was afraid that next time, she would not
be able to hide it. She was afraid that the world she had built, the one that kept the chaos at bay,
was crumbling. What she wanted, more than anything, was for the memories to stop feeling so real,
for the terror to stay in the past where it belonged. But she was also terrified of what it would
mean to let them out.
