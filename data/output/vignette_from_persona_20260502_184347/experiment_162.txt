control. At seventy-six, she was a retired radiologist, a profession that had rewarded her
meticulous eye and her ability to see danger hidden in plain sight—a shadow on a lung, a fracture
line barely visible. But the danger she now scanned for was not on an X-ray. It was everywhere else.
The trauma that had rewired her world had happened three years prior, during a brief visit to a city
that erupted into armed conflict while she was there. She had been caught in a crossfire in a
marketplace, pinned behind a concrete planter for what felt like hours, the air thick with smoke and
the sound of automatic weapons. A man next to her, a stranger, had been shot in the head. She
remembered the wet, percussive sound of the bullet striking bone, the way his body slumped, the
blood spreading across the white stone. What she could not do, however, was retrieve the memory in
any orderly way. When she tried to deliberately recall that day, her mind went blank, a white wall
of static. The memory existed only as a series of fragments—the smell of cordite, the sensation of
grit against her cheek, the unbearable heat of the sun on her neck—but it would not come together as
a story. She could not put it into words. The shame that accompanied these involuntary flashes was
profound; she felt a deep, irrational sense of culpability, as if her very survival had been a form
of cowardice.

Because she could not control the memory, Eleanor had come to believe that the only way to manage it
was to control everything else. Her avoidance had become a full-time occupation. She had stopped
making any plans for the future, refusing to book a vacation or even a dinner reservation more than
a day in advance, because the act of looking ahead felt like an invitation for catastrophe. She
spent long hours on her tablet, playing solitaire or scrolling through news feeds, a screen that
served as a barrier between her and the intrusive fragments. When the memory surfaced—a sudden,
unbidden image of the man’s face—she would immediately reach for her phone, the act of swiping and
tapping a ritual that allowed her to escape the feeling. This, in turn, deepened the problem. By
refusing to engage with the memory, she never practiced the act of recalling it on her own terms,
which made the involuntary intrusions feel even more alien and terrifying when they broke through.
The shame grew, too, because she felt she was hiding from something she should have been able to
face.

The memory also dictated her sense of threat. The world outside her home had become a field of
potential ambushes. She constantly scanned her surroundings for signs of danger, her eyes darting
from person to person, from doorway to doorway, a clinical vigilance that exhausted her. She avoided
unfamiliar places entirely, because she could not pre-assess the risk. Even a simple trip to a new
grocery store felt like navigating a minefield. She felt threatened by strangers approaching her,
even in ordinary situations; a person asking for directions on the street would make her heart pound
and her breath catch. This hypervigilance, in turn, fed back into her memory. The more she scanned
for danger, the more her brain was primed to interpret any ambiguous cue—a raised voice, a sudden
movement—as a signal of returning threat, which would trigger a flash of the marketplace, the sound
of the gunshot, the feeling of the concrete against her back. Her threat-monitoring was not a
neutral state; it was a mechanism that repeatedly activated the traumatic memory, keeping the wound
fresh.

The cycle had become self-reinforcing. Her avoidance of feelings and plans meant she had no buffer
against the threat, and her constant scanning meant she was never truly safe. The only way Eleanor
had found to quiet the loop was to retreat further into her strategies. She told Dr. Harris that she
had not left her house in over two weeks, except to sit in her backyard, where she could see all
approaches. She had stopped answering the phone. Her husband, a kind but bewildered man, had taken
over all the shopping and errands, but he could not understand why she would not even talk about
what had happened. What finally brought Eleanor to seek help was not the fear, but the shame. She
had realized, with a clarity that felt like another blow, that she was disappearing. The woman who
had once diagnosed cancer from a shadow was now afraid of a stranger’s glance. She was not living;
she was hiding. What she feared most was that there was no way out of the loop—that the memory would
always be in control. What she hoped for, barely daring to admit it, was that Dr. Harris might help
her find a way to look at it, just once, without being destroyed.
