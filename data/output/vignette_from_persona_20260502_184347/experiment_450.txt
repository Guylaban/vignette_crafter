Zainabu was twenty-two when the military hospital in Mogadishu came under attack. She had been a
surgical resident for barely four months, still learning how to keep her hands steady when the chest
cavity opened and the blood came. That afternoon, the first explosion hit the generator building.
She remembers the sound not as a bang but as a pressure that passed through her ribs and left her
ears ringing for days afterward. What she remembers most, though, is the texture of the gauze she
was pressing into a soldier’s femoral wound when the second explosion came—the way the wet cotton
shifted against her gloved fingers, the sudden slickness as the soldier’s body went still beneath
her hands. She cannot describe this in words when she tries. The memory comes back to her not as a
story she can tell but as a physical sensation: the weight of the gauze, the smell of diesel smoke,
the rotor blades of the helicopter that landed on the roof twenty minutes later to evacuate the
wounded. She does not recall being afraid at the time. She recalls only the texture.

Now, three years later, Zainabu is a surgeon in a Level 1 trauma center in Chicago. She is good at
her work. But she has begun to notice that she cannot enter the operating room without first
scanning the room for exits. She checks the location of every door, every fire extinguisher, every
place she could crouch if something happened. She sits with her back to the wall of the lounge
during breaks, positioning her chair so she can see the entire room. These habits began as something
she told herself was vigilance, good situational awareness for a trauma surgeon. But they have grown
into something else. The moment she hears a helicopter—even the distant thrum of a news chopper over
the highway—she feels the terror of that afternoon surge back into her chest without warning. Her
hands begin to tremble, and she finds herself mentally mapping the route from wherever she is
standing to the nearest stairwell. She has come to believe that if she does not plan her escape in
advance, she will be caught, trapped, and she will not survive.

The causal shape of her days has become a loop she cannot name. The memories arrive without
warning—a sudden bang from a dropped instrument tray, the feel of wet gauze against her palm during
a wound packing, the distant sound of rotors—and each one triggers a fresh wave of the original
terror. In response, she tightens her vigilance. She checks the room again. She repositions her
chair. She rehearses the route to the fire exit. And the more she scans, the more she notices: the
ventilation grate that could be an explosion point, the ceiling tiles that could fall, the window
that faces the helipad. Each scan feeds new triggers into her awareness. The world has become a room
full of signs she cannot stop reading.

The weight of these strategies has begun to strain her work. Her scrub nurse has noticed that
Zainabu always positions herself so that her back is to the wall of the OR, even when it means
standing at an awkward angle to the table. A colleague asked her about it once. Zainabu said she was
protecting her sterile field. She knew, even as she said it, that this was not the truth. What
frightened her most was the helicopter. Every time the Life Flight chopper landed on the roof, she
felt the memory press against her like a physical force. She had begun to check the hospital's
flight schedule at the start of each shift, and she had considered requesting a transfer to the
outpatient clinic, where the helicopters were farther away. She had not done this yet. She had not
told anyone.

What finally brought Zainabu to the clinic was a Tuesday morning in the OR. A trauma case was
wheeled in—a young man, a car accident, bleeding from his thigh. As she packed the wound, the gauze
turned wet and warm in her hand, and she froze. For a few seconds she could not move, could not
breathe, could not see the patient in front of her. She saw only the soldier in Mogadishu. She
excused herself, handed the case to her attending, and walked to the locker room. She sat on the
bench with her back to the wall and she cried. She is afraid that if she does not find a way to stop
this, she will lose her hands in the moment she needs them most. She is afraid that the next time
she freezes, someone will die.
