María is a 19-year-old Latina woman who works as a train conductor, a job she took right out of high
school because it offered steady hours and a clear path forward. She lives alone in a small
apartment in a quiet part of the city, and she comes to therapy at the urging of her supervisor, who
noticed she had been missing shifts and seemed increasingly jumpy on the job. What emerges over the
first few sessions is a childhood trauma that has shaped her entire sense of safety and self: when
she was seven years old, she witnessed her father beat her mother in their darkened living room.
María had been standing in the doorway, frozen, as her mother screamed and her father’s fists
landed. She remembers the taste of the cheap red wine her father had been drinking—sour and
metallic—because it splashed onto her lips when he knocked over a glass. That taste still returns to
her unexpectedly, vivid and unwelcome, and when it does, the memory feels like it is happening right
now, not like something from twelve years ago. She cannot put the events of that night in the right
order; the images come in fragments—her mother’s face, the sound of a lamp breaking, the dark—and
she is never sure what came first.

The darkness is the worst trigger. When she steps into a dark room, or when the train she is driving
passes through a tunnel, her heart starts racing, and that pounding in her chest immediately pulls
her back into the memory. She has come to believe that she cannot stop bad things from happening to
her, and that she failed to protect her mother that night because of the way she acted—or rather,
did not act. She was just a child, but that does not matter to the feeling: she should have done
something, and her helplessness proves she is fundamentally weak. These appraisals are strongest
when a trigger hits; the loud noise of a train horn or a sudden shout in the station makes her heart
pound, and in that moment she is certain that she is about to fail again, that something terrible is
coming and she will be powerless to stop it.

To cope, María has developed a set of strategies that she relies on daily. She keeps herself busy
constantly—working double shifts, cleaning her apartment at odd hours, scrolling through her
phone—because when she is still, the memories rush in. She also restricts her eating, skipping meals
or eating very little, because she has noticed that hunger keeps her mind sharp and focused on the
present, away from the past. And she finds herself thinking, over and over, about how different her
life would have been if she had yelled or run for help that night; these counterfactual thoughts are
exhausting but feel necessary, as if she might find an answer that finally lets her stop blaming
herself.

Her sense of threat is pervasive. At night, she finds it harder to feel safe than during the day, so
she checks the locks on her door and windows repeatedly before she can even try to sleep. She is on
high alert whenever she hears unexpected or loud sounds—a car backfiring, a door slamming—and that
hypervigilance itself becomes a trigger: the alarm in her body tells her something is wrong, which
makes her heart race, which plunges her back into the memory. The more she scans for danger, the
more she finds it, and the more she finds it, the more she believes she is helpless and that she
failed. She has started avoiding the evening shift at work because the darkness of the tunnels and
the night sky feel unbearable, and she has begun calling in sick on days when she knows she will be
working late.

These strategies, however, only deepen the cycle. Keeping busy prevents her from processing the
memory, so it stays disjointed and present-tense. Undereating leaves her tired and irritable, which
makes her feel even more out of control, reinforcing her belief that she cannot protect herself or
anyone else. And the counterfactual thinking keeps her stuck in a past she cannot change, feeding
her self-blame. The more she avoids the triggers, the more they seem to multiply: a dark hallway at
work now makes her heart pound, and that pounding immediately brings the taste of wine back to her
mouth, and she is seven years old again, frozen in the doorway.

What finally brought María to therapy was a moment on the train last week. She was driving through a
tunnel when a passenger dropped a metal water bottle; the clatter was sharp and sudden. Her heart
exploded in her chest, she lost her sense of where she was, and she nearly let go of the controls.
Her supervisor pulled her off the route and told her she needed help. María is terrified that she
will lose her job, but what frightens her most is the feeling that she is still that little girl,
standing in the dark, unable to move. She hopes, more than anything, that therapy can help her feel
like an adult who has a say in what happens next.
