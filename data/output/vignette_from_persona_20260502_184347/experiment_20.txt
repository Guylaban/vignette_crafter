Meena is a 44-year-old retail worker who lives with her husband in a quiet suburban neighborhood.
The event that changed everything happened eighteen months ago, during a routine evening shift at
the department store where she stocks shelves. She was walking through the home-goods aisle when she
heard a commotion near the register—raised voices, then a single sharp crack that she later learned
was a gunshot. A customer had collapsed, shot during an attempted robbery. Meena did not see the
shooter, but she saw the aftermath: the man on the floor, the spreading dark stain, the paramedics
working frantically. She was not injured, but she stood frozen for what felt like an eternity,
unable to move or look away.

In the weeks that followed, the memory of that moment refused to settle into a clear sequence. When
Meena tries to piece together what happened, the images come in fragments—the tile floor, the
paramedic's voice, the color of the man's shirt—but she cannot arrange them in order. What stays
with her most powerfully is not the factual sequence but the overwhelming grief that floods her when
she remembers. She finds herself weeping without warning, the emotion as fresh as if the event were
still happening. When the memory returns, it is so overwhelming that she has to stop whatever she is
doing, unable to speak or move until the wave passes.

Meena has come to believe that her mind is broken, that she cannot trust her own memory or her own
reactions. This fear has driven her to scan her surroundings constantly, watching for any sign of
threat. At work, she finds herself checking the exits, studying customers' faces, listening for
raised voices. The hypervigilance is exhausting, but she is certain that if she lets her guard down,
something terrible will happen again. She has become easily startled—a dropped box in the stockroom
makes her heart race for minutes afterward. The more she scans for danger, the more the memory of
that night intrudes, as if her vigilance itself triggers the images and the grief.

The memory loops back into her strategies in a tightening spiral. When the overwhelming recall
hits—usually after a customer raises their voice or after she hears a sound that reminds her of that
night—Meena finds herself thinking obsessively about why this happened to her, searching for a
meaning that never arrives. She has also begun to go along with whatever others want, agreeing to
extra shifts, staying late, never saying no. She has come to believe that if she keeps everyone
around her happy, she will be safe. But this people-pleasing only heightens her sense of being out
of control, which makes her scan even more vigilantly, which in turn makes the memories more vivid
and frequent.

The cycle has worn her down. She rarely talks about the event with anyone, but when she does—when a
coworker asks what happened that night—the act of speaking about it triggers the memory so
powerfully that she becomes dizzy, lightheaded, her vision swimming. She has started crying at
unpredictable moments, sometimes in the break room, sometimes at home while watching television, and
she cannot explain why. The dizziness and the crying have become additional triggers, so that now
even her own emotional and physical responses can send her back into the fragmented, grief-soaked
memory.

Meena's husband has noticed the change. She used to be warm and talkative; now she is watchful and
withdrawn. She has stopped visiting her sister, who lives in the city, because the drive feels too
exposed. At work, she has been written up twice for leaving her station without explanation—she had
been overwhelmed by a sudden memory and needed to hide in the bathroom until it passed. She is
terrified that she will lose her job, but she is more terrified that something will happen again and
she will freeze, unable to protect herself or anyone else. What finally brought her to seek help was
a moment last week when her husband found her sobbing in the laundry room, unable to say why. She
looked at him and said, "I think I'm losing my mind." She is afraid that is true. But somewhere
underneath that fear is a quieter hope: that someone can help her make sense of the fragments, and
that the grief might one day stop arriving without warning.
