proposal for the upcoming school year. He remembered the sudden, catastrophic pressure behind his

own body becoming a trap he could not escape. He had been alone. His wife, Lena, had died three
years earlier from ovarian cancer, and in that moment, as he lay on the floor unable to move his
left side, he was certain he was following her. The emergency surgery saved his life, but it did not
save the man he had been.

Now, fifteen months later, Marcus could not deliberately recall the details of that night. When he
tried, the memory slipped away like water through his fingers, leaving only a frustrating blank. Yet
the emotional weight of it would hit him without warning—a wave of pure, animal terror that surged
through him in the middle of a staff meeting or while he was standing in the grocery store, with no
image attached, only the feeling of his heart trying to escape his chest. Worse still were the
bodily reenactments: a sudden stiffness in his left arm, a feeling that his head was being tilted
back, and he would be back on that cold floor, paralyzed and waiting to die.

The world had become a landscape of traps. A whiff of hand sanitizer in the hallway at school could
send him reeling, the antiseptic smell instantly conjuring the ICU. The sharp, sweet scent of
alcohol from a parent’s breath at a conference made his stomach clench, a visceral reminder of the
night he had been told he might not wake up. He began to dread his own annual physical, the cold
stethoscope and the probing questions feeling like a prelude to another catastrophe. These triggers
did not just disturb him; they confirmed a terrible new truth. He had come to believe that his own
body was fundamentally unreliable, a ticking bomb he could no longer trust. The person he saw in the
mirror—this hypervigilant, trembling man who flinched at a cough—was unrecognizable to him. He was
certain he could not rely on himself to handle even a minor medical appointment, let alone another
crisis.

cancelling dinners and ignoring calls, because every conversation felt like a minefield where
someone might ask how he was doing. Before any interaction, whether with a teacher or a district

head until it felt safe. He isolated himself completely, spending his evenings alone in his house,
the silence a relief from the terror of unexpected questions. He refused to enter a hospital for any
reason, even to visit a sick colleague, and he cancelled his annual physical three times. These
strategies offered him a brittle sense of control, but they deepened his belief that he was a broken
man. The more he isolated, the more he felt like a stranger to himself, and the more he rehearsed,
the more he confirmed that he could not trust his own spontaneous responses.

The isolation also starved him of the very social support that might have helped his memory settle.

powerful. Each sudden, image-less wave of panic or bodily reenactment further cemented his sense of
being fundamentally damaged, a man who had lost his own autobiography. This, in turn, made him even
more vigilant, scanning every room for the smell of antiseptic, every conversation for a medical
reference. The cycle tightened until his world had shrunk to the size of his house, his job, and a
narrow, rehearsed path between them.

failure. During a fire drill at school, a first-grader had scraped her knee. The sight of the blood,

the entire student body. He had frozen, unable to speak, his left arm stiffening as he felt himself
back on that floor. The school board insisted he take a leave of absence. Sitting in the waiting
room, he was terrified—not of the therapy itself, but of what it might unearth. He was most afraid
that if he tried to remember that night deliberately, the memory would finally surface, and it would
destroy whatever was left of him.
