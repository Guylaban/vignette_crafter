years later, now a 65-year-old widow living alone in a quiet suburb, she cannot shake the feeling
that the world is a place where disaster waits around every corner.

sedan’s rear bumper, or the screech of tires—each one yanks her back into that moment. When she
hears an unexpected loud sound, like a car backfiring or a truck’s air brake, her entire body snaps
to attention. Her heart hammers, her palms sweat, and she feels a cold certainty that another
collision is imminent. This constant vigilance has left her physically exhausted; her body feels
perpetually tense, as if bracing for a blow that never lands. The worst episodes are the flashbacks,
which strike without warning. While standing in her own kitchen, the sound of a dish breaking will
suddenly transport her back to the Humvee, the smell of gasoline filling her nostrils, and for a
terrifying few seconds she is no longer in her home but trapped in that crumpled vehicle, reliving
the impact as if it were happening right now.

To escape this, Margaret has developed a set of habits that she knows, in her quieter moments, are
not healthy. When the anxiety becomes unbearable, she eats—not out of hunger, but to fill the hollow

barely tasting it, just trying to drown out the racing thoughts. At other times, she throws herself
into distraction: turning on the television at high volume, calling her daughter for a meandering

tries to push the crash away, the more she finds herself scanning for similar cars on the road, a
compulsive vigilance that only creates new triggers.

makes her more irritable and less likely to go out, which means she spends more time alone with her
thoughts. The distraction works only briefly; as soon as the television is off or the phone call
ends, the silence rushes in, and she is once again on high alert. Last week, she tried to suppress a
