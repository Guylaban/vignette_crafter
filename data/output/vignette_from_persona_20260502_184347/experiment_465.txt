Elena is a 39-year-old Latina woman who works as a program coordinator for a community non-profit in
a mid-sized city. She is married and has two children. Two years ago, she was assaulted in her own
home. A man broke in through the back door late one evening while she was alone, holding a kitchen
knife he had grabbed from her own block. He forced her to undress at knifepoint, then beat her and
raped her. She has never been able to put the sequence of events in order—the moments blur together,
and she cannot say whether the beating came before or after the rape, or how long it lasted. When
she tries to describe what happened, she finds herself unable to find words, her throat tightening
as if the memory itself has stolen her voice. What she feels most sharply when she recalls the
assault is not fear but rage—a burning, helpless fury that leaves her shaking and exhausted for
hours afterward.

Elena now lives in a state of constant watchfulness. She startles at the slightest unexpected
sound—a car backfiring, a door slamming, a dropped pan in the kitchen. She has come to believe that
she is a weak person, that someone else would not have gotten into this situation, that she should
have been able to prevent it. These thoughts surface every time she sees a knife in her own kitchen,
or a man holding any object that could be used as a weapon—a screwdriver, a heavy flashlight, even a
long metal spoon. Each such sighting reinforces her belief that she failed, and that failure
confirms her weakness. She finds it impossible to feel safe even when her husband tells her the
doors are locked and the alarm is set. At night, her vigilance sharpens unbearably; she lies awake
listening for footsteps, certain that danger is closer in the dark.

The connection between seeing a weapon and her sense of shame has become automatic. When she catches
a glimpse of a kitchen knife, she does not just feel afraid—she feels again the certainty that she
is the kind of person this happens to, that she lacked the strength or cleverness to fight back.
This appraisal, in turn, sends her scanning her environment for further threats, unable to trust any
reassurance. She has stopped cooking with knives altogether, switching to pre-chopped vegetables and
takeout, because the sight of a blade triggers such a loop of self-contempt and hypervigilance that
she cannot function.

Her memory of the assault intrudes unpredictably, and when it does, it brings not only rage but a
fresh wave of self-blame. She wonders why she cannot remember the order of events—whether that
confusion itself is a sign of weakness, a failure to stay present and fight. These intrusions have
made her avoid her own kitchen entirely after dark. She now eats dinner in the living room with the
lights on, and she has asked her husband to lock all knives in a drawer she never opens.

Elena’s work at the non-profit has suffered. She coordinates community outreach events, many of
which take place in the evenings. She has begun declining evening shifts, making excuses about
family obligations, because the thought of being out after dark—or walking to her car in an empty
parking lot—feels impossible. Her supervisor has noticed the pattern and has expressed concern, but
Elena cannot bring herself to explain. She has lost touch with the community she once served with
pride; she no longer attends the cultural festivals or neighborhood gatherings that used to ground
her. She feels cut off from her own heritage, from the people who once felt like family. She has
come to believe that the assault stole not just her sense of safety but her place in the world.

What finally brought Elena to seek help was a morning last week when she saw a butter knife on the
counter and began shaking uncontrollably, unable to enter the kitchen for over an hour. Her husband
found her sitting on the bedroom floor, crying with rage and shame. She agreed to come to therapy
because she is afraid that if she does not, she will lose her job, her marriage, and whatever is
left of herself. What frightens her most is the thought that she may never feel at home in her own
life again.
