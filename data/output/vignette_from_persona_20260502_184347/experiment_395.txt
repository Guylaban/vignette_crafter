The assault happened in the prison’s east wing during a lockdown. A younger guard had radioed for
backup, and by the time Nadia arrived, the inmate had already slammed the guard’s head against the
concrete floor. She was fifty-six then, still quick, still sure of her body. She pulled the inmate
off, pinned him, and held him until the response team came. But in the days that followed, what
stayed with her was not the struggle itself—it was the thought that she should have been faster,
should have seen it coming, should have protected that young guard. She had been a prison guard for
thirty-two years, and she had never failed before. Now she believed she had failed the one time it
mattered. That belief settled into her bones: she could not be trusted to keep anyone safe, and
because of that, she was no longer part of the world that trusted itself. She was an outsider now,
even among her own colleagues.

In the months after the assault, Nadia began to feel the danger before she could name it. Her body
would react—her stomach would clench, her pulse would spike—and only then would she understand that
a door had slammed too hard or that a hallway had gone quiet. The silence itself became a signal.
She would be walking through the prison’s administrative corridor, and the sudden absence of noise
would make her stop, her breath shallow, her hands cold. She had come to believe that silence meant
something was about to happen, that the world was holding its breath before it struck. This was not
a thought she could argue with. It was a feeling that arrived in her chest before her mind could
catch up, and it was always right.

To manage this, Nadia began to change how she worked. She started checking her surroundings
constantly—scanning doorways, watching reflections in glass, noting the position of every officer
and every inmate. She also began to drop responsibilities that once felt routine. She stopped
volunteering for lockdown duty. She avoided the east wing entirely, even though that meant taking
longer routes and leaving her usual post unattended. She told herself this was reasonable, that she
was being careful, but she also knew, in a quieter part of her mind, that she was giving up the
things that made her good at her job. And that made her feel even more like an outsider—like someone
who no longer belonged in the uniform she had worn for three decades.

The checking and the avoidance did not calm her. They made her hypervigilance worse. The more she
scanned, the more she found to fear. The more she avoided, the more she believed she was too weak to
handle the job. Her sleep suffered. She would lie awake at night, replaying the assault, imagining
what else she could have done, and then she would be exhausted the next day, which made her feel
even less capable. She began to socialise constantly—not because she wanted company, but because
being alone with her thoughts felt unbearable. She would stay in the staff break room during her
breaks, talking to anyone who would listen, even when she had nothing to say. She would call her
daughter every evening and keep her on the phone for an hour, just to fill the silence. But after
each conversation, she felt a new wave of shame. She was using people to keep herself safe, and that
made her feel even more untrustworthy, even more separate from them.

What frightened Nadia most was the loop she could not escape. She believed she had failed, so she
scanned for danger, and the scanning made her feel more threatened, which confirmed that she was
failing again, which made her avoid more, which made her feel even more like an outsider. By the
time she came to the clinic, she had been sleeping three hours a night for six weeks. She had

simple: she wanted to stop feeling like the world was about to break open, and she wanted to
believe, just once, that she could still protect someone.
