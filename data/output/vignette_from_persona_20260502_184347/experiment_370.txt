his chair, hands clasped, and described a night eight months ago when he woke in a hospital bed with

emergency room, the bright lights, the sharp smell of antiseptic, and the sight of a nurse inserting
a central line into his neck. He had been certain he was dying. The weeks that followed were a blur
of IV antibiotics, daily blood draws, and the constant hum of monitors. What stayed with him,
though, was the moment he looked down at his own arm and saw the bruises from the needles, and felt
that his body had become something foreign, something that had turned against him.

Leo returned to work at a community counseling center three months after his discharge. He was proud

visit would make his chest tighten. The faint scent of hand sanitizer in the hallway would trigger a
wave of nausea and a racing heart. He began to avoid thinking about anything discussed in therapy
sessions that touched on physical health, and he found himself staying up until two or three in the
morning, delaying sleep because the moment he closed his eyes, the memory of the ventilator would

state of hypervigilance, his body bracing for a threat that wasn't there. He started to avoid
hospitals entirely, driving an extra twenty minutes to work to bypass the one near his apartment,
and he cancelled his annual physical, telling himself he would reschedule later.

The deeper problem was what Leo had come to believe about himself. He could no longer trust his own
body. Every unexpected ache, every minor cough, felt like the beginning of another catastrophe. His

palms would sweat, and he would flinch at sudden movements, like a coworker approaching his desk
from the side. This physical reactivity made him feel that his body was an enemy, something that
could betray him again at any moment. Nothing in his life felt meaningful anymore. He used to find
