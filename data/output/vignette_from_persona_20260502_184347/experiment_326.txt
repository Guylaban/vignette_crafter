six-week relief deployment after a humanitarian crisis in a neighboring region. He had been trained
to run toward danger, to pull people from rubble, to triage the wounded in the chaos of collapsed
buildings. What he had not been trained for was the quiet that followed. After the shelling stopped,
he helped dig bodies from a schoolyard. He held a child whose legs were gone. He watched a
man—another relief worker—turn away from a dying woman because she was from the wrong village. That
moment, the moral violation of it, lodged in Kiet’s chest like a piece of shrapnel he could not feel
but knew was there. He came home, returned to his station, tried to resume his life. His marriage,
already strained, dissolved within two years. He told no one what he had seen.

Now, at forty-six, Kiet lives alone in a small apartment near the firehouse. He works the overnight
shift when he can, because the silence of the day is unbearable. When the station is quiet between
calls, he feels a pressure building in his ears, a certainty that something dangerous is about to
happen. He has come to believe that silence itself is a threat, a prelude to catastrophe. This
belief began during the deployment, when the quiet before an attack was always the worst part, but
it has since attached itself to every still moment in his life. At the grocery store, in the hallway
of his building, during the lull between radio transmissions—he scans for exits, for signs of
trouble, for the sound of a bang that will confirm his dread.

The bang itself is the worst trigger. A car backfiring, a door slamming, a dropped pan in the
station kitchen—each one sends a jolt of physical pain through his ribs and his left arm, exactly
where he felt the shockwave during the explosion that leveled the schoolyard. The memory returns
with the pain, but it is never in order. He sees the dust, then the child’s face, then the
overturned desk, then the man who walked away—but he cannot sequence them. He knows what happened,
but when he tries to describe it to the department psychologist, the words dissolve. He cannot find
a sentence that holds the truth of it. This inability to speak, to sequence, to make the memory obey
him, has convinced Kiet that he acted in ways that go against who he is. He believes his life has
been destroyed by that deployment. He feels he no longer belongs anywhere—not at the station, not
with his ex-wife, not among civilians who have never seen what he saw. He is certain that the moral
failure he witnessed, and his own helplessness in that moment, have made him unfit for any world he
once inhabited.

hurts himself physically to release the overwhelming feelings. He will press his knuckles into the

station. He has also developed mental rituals he must perform to prevent bad things from happening
again—counting the beats of his heart in sets of four, repeating a phrase under his breath before
opening a door, touching the same three surfaces in his locker before he can leave. He tries hard

rituals and the suppression, in turn, make the threat feel more real; when he cannot complete a
ritual, his anxiety spikes, and he becomes irritable, snapping at younger firefighters for minor

belief drives him to hurt himself that night, and the self-harm, briefly numbing, is followed by a

invitations to crew dinners. He no longer calls his brother. The silence in his apartment grows
deeper, and he is certain something terrible is about to happen again.

steel pan in the kitchen, and Kiet, without thinking, threw a chair across the room. The recruit was

else, that he will be asked to leave the only job he has ever loved, and that the silence that
follows will finally prove him right: that he belongs nowhere, not even here.
