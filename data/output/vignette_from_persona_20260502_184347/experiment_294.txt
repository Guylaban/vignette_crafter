Amina is a 63-year-old financial analyst who fled her home country during a period of ethnic
cleansing, an experience that fundamentally reshaped her understanding of safety and control. The
event itself unfolded over several days: soldiers in uniform arrived at her neighborhood, rounding
up families and forcing them into trucks at gunpoint. She was separated from her younger brother,
who was pulled from her grasp and never seen again. They were driven deep into a dense, humid forest
and left there without food or water, forced to walk for hours under the cover of thick canopy, the
sounds of distant gunfire and shouting echoing through the trees. For Amina, the loss of autonomy in
those moments—being herded, commanded, and utterly powerless—became the central wound. Now, decades
later, she lives a quiet, carefully managed life in a Midwestern city, working remotely as a
financial analyst for a small firm. Her apartment is her sanctuary, and she has built her daily
routine around avoiding anything that might trigger the visceral terror of that time.

This vigilance has shaped her world into a narrow, predictable loop. Any situation that might
involve disagreement or confrontation is immediately avoided; she has become expert at deflecting
conversations, agreeing with colleagues even when she disagrees, and withdrawing from social
invitations where opinions might clash. More acutely, she cannot tolerate being around people in
authority or uniform. A police car parked on her street, a security guard in a lobby, or even a mail
carrier in a dark blue shirt sends a spike of cold fear through her chest. She has come to believe
that anyone in a position of authority holds the power to take away her choices, to force her into
submission again. This fear has a direct, self-reinforcing consequence: the more she avoids these
triggers, the more her world shrinks, and the more she perceives authority figures and potential
conflicts as omnipresent threats. Her avoidance is not a solution but a confirmation that the world
is dangerous—every successful escape from a confrontation or a uniformed figure proves to her that
she was right to flee.

Amina’s daily life as a financial analyst, a role she performs with meticulous precision from her
home office, has become a stage for this cycle. She is terrified of phone calls with her boss, a man
with a commanding voice, and she will often let calls go to voicemail, spending hours drafting a
perfectly neutral email response to avoid a direct conversation. She has come to believe that any
disagreement, even a minor one over a spreadsheet figure, could escalate into a confrontation where
she will be humiliated or overpowered. This fear has concrete occupational consequences: she has
been passed over for a promotion twice, as her manager noted she seemed "unwilling to advocate for
her ideas" during team meetings. She worries obsessively about bad things happening to people she
cares about—particularly her adult daughter, who lives in another state. Every news report of a
traffic accident, a police shooting, or a natural disaster sends Amina into a spiral of catastrophic
imagining, and she will call her daughter multiple times a day until she hears her voice, convinced
that something terrible has occurred. This hypervigilance, this constant scanning for threat, means
she is rarely at rest. The natural environment itself can betray her; a weekend trip to a state
park, suggested by a well-meaning friend, became unbearable when the trail wound into a dense patch
of trees, the canopy overhead triggering a flashback so vivid she could smell the damp earth and
hear her brother’s cries. She has not left the city limits in over two years.

What finally brought Amina to seek help was a crisis of isolation. Her daughter, worried by her
mother’s increasing withdrawal, flew in and found her apartment a fortress—blackout curtains drawn,
a chair wedged under the doorknob, and a months-old pile of unopened mail. Amina admitted, in a
quiet voice, that she had not been to a grocery store in six weeks, subsisting on delivery orders,
because a new police substation had opened two blocks away. She is most afraid that seeking help
will mean surrendering the only control she has left, that a therapist will tell her she has to face
her fears and that doing so will destroy her. But she is also, beneath that terror, exhausted. She
wants to be able to see her granddaughter without panicking at the sight of a crossing guard. She
wants to walk outside without her heart hammering at the sound of a car door slamming. She is
hoping, perhaps for the first time in years, that there might be a way to feel safe without having
to hide.
