Maya is a 19-year-old dancer who lives alone in a small apartment near the studio where she trains
six days a week. Nine months ago, she was caught in a mass shooting at a crowded outdoor festival.
She had gone with friends to see a headliner, but when the gunfire started, the crowd surged and she
was knocked to the ground. For what felt like an eternity, she lay pinned beneath strangers, unable
to move, her face pressed into the dirt, the acrid smell of smoke and cordite filling her lungs. She
remembers the weight of bodies on her back, the screams, and the terrifying certainty that she was
going to die. She eventually crawled free, but the memory has never loosened its grip.

When Maya thinks about that night, the memory returns with a horrifying sensory completeness. She
can smell the smoke and the sharp, metallic tang of blood as if she were still lying on the ground,
and the physical sensation of being crushed and restrained is so vivid that she sometimes gasps and
tries to push the air away with her hands. Yet when she tries to describe what happened to her
therapist, she finds herself unable to find the words—the experience feels too vast and formless to
put into language, and she sits in silence, shaking her head. She has come to believe that if she
lets herself think about the event in any detail, she will not be able to handle it, and this belief
has led her to actively avoid her own memories. She is also haunted by a deep sense of injustice;
she watches news reports of the investigation stalling, and she feels that what happened to her was
profoundly unfair and that nothing has been done about it. This belief fuels her avoidance: she
tells herself there is no point in talking about it because no one will make it right.

In the months after the shooting, Maya began to notice changes in how she moved through the world.
She started avoiding going out, especially at night, and she gave up the pleasant activities she
used to enjoy—meeting friends for coffee, browsing at the weekend market. She found herself covering
her face with her hands whenever she walked past a police car or heard a siren, a reflexive gesture
she could not explain. These strategies, however, have only made things worse. The more she stays
home, the more exhausted she feels, and that exhaustion itself becomes a trigger: when she is tired,
her guard drops and the memory surges back with full sensory force. She now wakes during the night

day, she has difficulty concentrating; in dance class, she loses her place in the choreography, and
her instructor has noticed. She has also become hypervigilant to unexpected movement—when another
dancer rushes past her in the studio, she flinches hard, her heart pounding, as if the danger is
still present. This constant state of alertness leaves her drained, and the fatigue then loops back,
making her more vulnerable to intrusive memories and to feeling followed or tracked when she is
alone on the street. She is certain she is being watched, even when no one is there.

arriving late to rehearsals to avoid the crowded changing room, and she has stopped going out with

will never fade, and she is desperate to find a way back to the person she was before—the dancer who
moved freely, without checking every shadow.
