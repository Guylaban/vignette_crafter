Marco, a 28-year-old prison guard living in a small Texas border town, had always considered himself
steady—someone who could handle the pressure of the job without letting it follow him home. That
changed fourteen months ago, when his younger brother, Daniel, was killed in a drive-by shooting

time he got the call, Daniel was already gone. He had not been there to protect him, and that
thought had taken root in his chest like something living.

In the weeks after the funeral, Marco began to notice that certain things would pull him back into
that night with a force that felt physical. A news report about gang violence, even a brief mention
on the radio, would leave his hands shaking. When a coworker mentioned losing a cousin to gunfire,
Marco felt his mind go distant—the room around him seemed to become thin and unreal, as if he were
watching himself from somewhere far away. He had come to believe that these moments proved something
terrible about him: that he was no longer the person he used to be. He would think, *I used to be a
happy person, but now I am always miserable*, and the certainty that he had no future would settle
over him like a weight.

To cope, Marco started covering his face with his hands whenever a grief reminder appeared—an old
photograph, a song Daniel had liked, a conversation about someone losing a loved one. He would press
his palms against his eyes until the pressure drowned out the image. But the strategy never held.
The more he hid, the more convinced he became that he had failed his brother, that he was weak, that
he was broken beyond repair. And those appraisals, in turn, made the triggers feel more dangerous.

You'll never escape it.*

At work, Marco started picking arguments with other guards over small things—a schedule change, a

worse afterward. He would replay the exchange and tell himself that he was becoming someone his

creating a crisis of his own.
