Michael is a 34-year-old firefighter, married for eight years, who sought help after a year of
escalating distress following his deployment as a relief worker in a war-torn region. The event that
fractured his sense of safety happened on his third week there. He was part of a team distributing
supplies in a contested neighborhood when a mortar round struck a building fifty feet away. In the
chaos that followed, a young boy ran toward him, screaming, and Michael instinctively grabbed him
and pulled him behind a wall—only to watch a second round land exactly where the boy had been
standing. The boy survived, but Michael carried him to the aid station with blood streaming from a
shrapnel wound. In the months since returning home, that moment has never left him.

Back in his daily life, Michael finds himself unable to shake the hypervigilance that kept him alive
overseas. At the fire station, he sits with his back to the wall and scans the room for exits before
he can focus on a conversation. At home, he feels on guard even inside his own house, his eyes
darting to windows and doors whenever his wife moves through a room. Strangers approaching him in
ordinary situations—a cashier leaning forward to hand him change, a jogger passing on the
sidewalk—trigger a jolt of alertness that he cannot rationalize away. He has come to believe that
danger is always one misstep away, and that letting his guard down will cost someone their life.

To manage this constant sense of threat, Michael has developed a set of habits that have slowly
reshaped his life. He avoids exercise because the raised heart rate and breathlessness feel too
close to the panic he felt during the attack, and he worries that losing control of his body in that
way will make him vulnerable. He pushes away positive emotions, refusing to let himself feel joy or
contentment, because those feelings feel unsafe—he is certain that the moment he relaxes, something
terrible will happen. As soon as he enters any room or building, he scans for exits, and he makes
sure to stay close to one at all times, often choosing seats near doors even if it means sitting
apart from his crew. His wife has noticed that he no longer laughs at her jokes, and he has stopped
going to the gym with his colleagues, offering vague excuses about being tired.

These strategies, however, have only deepened his sense of threat. By avoiding exercise and positive
feelings, he has cut himself off from the very experiences that might have helped him recalibrate
his nervous system. Staying near exits and scanning rooms reinforces his belief that danger is
imminent, because each time he checks a door, he is reminding himself that he might need to flee.
The more he avoids, the more vigilant he becomes, and the more vigilant he becomes, the more he
feels the need to avoid. This cycle has tightened over the past year, leaving him exhausted and
isolated.

What finally brought Michael to seek help was a moment at the fire station last month. A new recruit
clapped him on the shoulder from behind, and Michael spun around with his fists raised, his heart
pounding, before he realized where he was. The look of shock on the young man's face stayed with
him. His wife had been urging him to see someone for months, but it was that moment—seeing himself
through another person's eyes, recognizing how far he had drifted from the man he used to be—that
made him pick up the phone. He is afraid of what he might become if this continues, but he is also
afraid that letting go of his vigilance will mean letting someone get hurt. He hopes therapy can
help him find a way to live without the constant weight of that fear.
