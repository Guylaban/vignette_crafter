Carlos is a seventy-year-old welder, a widower, who came to the United States from El Salvador forty
years ago. He sought help at the insistence of his daughter, who had grown alarmed by his increasing
isolation and the way he would sometimes freeze, mid-weld, his hands trembling as if he had heard
something no one else could hear. The event that haunts him happened in his early twenties, during
the Salvadoran Civil War. He was detained by a paramilitary squad and held for three days in a
small, windowless room. They beat him, burned his forearms with the tips of their cigarettes, and at
one point held a pistol to his temple and pulled the trigger on an empty chamber. They did this
twice. The third time, the gun fired, but the bullet passed through the fleshy part of his shoulder
rather than his skull. He survived because he had been compliant, had answered their questions, had
not fought back. That is what he tells himself. He was compliant, and that is why he lives. But he
also tells himself something else: that he should have done more, that he should have tried to
escape or fight, that his survival was a kind of cowardice.

The memory of that room does not feel like a memory. When it comes, it comes as a complete sensory
immersion: the smell of sweat and diesel, the pressure of the wall against his back, the cold metal
of the gun barrel against his temple. He cannot describe it to his daughter without losing the
thread, his sentences breaking apart, his hands beginning to shake as if the event were still
happening. And when the memory is present, it is not the fear that overwhelms him first—it is the
rage. A hot, wordless fury at the men, at himself, at the world that let it happen. He feels it in
his chest, a pressure that demands release, but he has nowhere to put it. So he pushes the memory
away. He tries hard not to think about it. He tells himself it is over, it is in the past, but the
memory does not listen.

The triggers are everywhere, though he did not always recognize them. A sudden loud noise—a car
backfiring, a dropped metal beam in the workshop—will send him back into that room. But the most
insidious trigger is the feeling of entrapment, of having no options. It happens when he is stuck in
traffic, when a door closes behind him in a small bathroom, when his boss gives him an order he
cannot refuse. In those moments, the present dissolves and he is twenty-three again, helpless. And
each time that happens, his old belief hardens further: you can never know who will harm you. He has
come to see threat in ordinary faces, in the casual authority of a foreman, in the raised voice of a
stranger. The world, he is certain, is full of people waiting for him to be weak.

When he feels threatened, his body remembers the solution that saved him once: compliance. He
becomes overly agreeable, his voice softening, his shoulders rounding, his answers becoming what he
thinks the other person wants to hear. He does this automatically, even with his daughter, even with
his doctor. He hates himself for it. He thinks, I will never forgive myself for not doing more to
stop what happened, and then he thinks, The event happened because of the way I acted. These
appraisals loop together, feeding each other, and they drive him back into the same compliant
posture. The more he blames himself, the more he expects danger, and the more he expects danger, the
more he scans for it. A raised voice in the grocery store. A man walking too close behind him. Each
perceived threat triggers the memory, and the memory triggers the rage, and the rage triggers the
urge to suppress, and the suppression leaves him exhausted and hollow.

Over the months, his world has narrowed. He no longer goes to the hardware store on weekends. He
avoids the freeway during rush hour. At work, he has started taking longer breaks in the supply
closet—a small, windowless space that should feel like a trap but somehow feels safer because he
chose it. His daughter has noticed that he flinches when she knocks on his door. He does not tell
her about the nights when he lies awake, the gunshot sound echoing in his ears, the rage building
until he has to get up and walk around the block. He does not tell her that sometimes, in the middle
of a weld, he feels the heat on his forearms and has to stop because his hands are shaking too much
to hold the torch steady.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a moment at work last month. His foreman, a heavyset man
with a loud voice, asked him to redo a weld. Carlos said yes. He said yes too quickly, too softly,
and the foreman looked at him strangely. Later that day, Carlos found himself in the supply closet,
his back against the wall, his heart pounding, the memory so vivid he could smell the diesel. He
stayed there for twenty minutes. When he came out, his supervisor asked if he was okay, and Carlos
said yes again, the same compliant yes, and he hated himself so much that he called his daughter
that night. He is afraid of what he might do if the rage ever breaks through. He is afraid that one
day he will not be able to push the memory away. He is hoping, though he cannot quite say it, that
someone will tell him it was not his fault.
