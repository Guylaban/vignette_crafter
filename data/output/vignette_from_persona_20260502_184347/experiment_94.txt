Farouk is a 55-year-old farmer who lives with his wife on the outskirts of a small agricultural town
in California’s Central Valley. He was attacked almost two years ago now, just after sunset, while
locking up a storage shed at the edge of his property. He had noticed a truck idling on the access
road, but told himself it was nothing. Then two men were on him—one grabbed his collar, the other
punched him in the ribs and face. He remembers the smell of sweat and cigarettes, the weight of a
boot on his chest while they emptied his pockets, and then nothing. The next thing he knew, he was
on his back staring at the stars, his jaw aching, his shirt torn. He has never been able to explain
what happened in between. When he tries to tell the story to his wife or to the sheriff’s deputy who
took the report, the thread comes apart in his hands—he loses the sequence, stumbles over missing
minutes, and eventually stops, frustrated. The memory feels foreign, like a scene from someone
else’s life, and what pieces he does have arrive in disconnected flashes: the smell, the pressure on
his ribs, the gravel against his cheek. None of it fits together into a story he can hold.

In the weeks after the assault, Farouk found he could not stop thinking about the men who had done
this. He would replay the same fragment—the boot on his chest—and then his mind would turn to how he
might find them, what he would do if he did. He spent whole evenings imagining scenarios: tracking
the truck, catching one of them alone, making them feel what he had felt. The revenge fantasies gave
him a strange, bitter energy, but they also kept the event alive in his mind, replaying it again and
again without resolution. At the same time, he started throwing himself into work with a ferocity
his wife had never seen. He rose before dawn, stayed in the fields past dark, took on repairs he had
been putting off for years. It was the only way he could quiet his mind—if he was exhausted enough,
the fragments would not surface when he lay down. But the harder he worked, the more his heart would
pound in his chest, and that pounding itself became a trigger. He would be shovelling compost or
fixing an irrigation line and feel his heart race from exertion, and suddenly he was back on the
ground, the boot pressing down, and the smell of sweat—his own or someone else’s, he could no longer
tell—would flood his senses. Each time this happened, he would stop what he was doing, breathe hard,
and then pick up his tools again, working even faster to push the memory down.

He began to avoid the anniversary of the attack entirely, making sure he was deep in the fields on
that date with no radio and no reminders. He also started to notice people more—anyone who moved
differently, any truck that slowed near his property. A stranger at the feed store who lingered too
long near the register, a car that circled the block twice, a neighbour’s son who wore a cap pulled
low—all of it felt loaded with threat. He had come to believe that the world was full of people
waiting for a moment of weakness, and that his only safety lay in constant vigilance and constant
labour.

Over the months, the cycles tightened. His heart would race, and the memory fragments would surface;
the fragments would feed his need to ruminate about revenge, which would exhaust him, which would
make him work harder, which would make his heart race again. His wife began to notice that he was
not sleeping, that he flinched when she touched his shoulder from behind, that he no longer sat with
her in the evenings. She asked him to see a doctor. He refused at first, but last week, after his
heart pounded so hard during a routine walk through the almond rows that he had to sit down in the
dirt, he finally agreed. He is not sure what he expects from therapy, but he knows he cannot keep
living this way—haunted by a story he cannot tell, working himself toward collapse, and watching his
wife’s worried face grow more familiar than his own reflection.
