Priya was forty-one when she nearly died from septic shock following a routine dental procedure. The
infection had been fast and silent; one evening she felt a little tired, and by the next morning she
could not catch her breath. In the emergency room she remembers the antiseptic smell hitting her
before she even saw a bed, remembers the way the ceiling tiles blurred as they wheeled her down a
corridor, remembers a nurse saying her blood pressure was dropping. What she remembers most vividly
is the moment she realized her own lungs would not obey her—that she was trying to draw air and
nothing was happening. She was conscious for most of it, and she was certain she was going to die
alone on a gurney while strangers in blue scrubs moved around her. She survived, but something in
her understanding of the world did not.

Four years later, Priya still works as a plumber, but her relationship to her body has fundamentally
changed. She used to take pride in her physical stamina—crawling under houses, lifting pipe
sections, working twelve-hour days without complaint. Now she has come to believe that her body is
fundamentally unreliable, that it can betray her without warning. This conviction crystallized
during a minor cold last winter. She felt a familiar tightness in her chest and immediately became
convinced she was septic again. She spent the afternoon checking her temperature, timing her
breaths, and searching online for signs of sepsis. The more she monitored her body, the more she
noticed small sensations she would have ignored before—a flicker of dizziness, a moment of
breathlessness—and each one felt like proof that her body was failing her again. She began to avoid
anything that might put her under physical stress. She stopped taking stairs. She started turning
down jobs that required extended periods of bending or lifting. She told herself she was being
sensible, but underneath that she was terrified that if she pushed her body, it would collapse.

The dizziness and breathlessness that had once been fleeting now arrived whenever she encountered a
hospital smell. A patient leaving a pharmacy ahead of her, the antiseptic waft from a doctor's
office door held open for someone—these were enough to make her lightheaded, and each time she felt
that spin in her head, she interpreted it as her body starting to fail. She had come to believe that
she could not rely on other people to keep her safe; the dentist had said everything was fine, and
she had nearly died. So she stopped going to doctors altogether. She avoided medical appointments,
refused physical exams, and when a routine plumbing job took her to a hospital basement last year,
she walked off the site and told her supervisor she had a migraine. She was certain that if she let
anyone check her vital signs, they would find something wrong, and she would be back on that gurney.

What frightened her most was the unpredictability of it all. She had developed a constant vigilance
that she described as "always watching for the next thing." She scanned her body for signals,
scanned her environment for triggers, and the scanning itself kept her on edge. The smell of
antiseptic could send her into a spiral of hyperawareness that lasted hours. She avoided hospitals,
yes, but also avoided the neighborhoods near hospitals, avoided television shows with medical
scenes, avoided conversations about health. This vigilance had become exhausting, but she could not
imagine letting it go—because letting it go, she believed, meant letting herself be caught off guard
again. She knew, intellectually, that she could not live like this forever. But what finally brought
her to seek help was not the exhaustion. It was the moment she realized she had stopped answering
calls from her sister, because her sister had recently started nursing school and Priya could not
bear to hear about it. She was losing not just her peace of mind, but the people she loved. She came
to therapy afraid that she was broken beyond repair, but also—beneath that—afraid that she might be
right.
