emergency room of a major metropolitan hospital. He had seen everything—gunshot wounds, car
accidents, the aftermath of domestic violence—and he had always prided himself on his composure. But
nothing had prepared him for the morning of the bombing. He had been walking to the bus stop, as he
did every Tuesday, when the first explosion tore through the crowded terminal. The sound was not
like anything in the OR; it was a physical force that threw him against a concrete pillar. In the
chaos that followed, he remembered the shouting—authority figures, police officers, screaming for
everyone to stay down, to not move, to get back. He had lain there, pinned by debris and the weight
of strangers, unable to do anything but listen to the orders and the screams. He had always been the
one giving orders, the one in control. In that moment, he was just a body on the ground, waiting to
see if the next blast would find him.

In the months that followed, Marcus found himself unable to shake the feeling that the world had
fundamentally changed. He had come to believe that the city he loved was a dangerous place, that
beneath the ordinary surface of things, violence could erupt at any moment. This belief colored
everything. When he saw a news report about a violent incident, even one far away, his mind would
immediately race back to the bombing, and he would feel the old terror rising. The world is not
safe, he would think. This is what it really is. These triggers—images of violence, even on a
screen—would reignite his sense that his life had been destroyed, that the peace he had known was an
illusion. He began to distrust the people around him, too. Patients, colleagues, even his wife of
forty years—he would catch himself studying their faces, wondering what they might be capable of.
People are not what they seem, he would mutter under his breath, and the thought would make him feel
more isolated, more certain that he could no longer rely on anyone.

This pervasive sense of danger drove him into a state of constant vigilance. He had always been a
light sleeper, but now he would lie awake at night, listening for sounds that might mean someone was
entering his home. Every creak of the floorboards, every gust of wind against the window, became a
potential threat. He felt on guard even inside his own home, a place that had once been his
sanctuary. At work, where he had returned to a part-time administrative role, his irritability grew.
He snapped at a nurse who asked him a routine question, and later, he could not explain why. The
anger felt like it came from nowhere, but he knew, deep down, that it came from the constant
pressure of being ready for the next attack. The more he scanned for danger, the more he found
evidence that the world was hostile, and the more he retreated into his own guarded silence. It was
a loop that tightened around him: every trigger confirmed his belief that the world was dangerous,
and that belief made him more vigilant, and that vigilance made him more susceptible to the next
trigger.

supervisor—"Marcus, could you review these charts by three?"—would send a jolt of adrenaline through
him. He would hear the tone of authority and be back on the ground, pinned by debris, a disembodied
voice screaming at him to stay down. The memory was not just visual; it was visceral, a full-body
response that left him trembling. He began to avoid situations where he might be directed. He took a
leave of absence from the hospital, citing exhaustion. He stopped taking the bus entirely, driving
everywhere instead, even though it added an hour to his commute. He had come to believe that if he
could just control his environment, he could keep the fear at bay. But the fear did not stay at bay.

His wife, Elena, noticed the change. He no longer wanted to go to their favorite restaurant, a

his body tense, ready to move. She tried to reassure him, but he could not accept it. *She doesn't
understand*, he thought. *She wasn't there.* The distance between them grew. He spent more time in
his study, reading old medical journals, listening for sounds. He had come to believe that his life
had been destroyed, and in a way, it had been—not by the bomb itself, but by the fear that followed.
He was no longer the man he had been.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a moment of rupture. He was driving home from a grocery

interpreted it as a command, a threat. He pulled over, but his hands were shaking so badly he could

speak. He sat there, staring straight ahead, until the officer let him go. That night, he told Elena

beneath the surface, afraid that the world he had built for himself was gone for good. He did not
know if therapy could bring it back, but he knew he could not go on like this.
