decisions under impossible pressure. He is married, with two adult children. The event that
fractured him was not an explosion but a hospital room. Five years ago, his youngest son, then
twelve, was diagnosed with a rare, aggressive form of leukemia. For six months, Rajesh watched his
son endure chemotherapy, his small body ravaged by treatments, the air in the pediatric oncology
ward thick with the sterile, chemical smell of antiseptic and the quiet hum of machines. He
remembers the exact moment the doctor told him the treatment might not work—the way the fluorescent
lights buzzed, the cold plastic of the chair beneath him. That memory is not a story he tells; it is
a series of vivid, uninvited pictures that slam into him without warning. A flash of the IV line,
the beige linoleum floor, the sound of a child crying down the hall. It all comes back, full sensory
reliving, as if he is right there again, his son’s hand limp in his own.

Today, the triggers are everywhere. The sharp smell of rubbing alcohol in a public restroom can stop
him cold. A child’s laughter in a grocery store, a glimpse of a syringe in a doctor’s office
window—these things instantly bring back the full memory, the helplessness of that room. He has come
to believe that his reactions since the event mean he is going crazy. A strong man, a man who
defuses bombs for a living, should not be undone by a scent. Each time the memory ambushes him, his
heart hammers, his palms sweat, and he feels a crushing wave of fear and shame. He tells himself he
is a weak person. This belief deepens with every trigger. When he catches the antiseptic smell in

and he immediately thinks, *I can’t even handle this. I’m losing my mind.* That negative
appraisal—that he is weak, that he is going crazy—makes him more vigilant. He starts scanning his
environment for the smell, for any reminder, so that he can brace himself. This hypervigilance, in
turn, makes him more likely to notice the triggers. He finds himself avoiding the break room near
the decon unit, a habit that has drawn quiet questions from his younger colleagues.

The strongest cycle is between his memory and his sense of helplessness. The triggered, full-sensory

right—that he is going crazy, that he is weak, and that nothing he does will ever stop the bad
things from happening. He hopes, quietly, that someone can prove him wrong.
