At seventy-two, Tranh had spent decades moving toward danger. As a refugee aid worker, he had walked
into bombed-out villages, negotiated with armed militias, and pulled survivors from rubble. But
nothing in those years prepared him for the morning the market square in his own city became a war
zone. He had been buying fruit when the first explosion came from the café, a sound so close it
seemed to enter his chest before his ears registered it. Then the second blast, closer still, and
the screaming, and the smell of dust and something else he could not name. He had pressed himself
against a concrete planter, watching pieces of a metal chair spin through the air, and he had
thought, with terrible clarity, *this is how it ends*. He survived physically unscathed, but
something in him had been torn loose.

In the months that followed, Tranh found that the memory refused to settle. It arrived not as a
story he could tell, but as images that seized him without warning: the red of a child’s sandal
lying alone in the dust, the white of a man’s eyes as he fell. Sometimes he heard it too—the
particular pitch of the first blast, a sound that seemed to vibrate behind his teeth. The memory
felt so extreme, so distant from the ordinary life he had once known, that he sometimes struggled to
believe it had actually happened to him. He would catch himself staring at his own hands, wondering
if they belonged to the same man who had been buying fruit that morning. When these images and
sounds surfaced, he found himself replaying what he could have done differently—if he had walked
faster, if he had warned someone, if he had chosen a different stall. He blamed himself for not
seeing it coming, for being in the wrong place, for surviving when others did not. The self-blame
became a loop he could not exit: the memory triggered the rumination, and the rumination made the
memory feel more vivid, more present, more impossible to escape.

The memories also changed how he moved through the world. Every ordinary sound now carried the
potential of that first blast. A car backfiring, a door slamming, a dropped tray in a
restaurant—each one sent a jolt through his body, his heart hammering before his mind could catch
up. He became hypervigilant in ways that exhausted him, scanning every street corner, every rooftop,
every parked vehicle for signs of threat. The images from the market square would flash through his
mind in these moments, as if his brain were reminding him what could happen, what had happened, what
would happen again if he let his guard down. He came to believe that safety was an illusion, that
danger was always waiting, and that only a very few places—his bedroom, his wife’s car, the back
office of the aid agency—offered any real protection.

Tranh stopped driving. Being a passenger was worse; he would grip the door handle, brace for impact,
and find himself scanning the road for abandoned bags or parked vans. He also began avoiding any
situation that might involve disagreement or confrontation—he stopped visiting his brother, stopped
attending community meetings, stopped arguing with his wife about even small things. Conflict, he
had come to believe, could escalate unpredictably, and he could not afford to be caught off guard
again. His wife noticed. She asked why he would not talk to her about the bills, about their son’s
visit, about anything. He told her he was just tired. But what he could not say was that every
conversation felt like a minefield, and that the memory of the market square made him certain he
would not survive another explosion—whether of sound or of anger.
