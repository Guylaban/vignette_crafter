Mai is a 38-year-old stock broker of South Eastern Asian descent who lives with her partner in a
quiet suburban neighborhood. She came to therapy after a year of escalating distress, unable to
explain why her life had become so small.

The stalking began two years ago, when a former client she had briefly dated started appearing
outside her office, then at her gym, then at the grocery store she visited on Saturday mornings. He
left notes on her car windshield, called her phone from blocked numbers, and once, when she came
home late, she found him standing in the shadows of her apartment building’s parking garage, reeking
of alcohol. The police were involved, but he was never charged—he had plausible deniability, he was
always just *there*, never quite threatening enough to act on. The night that broke her, he followed
her car for twelve miles, and when she finally pulled into a well-lit gas station, he rolled down
his window and smiled. She could smell the whiskey on his breath from ten feet away. She drove home
shaking, locked every door, and did not sleep.

What stayed with Mai was not the sequence of events but the feeling that the memory itself could not
be real—that it was too extreme, too cinematic to have actually happened to her. Yet when she closed
her eyes, she was back in that gas station, the terror flooding her chest as if it were happening
right now. The smell of alcohol, even a faint whiff from a colleague’s breath after lunch, snapped
her into that parking lot. She would freeze at her desk, her heart hammering, the keyboard blurring
in front of her. The memory felt so vivid, so present, that it made the world around her feel
thin—and then any loud noise, a car backfiring or a door slamming in the hallway, would send her
spinning back into that night, the sound merging with the memory of his car engine idling behind
her.

Over the months, Mai began to organize her life around avoiding these triggers. She stopped going to
the gym and instead exercised at home in silence. She asked her partner to do all the grocery
shopping, and she stopped meeting friends at bars or restaurants where alcohol was served. At work,
she moved her desk away from the breakroom, where the coffee machine sometimes hissed and where
someone might bring in wine for a celebration. She had come to believe that the world was full of
things that could pull her back into that memory, and that if she was not vigilant, she would be
trapped there forever. What frightened her most was that the memory might never stop feeling
real—that she would always be that woman in the gas station, helpless and alone.

The loop tightened. The more she avoided, the more her brain treated every loud noise, every happy
family she passed on the street (because his family had seemed so normal, so loving, and that had
been part of the trap), every whiff of alcohol as a threat. Her partner grew frustrated—he wanted to
go out, to see friends, to have a normal life. Mai knew she was pulling away, but she was certain
that if she let her guard down, the memory would swallow her whole. She finally sought help after a
colleague slammed a filing cabinet and she burst into tears at her desk, unable to explain why. What
she hopes, more than anything, is that she can learn to live in the present without the past always
leaking in.
