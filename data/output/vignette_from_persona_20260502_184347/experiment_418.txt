had to call his wife.

In the weeks that followed, Maya found that she could not stop seeing it. The memory was not a story
she told herself but a film that played over and over, frame by frame, as vivid as the photographs
she had studied in her pre-flight briefings. She would be sitting in the mess hall, and something in
her surroundings—the particular angle of the afternoon sun through a window, the sound of a jet
engine throttling up on the tarmac—would instantly bring the full memory back without warning, and
she would be there again, circling, waiting, knowing. These unbidden replays made her feel that
there was something fundamentally wrong with her as a person, a defect she could not name but could
feel settling into her chest like a second heartbeat. She began to believe that she could not rely
on other people, because she had already failed the one person who had relied on her most.

To stop the memory, Maya tried hard not to think about the trauma. She would physically shake her
head when the film began to unspool, or press her palms against her eyes until she saw stars. But
the suppression only seemed to sharpen the images. The harder she pushed the memory away, the more
insistently it returned, triggered without warning, and the more convinced she became that her
failure to protect her wingman was not an accident of circumstance but a verdict on who she was. She
had come to believe that she should have done something different, that she should have seen
something, called something out, and because she had not, she was untrustworthy, broken, someone no
one should rely on.

again behind her eyes, and she had to excuse herself to the bathroom. She began avoiding new
friendships and relationships entirely, because she was certain that anyone who got close to her
would eventually discover what she already knew: that she was someone who let people down. The
weight of this conviction made her feel uncomfortable in almost any social setting, and that
discomfort itself became a trigger, looping back to the memory of the moment she had failed.

Over the months, the cycle tightened. The memory drove her avoidance, and the avoidance kept the
memory fresh and unprocessed, a wound that never closed. She started requesting solo flights, not

one thing she had trained her whole life to do: trust herself with another person’s safety. She is
terrified that she will never fly again, and she is equally terrified that she will have to.
