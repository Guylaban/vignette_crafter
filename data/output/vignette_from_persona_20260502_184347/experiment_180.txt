David is a 33-year-old graphic designer who was divorced two years ago, shortly after the night that
reshaped his life. He was driving home late from a client meeting when a police cruiser, its lights
off, pulled out from a side street directly into his path. He swerved, clipped a guardrail, and the
cruiser’s door was torn open. An officer, responding to a routine call, was thrown from the vehicle.
David watched the paramedics work for twenty minutes before they called the time of death. He never
received a citation, but the image of the officer’s body on the asphalt, the smell of gasoline and
torn metal, and the sound of the dispatcher’s voice over the cruiser’s radio have not faded.

In the months that followed, David began to notice that certain moments would pull him back into
that night. The most immediate was the dark: waking in the middle of the night, the bedroom pitch
black, his heart already racing before he knew why. He would lie still, certain that headlights were
about to cut through the window, that the screech of tires was just outside. Crowds also became
unbearable—any place where people pressed close, like a grocery aisle or a subway platform, made his
skin crawl, because he could not see who was moving toward him, could not predict the next sudden
turn. And then there were the cars. Not all cars, but specific models—the same make and color as the
cruiser that had pulled out. He would spot one in traffic and feel his hands go cold, his throat
tighten, as if the collision were happening again.

David’s response to these triggers has been largely automatic and, he knows, self-defeating. When
the panic rises—say, in a crowded bar after work, or when he catches a glimpse of a dark sedan in
his rearview mirror—he reaches for a drink. He drinks alcohol, sometimes takes a leftover
prescription sedative, to dull the edge of the hyperarousal. He has come to believe that if he does
not numb himself in those moments, the memory will overwhelm him completely, that he will lose
control. This has become a nightly ritual: a glass of whiskey before bed to ensure he does not wake
in the dark, and sometimes another in the middle of the night when he does wake anyway. He also
avoids police uniforms and weapons with a vigilance that has altered his daily route. He takes a
longer, more circuitous path to his office to avoid the precinct station, and he has stopped walking
past the courthouse. At work, his desk is positioned so that he can see the door; he keeps his back
to the wall. When he hears a siren outside, he covers his face with his hands, pressing his palms
against his eyes until the sound fades, a habit he developed in the first weeks after the accident
and has not been able to break.

The most significant consequence has been relational. His ex-wife, who initially tried to support
him, grew exhausted by his refusal to go to restaurants or movie theaters, by the drinking, by his
jumpiness in the car. The divorce was finalized six months after the accident. Now, David lives
alone, and his social life has narrowed to the occasional after-work drink with colleagues, but even
that is precarious—he has left two such gatherings early, his heart pounding, after spotting a man
in a dark jacket who reminded him of the officer. He has not been on a date since the divorce.

What finally brought him to therapy was a moment at his desk last week. He was designing a logo for
a car dealership, and the client’s reference image included a dark sedan. David froze, his hands
shaking, and he had to excuse himself to the bathroom, where he stood with his face in his hands for
ten minutes. He is afraid that if he does not get help, he will lose his job. He is most afraid of
the dark, of waking up and not knowing what is real. He is hoping that someone can teach him how to
stop the cycle before it starts.
