competence and reliability—qualities that had served her well in her career and in her marriage.
That foundation cracked three years ago when she was diagnosed with a life-threatening illness. What
began as a routine check-up spiraled into months of hospitalizations, surgeries, and a constant
parade of needles, drips, and invasive procedures. She remembered the moment a nurse had inserted an
IV line into her arm; her body had gone rigid, and she had felt a wave of shame wash over her—shame
at how her own flesh had betrayed her, at how vulnerable and exposed she had become. That shame had
taken root, and she had come to believe that her body was no longer trustworthy, that it had
destroyed the life she had built. She could not put the experience into words; whenever she tried to
explain what had happened, the memory would surge back in a flood of sensory detail—the cold metal
of the examination table, the antiseptic sting in the air, the precise angle of her arm as it lay
pinned beneath the weight of the procedure. In those moments, her body would reenact the position,
her muscles locking as if she were still there, and the feeling was so complete that it erased the
present.

Over the months that followed, Leilani found herself increasingly on edge. A colleague would bring
up health insurance at a team meeting, and her heart would race; she would snap at them with an
irritability that surprised even her. She had difficulty concentrating on project timelines, her
mind drifting back to the hospital room. When her husband touched her shoulder unexpectedly, she
would flinch, her startle reflex so sharp that she would feel a surge of anger. The shame she
carried—about what her body had endured and what it had become—seemed to feed the memories, making
them more vivid and more frequent. She began to avoid certain conversation topics: whenever a friend
mentioned a doctor's appointment, she would change the subject or excuse herself to the restroom.
She scheduled her annual physical for a date she knew she would cancel, and she asked her husband to
keep all medical supplies out of sight. Yet the triggers were everywhere: a passing glimpse of a
syringe in a television show, the faint beep of a hospital monitor in a waiting room, the smell of
hand sanitizer at the grocery store. Each one would yank her back into the memory, and the memory
would amplify her fear—her heart would pound, her concentration would scatter, and she would feel a
fresh wave of shame that made her certain her life was permanently broken.

The cycle tightened. The more her body felt unreliable, the more she scanned her environment for
reminders of medical settings, and the more she found them. A single conversation about a
colleague's surgery would leave her irritable and unable to focus for the rest of the day. The
memory would intrude, and with it came the sense that her body was betraying her again, that she
could never trust it again. Her husband tried to comfort her, but his touch startled her, and she
would pull away, which only deepened her shame. At work, she began declining project assignments
that involved health-related clients, a pattern that her supervisor noticed. She told herself it was
a preference, but she knew it was fear.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was a moment at her desk: she saw a paperclip on her
keyboard, and for a split second, she thought it was a needle. Her breath caught, her hands
trembled, and she had to excuse herself to the bathroom to compose herself. She was terrified that
she was losing control, that her life had been destroyed not just by the illness, but by the
aftermath. She wanted to believe she could reclaim her days, but she was certain that her body would
betray her again.
