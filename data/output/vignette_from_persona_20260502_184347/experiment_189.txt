Mariama was 72 years old, a widow, and a forester who had spent her life reading the quiet language
of trees. But the forest had become a place of shadows. The trauma that had rewritten her world
occurred decades ago, during the civil war in her West African homeland. She had been walking home
at dusk when a mortar shell landed fifty yards ahead of her, tearing through a market stall where
she had bought bread moments earlier. The bang was so immense it felt like a physical blow; the
flash seared itself onto her retina. In the chaos that followed, people ran past her—some screaming,
some silent—and she saw a man with a frantic, searching look that she later understood meant he was
looking for survivors or for enemies. She had never known which. That moment—the explosion, the
darkening sky, the suspicious faces—had never left her.

In the years since, Mariama had built a life in America, working as a forester in a state park. The
work suited her: solitary, rhythmic, far from crowds. But the old war lived inside her, and it
surfaced in the dark. Nighttime was the worst. Waking in the pitch-black of her cabin, she would
feel the bang echo in her chest before she was fully conscious, her heart hammering as if the mortar
had just landed. She had come to believe that the dark itself was a kind of trap—that danger could
approach unseen, that any sudden sound could be the start of something final. So she had developed a
routine. Whenever she felt that rising alarm, she would turn on loud music or the TV, filling the
silence with noise until her ears rang and her mind could no longer listen for what might be out
there. She also constantly scanned her environment, checking the corners of her cabin, the gaps in
the curtains, the shape of every shadow. She was vigilant for dangerous people, too—anyone who moved
too quickly, who looked at her too long, who seemed to be watching.

But her strategies worked against her. The loud music and television kept her awake longer, and the
scanning kept her alert, so that she never fully relaxed. The more she checked, the more she found
to be alarmed about. One night, she heard a car door slam outside her cabin—a sound that, in her
heightened state, became an explosion. She spent the next hour peering through the blinds, convinced
someone was approaching. The constant vigilance made her sensitive to the smallest cues: a branch
snapping in the wind, a neighbor’s footsteps on the gravel path, the way a hiker in the park looked
over his shoulder twice. Each time she noticed something, her mind supplied a threat narrative, and
her body responded as if the war had begun again. The triggers multiplied because her strategies
kept her in a state of readiness, turning neutral events into warnings.

At work, this became a problem. Mariama had always been thorough, but now she found herself unable
to focus on her duties. She would stop mid-task to listen, to scan the treeline, to watch a
visitor’s hands. Her supervisor noticed she was taking longer on her routes, and once, she failed to
mark a hazard tree because she had been distracted by a distant bang from a construction site. She
began avoiding the park’s more remote sections at dusk, claiming she preferred the morning light,
though she knew the real reason. Her colleagues, once friends, had become people she watched warily.
She had stopped joining them for coffee breaks, preferring the safety of her truck. The isolation
was a slow, quiet consequence, but it was real.

What finally brought Mariama to seek help was a night two weeks ago. She had woken in the dark,
heart pounding, and had turned on the TV so loudly that her neighbor banged on the wall. She had
stood frozen, convinced the banging was the start of an attack. In that moment, she realized she
could no longer tell the difference between a memory and a present danger. She was afraid of the
dark, of loud sounds, of people—but more than that, she was afraid of what her own mind had become.
She hoped, she said, to find a way to sleep without the war waking her.
