Jenna is a 38-year-old bartender who has been with her partner, Marcus, for six years. She came to
therapy at her sister’s urging, though she was not sure talking would help. She had spent months
trying not to think about what happened, and the idea of revisiting it felt dangerous—like prying
open a door she had worked hard to seal shut.

The event itself had been brief but indelible. One evening after her shift, Jenna was walking to her
car when she saw a man collapse on the sidewalk a few feet ahead. He was middle-aged, alone, and
clutching his chest. She ran to him, called 911, and stayed with him until the paramedics arrived,
holding his hand and speaking to him as his face went gray and his eyes lost focus. He died before
the ambulance reached the hospital. Jenna had never seen someone die before. She had never held a
stranger’s hand through their last minutes. What stayed with her was not the chaos but the
stillness—the way his hand had gone slack in hers, the way his eyes had looked through her at
something she could not see.

Her memory of that night was strange and uneven. Some details were so vivid they felt like they were
happening again: the exact pattern of cracks in the sidewalk, the sound of his breathing shifting
from ragged to quiet, the weight of his hand. But other parts were gone entirely. She could not
remember dialing 911, though she knew she had. She could not recall what she had said to the
dispatcher. The gaps bothered her more than the images. She had come to believe that if she could
not remember everything, she must have done something wrong—missed a step, failed to act in time.
The event happened because of the way I acted, she told herself, even though she could not specify
what she should have done differently.

That belief had taken root and spread. She had once trusted people easily, but now she found herself
watching customers at the bar with a wary eye, reading hidden motives into casual comments. People
are not what they seem, she thought more than once. She had also stopped relying on Marcus in the
way she used to—she did not tell him about the nightmares or the way her chest tightened when she
saw an ambulance on the street. I can’t rely on other people, she had decided. If she could not
trust herself to act correctly in a crisis, how could she trust anyone else?

In the months that followed, Jenna developed a set of habits she did not call avoidance but that
governed her life. She stopped watching the news entirely, changing the channel or leaving the room
whenever a story about a sudden death or a medical emergency came on. She had come to believe that
if she let herself feel the full weight of those stories, she would lose control completely. So she
tried hard to control her emotions instead, clamping down on the sadness or fear that rose up
unbidden. She also refused to see a doctor, even for routine things. The idea of sitting in a
waiting room, of hearing someone’s name called over the intercom, of being in a place where people
were sick or dying—it felt like walking into a trap. She had told herself that seeking medical or
psychological help would only make things worse, that talking about it would bring the memories
rushing back, and that she needed to keep them at bay to function.

But the memories did not stay at bay. They surfaced at work, triggered by the sound of glass
breaking or the sight of a customer’s hand trembling as they reached for a drink. Each time, the
vivid images returned—the man’s face, the sidewalk cracks, the slack hand—and each time, Jenna felt
the self-blame sharpen. I should have done more. I should have known what to do. The more she tried
to control her emotions, the more the memories seemed to ambush her. And the more the memories came,
the more she believed she could not rely on anyone, because no one else had been there, and no one
else understood.

The cycle had worn her down. She was sleeping poorly, drinking more after her shifts, and pulling
away from Marcus without quite knowing how to explain why. What brought her to therapy was not
insight but exhaustion. She was tired of bracing herself, tired of the hypervigilance that made
every sudden sound feel like a warning. She was afraid that if she kept going this way, she would
lose Marcus too. But she was also afraid of what she might find if she let herself remember fully.
She was not sure which was worse—the gaps or the images. She only knew she could not keep carrying
both alone.
