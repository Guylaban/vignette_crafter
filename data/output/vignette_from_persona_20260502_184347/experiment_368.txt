Kwame is a 72-year-old retired Coast Guard officer who lives with his wife in a quiet coastal town,
where the salt air and the rhythm of the tides once felt like home. For thirty years, he served on a
cutter responsible for chemical spill response, and one incident—a 2003 containment failure aboard a
cargo vessel—has never left him. He was part of the team that entered the hold without adequate
respiratory gear, and for hours they worked in a haze of volatile organic compounds. Kwame remembers
the moment with photographic clarity: the way the emergency lights cast the scene in a sickly
yellow, the exact configuration of the leaking drums, the metallic taste that filled his mouth. He
can still replay the sequence like a film, frame by frame, and when the memory returns, he feels so
overwhelmed by emotion that he has to stop whatever he is doing. His wife has learned to recognize
the signs—the way he freezes mid-sentence, his eyes going distant—but what frightens Kwame most is
his own reaction. He has come to believe that if he truly lets himself think about the event, he
will not be able to handle it, and that conviction has made him avoid not only the memory but any
thought that might lead there.

Over the years, Kwame’s sense of safety has eroded in ways he cannot fully explain. He finds the
world to be a dangerous place now, and this belief colors everything. When the house falls
silent—when his wife steps out to the garden or the television goes quiet—that unusual silence makes
him feel something dangerous is about to happen. He is certain that any moment of calm is a prelude
to catastrophe, and so he keeps the radio on at low volume, a constant hum to ward off the
stillness. His body reacts before his mind can catch up: a sudden siren on the street, the smell of
diesel fuel, or even the sight of a chemical truck can trigger strong physical reactions—his heart
pounds, his hands shake, and he feels a wave of nausea that takes minutes to subside. These moments
of reactivity, in turn, bring the memory flooding back with renewed force, as though his body is
reminding his mind of what it refuses to forget. He has come to believe that he cannot deal with
even the slightest upset, and this low coping appraisal makes him hypervigilant, always scanning for
the next threat. The cycle tightens: the more on guard he feels even inside his own home, the more
the memory of the chemical hold intrudes, and the more helpless he feels against it.

This self-reinforcing loop has reshaped his daily life. He now avoids the garage, where he once kept
his tools, because the smell of paint thinner triggers him. He no longer watches the evening news,
afraid of industrial accident reports. His wife has taken over all shopping trips, because Kwame
cannot walk past the cleaning aisle at the hardware store without his chest tightening. The most
concrete consequence is that he has stopped attending the Coast Guard retirees’ breakfasts, a
gathering he had enjoyed for years. The conversation there would inevitably turn to old missions,
and he could not bear the thought of being unable to handle it if someone brought up the 2003 spill.
His friends have stopped calling, assuming he is ill or uninterested. Kwame knows he has isolated
himself, but he feels trapped: the world feels dangerous, his own mind feels like a minefield, and
he no longer trusts his body to stay calm. What finally brought him to seek help was a morning last
week when the silence in his own kitchen became so unbearable that he found himself standing at the
back door, ready to leave his own home. He is afraid of what he might do if this gets worse, but
more than that, he is afraid of what he will find if he ever stops running from the memory.
