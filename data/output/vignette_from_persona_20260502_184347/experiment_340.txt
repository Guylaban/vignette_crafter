Maya was thirty-three, a paramedic in a busy urban system, and she had been married for six years to
a man who worked in IT and who, until recently, had believed they were happy. The event that undid
her happened on a Tuesday afternoon in late October, during a routine call for a middle-aged man
with chest pain. She had done everything by the book—oxygen, aspirin, nitro, a rapid transport to
the closest cardiac center—but at the hospital, the man arrested on the gurney during handoff, and
despite the team’s efforts, he died. Maya had been the one to place the IV, to push the medications,
to ride in the back with him alone, and afterward, the review board found no error. But in the weeks
that followed, what she remembered was not the sequence of clinical decisions; it was a series of
fragments, each one a snapshot that arrived without warning and then vanished: the man’s hand
gripping the stretcher rail, the beige linoleum of the ambulance floor, the exact shade of blue his
lips had turned. Each time one of these pieces surfaced, it brought back the same raw terror she had
felt in that moment—the certainty that she was watching someone die because of something she had
done or failed to do—and the terror did not fade; it stayed with her for hours, sometimes days, a
cold dread that made her chest tighten and her hands shake.

To cope, Maya had developed a set of habits that she thought of as survival strategies. She
drank—not heavily, but reliably, a glass of wine that became two, then three, every evening after
her shift, until the edges of the memory blurred and she could fall asleep. She also began to
distract herself obsessively: during any downtime at the station, she would scroll through her
phone, play mindless games, or clean the equipment bay with a focus that her colleagues found
admirable but that was actually a frantic effort to keep her mind from wandering into those
fragments. And she stopped talking about the call entirely. When her husband asked how her day had
been, she said “fine” or “busy” and changed the subject. When a fellow paramedic mentioned the man’s
name in passing, she felt her throat close and walked away. The avoidance worked, briefly, but it
also had a cost: the more she pushed the memory away during the day, the more it seemed to ambush
her at night, when she was alone with her thoughts and the wine had worn off.

Night had become the hardest time. Maya found herself unable to feel safe after dark, even in her
own home. She would check the locks on the doors three times before bed, then lie awake, hyperalert
to every creak and rustle, her heart pounding at the sound of a car passing outside. During the day,
too, she was watchful in a way she had never been before—scanning the faces of strangers in the
grocery store, tensing at sudden noises, finding it nearly impossible to concentrate on a
conversation or a television show because her attention was always half-focused on the possibility
of threat. She had come to believe that the world was more dangerous than she had once thought, that
catastrophe could strike at any moment, and that she was responsible for preventing it. This
hypervigilance, in turn, made the memories worse: the more she scanned for danger, the more her mind
supplied images of the man’s face, the beeping of the monitor, the feel of the ambulance lurching
through traffic. It was a loop she could not break.

Over time, the drinking and distraction began to backfire. On nights when she drank more than usual,
she would wake in the early hours with a start, the snapshot already forming: the man’s hand, the
linoleum, the blue lips. The alcohol seemed to loosen the grip of the memory temporarily, but then
to invite it back with greater force, and the next day she would feel foggy, irritable, and even
more vigilant, as though her guard had been lowered and she needed to double it. She had also
started taking an occasional benzodiazepine left over from an old prescription, telling herself it
was just for emergencies, though the emergencies were coming more frequently now. Her husband had
noticed the change. He had asked, gently, whether she wanted to talk to someone, and she had snapped
at him, then apologized, then retreated into silence. She was afraid that if she started talking
about what happened, the fragments would stop being fragments and become something she could not
contain. What frightened her most was the idea that the memory was not just a record of the past but
a revelation of something true about her—that she was not competent, not safe, not the person
everyone thought she was.

What finally brought Maya to seek help was a single moment on a night shift three weeks ago. She had
been dispatched to a call for chest pain, and as she climbed into the back of the ambulance, the
smell of the disinfectant—the same brand they used in her station—hit her, and suddenly she was back
in the moment, the man’s hand gripping the rail, the monitor alarming, her own voice shouting
something she could not hear. She froze. Her partner had to touch her arm twice before she
responded. She completed the call mechanically, but afterward, she sat in the station for an hour,
unable to move, the terror washing over her in waves. That night, she told her husband she needed
help. She was afraid of what would happen if she did not.
