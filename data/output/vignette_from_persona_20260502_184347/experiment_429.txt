Tama, a 71-year-old painter who lived alone in a small coastal town in California, had spent most of
his life working on large-scale murals and fine art commissions, his reputation built on steady
hands and a calm presence. That changed one afternoon three years ago, when he was waiting for a bus
near a crowded market. A bomb hidden in a trash can detonated fifty feet away. The blast threw him
to the ground. He remembered the pressure in his chest, the ringing in his ears, and the sight of a
woman's shoe lying beside his face, still laced. He had not been physically injured, but something
in him had shifted that day and never settled back.

In the weeks after, Tama found that the memory of the explosion would force its way into his mind
without warning. He would be mixing paint on his palette, and suddenly he would feel his body tense
as though he were again lying on the pavement, his arms pinned beneath him, his cheek pressed
against the cold concrete. The memory was not just visual; it was bodily. He could feel the exact
angle of his neck and the grit against his skin, and the clarity of those sensations terrified him.
He had come to believe that the world was a dangerous place, and that he could not rely on other
people to keep him safe. He also began to feel that he could not rely on himself, because his own
mind kept pulling him back into that moment without his permission, and he could not stop it.

To manage this, Tama tried hard to control his emotions. When he felt the familiar tightness in his
chest or the surge of fear, he would clench his jaw and force himself to focus on something
neutral—the grain of a wooden floorboard, the pattern of light on the wall. He also began asking his
neighbor, a younger man named Leo, to check in on him each evening. Tama would call Leo and say,
"I'm fine, right? Everything is okay?" and Leo would reassure him that he was safe and that nothing
bad was happening. This helped for a few minutes, but then the memory would return, and Tama would
feel the need to call again. The more he sought reassurance, the more the memories intruded, and the
more convinced he became that he could not manage on his own.

He stopped going out. He had always worked from a studio attached to his house, so the practical
impact was not immediate, but over months it became severe. He stopped buying fresh paint supplies.
He stopped walking to the corner store for coffee. He told himself that the world outside was
unpredictable, that he could not control what might happen. What frightened him most was the thought
that his own reactions would fail him—that if something unexpected occurred, he would freeze or fall
apart, and that no one would be there to help. This belief reinforced his need to control his
emotions and to ask for reassurance, which in turn made the memories more frequent and more vivid.
He noticed that after he called Leo, the memory would surface again within an hour, sometimes with
even more detail.

The cycle tightened over two years. Tama's painting slowed, then stopped. He had not completed a
commission in over a year, and his savings were running low. Leo had begun to sound tired on the
phone, and Tama was certain that he was annoying him, but he could not stop calling. He was afraid
that if he stopped seeking reassurance, the fear would become unbearable and he would have no way to
manage it. He was also afraid that if he let himself feel the emotions fully, he would not be able
to stop them.

What finally brought Tama to seek help was a morning when he could not bring himself to open his
front door to collect the mail. He stood in the hallway for twenty minutes, his hand on the lock,
his heart pounding, his body remembering the concrete and the blast. He called his doctor that
afternoon. He was hoping that someone could tell him how to make the memories stop, and he was
afraid that nothing would work.
