The fire started in the server room at 2:47 AM. Héctor remembered the time because he had glanced at
his watch just before the first alarm, a habit from twenty-three years as a forensic analyst—always
noting the precise moment when evidence began to accumulate. He had been working the night shift,
alone in the annex building, reconstructing a chain of digital signatures for a fraud case. The
smoke hit him before the sprinklers did, a chemical burn in his throat that tasted of melted plastic
and copper. He made it to the stairwell, but the door had locked behind him, a safety feature that
had become a trap. For twelve minutes—he counted—he had pounded on that door, his lungs seizing, his
vision tunneling, certain that he would die in a building he had walked through every day for a
decade. A janitor heard him. The janitor opened the door. Héctor stumbled out into the parking lot,
coughing black phlegm onto the asphalt, and watched the annex burn.

That was fourteen months ago. Héctor had returned to work after three weeks of medical leave, but
something fundamental had shifted. He had always prided himself on his composure, his ability to sit
with the darkest digital evidence without flinching—child exploitation images, encrypted terrorist
communications, the meticulous financial records of murder-for-hire schemes. He was the analyst who
could look at anything. Now, he could not look at the stairwell. He took the elevator every day,
even though it made him feel trapped in a different way, a smaller metal box. He told himself it was
practical. But he knew the truth: his reactions since the fire had shown him something about himself
he did not want to see. He was a lousy coper. He had always believed he was capable, steady,
reliable—the man his wife and three adult children leaned on. Now he was no longer capable of doing
the things that defined him. The world had become a dangerous place, and he was not equipped for it.

His body stayed on alert. He woke each morning with his shoulders already tight, his jaw clamped, as
if he had been bracing all night. At his desk, he found himself scanning the room for exits, for
fire extinguishers, for anything that could burn. The startle response had become a part of him: a
dropped file folder in the office next door made him flinch so hard he knocked his coffee across his
keyboard. His colleagues had stopped joking about it. His wife, Elena, had started to watch him with
a worried quietness he recognized—she was the one who had always read him best. He had begun to
avoid unfamiliar places. He could not explain it to her in a way that made sense. It was not that he
was afraid of new restaurants or grocery stores. It was that he could not assess the danger. In a
place he had never been, he did not know where the exits were, how the building was constructed,
whether the fire alarms worked. He could not see the threat, and that was worse than seeing it.

The avoidance had consequences. His youngest daughter had graduated from college in May, and the
ceremony was held in a convention center Héctor had never visited. He told her he had a case
deadline. She had not said anything, but he saw the disappointment flicker across her face before
she turned away. Elena had not said anything either, but she had started sleeping on the sofa three
nights a week, claiming the bed was too soft for her back. Héctor knew better. He was certain that
she was tired of lying next to a man who jolted awake at every creak of the house, who lay rigid in
the dark, listening for the sound of smoke detectors that were not beeping.

What finally brought him to seek help was a routine fire drill at the office. The alarm sounded, and
Héctor had frozen at his desk, his hands gripping the armrests, his breath gone. He could not stand.
He could not move. A colleague had to take his arm and guide him down the stairs—the stairs—and
Héctor had wept in the parking lot, silently, his face turned away from the others. The next
morning, he called a therapist. He was afraid, he told Elena, of what he had become. He was afraid
that the man who had walked through fire and come out the other side was gone, replaced by someone
who could not walk through a door. He did not know if therapy could bring the old Héctor back. But
he was even more afraid of what would happen if he did not try.
