Layla was fifty-three years old, a ship captain who had spent three decades commanding cargo vessels
across the Atlantic, but she had never spoken of what happened in the small house in Basra when she
was seven. Her father, a quiet man who worked at the port, would come home and beat her with a
leather belt for small infractions—a glass left unwashed, a word spoken too loudly. The world had
taught her early that safety was an illusion, that the people who should protect you could be the
ones who hurt you most. She had come to believe that institutions—family, community, even the
law—were hollow promises; her father had been respected in their neighborhood, and no one had
intervened. This belief had settled into her bones, and it colored everything. She now saw the world
as a fundamentally dangerous place, and this conviction drove her to act in ways that kept her alert
and guarded. She tried hard not to think about the trauma, pushing images of the belt and the sting
away, but the effort itself was a constant, exhausting presence in her mind.

The memories came in fragments. Sometimes she would feel the full emotional intensity—the shame, the
helplessness—without any clear image, as if a wave of terror washed over her while she stood in her
galley stirring tea. Other times, she could recall what happened but felt completely numb, as if it
had happened to a stranger, a little girl she watched from a great distance. Her memories were
mostly pictures or images: the pattern of the linoleum floor, the buckle of the belt catching the
light. These images would surface unbidden, and each time they did, she would dwell on how the event
could have been prevented—if she had been quieter, if she had hidden better. This rumination only
deepened her conviction that the world was unsafe, and it made the memories feel more vivid, more
present. She also found herself dwelling on who she used to be before the event, a child who had not
yet learned to be afraid, and the contrast between that girl and the woman she had become was a
source of quiet grief.

On her ship, Layla was known for her composure, but at home in her small coastal apartment, she
could not relax. Even when she knew she was in a safe place, her body remained tense, her ears tuned
to the creak of the floorboards or the hum of the refrigerator. She listened for sounds that might
mean someone was entering her home, and she found it harder to feel safe at night than during the
day. This hypervigilance fed directly into her memories: the dark of her bedroom would trigger a
flash of the dim hallway where her father had stood, belt in hand, and the image would come
unbidden. In response, she would try to suppress the thought, pushing it down, but the effort only
made her more alert, more unable to relax. The cycle tightened: the threat monitoring led to more
intrusive memories, which led to more rumination about prevention, which led to more tension, which
made her listen harder for sounds in the night.

She had stopped visiting her local Middle Eastern community center two years ago, after a
conversation about family obligations triggered a wave of shame and anger. She had lost her
connection to her community and culture, and she knew it, but the thought of returning felt
impossible—what would she say? That she had been beaten by her father and no one had helped? The
belief that institutions and communities had failed her kept her isolated, and the isolation made
the memories louder. She tried to suppress them, but the suppression itself became another habit,
another layer of tension that made her jump at the sound of a door closing in the hallway of her
apartment building.

What finally brought her to seek help was a night last month when she was standing on the bridge of
her ship, the sea calm under a full moon, and she felt the full emotional intensity of the childhood
terror without any image—just the panic, the helplessness, the certainty that she was about to be
hurt. She had gripped the railing and wept, and her first mate had found her there. She told him she
was fine, but she was not. She was afraid that if she did not do something, she would lose her
command, or worse, lose herself entirely. She hoped that talking about it might loosen the grip of
the past, but she was also terrified that the memories would become worse if she let them in.
