Samir is a 41-year-old single man who works as a construction worker. He grew up in a household
where, between the ages of seven and ten, he repeatedly witnessed his father assaulting his mother.
The memories of those nights are fragmented and strange to him now. Some moments are extremely
clear—he can see the exact pattern of the living room rug, the way his mother’s hand gripped the
doorframe—while other stretches are completely blank, as if someone erased parts of the film. When
he tries to deliberately remember what happened, he finds it difficult to access the memory; the
whole thing feels slippery, like trying to hold water. Yet when something reminds him, the memory
comes back like a photograph, unbidden and vivid, as if he is looking at a still image in his mind.

In the weeks and months after the violence ended—his father eventually left—Samir began to notice
that certain situations would pull those images to the surface. Confrontations, even small ones,
were the worst. A coworker raising his voice over a misplaced tool would make Samir’s chest tighten,
and suddenly he would see his mother’s face, the way she looked just before the first blow. The
memory would surge up, and Samir would find himself drifting off into a world of his own, a fantasy
escape where none of it was happening. He also started to dwell on how he used to be before the
event—a boy who didn’t flinch at loud voices, who didn’t scan every room for danger. That boy felt
impossibly distant.

At work, Samir learned to avoid any situation that might involve disagreement or confrontation. He
never spoke up when a foreman gave unfair orders, and he ate lunch alone rather than risk a debate
about sports or politics. When a police car appeared on site during a routine safety inspection,
Samir felt his stomach drop and his palms go slick with sweat. His physical reactions were immediate
and overpowering—his heart hammering, his breath shallow—and in that moment the memory of his
father’s heavy footsteps on the stairs would lock into place, as clear as a photograph. He had come
to believe that authority figures carried the same threat his father had, that their presence meant
something bad was about to happen.

Samir began studying people’s faces and body language constantly, trying to detect if they were a
threat. He would watch a coworker’s jaw tighten and assume an argument was coming, even when the man
was just concentrating. When a delivery of lumber took longer than expected, Samir was certain that
something bad was happening—maybe a fight had broken out, maybe someone was hurt. This threat
monitoring only made the memories more accessible; the more he watched for danger, the more his mind
supplied him with images of his father’s rage. And those images, in turn, made him avoid people even
more. He stopped going to family gatherings. He told himself he was fine alone.

Over time, a cycle tightened. A trigger—a raised voice, a police car, a question about his plans for
next year—would bring the memory rushing in. The memory would spike his physical reactivity and his
scanning for threat. That threat state would drive him deeper into fantasy escape and past dwelling.
And those maladaptive strategies, in turn, made the triggers feel more frequent and more dangerous,
because he was never present enough to test whether a situation was actually safe. Talking about the
future became especially unbearable; when a friend asked what he wanted to do next year, Samir felt
a cold dread and saw his mother’s face in the doorway, and he changed the subject.

Now, at 41, Samir has come to therapy because he can no longer function at work. He was written up
for failing to respond to a foreman’s instruction—he had been so lost in a daydream that he didn’t
hear the man calling his name. What frightens him most is the feeling that he is losing his grip on
the present entirely, that he is living more and more inside those old photographs. He hopes,
quietly, that someone can help him find a way back to the person he was before, though he is not
sure that person exists anymore.
