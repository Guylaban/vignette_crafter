fails to hold it; the experience exists only in fragments of smell and pain and a visceral sense of
helplessness that bypasses all narrative.

relative who had known her, called to check in, Raj ended the conversation abruptly, his chest

or a stranger on the subway, would trigger a full sensory replay: the crushing weight, the pain, the
shame. He started to avoid people altogether, canceling plans with friends and retreating to his
apartment after shifts. He stopped wearing the loose-fitting shirts he had worn that night,
associating the fabric with the moment of violation. Even exercise, which had once been his anchor,
became impossible; the physical exertion produced sweat, and sweat meant the memory, and the memory
meant he was back in that room, unable to escape.

The weight of what had happened grew heavier with each passing month. Raj had come to believe that
the people responsible—his ex-partner, who had faced no consequences, and the system that had let
her walk away—had made a mockery of justice. But more corrosive was the thought that had taken root
deep inside him: there was something wrong with him as a person. A medic who could not protect
himself, a man who could not defend his own body. This belief colored everything. Nothing in his
life felt meaningful anymore; his work, once a calling, became a mechanical routine. He was certain
that if others knew the truth, they would see him as broken, and so he withdrew further. The
defective self he carried made him feel unworthy of connection, and that feeling drove him to avoid
even the colleagues who had been his brothers-in-arms.

backfiring, a door slamming—made him jump, his heart hammering as if the attack were beginning
again. This state of alertness fed directly back into his memory: the more vigilant he was, the more
easily a sound or scent triggered a reliving episode. And each reliving reinforced his belief that
he was fundamentally unsafe, that the world was a place where violence could erupt from intimacy at
any moment.

His maladaptive strategies, born of desperation, only tightened the cycle. Avoiding people meant
fewer triggers, but it also meant he spent long hours alone, and in the silence, the memory surfaced
more vividly. Avoiding exercise kept his body still, but the lack of physical release left him tense

confirmed to himself that he was too damaged to function—a defective person who could not even

he was beyond repair.
