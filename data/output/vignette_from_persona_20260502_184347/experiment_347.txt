month’s food distribution schedule. He never saw the pickup truck that ran the red light. He only
remembered the sound—a shattering, metallic roar that seemed to come from inside his own skull—and
then the feeling of his car spinning, the seatbelt cutting into his chest, and his hands gripping
the steering wheel with a strength he didn’t know he had. When the world finally stopped moving, he
was facing oncoming traffic, and the air smelled of gasoline and hot rubber. He was not physically
injured, but something in him had been torn loose. For weeks afterward, every time he closed his
eyes, he saw the grille of that truck growing larger, and the terror he had felt in that moment
returned as fresh as if he were still sitting in the wreckage. The images came to him unbidden,
mostly pictures—the shattered windshield, the dashboard twisted sideways—and when they arrived, he
felt himself back inside the car, his heart hammering, his breath short, his body tensed for an
impact that had already happened.

Ravi had always been a calm man, the sort of person colleagues described as unflappable. That
changed. He began to notice that certain textures—the rough fabric of his car’s seat cover, the
stiff denim of his jeans—would suddenly pull him back into the crash, as if his skin remembered the
way the seatbelt had pressed against him. He learned to avoid those textures, choosing softer
clothes, sitting only on smooth surfaces, but the world was full of triggers he could not
anticipate. A car accident on the evening news, or a colleague mentioning a fender bender, would
drop him straight into a reliving episode, his hands shaking, his vision tunneling. He started to
dread conversations, steering them away from any topic that might lead to talk of accidents. At
work, he found himself excusing himself from meetings when someone began describing a minor
collision, his pulse already racing before he had consciously registered the topic.

The worst moments came without warning. A sudden loud sound—a car backfiring, a door slamming, a
dropped tray in the cafeteria—would send a jolt through him, and he would be on his feet, scanning
for danger, his body braced for a blow. He hated this hypervigilance, the way it made him feel like
a stranger in his own skin. And yet, the more he scanned, the more he noticed movement at the edges
of his vision, and each unexpected motion—a child darting across a parking lot, a cyclist weaving
through traffic—triggered the same startle response, the same flood of fear. These moments, in turn,
seemed to summon the memories. A sudden jerk of his head toward a sound, and he would be back in the
car, watching the truck approach, his hands frozen on the wheel. It was a loop he could not break:
the memories made him watchful, and the watching brought the memories back.

By the time he sought help, six months after the crash, Ravi had stopped driving entirely. He took
the bus to work, but even that felt precarious; every sudden brake, every horn blast, sent him into
a spiral. He had begun to avoid going out at all. His wife, Meera, had grown frustrated with his
silences, with the way he would flinch when she touched his shoulder from behind. He could not
explain it to her—how the texture of her hand on his shirt could, in a fraction of a second, become
the pressure of the seatbelt, the smell of gasoline, the sound of metal tearing. What frightened him
most was the feeling that the crash was still happening, that he was still in that spinning car,
waiting for the world to stop. He came to therapy because he was tired of being afraid, and because
he had started to wonder if he would ever feel safe again.
