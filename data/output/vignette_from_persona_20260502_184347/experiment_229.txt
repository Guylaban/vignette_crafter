Leo is a 49-year-old fashion designer, a man whose professional life is built on precision,
foresight, and the ability to envision a finished garment from a bolt of fabric. He came to therapy
after his wife, Mira, was diagnosed with an aggressive form of breast cancer two years ago. The
trauma was not his own body, but the sight of hers—the moment in the oncologist’s office when the
word “malignant” landed in the room and he watched her face go slack. That moment is burned into
him, but in fragments: the fluorescent hum of the lights, the doctor’s pen tapping the edge of a
clipboard, and then nothing—a blank space where the next few minutes should be. Some parts are
excruciatingly clear, like the way Mira’s hand tightened on his arm, while others, like the drive
home, are a complete void. This partial clarity haunts him because he feels he should remember
everything, and the gaps feel like a betrayal, a failure to bear witness.

The memory of that diagnosis, when it surfaces, is not a calm recollection but a wave of
overwhelming emotion that forces him to stop whatever he is doing. He will be at his drafting table,
sketching a new line of evening wear, and the smell of a particular hand sanitizer—the one from the
clinic—will flood his senses. He is then derailed, his heart pounding, his breath shallow, and he
has to put down his pencil and walk away. The physical reactivity is immediate and uncontrollable,
leaving him feeling raw and exposed. In the wake of these episodes, he is consumed by a crushing
sense of guilt. He replays the moment he thinks he hesitated before asking the doctor a follow-up
question, or the time he got irritated with Mira for forgetting to take a supplement. He is certain
his inadequacy as a husband contributed to her suffering, and this guilt makes the memory even more
potent and painful the next time it intrudes.

Because the memory is so overwhelming, Leo has developed a rigid set of strategies to contain it. He
tries hard not to think about the trauma at all, a form of mental suppression that is exhausting. He
keeps his mind occupied all the time, filling every waking moment with work, podcasts, or complex
crossword puzzles. He has also stopped making any plans for the future. When his colleagues ask
about the spring collection or a vacation with Mira, he deflects with a vague comment about “taking
it day by day.” This avoidance of future plans is a direct consequence of his memory: every time he
tries to imagine a happy event—a holiday, a retirement party—the image is instantly replaced by the
blank, fluorescent-lit room of the diagnosis. Planning feels like tempting fate, an invitation for
the other shoe to drop.

The threat he feels is constant and diffuse. He experiences strong negative feelings—fear, anger,
and a deep, corrosive shame that he cannot protect Mira. He has trouble falling asleep because his
mind races with worst-case scenarios; he lies awake monitoring his own heartbeat, listening for any
sound from her side of the bed. This hypervigilance, in turn, feeds back into his memory. When he is
already on edge, the intrusive memories feel more vivid and more terrifying, creating a self-
reinforcing loop where his anxiety primes him for a flashback, and the flashback ratchets his
anxiety even higher. He will catch himself scanning the faces of strangers at the grocery store,
looking for signs of illness, a habit that leaves him drained.

His suppression and mental distraction, the very strategies meant to control the memory,
paradoxically strengthen it. The more he tries to push the memory away during the day, the more it
erupts in fragmented, guilt-laden intrusions at night or in quiet moments. His mind, starved of
processing time, seems to fight back. The only consequence of this cycle that Leo can name is his
growing distance from Mira. He is so busy keeping his mind occupied and avoiding all
reminders—including conversations about the future—that he has become a silent, shadowy presence in
their home. He is present but unreachable, a husband who has turned his back on the very thing he is
most afraid of losing.

He finally sought help not for himself, but because Mira told him she felt like she was going
through her cancer treatment alone. What frightens him most is not the memory of the diagnosis, but
the possibility that his fear of the past is destroying his chance at a future with the woman he
loves.
