At sixty-seven, widowed for nearly a decade, Tomas had built a quiet life as an interior designer in
Honolulu, his days filled with fabric swatches and client consultations. That life ended on a
Tuesday afternoon three years ago during a work trip to Manila, when he was taken at gunpoint from
his hotel lobby and held for eleven days in a concrete room by men who shouted at him in Arabic. He
never learned why they chose him, and he never learned why they let him go. What stayed was the
sound—the guttural rhythm of their voices, the way the words crashed against the walls of the small
windowless space where he was kept. Now, when he hears Arabic shouted anywhere—at a market stall,
from a passing car—the memory arrives whole and unbidden, and his body reacts before his mind has
time to catch up: his chest tightens, his palms sweat, and he is already looking for the nearest
exit before he has consciously registered why.

That bodily reaction has become his most reliable compass. Tomas has come to believe that danger
lives in the air around him, that his own nervous system is the only early warning system he can
trust. He feels unsafe unless his back is against a wall where he can see the entire room, and he
has rearranged his small apartment so that every chair faces the door. This vigilance feeds directly
into the memories themselves: the more he scans for threat, the more the auditory fragments
surface—the shouting in Arabic, the scrape of a metal door, the sound of his own breathing in the
dark. And each time a memory comes, it deepens his conviction that the world remains a place where
he can be taken at any moment, without warning or reason. He has come to dread the strong negative
feelings—fear, anger, shame—that wash over him when the recall is vivid, though sometimes the
opposite happens: he recalls what happened but feels completely numb, as if it happened to a
stranger, and that dissociation frightens him even more because it makes him feel untethered from
his own history.

To manage these intrusions, Tomas has developed a set of habits that have slowly shrunk his life.
Before every interaction—a phone call with a client, a brief conversation with his neighbor—he
rehearses what to say, running through possible responses until the script feels safe. He has also
stopped going to his design studio entirely, telling himself that he can work from home, that
clients prefer the intimacy of a residential consultation. In truth, what he cannot tolerate is the
office elevator, the narrow hallway to the break room, the sense of being enclosed without a clear
path out. This avoidance has a paradoxical effect: the less he goes out, the more the memories press
in. When he stays home, the auditory recalls come more frequently—the shouting, the scrape, the
sound—and his rehearsed conversations begin to feel hollow, as if he is talking through a wall. He
becomes more afraid, more convinced that his body is telling him something his mind cannot
articulate, and this fear loops back into more vigilance, more scanning, more retreat.

The weight of these cycles has become unbearable. His business is faltering; clients have noticed
that he no longer visits sites, that he cancels meetings at the last minute. He has lost two major
contracts this quarter. What finally brought him to seek help was a moment last week when he found
himself standing in his own living room, back pressed to the wall, heart pounding, because a
delivery truck had honked outside and the sound had triggered a cascade of fear he could not stop.
He is afraid that he is losing his mind. He is hoping, quietly, that someone can help him understand

body feels like it belongs to someone else.
