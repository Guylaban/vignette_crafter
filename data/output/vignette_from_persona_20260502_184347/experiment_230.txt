Amir is a 54-year-old software engineer, single, living alone in a quiet suburban apartment outside
a mid-sized city. For twenty years, he has worked remotely for a financial technology company, a job
that allows him to control his environment with near-total precision. He rarely leaves his home
except for pre-dawn grocery runs to a 24-hour store where he knows the aisles will be empty. He has
not had a visitor in over a year. What brought him to therapy was a panic attack in his own kitchen,
triggered by a distant siren that he heard through a closed window—a sound that collapsed the years
between then and now.

The event that changed everything happened when Amir was 32. He had recently moved to the United
States from Lebanon, working as a junior developer for a government contractor. One evening, after a
late shift, he was stopped by two police officers near a construction site. They accused him of
trespassing, though he had simply taken a wrong turn on his way to the parking garage. What followed
was a sexual assault—he was forced to his knees, groped, and threatened with arrest if he ever spoke
of it. He did not speak of it. He quit his job the next week, moved to a different state, and told
no one. He was certain, at the time, that he had brought it on himself by being in the wrong place,
by not having his papers in order, by the way he had acted nervous when they first called out to
him.

For years, Amir managed. He built a quiet life around code and solitude. But the sirens never
stopped. Every time he heard one—whether from an ambulance, a fire truck, or a police cruiser—his
body would lock. He began to avoid leaving the house during certain hours, then altogether. He
stopped driving. He cancelled a subscription to a streaming service because one of its original
series featured a police procedural. The avoidance felt logical to him: if he stayed home, he could
control what entered his awareness. He came to believe that his only safe spaces were his apartment
and the one grocery store he knew by heart. Any deviation from this routine felt like an invitation
for something terrible to happen again. He had convinced himself that his emotions were permanently
broken, that he would never feel anything normal again, and that the injustice of what happened—that
nothing had been done, that no one had been held accountable—was simply the way the world worked.

The causal logic of his life had become a tightening spiral. His constant scanning for danger, which
he described as "always having my head on a swivel," meant that he noticed every siren, every
uniformed figure in a passing car, every man who walked too close to him in a parking lot. These
triggers, in turn, reinforced his avoidance: he stopped going to the pharmacy, then the bank, then
the post office. He arranged for all deliveries and had his mail held at a box he visited once a
month at 5 a.m. Each time he avoided a situation, he felt a momentary relief, but the relief was
followed by a deeper conviction that he was right to be afraid—that the world was dangerous, that he
was powerless, that his own judgment had failed him once and would fail him again. This self-blame,
in turn, drove further avoidance. He believed that if he put himself in a situation where he might
encounter authority figures—police, security guards, even a building superintendent—he would somehow
trigger another catastrophe, and this time it would be his fault.

The weight of these loops had accumulated into a life so narrow that his therapist, in their third
session, asked him to describe a typical week. Amir paused for a long time. "I wake up," he said. "I
work. I eat. I go to sleep. I don't talk to anyone." The only person he had spoken to in the past
month was a delivery driver, and that conversation had left him shaking for hours because the driver
was a large man with a deep voice. Being alone with men had become impossible; he could not explain
why, but he knew that his body reacted before his mind could catch up. He had stopped attending the
mosque, stopped visiting his brother's family, stopped everything.

What finally brought Amir to seek help was not a crisis of insight but a crisis of function. His
employer had announced a mandatory quarterly in-person meeting, and he knew he could not go. He
could not explain why. He could not face the commute, the open-plan office, the security guard at
the entrance, the men in suits, the possibility of a siren passing by the window. He called a
therapist's office from his kitchen, his voice barely above a whisper, and said he needed to talk to
someone. What he is most afraid of, he told her, is that the meeting will cost him his job, and that
without his job, he will have no reason to leave his apartment at all.
