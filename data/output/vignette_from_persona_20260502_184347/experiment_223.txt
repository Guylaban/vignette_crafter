Kwame is a 68-year-old widower, a policy analyst who once prided himself on his ability to hold
complex regulatory frameworks in his mind. His trauma began three years ago, not with a single
violent event, but with a diagnosis of advanced sepsis that nearly killed him. He remembers the
hospital room with a clarity that disturbs him: the beeping monitors, the cold plastic of the IV
line, the way his own hand looked pale and foreign against the white sheets. The doctors told his
family he might not make it through the night. He did survive, but something fundamental shifted
inside him.

Now, when the memory returns—and it returns often, feeling less like a recollection and more like a
re-living—Kwame is overwhelmed by a wave of emotion so intense that he has to stop whatever he is
doing. He will be standing at his kitchen counter, making tea, and suddenly the antiseptic smell of
the ICU fills his nostrils, and his heart hammers as if he is back on that gurney. The experience is
so vivid that the present moment dissolves, and he is certain he is dying again. At other times,
paradoxically, he can recall the event with a cold, emotionless detachment, as if watching a
stranger’s life on a screen. This numbness frightens him almost as much as the overwhelm.

He has come to believe that his body is no longer his ally. His body keeps reacting as if he is
still in danger, even when he is safe at home, and he has concluded that he can no longer trust his
own physical sensations. A slight rise in his heart rate from climbing stairs sends a spike of panic
through him. A mild ache in his chest convinces him that his organs are failing again. Because he
believes his body is fundamentally unreliable, he has begun to monitor it constantly, scanning for
signs of betrayal. This hypervigilance means his body is perpetually tense, ready to react to
danger, and he feels on guard even inside his own home. The constant threat monitoring, in turn,
drags his memory back to the hospital. A sudden tightness in his throat triggers a full sensory
flashback of the breathing tube. The more he scans, the more he remembers; the more he remembers,
the more convinced he becomes that his body is a ticking bomb.

This cycle has hollowed out his daily life. As a policy analyst, he once spent hours reading dense
documents. Now, he cannot concentrate for more than a few minutes before his mind is pulled back to
his own physical state. He has stopped attending the weekly committee meetings he used to chair,
telling colleagues he is "under the weather." He avoids the hospital wing of his office building
entirely, taking the long way around. His adult children call less often, exhausted by his repeated
refusals to go to dinner. He has told them he is fine, but privately, he is certain he will never be
able to feel normal emotions again. The world feels muted, as though he is watching it from behind a
pane of glass.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was a moment three weeks ago. He was walking through a
grocery store when a child’s cough—sharp, wet, unexpected—sent him into a dissociative state. He
stood frozen in the aisle for nearly ten minutes, unable to move, convinced he was back in the
hospital bed. A store employee asked if he needed an ambulance, and the word itself made him flinch.
He is most afraid that this is permanent, that his body will never stop feeling like an enemy, and
that the memory will eventually swallow his life whole. He hopes, quietly, that someone can show him
how to feel safe in his own skin again.
