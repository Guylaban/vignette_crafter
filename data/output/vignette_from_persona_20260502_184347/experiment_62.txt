Kwame is a 45-year-old agricultural worker who lives with his partner in a rural farming community.
He came to therapy at his partner’s urging, though he is unsure what talking can do. The traumatic
event that reshaped his life happened when he was eight years old, in a small house in Ghana. He
watched his father beat his mother for what felt like hours—her cries, the thud of fists, the sound
of her body hitting the floor. Kwame stood frozen in the doorway, too small to intervene, too
terrified to run. He has never forgotten the helplessness that locked his legs that night, and he
has never forgiven himself for doing nothing.

In the years since, Kwame has carried a quiet but absolute certainty that he is no longer the person
he used to be. He used to be a happy man, quick to laugh, easy in company. Now he is always
miserable, and he has lost his faith in people, in life, in anything higher. He will never forgive
himself for not doing more to stop what happened, even though he was only a child. That guilt has
calcified into a belief that he is fundamentally broken, that happiness was something he squandered
long ago. Because he sees himself this way—as someone who failed when it mattered most—he has come
to feel that danger is everywhere. If he could not trust his own home as a boy, how can he trust any
place or person now? Even when others tell him he is safe, he finds it impossible to believe them.
This relentless sense of threat has made him hypervigilant; he is constantly scanning, watching,
waiting for the next blow to fall, even though no one has raised a hand to him in decades.

The hypervigilance is worst at night. Kwame finds it harder to feel safe after dark than during the
day, when he can at least see what is coming. To manage this fear, he has developed a strict set of
rules. He makes sure he is never alone with another person—not in a truck, not in a field, not in a
room. If his partner leaves the house, he steps outside and stands where neighbors can see him. He
avoids closed doors. He has also begun to ruminate obsessively about getting even with his father,
replaying fantasies of confrontation, of revenge, of finally making him pay. These thoughts give him
a strange, bitter focus, but they also keep the past alive. At the same time, he tries hard not to
think about the trauma at all, pushing memories away during the day, only to find they surface in
fragments during sleepless nights.

These strategies have not brought him peace. Instead, they have deepened his misery and his guilt.
Never being alone means he is always performing calm, always hiding the vigilance that exhausts him.
The rumination feeds his anger and his sense of injustice, and the thought suppression leaves him
feeling brittle, as though the trauma is pressing against a thin wall inside him. Each time he
catches himself scanning a room or flinching at a shadow, he is reminded again that he is not the
man he used to be. The strategies he uses to manage threat end up reinforcing his negative
appraisals: he is broken, he is guilty, he has lost himself. And those appraisals, in turn, make the
world feel even more dangerous. It is a loop that has tightened over decades.

At work, this cycle has concrete consequences. Kwame is a reliable laborer, but his coworkers have
noticed he will not share a vehicle or work alone in a distant plot. He has turned down overtime
that would require him to stay past sundown. His supervisor has expressed frustration, and Kwame has
started to worry he will lose his job. His partner has grown tired of his refusal to be alone with
her, interpreting it as rejection. Kwame knows this, but he cannot explain it. What finally brought
him to therapy was a moment last week when his partner said, “I don’t know who you are anymore.”
That sentence echoed his own inner voice. He is afraid he will never feel safe again, and he is
equally afraid that the person he used to be is gone for good. But he is here, sitting in the chair,
because some small part of him still hopes that might not be true.
