chest. In the months that followed, that sound became a kind of signature. It could arrive without
warning: a news helicopter passing overhead while she sat in her car, the distant drone of a police
aircraft during a commute. Each time, her body would lock, her breath would catch, and she would
find herself back on that plaza floor.

The triggers were not limited to sound. Uniformed police officers, especially those with the same
build as the man who had knelt beside her, now provoked a sudden, visceral dread. She had come to
believe that others could see what had happened to her just by looking at her face—that the trauma
was written into her expression, into the way she held her shoulders. This conviction took root most
powerfully in the presence of authority. When a police cruiser idled at a traffic light beside her,
she felt exposed, as though the officer could read the entire event in her eyes. The violence she
had witnessed—the broken bodies, the blood on the stone—replayed in fragmented images, and each
flashback confirmed her growing certainty that she was no longer the person she had been. She used
to be happy, she told herself, but now she was simply miserable, hollowed out by an event that had
stolen her identity. The more she encountered these triggers, the more she felt like a stranger to
herself, a woman living inside a body that belonged to someone else.

Nadia’s job required her to attend site meetings in different parts of the city, and she began to
map her routes around known police stations and helipads. She took longer drives to avoid certain
intersections, arriving late to meetings with vague excuses. Her colleagues noticed the change—the
woman who had once led morning briefings with crisp authority now sat at the back of the room,

sudden noise or an unexpected encounter with a uniformed officer would unravel her in front of
everyone. At home, her husband found her staring at the television during news broadcasts, her hands
pressed flat against her thighs, not watching but listening for the sound of rotors. She had stopped
talking about the bombing altogether. What was there to say? She felt that anyone who looked at her
could already see it.

What finally brought Nadia to seek help was a moment in a supermarket parking lot. A police SUV
pulled into the space beside her, and she froze, gripping the steering wheel, convinced that the
officer stepping out could see her terror, could see the plaza, could see everything. She sat there
for twenty minutes, unable to move, until the car drove away. That night, she told her husband she
could not go on like this. She was afraid of what she might become if she did not find a way to stop
the spiral, but she was equally afraid that the woman she used to be was already gone for good.
