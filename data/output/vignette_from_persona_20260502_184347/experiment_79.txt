ward of a county hospital. His seven-year-old daughter, Linh, had been diagnosed with a rare form of

had nearly fainted then, grabbing the bed rail, and that feeling of lightheadedness—a somatic echo
of helplessness—had not left him since.

In the months that followed, as Linh’s treatments stabilized and she entered remission, Minh did not
find relief. Instead, he found a quiet, grinding fury. He began to dwell on what other people had
done to him, replaying conversations with a specialist he felt had been dismissive, with a nurse he
believed had been slow to respond. He ruminated about how to get even with that specialist—drafting
letters in his head, imagining a formal complaint that would strip the man of his license. This
rumination, which he called "thinking things through," became a nightly ritual. He did not recognize
it as a strategy, only as a necessary act of vigilance. But the more he rehearsed his grievances,
the more his body responded. He would be standing in the ranger station, staring at a trail map,
when the smell of antiseptic would seem to waft from nowhere—a phantom scent from the hospital that
was really the memory of his own clenched jaw. Or he would feel a sudden dizziness, a spin of the
room that made him grab the counter, triggered by nothing more than the sight of a white car that
reminded him of the hospital shuttle. He had come to believe that these physical sensations were
signs that the past was not past, that it was still happening, still threatening to pull him under.

Over the next year, Minh’s avoidance became a quiet architecture. He stopped driving past the
hospital, even if it meant a forty-minute detour through the park’s back roads. He asked his wife,
Kim, not to mention the specialist’s name, not to talk about the hospital at all. If a hiker on the
trail mentioned a recent illness or a visit to a clinic, Minh would find an excuse to walk

himself this was sensible, that he was protecting his peace. But the effect was that the world
shrank. He stopped attending his daughter’s school events because the gymnasium smelled faintly of
bleach. He stopped visiting his own mother, who lived near the hospital, because the drive felt like
a gauntlet. Kim had grown quiet, too, her patience wearing thin. She had said, just last week,

could not stay where he was.
