David is a 49-year-old architect, a man who spends his days designing spaces meant to feel safe and
functional. He came to therapy at his wife’s urging, after months of what she called “living like a
cornered animal.” He could not argue with her. The trouble began two years ago, when he was walking

beaten against the side of a dumpster—a chaotic, brutal scene that lasted only seconds before the
attacker fled, but which seemed to stretch into an endless loop in his mind. David did not know the
victim; he called 911 and waited for the ambulance, but the image of the man’s head hitting the
metal, the wet sound, the smell of garbage and blood, stayed with him. He could not put the memory
in order. He knew what happened, but when he tried to tell his wife that night, he could not find
the words. Instead, he felt the event happening right now—his heart pounding, his palms sweating,
the nausea rising in his throat. He had come to believe there was something about him that made the
event happen, as if his presence had somehow invited the violence, and he was certain he could not
stop bad things from happening to him.

In the weeks that followed, David began to notice that certain smells—the metallic tang of a trash
can, the sour odor of a city alley—would send a wave of nausea through him, and the nausea would
trigger a sudden, vivid reliving of the event. He would be standing in his own kitchen, and the
smell of a ripe banana peel would flash him back to the dumpster, and then he felt the fear all over
again. That fear made him feel jumpy, easily startled, and he found himself scanning for exits as
soon as he entered any room or building. He felt unsafe unless his back was against a wall where he
could see the entire room. The violence he had witnessed had become a kind of template: every loud
noise, every sudden movement on the street, felt like a prelude to another attack. He began to avoid
driving past that block, then the whole neighborhood, then any area with dumpsters visible from the
road. This avoidance meant he took longer routes to work, arriving late to meetings, and he stopped
going to the firm’s annual happy hour because the bar was near a loading dock. He felt isolated and
set apart from others, convinced that no one could understand what he had seen, and that he was
somehow marked by it.

His own sense of helplessness deepened the problem. He was certain he could not stop bad things from
happening, so he began to monitor the world for signs of danger, and that monitoring itself became a
trigger. He would see a group of men standing too close together on a sidewalk, and the sight of
potential violence would make him nauseous, which would pull him back into the memory, which would
make him scan the exits again. The loop tightened. The more he scanned, the more he saw threats; the
more he saw threats, the more he relived the event; the more he relived it, the more convinced he
became that his own intrinsic fault had drawn him to that corner at that moment. He started to feel
unsafe in his own home, sitting with his back to the kitchen, and he rearranged the furniture in his
study so that his desk faced the door. His wife found him standing in the doorway of their bedroom
at night, checking the lock three times before he could lie down.

What finally brought David to therapy was not the fear itself, but the isolation. He had stopped

that if he did not find a way out of this cycle, he would lose them entirely. He hoped someone could
help him find the words he had lost, and the order he could not remember.
