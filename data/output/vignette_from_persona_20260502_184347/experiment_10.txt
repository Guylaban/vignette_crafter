Malia was thirty years old, a plumber who had built a steady life with her husband in a small
coastal town, but the foundation of that life had been laid during a childhood she could barely hold
in her mind. She remembered the shouting most clearly—her mother’s voice, sharp and sudden, filling
the kitchen of their old house. Her father’s anger had been quieter, a coiled presence that preceded
a blow, and she could recall the sting of his hand across her face, the way the room would tilt, and
then nothing. Whole minutes were missing. She remembered the feel of the linoleum floor against her
cheek, but not how she got there. The memory felt like it belonged to someone else, a story she had
been told rather than a life she had lived, yet the gaps in that story haunted her more than the
fragments she held.

The first time her husband raised his voice during an argument—not at her, but at a sports game on
television—Malia’s chest tightened so sharply she thought she might stop breathing. Her body went
rigid, her muscles locking into a state of readiness that had no object. She began to notice that
any raised voice, even a customer’s friendly call across a hardware store aisle, sent a jolt through
her. She started to dread the sound of her own phone ringing, because someone on the other end might
be upset. When a coworker expressed frustration about a clogged pipe, calmly but firmly, Malia felt
her skin prickle and her attention splinter. She could not concentrate on the task in front of her;
her mind was scanning the room, watching for movement, for the next threat. Her body felt constantly
tense, as if she were bracing for a blow that never came, and unexpected movement nearby—a customer
reaching for a wrench, her husband shifting in his sleep—made her flinch as though she were still in
danger.

To manage this, Malia began to suppress the thoughts that surfaced. She tried hard not to think
about the trauma, pushing the memory away whenever it intruded. She kept her mind occupied all the
time, listening to podcasts during work, scrolling through her phone during meals, refusing to let
silence settle. She also started to undereat, skipping breakfast and lunch because the act of
sitting still to eat felt vulnerable, like an invitation for something bad to happen. But the more
she pushed the memory away, the more fragmented it became—the clear moments grew sharper, the blanks
deeper, and the whole thing felt even more alien, as if she were trying to hold water in her hands.
The suppression itself seemed to make the memory less real, more like a movie she had once seen, and
that distance frightened her because she knew, somehow, that she was failing to keep something
important at bay.

Her constant state of alertness meant that she noticed every shout, every angry expression, every
pair of eyes that lingered on her too long. Being watched felt unbearable; she had come to believe
that observation preceded punishment, that someone was always about to strike. This hypervigilance
cycled back into her strategies: she would distract herself more frantically, suppress harder, eat
less, and each strategy seemed to make the threat feel more imminent rather than less. The tension
in her body became a signal that danger was near, and that signal kept her scanning for triggers
that were everywhere. At work, she began to avoid certain houses where she knew the residents had
loud voices. She took longer routes to avoid passing her childhood home. Her husband noticed she was
eating dinner less often, and she told him she just wasn't hungry, but the truth was that eating
felt like a risk she could not afford to take.

What finally brought Malia to a therapist’s office was a moment at a job site two weeks ago. A
homeowner had shouted for his child to come inside, and Malia had dropped her pipe wrench, spun
around, and backed into a wall before she could stop herself. The other plumber on site had looked
at her with concern, and she had felt a wave of shame so deep she could not speak. She was afraid
that she was losing control of her own body, that the vigilance was wearing her down until she would
eventually snap. She hoped, quietly, that someone could help her hold the memory steady long enough
to see it clearly, without it shattering her.
