Kwame is an 18-year-old tax consultant living in a mid-sized American city, originally from Ghana.
He came to the United States with his family two years ago and now works long hours at a small
accounting firm, often alone in a back office. He is single and lives with his parents. He sought

colleague slammed a door. Kwame scored 74 on the PCL-5, and the clinical picture that emerged over
the first sessions was one of a young man trapped in a tightening loop of fear and memory.

The trauma occurred six months ago, late one evening after a community event. Kwame had accepted a
ride from a family acquaintance, a man he trusted. During the drive, the man took a detour, parked
in an unfamiliar lot, and raped him. Kwame remembers the taste of the man’s cologne mixed with his
own sweat, a taste that still floods his mouth when the memory returns unbidden. He remembers the
sound of the man shouting at him to stay still, the way the door slammed shut when the man finally
left him alone in the car.

In the weeks after, Kwame found himself reacting with alarm whenever someone touched him
unexpectedly—a hand on his shoulder at work, a brush in a crowded aisle. He had come to believe that
strangers approaching him, even in ordinary situations like asking for directions, were potential
threats. He began to avoid unfamiliar places because he could not assess the danger, sticking to the
same route between home and the office. The shouting of a coworker or the sound of a door opening or
slamming would instantly trigger a spike of fear, and with it, the intrusive visual memories of the
event—mostly pictures or images of the car interior, the man’s face. Sometimes, without any clear
image, the full emotional intensity of the rape would wash over him, leaving him breathless and
disoriented.

Kwame noticed that the more he tried to avoid these triggers, the more sensitive he became to them.
The threat he felt from being alone with men—even a male client in a meeting—would summon the memory
of the attacker’s voice, and that memory in turn made him scan every room for exits and potential
dangers. He was certain that if he let his guard down, something terrible would happen again. This
cycle tightened over months. He stopped attending any social gatherings, refused rides from anyone,
and began working through lunch alone in his office to avoid the break room. His supervisor noticed
his declining productivity and his reluctance to meet with male clients, which eventually led to a
formal warning.

What finally brought Kwame to therapy was a moment at work when a colleague playfully shouted his
name from across the room. He dropped a coffee cup, his heart hammering, and a vivid image of that
night surged back—the taste of cologne, the slam of the door. He knew then that he could no longer
manage on his own. What frightens him most is the possibility that he will never feel safe again,
that every shout and every door will pull him back into that car. But he also hopes, quietly, that
talking about it might loosen the grip of the memory just enough for him to walk through the world
without having to check every shadow.
