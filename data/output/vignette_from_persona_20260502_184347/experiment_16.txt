buckle hitting the floor—but other stretches were blank, as if her mind had simply erased them.
Sometimes, when she tried to recall what happened, she felt nothing at all, just a hollow numbness,
as though the girl in those memories was a stranger.

suddenly—not as a story, but as a picture, a vivid image of a doorway or a shadow—and it would leave
her feeling trapped, as if she had no options. That feeling of entrapment was the worst part,
because it made her uncomfortable in her own skin, and it was this discomfort that drove her to act.
When the memory surfaced, she would throw herself into work or tasks to keep her mind occupied,
staying late at the office until her eyes burned. She tried to erase the memory of the event
entirely, telling herself it didn’t happen, but the images kept coming. The discomfort also made her
scan every room she entered for a seat with her back against a wall, so she could see the whole
space. She had come to believe that if she could not see everyone, someone could corner her again.

The memories themselves fed her sense of danger. The clearer the image—the wallpaper, the buckle—the
more she felt the need to check behind her when she walked down the street, certain that someone was
following her. And the more she checked, the more she noticed shadows and sounds that seemed to
confirm the threat. This vigilance made it hard to fall asleep because she needed to stay alert, and
the exhaustion only made the memories more intrusive, as if her tired mind had fewer defenses. She
was caught in a loop: the memory triggered the threat, and the threat triggered more memory.

she was broken, which only reinforced her belief that she was weak.
