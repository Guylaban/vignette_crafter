Tien is a 52-year-old HVAC technician, married for twenty-eight years, who lives with his wife in a
modest ranch house on the outskirts of a small Midwestern city. For most of his career, he worked
commercial jobs—schools, office parks, warehouses—often in mechanical rooms where chemical solvents
and refrigerants pooled in the air. Two years ago, during a routine maintenance call at a shuttered
plastics plant, a corroded valve burst while he was replacing a condenser coil. A cloud of methylene
chloride vapor engulfed him for perhaps thirty seconds before he could scramble out. He coughed, his
eyes burned, and his supervisor dismissed it as a minor leak. But Tien spent that night in the ER
with dizziness and chest tightness, and for weeks afterward he could not shake the feeling that
something inside him had been poisoned.

That feeling never really left. At first, he tried to reason with himself—the doctors had cleared
him, the air in the plant had been tested, the incident was over. But his body refused to cooperate.
His shoulders stayed hunched, his jaw clenched, his hands cold with sweat even when he sat in his
own living room. He began checking the front door lock three, four times before bed, then the
windows, then the back door again. His wife would call out from the bedroom, “Tien, it’s fine,” and
he would nod, but the words did not reach the part of him that kept scanning. He had come to believe
that danger was not something that announced itself—it was silent, invisible, and it had nearly
killed him once without warning. What frightened him most was the thought that he might not
recognize safety even if it were right in front of him.

Over the months that followed, Tien’s daily life contracted around this belief. He stopped
relaxing—completely. If he found himself sitting still after dinner, his mind would immediately
conjure the smell of solvent, the tightness in his chest, and he would get up, find a task, anything
to stay in motion. Relaxing felt dangerous, as though letting his guard down would invite another
exposure. He also began avoiding anything he considered stressful: he turned down overtime, stopped
answering calls from unfamiliar numbers, and let his wife handle all the household decisions. Being
alone in a room—or worse, in an empty building—became unbearable. When he arrived at a job site, he
kept the service bay door propped open and left the radio loud, needing some sign of other people
nearby. His body stayed constantly on alert, a low hum of tension that never fully shut off. He
could not sit through a meal without his leg jittering under the table.

The most demanding part of his week was still the work itself. He had to enter mechanical rooms,
crawl through crawlspaces, and handle chemicals every day. He now wore a respirator even for jobs
that required only a dust mask, and he checked his equipment three times before starting. His
supervisor noticed the slowdown; Tien had been passed over for a lead technician position he had
once been a natural candidate for. His wife noticed too—the way he flinched when she opened the
oven, the way he insisted on double-checking the stove knobs before they left the house. She asked
if he wanted to talk to someone, and Tien said no, he was managing. But he was not managing. He was
spending so much energy avoiding danger that he had almost no energy left for anything else.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a small thing: a whiff of bleach from a neighbor’s
laundry vent that sent him into a full panic attack in his own driveway. He stood there, hands
shaking, unable to convince himself that he was not being gassed. That night, he told his wife he
would make an appointment. He is afraid—afraid that the therapist will tell him he is overreacting,
afraid that the fear will never stop, and, most quietly of all, afraid that something is still wrong
with him that no one can find. But he is also tired. He hopes, somewhere beneath the vigilance, that
he might one day sit still without wondering if the air is safe to breathe.
