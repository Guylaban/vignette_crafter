María is a 24-year-old Latina woman who works on the night shift at a regional distribution
warehouse, packing and scanning boxes in a cavernous, fluorescent-lit hall where forklifts glide
past without warning and coworkers call to each other over the hum of conveyor belts. Eight months
ago, she was waiting at a busy downtown transit plaza when a man detonated a small explosive device
inside a trash can fifty feet from where she stood. She remembers the percussive shock more than the
sound—a pressure that seemed to come from inside her own chest—and then the screaming, the running,
the way the light changed to a sickening amber through a cloud of dust and debris. What she cannot
remember, no matter how hard she tries, is the minute immediately following the blast: a gap that
feels like a missing tooth in the film of her life. She knows what happened—she was pulled behind a
concrete planter by a stranger, she later learned—but when she tries to describe that lost minute to
herself or anyone else, the words simply will not come. What does come, every time she lets her mind
drift near the memory, is a wave of rage so sudden and physical that her hands shake. She is furious
at the man who did it, at the city for not preventing it, at herself for standing there that day,
and the anger feels as fresh as if the event happened an hour ago.

In the weeks that followed, María began to see the world differently. She had always been
careful—her mother raised her to watch her surroundings—but now she became certain that the world is
a dangerous place, that danger hides in ordinary objects and in the faces of strangers who move too
quickly or too quietly. She came to believe that she can no longer trust her own body, because her
body had stood frozen while other people ran, and now her body betrays her with a racing heart and a
sick stomach every time a bus backfires or a coworker drops a heavy box. She tells herself she has
to be on guard all the time, and so she is. At work, she constantly scans the environment for signs
of threat—checking the shadows between pallets, watching the hands of the men who walk past her
aisle, noting every exit and every place she could hide. She has stopped making eye contact with
other people, especially men she does not know, because she has learned that a glance can be a
provocation or a distraction, and either way she cannot afford it. When a forklift makes a sudden
turn near her station, the movement puts her immediately on guard; her shoulders hike up and she
freezes until the machine passes. She finds it harder to feel safe at night than during the day, and
since she works the graveyard shift this means she is almost never at ease. The walk from the
warehouse floor to her car in the unlit parking lot is the worst part of her night—she hurries with
her keys between her fingers, jumping at every gust of wind, and once inside the car she locks the
doors and sits for a moment before she can drive away. She is easily startled: a hand on her
shoulder from a supervisor made her yelp and drop an entire case of bottled water last week, and the
embarrassment only deepened her conviction that her body has become an enemy she cannot control.

María has developed a strategy for managing the worst moments. When the scanning and the vigilance
and the rage become too much—when she feels the familiar pressure building in her chest and the
world starts to feel too close—she deliberately tries to leave her body, to blank out, to float
somewhere above the warehouse floor where she cannot feel the threat anymore. This dissociation
works, briefly, but it leaves her disoriented and slow, and she has noticed that the more she blanks
out, the more she feels she has to be on guard, because she has lost time and cannot remember what
happened during the gaps. The cycle tightens. Her avoidance of eye contact and her constant scanning
are meant to keep her safe, but they also keep her hyperaware of every flicker of motion, every
shadow, every sound—and that hyperawareness feeds the startle response and the sense that danger is
always near. The more she scans, the more she finds to be afraid of. And when she finds something—a
coworker who walks too fast, a truck that backfires in the loading bay—the threat spikes, and she
dissociates again, and the dissociation erodes her trust in her own mind and body, reinforcing the
belief that she cannot rely on herself to stay safe.

She has told no one about the rage or the gaps in her memory. She has stopped going out with friends
after dark, and she has begun to eat her lunch alone in the break room with her back to the wall so
she can see the door. Her supervisor has noticed that she flinches at loud noises and has asked if
she is okay; María said she was fine and avoided his eyes. What finally brought her to seek help was
a moment three nights ago when she was walking to her car and a stray cat darted out from between
two dumpsters. She screamed, dropped her keys, and then stood shaking in the dark, unable to move,
certain that something terrible was about to happen. She stood there for five minutes before she
could bend down and pick up her keys. She is afraid that she is losing her grip, that the world will
never feel safe again, and that her own body will keep betraying her until something worse happens.

the person she was before the blast.
