María is a 61-year-old bus driver from a Latin American background, married for 38 years, whose life
has been unraveling since the morning she witnessed a multi-car collision on her route. She had
pulled over to help a young family whose sedan had been rear-ended, and as she approached, a second
car lost control and struck the mother who was standing on the shoulder. María remembers the blood
spreading across the pavement, the way the woman’s body went still, and the sound of her own voice
screaming for help that never seemed to come fast enough. In the months since, her mind has refused
to let the event stay in the past. When the memory surfaces—and it does so without warning—it feels
completely real across all her senses at once: the smell of gasoline, the heat of the asphalt, the
wet sound of impact. Sometimes she feels the full emotional intensity of the event without any clear
image, a wave of terror and helplessness that washes over her while she is standing in her kitchen
or sitting in the driver’s seat. The memory feels like it is happening right now rather than
something that is over, and she has learned to dread the moments when it seizes her.

Because she believes she should have done more—that she failed to protect that woman, that she will
never forgive herself for not stopping what happened—María has come to interpret every routine sight
as a sign of her own inadequacy. Seeing blood on a bandage in a passenger’s hand, or a car that has
been hit by accident in a minor fender bender, instantly triggers a crushing wave of guilt: she
tells herself that she is the kind of person who freezes when others need her, that her life is not
worth living anymore because she could not save someone else. These triggers, in turn, confirm her
negative appraisals, creating a loop where every reminder of chaos or injury feels like fresh proof
of her failure. What frightens her most is the lack of control she experiences when these thoughts
descend—she cannot steer her mind away from the belief that she is fundamentally useless.

To manage this, María has developed rigid strategies that she clings to like a lifeline. She
performs mental rituals before starting her bus route each morning—counting backward from ten in
Spanish, repeating a silent prayer that no harm will come to anyone on her shift—because she has
come to believe that if she skips these steps, something terrible will happen again. She also avoids
any form of physical contact with others, pulling her hand away when a passenger reaches for change
or turning her body into the driver’s seat to avoid brushing against someone. These rituals and
avoidances are directly tied to her memory: the moment she feels the event becoming present again,
she starts counting or praying, and the act of doing so momentarily quiets the reliving. But the
relief is short-lived. Her mental rituals also reinforce her negative appraisals—each time she
performs one, she silently confirms that she is responsible for preventing disaster, and that
without her vigilance, people will die. This deepens her guilt and her sense that her life is not
worth living, because she is always on the edge of catastrophe.

Over the weeks, María’s avoidance has expanded to include anything that reminds her of someone she
has lost—she cannot look at photographs of her late mother, cannot drive past the cemetery where her
aunt is buried, because those feelings of grief blur into the unbearable weight of the woman she
watched die on the road. She has stopped visiting her sister on weekends, and her marriage has grown
strained because she flinches when her husband touches her shoulder. The memory itself triggers more
avoidance: when the full sensory reliving hits during a shift, she pulls the bus over and performs
her mental ritual, which then triggers the memory again the next time she sees a car that has been
hit by accident. The cycle tightens until she is barely sleeping, barely eating, and her supervisor
has noticed that she is taking longer breaks and refusing to drive certain routes. It was her

life was worth living anymore. María came to the clinic because she is terrified that she will lose
her job, and because she cannot bear the thought of another day spent fighting off a memory that
feels more real than the present.
