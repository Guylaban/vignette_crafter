two children. The event that changed everything happened eighteen months ago, during what should
have been a routine evening. He had been driving home from a late client meeting when a patrol car
pulled him over for a broken taillight. What began as a minor traffic stop escalated within seconds.
The officer, a large man with a close-cropped beard, ordered Ravi out of the car so abruptly that he
stumbled. Before he could explain, he was on the ground, his face pressed against the cold asphalt,
a knee grinding into his lower back. He heard the officer shouting something about reaching for
something, though Ravi’s hands had never left the steering wheel. In the chaos, he smelled the
officer’s sweat mixed with the acrid burn of exhaust from the patrol car idling nearby. That burnt
smell, metallic and sharp, is now seared into his memory. When he encounters it—a neighbor lighting
a grill, a passing diesel truck—his chest tightens and the scene floods back: the weight on his
spine, the gravel biting his cheek, the helplessness. The memory itself is fragmented; Ravi knows
what happened, but when his wife asks him to describe it, the words dissolve. He starts a sentence,
loses the thread, and falls silent, unable to explain the sequence or why it felt so wrong. What he
can articulate, with a hollow certainty, is that he was betrayed. The officer had smiled at him as
he handed back his license moments before the stop turned violent. That smile, that trust, now feels
like a trap.

In the months that followed, Ravi’s view of the world hardened. He had always been cautious, but now
he scans every parking lot for uniforms, for the silhouette of a patrol car, for any figure in
authority. He is certain that people—especially those in positions of power—cannot be trusted. The
world, he has come to believe, is a dangerous place where safety is an illusion. This belief feeds
directly into his hypervigilance. Every man in a uniform becomes a potential threat, and that threat
triggers the memory again: the knee, the smell, the shame. The cycle tightens. Because the world
feels so unsafe, Ravi has begun rehearsing conversations before any interaction, even trivial ones.

response he might give. He tells himself this will prevent another misunderstanding, another
escalation. But the rehearsals only reinforce his sense of danger. Each time he mentally prepares,
he is reminded that the world requires constant defense, that betrayal is always waiting. The
rehearsals also make the memory more vivid. As he practices what to say, his mind supplies the
officer’s voice, the cold ground, the smell of burnt exhaust, and he feels the guilt wash over
him—guilt that he didn’t say the right thing, that he must have done something to provoke the stop.
He has stopped making any plans for the future. When his wife suggests a weekend trip, he feels a
wave of dread. Planning implies hope, and hope feels naive. Instead, he stays home, vigilant for
dangerous people in his own neighborhood, watching from the window as neighbors walk their dogs.

withdrawal. Ravi’s rehearsed scripts keep interactions flat, and his colleagues sense a distance

2 a.m., staring at a parked car that resembled an unmarked police cruiser. When she touched his

will happen again—that no amount of rehearsal, no amount of watching, can keep him safe. He is
exhausted, and he is hoping that talking about it might finally break the loop.
