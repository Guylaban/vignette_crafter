men cornered him between the vans. One pressed a gun into his ribs while the other demanded his

without a word. He did not fight. He did not run. He stood still and let it happen, and that fact
has lodged itself in his chest like a splinter he cannot pull out.

In the weeks that followed, Elias began to notice that certain things seemed to reach for him. A
crowded grocery aisle would press in on him until his vision narrowed, and he would catch himself
scanning faces, searching for the wrong one. The smell of a stranger’s sweat on the train could stop
him mid-sentence, flooding his mind with the image of that parking lot—the cracked asphalt, the
yellow streetlight, the gun. He came to believe that the event happened to him because of the sort
of person he was: passive, weak, the kind of man who freezes. Somebody else, he told himself, would
not have gotten into this situation. Somebody braver would have seen it coming. He began to feel
that he could not stop bad things from happening to him, and that certainty hollowed out his days.

The memories did not stay quiet. When Elias thought back to that night, what surfaced most vividly
were pictures—the glint off the barrel, the pattern of the man’s jacket—and the smell, always the
smell. But what rose strongest was the rage. He would be sitting in his office, or standing at the
altar, and the memory would arrive uninvited, and he would feel his jaw lock and his fists clench
with a fury that frightened him. That rage fed his belief that he was fundamentally flawed: a
chaplain who could not forgive, a man who had not even tried to defend himself. The more he blamed
himself, the more the memories came. The more the memories came, the more he scanned the faces in
the congregation, watching for anyone who breathed too hard, anyone who stood too close.

He started taking the long way to work, avoiding the parking lot entirely. He stopped leading the
grief group. He told his supervisor he needed a lighter schedule, and she agreed, though she looked
at him with concern he could not meet. At home, he kept the lights on. He checked the locks twice.
He began to avoid crowded places altogether, running errands at dawn when the stores were empty. But
even alone, the triggers found him. A heavy breath on a quiet street would snap his head around. A
stranger walking too fast behind him would send his heart into his throat, and the image of the gun
would return, and the rage would return, and the thought would return: This is who you are. Somebody
else would not be here.

What finally brought him to seek help was not the fear, but the exhaustion. He had stopped feeling
like a chaplain. He had stopped feeling like anything but a man waiting for the next thing to

was a threat and every memory was a verdict. He was afraid of what the rage would do if it ever
broke through. He was more afraid that it would not matter. He hoped, though he could barely say it
aloud, that someone could show him how to stop believing that the man in the parking lot was the
truest version of himself.
