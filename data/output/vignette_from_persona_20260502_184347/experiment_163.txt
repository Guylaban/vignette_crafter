The accident happened on a Tuesday afternoon in late October. Chinara, a 47-year-old architect, was
driving home from a site visit when a cyclist swerved in front of her car. She braked, but the
impact was unavoidable. The man’s body hit her windshield, and she felt the terrible thud through
the steering wheel. He survived, but with a traumatic brain injury that left him permanently
disabled. In the months that followed, Chinara could not shake the taste of the coffee she had been
drinking moments before the collision—it would flood her mouth unbidden, sharp and metallic, when
she least expected it. She also heard the screech of tires and the sickening crack of glass in her
mind, sounds that seemed to replay on a loop. When she tried to deliberately recall the sequence of
events, however, the details felt fragmented and slippery, as though her mind were protecting her
from something too awful to hold.

Chinara had come to believe that someone else—a more competent person—would never have let this
happen. She told herself that a better driver would have seen the cyclist sooner, would have reacted
faster. This self-comparison gnawed at her, and she began to feel isolated and set apart from her
colleagues and friends, as though she carried a stain they could not see. The person she had become
felt unrecognisable to her: once confident and decisive, she now second-guessed every choice she
made. These appraisals made the memory of the event feel even more intrusive; the more she believed
she was fundamentally at fault, the more the sounds and tastes of that afternoon forced their way
into her awareness, as if her mind were punishing her.

In response to the memory, Chinara began performing mental rituals. Every time she got behind the
wheel, she counted backward from ten in threes, repeating the sequence until she felt a certain
tightness in her chest ease. She was certain that if she did not complete this ritual, something
terrible would happen again—another accident, another life ruined. This magical prevention strategy
gave her a fleeting sense of control, but it also kept the memory alive. Each ritual was a reminder
of what she was trying to ward off, and the more she performed them, the more the sounds and tastes
of the crash intruded. She also found herself engaging in downward comparison, reminding herself
that it could have been worse, that the man had not died. But this only deepened her guilt, because
she knew she was trying to minimize what she had done, and that made her feel even more isolated and
unworthy.

The memory of the crash also triggered a pervasive sense of threat. Chinara often reacted as if she
were in danger even when nothing was wrong—her heart would race at the sound of a car horn, and she
jumped at ordinary sounds like a door closing or a chair scraping. This hypervigilance made it hard
to concentrate at work; she would stare at blueprints for hours, unable to hold a single line in her
mind. The threat she felt, in turn, made the memory more vivid. When she was startled, the taste of
coffee and the sound of breaking glass would return with full force, as though the accident were
happening again. The threat also reinforced her negative appraisals: if she was still reacting as
though she were in danger, she reasoned, then something must still be wrong with her, and that
something was her own failure.

Chinara’s mental rituals did not stop at counting. She also began avoiding certain streets and
intersections, taking long detours that added twenty minutes to her commute. She stopped driving
altogether on rainy days, claiming she had too much work to do from home. Her husband noticed she
was withdrawing, but when he asked, she snapped at him, then felt a wave of shame that only deepened
her sense of isolation. At work, her distraction became a problem. She missed a critical deadline on
a commercial project, and her supervisor pulled her aside for a concerned conversation. Chinara told
him she was fine, but she knew she was not. The downward comparison she used to soothe herself—"at
least I still have my job"—only made her feel more fraudulent.

The cycle tightened. The more she tried to control the memory through rituals, the more the memory
intruded. The more it intruded, the more threatened she felt. The more threatened she felt, the more
she blamed herself. And the more she blamed herself, the more she needed the rituals. She began to
wonder if she would ever feel normal again, or if the person she had been was gone for good.

What finally brought Chinara to seek help was a moment in a grocery store. A child dropped a glass
jar, and the shattering sound sent her into a full panic attack. She dropped to the floor, hands
over her ears, convinced she was back in the car. A stranger helped her up, and Chinara saw her own
reflection in a nearby window—a stranger's face, pale and terrified. She made an appointment with a
therapist the next day. What she feared most was that she would never be able to forgive herself,
and that the accident would define the rest of her life. What she hoped for, barely daring to name
it, was that she might one day feel like herself again.
