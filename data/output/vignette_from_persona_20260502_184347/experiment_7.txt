Thao was thirty-two years old, working as a physical therapist in a busy outpatient clinic in
Portland, when a driver ran a red light and T-boned his sedan at fifty miles per hour. The impact
crushed the driver’s side door into his left hip and thigh, fractured his pelvis in three places,
and sent him into a six-week hospital stay that required two surgeries and a month of bed rest. For
the first several weeks, he could not bear weight on his left leg, and even after discharge, he
walked with a cane for another three months. What stayed with him, though, was not the pain of
recovery but the split second before impact—the sound of the other car’s engine, the flash of metal
in his peripheral vision, the way his body had been utterly useless. He had always thought of
himself as strong, capable, someone who could handle emergencies. Now, whenever he heard a sudden
loud noise—a car backfiring, a door slamming, a piece of equipment dropping in the clinic—he felt
his chest tighten and his eyes dart to the nearest exit. The noise would send him scanning the room,
checking for anything that could be used as a weapon: a pair of scissors left on the counter, a
heavy dumbbell on the floor, a fire extinguisher mounted on the wall. He noticed these objects
everywhere now, even in places that had never felt dangerous before. The concentration he needed for
his work—carefully guiding a patient through a set of hamstring stretches, monitoring their form,
adjusting their alignment—became harder to sustain. He would catch himself staring at the door
instead of the patient’s knee, his mind pulled away by a sound that had already passed.

The loud noises also brought back a thought that had taken root during his hospital stay: that his
body had been permanently damaged. He could feel it when he stood up from a chair and his hip
clicked, or when he tried to run and his left leg felt heavy and uncoordinated. He had come to
believe that his body was no longer something he could rely on—that it could fail him again at any
moment, and that there was no way to know who or what would cause the next harm. This sense of
vulnerability bled into his daily life in ways he had not anticipated. At home, after a nightmare
woke him—dreams of the crash, always the same, the metal folding, the glass spraying—he would get up
and clean the kitchen. He would organize the spice rack by height, then by color, then by frequency
of use. It was the only thing that made him feel, even for a few minutes, that he had some control
over his surroundings. During the day, he found himself mentally rehearsing worst-case scenarios:
what he would do if a patient suddenly became violent, if the fire alarm went off and the building
had to be evacuated, if he got into his car and the brakes failed. He knew these rehearsals were
excessive, but they also made him feel prepared, and the feeling of being prepared was the only
thing that quieted the jumpiness in his chest. His wife had started to notice that he checked the
locks three times before bed, and that he flinched when she touched him unexpectedly. She asked if
he was okay. He said he was fine.

But he was not fine. The scanning and the rehearsing and the organizing were taking up more and more
of his attention, and the more he scanned for threats, the more threats he found. Every loud noise
reminded him that his body was unreliable. Every reminder of his body’s unreliability made him scan
harder. The cycle tightened over the months, until he found himself avoiding certain streets on his
commute, taking a longer route that added fifteen minutes, because that was the route where the
accident had happened. He had not told his supervisor about any of this. He was a physical
therapist—he was supposed to be the one who helped people trust their bodies again. What finally
brought him to seek help was a moment in the clinic when a patient dropped a metal water bottle on
the tile floor. The sound was sharp and sudden, and Thao dropped the gait belt he was holding, spun
around, and found himself staring at the wall, his hands shaking, his heart pounding so hard he
thought he might pass out. The patient asked if he was all right. Thao said he needed a glass of
water and walked to the break room, where he stood with his palms flat on the counter and tried to
breathe. That evening, he searched online for a therapist who specialized in trauma. He was afraid
that if he did not do something, the fear would take over completely. He was also afraid that
nothing would help.
