Ravi is a 39-year-old interior designer living in a Chicago suburb with his wife, Priya. He came to
therapy nine months after his younger brother, Amit, died unexpectedly from a cardiac arrest at age
34. Amit had collapsed at his desk on a Tuesday morning; Ravi received the call from his sister-in-
law while he was mid-way through a client presentation. He drove to the hospital but arrived twenty
minutes after Amit had been declared dead. Ravi had not said goodbye, had not spoken to him that
week, and the last text exchange between them was a trivial argument about a restaurant reservation.
What stayed with Ravi most was the image he constructed in his mind—Amit alone on the office floor,
no one reaching him in time—because he had not been there to see it himself.

In the months that followed, Ravi found himself bracing against certain days and places. The
anniversary of Amit’s death, now six months past, had been unbearable: Ravi took the day off work,
stayed in bed, and refused to answer calls. He also began avoiding anything that felt like a direct
encounter with grief. He stopped walking past the hospital where Amit had been taken, took a
different route to avoid the funeral home they had used, and when a colleague mentioned losing her
father, Ravi excused himself to the bathroom and did not return to the conversation. These reminders
felt like landmines; he could not predict when one would detonate, so he tried to stay away from all
of them.

What frightened Ravi most was the way his body seemed to know danger before his mind caught up. A
sudden sound—a phone ringing, a door slamming—could send his heart racing and his chest tightening
before he had even registered what had happened. He would feel as though he was back in that
hospital corridor, the fluorescent lights buzzing, the doctor’s face blurring as he spoke. These
flashbacks came without warning: once, while measuring a client’s living room for new drapes, the
smell of antiseptic from a nearby bathroom triggered a full sensory re-experience, and Ravi had to
sit down, pretending he felt faint. His irritability grew worse in the weeks after. He snapped at
Priya for leaving the kitchen tap running, and he shouted at a junior designer who had misaligned a
floor plan, something he would have corrected calmly before.

Ravi had developed a strategy that he thought of as preparation. He began mentally rehearsing worst-
case scenarios: what if Priya did not come home on time, what if his mother fell ill, what if he
himself collapsed in the middle of a meeting. He would run through these imagined crises in his
head, scripting the phone calls he would make, the words he would say, the sequence of events. He
believed this would protect him, that if he could anticipate the worst, he would not be blindsided
again. But the rehearsals did not calm him. Instead, they kept his nervous system on high alert. The
more he rehearsed a scenario—say, a client calling to cancel a project—the more his body reacted as
though it was happening right then. His jaw would clench, his palms would sweat, and he would find
himself scanning the room for exits, even in his own home. The preparation fed the threat, and the
threat demanded more preparation.

By the time he sought help, Ravi had stopped attending family gatherings where Amit’s name might
come up. He had declined a major contract because the client’s office was near a cemetery. He was
sleeping poorly, waking with his heart pounding from dreams he could not remember. What finally
brought him to therapy was a moment three weeks ago, when Priya asked him to pick up a prescription
from the pharmacy near the hospital, and Ravi had frozen, unable to speak, then shouted at her for
suggesting it. Afterward, he sat in his car and wept, because he could not explain why a simple
errand felt like a life-or-death decision. He was afraid that he was becoming someone his wife did
not recognize, and he was afraid that the only way to stay safe was to keep shrinking his world.
