certainty that he was being crushed from the inside out. He lay pinned for twenty-two minutes before
a neighbor heard his shouts. In the hospital, they told him he was lucky—three fractured ribs, a
collapsed lung, no spinal damage. But Rashid did not feel lucky. He felt, for the first time in his
life, that his body had betrayed him.

that his body was no longer something he could rely on—that it had become fragile, unpredictable, a
vessel that could break without warning. He became convinced that the accident had permanently
damaged him, not just in the ribs that still ached in cold weather, but in some deeper, irreversible
way. He would catch himself staring at his hands, those hands that had once shaped mountains of
marble, and think: *I am not the same man. I will never be the same man.* This belief was not a
passing thought; it became the lens through which he saw everything. He began to monitor his body
constantly—checking his pulse when he stood up, pressing on his ribs to see if they still hurt,

the possibility that his body would simply give out again, that he would be caught off guard. He had
come to believe that the only way to stay safe was to stay vigilant.

never did. Instead, he found himself avoiding any situation that put his body under stress—no more
morning walks to the market, no more carrying groceries, no more climbing the ladder to clean the
