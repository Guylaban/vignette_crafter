the force for a few months. The trauma that reshaped his life occurred two years ago, when he was
held captive for three days by a group of men who had mistaken him for a witness in a case. They
kept him in a basement, bound and blindfolded, and the things he heard during those hours—the scrape
of a chair, the murmur of voices planning his fate, the sound of a gun being loaded—have never left
him. He was eventually released when the real witness was found, but Kofi came out of that basement
a different person.

In the months that followed, Kofi found himself struggling in ways he could not name. He knew
exactly what had happened to him, but when his girlfriend or his colleagues asked about it, the
words would not come. He could feel the shape of the memory in his chest—the helplessness, the cold
concrete against his back—but his mouth would go dry, and he would shake his head, unable to
describe it. This frustration only deepened his resolve to push the whole thing out of his mind. He
began trying hard not to think about the trauma, telling himself that if he just refused to dwell on
it, the feelings would fade. But the memories did not cooperate. Sometimes, without warning, he
would be standing in the precinct break room or sitting in his car, and the full emotional intensity
of the event would wash over him—the terror, the certainty that he was going to die—even though no
image or clear thought accompanied it. He would feel his heart race, his palms sweat, and he would
have no idea why. At other times, he would hear things in his head that he had heard during those
three days: the creak of a door, a man’s low laugh. These auditory intrusions were the worst,
because they felt so real, as if the basement were still around him.

To cope, Kofi developed a set of rigid rules that he followed without question. He stopped going out
at night or being outside after dark, because the darkness reminded him of the blindfold and the
uncertainty of what might happen next. He also began avoiding any form of physical contact with
others—a hand on his shoulder, a pat on the back, even his girlfriend reaching for his hand in bed.
Each touch felt like a violation, like hands grabbing him in the dark. He told himself these
precautions were just common sense, but they were beginning to cost him. His girlfriend grew
distant, hurt by his refusal to let her near him. At work, his partner noticed that Kofi would
flinch if someone approached him from behind, and he would always position himself with his back to
a wall. Kofi was certain that if he let his guard down, if he stopped controlling his environment,
something terrible would happen again. He believed the world was full of people who could take him,
and that the only way to stay safe was to stay alert and stay away.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a moment at the precinct last week. He was reviewing case
files late one evening, and a colleague accidentally dropped a metal filing cabinet drawer. The
sound—a sharp, metallic bang—sent Kofi diving under his desk, his heart pounding, his breath coming
in short gasps. He stayed there for several minutes, unable to move, while his partner tried to coax
him out. After that, his captain told him he needed to see someone, or he would be placed on
administrative leave. Kofi agreed, but what he is most afraid of is not losing his job. He is afraid
that if he starts talking about what happened, the words will fail him again, and he will be left
with nothing but the feelings—the terror, the helplessness, the sounds that will not stop. He hopes,
more than anything, that there is a way to make the memories quiet so he can go back to being the
person he was before.
