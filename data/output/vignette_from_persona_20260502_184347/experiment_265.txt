his wife, Elena, had lived for forty years. He remembered the roar, the way the sky turned the color
of a bruise, and the acrid, chemical smell of burning chaparral that filled the cab of his truck as
they fled. That smell had lodged itself somewhere deep in his sinuses, and now, years later, it
would ambush him without reason. A neighbor’s barbecue, a car backfiring, the dry, hot stillness of
an August afternoon—any of it could send him back to that moment, his hands gripping the steering
wheel, Elena’s voice tight with fear beside him. At seventy-five, Carlos was a retired QA engineer,
a man who had spent his career finding flaws in systems. But the system that had failed him was the
world itself, and he could no longer trust it.

The triggers came first, unbidden. A whiff of woodsmoke from a fireplace in December and his chest
would tighten, his heart hammering against his ribs as if it were trying to escape. He would feel
the heat on his skin, hear the crackling, and his hands would begin to tremble. These physical
reactions—the sweating, the racing pulse—were so immediate and overwhelming that they felt like the
fire itself was starting again, right there in his living room. He became hypervigilant, scanning
the horizon for smoke whenever he drove, checking the stove three times before leaving the house,
starting at the sound of a siren. A car door slamming too hard would make him jump, his whole body
flinching as if he were bracing for an explosion. The world had become a landscape of hidden
threats, and his body was always ready for the next one.

To manage this constant state of alarm, Carlos had developed a set of rigid mental routines. He
would replay the day of the fire in his head, scrutinizing every decision. *We should have left an
hour earlier. I should have checked the evacuation routes the night before. I waited too long to
fill the gas tank.* This self-blame rumination was a loop he could not escape, a way of trying to
impose order on chaos by finding fault in himself. He also tried to push the memories away,
clenching his jaw and forcing his mind to go blank whenever an image of the flames surfaced. But
these efforts only made the triggers more potent. The more he tried not to think about the fire, the
more the smell of smoke seemed to find him. The more he dwelled on his mistakes, the more watchful
he became, scanning for the next sign of danger. His hypervigilance fed his rumination—*What else
have I missed?*—and his rumination made him more alert, more primed to notice any cue that might
signal disaster.

This cycle grew tighter over the months and years. He stopped going for walks in the neighborhood
after a neighbor’s leaf-burning sent him into a panic. He no longer visited his son’s house in the
foothills, where the dry grass and tall pines felt like a tinderbox. He began to check the weather
obsessively, and when the Santa Ana winds picked up in the fall, his anxiety would spike so high
that he could not sleep. He would sit in the dark living room, listening to the wind, his mind
churning through worst-case scenarios. Elena tried to reassure him, but he could not explain that
the threat felt real, immediate, and inescapable. The physical reactivity he experienced at the
smell of smoke made him certain that another fire was imminent, and that certainty drove him deeper
into his mental rituals. He ruminated about how he could have prevented the first fire, and in doing
so, he kept the fear alive, his body primed for a disaster that never came but always felt just
around the corner.

What finally brought Carlos to seek help was a small thing: a neighbor’s trash fire, contained in a
metal barrel, that sent him into a full-blown panic attack. He had locked himself in the bathroom,
shaking and sobbing, while Elena called the fire department. He was ashamed, and that shame became
another voice in the loop of self-blame. He was tired of being afraid, tired of the way his life had
shrunk to the size of his living room. He came to the clinic hoping for a way out, but he was also
terrified that the therapist would tell him the only way to get better was to face the fire again.
He did not know if he could.
