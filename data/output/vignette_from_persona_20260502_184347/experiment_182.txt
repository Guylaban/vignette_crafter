The faint, tinny melody of a lullaby drifted from a passing car, and Naren’s hands tightened on the
steering wheel of his old pickup. The song, a simple tune his wife used to hum to their son, was now
a landmine. He was a farmer, a man of the soil and the sun, but for the past eighteen months, since
his five-year-old son, Arun, had been hospitalized with a sudden, aggressive form of meningitis, his
world had shrunk to the dimensions of a single, terrible room. The event itself was a series of
frozen tableaux: the frantic drive to the emergency room, the antiseptic smell, the beeping
monitors, and most of all, the sight of his son’s small, limp hand, the fingers pale and blue-
tinged, lying on the white hospital sheet. That image of the hand—the specific, unnatural stillness
of it—was the worst part. Even now, a glimpse of his own hand in a certain light, or the way his
wife’s hand rested on the kitchen counter, could trigger a sudden, vivid reliving. The memory would
not just return; it would *invade*. A fragment—the beeping of a machine, the echo of a doctor’s
serious tone—would crash into his present moment, and for a terrifying second, he was back there,
the fear as fresh and raw as it had been that first night.

Because of this, Naren had developed a complex set of strategies to manage his life. He had come to
believe that the only way to prevent these intrusions was to outrun them. He delayed sleep until two
or three in the morning, staying up in the living room with the lights blazing, watching mindless
television until exhaustion finally claimed him. He was certain that if he went to bed at a normal
hour, the darkness and the silence would be a perfect stage for the memory to replay. The darkness
itself was a trigger, so he avoided sleep to avoid the dark, and he avoided the dark to avoid the
memory. This cycle meant that during the day, he was perpetually tired, his reactions slow and his
mood brittle. He also began avoiding other people. The sympathetic looks from neighbors at the feed
store, the well-meaning questions about Arun’s health—all of it felt like an invitation for the
memory to surface. He would take the long way home from the fields, driving past the store entirely,
or pretend he hadn’t seen a friend approaching on the road. He had come to believe that social
interaction was a minefield, and the most dangerous trigger was a simple, kind question.

This avoidance, however, created its own problems. By staying up all night, he was often in the
kitchen or the living room in the deep, quiet hours, and the sudden creak of the house settling or
the hoot of an owl outside would startle him, sending a jolt of adrenaline through his body. That
jolt, in turn, would bring the memory rushing back—a sudden, shapeless fragment of the hospital
room’s fear. The maladaptive strategy of staying awake actually made the triggers more potent,
because his exhausted mind was more vulnerable to them. He found himself jumping at the sound of his
own footsteps on the porch, his heart hammering. The more he tried to control his environment, the
more unpredictable it seemed.

The memory itself felt present, not past. When he thought of Arun’s hand, it was not a recollection;
it was a re-experiencing. He could feel the cold, smooth texture of the hospital linoleum under his
shoes, hear the hiss of the oxygen machine. This quality of the memory made him terrified of any
sensory reminder. A song on the radio, especially the lullaby, could trigger a full reliving event,
where he would feel his throat tighten and his eyes sting with tears he had not shed in years. The
memory was a predator, and the triggers were its scent. He had learned to be hypervigilant, scanning
his environment for any potential threat—a song, a shadow, a particular shade of pale skin. He was
certain that if he could just control his surroundings perfectly, he could keep the memory at bay.

The breaking point came on a Tuesday. He was driving back from the feed store, having successfully
avoided everyone, when a song from a nearby house drifted through his open window. It was the same
lullaby. For a full ten seconds, he was not on the rural road; he was in the hospital room, watching
the doctor’s lips move without hearing the words, the world reduced to the terrifying stillness of
his son’s hand. He had to pull over, his hands shaking, his breath coming in ragged gasps. That
night, he did not even try to sleep. He sat in the dark of his own living room, which he had
previously avoided, because he was too exhausted to care. The next morning, his wife found him
staring at the wall, and for the first time, he spoke the words aloud: “I can’t keep doing this.” He
was terrified that the memory was not a thing of the past, but a permanent, present threat, and that
his own mind had become a place he could no longer safely inhabit. He needed to find a way to make
the past stay in the past, to stop the fragments from hijacking his present, and most of all, to be
able to look at his own hands without seeing his son’s.
