Chicago. Eight months ago, his older brother, Arun, died suddenly from a cardiac arrest at the age
of twenty-four. Ravi had been the one to find him collapsed on the bathroom floor, the paramedics
unable to revive him. The memory of that morning is fragmented but searing: the cold tile against
his knees, the phone slipping from his hand, the paramedic’s voice saying “I’m sorry” in a tone that
sounded rehearsed. What stays with him most is the silence afterward, the apartment feeling hollow
in a way he cannot describe.

In the weeks that followed, Ravi stopped making plans. He had been working on a novel, a sprawling
family saga, but he closed the document and did not open it again. He told himself he was just
taking a break, but the truth was that every attempt to imagine a future felt like a betrayal—or
worse, an invitation for more loss. He began asking his roommate, his mother, even his barista
whether he seemed okay, needing them to confirm that he was still functioning, still normal. He
avoided anything that reminded him of Arun: the corner deli where they used to buy samosas, the
playlist they shared, the smell of cardamom. He had come to believe that the world was a
fundamentally dangerous place, a place where a healthy twenty-four-year-old could drop dead without
warning, and that his own sense of safety had been an illusion. He used to be a happy person, he
told his therapist in their first session; now he was always miserable, and he was certain that
happiness was something he would never feel again.

Because Ravi saw the world as hostile and unpredictable, he remained on high alert. He could not
relax, even in his own apartment, which he had once considered a sanctuary. Ordinary sounds—the hum
of the refrigerator, a car door slamming outside—made him jump, his heart racing as if something
terrible was about to happen again. He had trouble falling asleep, lying awake for hours listening
to the building settle, certain that any creak could be a sign of catastrophe. This constant
vigilance, in turn, fed his sense that the world was dangerous; his body’s alarm system told him
there was reason to be afraid, so there must be.

His avoidance strategies deepened the cycle. By refusing to make plans, he never gave himself the
chance to experience a positive future, which only confirmed his belief that there was nothing to
look forward to. By seeking reassurance, he was always reminded of his own fragility, because the
words of comfort never lasted. His avoidance of grief reminders meant he never processed the loss,
so the world remained a place where loss could strike at any moment. And the more he avoided, the
more he jumped at sounds, because his nervous system had no chance to habituate to ordinary life. He
started working from home exclusively, telling his editor he needed quiet to write, but the truth
was that he could not bear the unpredictability of the outside world. His novel remained unfinished,

What finally brought Ravi to therapy was a panic attack in a coffee shop when a stranger coughed. He

also, quietly, hoping that someone can help him find a way back to the person he used to be—even if
he is not sure that person exists anymore.
