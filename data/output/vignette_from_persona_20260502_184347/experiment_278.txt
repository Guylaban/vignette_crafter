was not a single moment in adulthood, but a sustained period of childhood physical abuse at the

consequences, a belief that hardened into a bitter, permanent appraisal of the world as a place
where justice was hollow and trust was a liability.

that scent in a grocery store aisle, it does not simply bring back a memory; it instantly reinforces
his conviction that no one has been held accountable, that the world remains fundamentally unsafe.
This appraisal then sharpens his threat monitoring. In those moments, Arthur feels his body betray
him, reacting as if he is still that small boy, his heart hammering, his muscles tensing for a blow
he knows is not coming. He has come to believe that his own body is an enemy, a traitor that refuses
to accept that the danger is long past, and this belief only deepens his sense of helplessness and
betrayal.

unbidden pieces: the feel of a rough hand, the creak of a floorboard, and most potently, the
olfactory recall of that specific, cloying whiskey-and-sweat smell. These fragments feel more real
and vivid than any ordinary memory, yanking him from the present and depositing him back in the
hallways of his childhood home. The sheer vividness of these intrusions terrifies him, and they
directly feed his ongoing threat perception, convincing him that the danger is not truly over. The
memory fragments also serve as potent internal triggers, making him hyperaware of any sound that

jiggle of a lock, interpreting ordinary creaks and settling noises as potential threats.

mental effort exhausts him, and the underlying belief that he must work this hard to stay safe only
reinforces his negative appraisal that the world is a place of constant, hidden peril. He avoids all
television programs or films about family violence or abuse, and he has stopped reading the news
sections that cover crime.

his mind flooded with the smell of whiskey. The memory surged, the threat felt immediate, and he

his negative appraisal of his own body’s unreliability deepened, and he began to feel threatened by
even the most ordinary approach of a stranger on the street.

Arthur’s social world has steadily contracted. He has stopped attending his weekly poker game

brought him to seek help was a simple, terrifying moment: a siren wailed past his office window, and

certain that his life had become unmanageable, that his own mind and body were no longer his to
command. He is most afraid that he will never be able to feel safe, but he is also, quietly, hoping
that someone can finally explain why his body keeps fighting a war that ended sixty years ago.
