The chemical smell hit Mei first, a thick, sweetish tang that clung to the back of her throat and
made her eyes water. She had been working late on a custom phoenix piece, her needle tracing the
fine red lines of feathers into the client’s forearm, when the bottle of industrial-grade solvent
she used for cleaning her equipment tipped over. It was a new brand, one her supplier had
recommended as stronger, faster-acting. Mei had not thought much of it until her hands started to
shake, until the room tilted, until she was on the floor, gasping, the smell of it everywhere—on her
clothes, in her hair, coating the inside of her nose. Her husband, Leo, found her there minutes
later, her face pressed to the cold linoleum, and drove her to the emergency room. The doctors said
it was acute inhalation toxicity, that she would be fine after fluids and oxygen, but Mei never
forgot the way the antiseptic air of the hospital had mixed with the chemical still clinging to her
skin, creating a scent she could not separate from the feeling of her lungs closing.

In the weeks that followed, Mei tried to return to work. She wore a mask now, opened the window,
swapped the solvent for a milder formula. But the smell of anything clinical—the bleach her
assistant used to wipe down the counter, the hand sanitizer at the front desk, even the faint
medicinal note of her own tattoo numbing cream—would stop her mid-stroke, her heart hammering, her
breath shallow. She had come to believe that if she smelled that again, something worse would
happen, that her body would betray her the way it had in that moment on the floor. So she started
avoiding certain tasks. She asked her assistant to clean the equipment. She stopped using any
products with a strong chemical base. She began scheduling fewer appointments, telling clients she
was booked out, because the longer she worked, the more the studio smelled like a hospital to her,
and the more she felt watched.

It was the feeling of being watched that unsettled her most. Mei had always worked with people
staring at her—it was part of the job, clients watching her hands, watching the needle. But now she
felt their eyes differently. She was certain they were waiting for her to slip, to shake, to fall.
When a client leaned close to inspect a line, she flinched. When her assistant stood behind her to
observe a shading technique, Mei’s hands would go rigid, her jaw tight. She had started positioning
her chair so that her back was to the wall, so no one could approach from behind, but it did not
help. The feeling was inside her now—a constant, low-grade vigilance that made her jump at the buzz
of her own tattoo machine.

The worst was when a client asked her to do something she had not planned. A simple request—"Can you
make the wing a little bigger?"—would send a spike of heat through her chest. She hated being told
what to do, hated the way it reminded her of that moment on the floor, when her body had refused to
obey her. She had always prided herself on being adaptable, on turning a client’s vague idea into
something beautiful, but now she needed control. She needed to know exactly what she was doing, step
by step, and any deviation felt like a threat. She had started writing out every detail of a tattoo
before she began, and if a client asked for a change mid-session, she would say no, she could not,
it was too late. She had lost two regular clients that way.

Her marriage had thinned. Leo was patient, but he did not understand why she could not just use a
different solvent, why she could not just tell her clients to watch from a distance, why she could
not just breathe through it. Mei had stopped going to the grocery store because the cleaning aisle
made her nauseous. She had stopped visiting her mother at the nursing home because the smell of
disinfectant and old skin sent her into a spiral. She had stopped cooking with anything that
required strong spices or vinegar, because the steam carried a memory she could not name.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a panic attack in the middle of a session. The client had
asked her to re-dip the needle, and Mei had reached for the new bottle of solvent—the mild one, the
safe one—and the cap had come loose, and a single drop had landed on her wrist. She had frozen,
staring at it, and then she had run to the bathroom and vomited. The client left. Her assistant
drove her home. That night, Mei told Leo she could not do it anymore, could not keep pretending she
was fine, could not keep shrinking her life to avoid a smell, a look, a request. She was afraid of
what she was becoming—someone who could not work, could not cook, could not visit her own mother.
She was afraid that if she did not find a way out, she would end up back on that floor, gasping,
with no one to find her.
