The accident happened on a clear Tuesday afternoon in the fall. Tui, then fifty-nine, was driving a
vanload of young volunteers back from a community cleanup along the coastal highway. He remembers
the glare of the sun through a gap in the trees, and then the other vehicle crossing the center
line. He remembers wrenching the wheel to the right, the sound of metal folding, and the silence
that followed. What he cannot remember, no matter how hard he tries, is the ten seconds before the
impact. That gap is a wall in his mind. He can describe the volunteers’ faces afterward, the smell
of fuel, the paramedic who knelt beside him—but the moment of collision itself is simply gone. When
he tries to tell his therapist about it, his throat closes. He knows what happened, but the words
will not come.

In the months that followed, Tui noticed his body acting as though the danger had never passed. His
heart would pound at the sight of a police cruiser in his rearview mirror, even though the officers
who arrived at the crash site had been kind. He found himself avoiding the main street where a
neighbor, a retired Marine, often sat on his porch. The man was never angry—he would wave, call out
a greeting—but something about the uniformed figure, the set of his shoulders, made Tui’s stomach
drop. He began to take longer routes to the community center where he worked, telling himself it was
just a preference for quieter roads. He did not tell anyone that he had started to believe the
accident was his fault. If he had not been speeding to make the meeting on time. If he had checked
his mirrors one more time. The thought circled back, and each time it returned, it brought with it a
feeling of being hollow, dead inside, as though the man who had once organized block parties and led
youth workshops had been replaced by a shell.

Tui’s avoidance narrowed his world. He stopped attending city council meetings because someone there
might raise their voice in disagreement. Even calm, measured anger from a colleague made his chest
tighten. He had come to believe that any expression of anger was a precursor to disaster—that if he
stayed in a room where tension rose, something terrible would follow. This conviction grew stronger
each time he felt his body react before his mind could catch up. His body, he was certain, knew
things his brain did not. It braced for impact even when he was sitting at his kitchen table, safe,
alone. The more his body betrayed him, the more he trusted its warnings, and the more he avoided.

The memory gaps did not stay still. Over time, Tui found that the parts of the accident he could
recall were becoming sharper—the paramedic’s voice, the sound of a crying volunteer—but the gaps
themselves seemed to widen. He would spend hours trying to push through the wall, searching for the
missing seconds, and each failed attempt reinforced his belief that he was somehow responsible for
what he could not retrieve. He began to wonder if his mind was hiding something from him, something
he had done that was too shameful to remember. This thought made the uniforms on the street feel
more menacing, the angry voices more personal. He was watching for a threat he could not name.

By the time he sought help, Tui had been living this way for nearly four years. His work as a
community organizer had shrunk to tasks he could do from home—phone calls, emails. He had stopped
attending the weekly meetings where he used to mediate disputes between neighbors. His divorce,
finalized two years after the accident, had felt like a distant event, something that happened to
someone else. What finally brought him to a clinic was a moment in his own kitchen: his adult
daughter, visiting for dinner, raised her voice in frustration over a broken stove. Tui flinched,
dropped the plate he was holding, and stood frozen as it shattered at his feet. His daughter asked
him what was wrong, and he could not find the words. He could not explain why, in that instant, he
had felt the van flipping again. He could not tell her that his body had already braced for the
crash, even though he was standing in his own home, holding a ceramic plate, and the only danger was
a daughter who loved him and wanted him to get help.
