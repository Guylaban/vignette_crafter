Marisol is a 66-year-old divorced woman who works as an editor for a small publishing house in
Chicago. She was born in a small town in Central America, and the trauma that reshaped her life
happened when she was a teenager, during the civil war that swept through her country. She was
walking home from the market when a military truck rounded the corner and a soldier opened fire into
the crowd. She remembers the blast of the gunshot, the way the sound seemed to split the air itself,
and then the sight of a young man in uniform—his face twisted with something she could not
name—falling to the ground a few feet from her. That moment has never left her.

In the years that followed, Marisol built a quiet life in the United States. She raised two
children, divorced her husband, and found solace in the precision of words on a page. But the old
terror never fully faded. It surfaced in small ways at first—a flinch when a car backfired, a sudden
stillness when she saw a police cruiser in her rearview mirror. Over time, these moments became more
frequent and more consuming. She began to notice that when she heard a loud bang—a truck dropping a
crate, a door slamming shut in the hallway—her whole body would lock up, and her mind would race
with images she had not invited. The explosion sounds were the worst. They brought her back,
instantly, to that street corner, and she would feel a cold certainty that something terrible was
about to happen again.

This sense of impending danger did not fade once the sound stopped. It lingered, and it grew.
Marisol found that after a trigger—whether it was a bang, the sight of a uniformed officer on the
sidewalk, or the rare and terrifying moment she caught a glimpse of someone who reminded her of the
soldier—she could not shake the feeling that she was unsafe. Even when her daughter assured her that
the neighborhood was quiet, that the war was over, that she was in no danger, Marisol could not
believe it. The words felt hollow, like a script someone else was reading. She had come to believe
that safety was not something anyone could promise her. At night, this belief became unbearable. She
would wake at two or three in the morning, her heart pounding, and lie still in the dark, listening
for sounds that did not belong. She had developed a kind of nightly vigilance that she could not
turn off. She would check the locks, peer through the blinds, and then lie back down, knowing she
would not sleep again until the first light came through the curtains.

The cycle tightened. The more she scanned for danger, the more she found it. A car idling too long
outside her building, a neighbor's footsteps on the stairs at midnight—these became new signals, new
reasons to stay alert. And the more she stayed alert, the more she noticed the things that could be
triggers. She began to avoid the parts of the city where she might see a uniformed officer. She took
longer routes to work. She stopped going out after dark. Her editing work, which she had always done
from home, became her whole world. She told herself it was safer.

What finally brought Marisol to seek help was not the fear itself—she had lived with that for
decades—but the exhaustion. She was tired in a way that sleep could not fix. She had stopped seeing
her grandchildren because the drive home after visits felt too dangerous. Her daughter had started
to worry, and Marisol could hear the frustration in her voice when she said, "Mom, you are safe. Why
can't you just believe me?" Marisol could not answer. She did not know how to explain that the
feeling of safety had left her so long ago that she could no longer remember what it felt like. She
came to therapy because she was desperate to sleep through the night, and because she was afraid
that if she did not find a way to stop the cycle, she would lose the last pieces of the life she had
built.
