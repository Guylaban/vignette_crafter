Lanka and had moved to the United States as a teenager, but the war she had fled was not something

when a mortar shell had struck the market while she was walking home with her aunt. Her aunt had
been killed instantly. Priya had been thrown against a stall, her eardrum ruptured, her leg pinned
under debris, and for what felt like hours she had lain there, unable to move, listening to the
screaming and the silence that followed. She had not been able to help. She had not been able to do
anything.

In the years after, she built a life that seemed stable. She became the person others relied on—the
counselor who listened, the mother who planned, the wife who managed. But the attack had left
something underneath. She had come to believe that she was weak, that her survival had been a matter
of luck rather than strength, and that she had failed the one person she should have protected. That
belief did not announce itself; it simply sat beneath everything, shaping how she moved through the
world. When the news played reports of bombings in other cities, she felt her chest tighten and her
mind go blank. She began to avoid the news entirely, turning off the radio in the car, scrolling
past headlines, leaving the room when her husband watched the evening broadcast. She told herself it
was unnecessary exposure. What she did not say was that watching others suffer made her feel, again,
the helplessness of that afternoon, and that helplessness confirmed what she already feared: that
she was not strong enough to face it.

The avoidance spread. Public transport became unbearable—the crowded bus, the stopped train, the
sense of being trapped in a space she could not leave. She started driving everywhere, even short
distances, even when it meant circling for parking or arriving late. She began wearing concealing
clothing: loose, dark colors, scarves that covered her hair, sunglasses even on overcast days. She
told herself it was practical, that she did not want to be noticed. But what she meant was that she
did not want to be seen, because being seen felt like being vulnerable, and vulnerability felt like
the moment before the shell landed. She had come to believe that if she could make herself
invisible, she would be safe.

news or took the car instead of the bus or pulled her scarf higher, she felt a quiet confirmation
settle in her chest. *You are afraid. You are weak. You cannot do what you used to do.* She had once
thought of herself as capable—someone who could sit with a crying teenager, who could hold a crisis
line, who could manage the chaos of a school day. Now she found herself delegating the harder cases
to her colleagues. She avoided the students whose stories involved violence or loss. She told
herself it was about boundaries. But she knew, in the part of herself she did not examine, that she
was no longer capable of the things that had defined her. And that knowledge made her avoid more,
and the avoidance made her feel weaker, and the cycle tightened until her life had shrunk to the
perimeter of her car, her home, her carefully controlled routines.

would find if she stopped avoiding, but she was more afraid of passing that fear on. She called a
therapist that afternoon.
