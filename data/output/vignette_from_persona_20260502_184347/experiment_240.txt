Aisha is a 23-year-old environmental scientist who lost her younger brother, Kofi, to a sudden
asthma attack two years ago. She had been the one to find him in their shared apartment,
unresponsive, after he had forgotten his inhaler during a late-night study session. In the moments
before she called for help, she had been on the phone with a friend, and she has since become
convinced that if she had checked on him sooner, or been a different kind of sister—more vigilant,
more responsible—she could have stopped it. She carries the belief that someone else, a better
person, would have prevented his death, and this thought has hardened into a sense that the event
happened because of the sort of person she is: careless, unworthy of trust. What happened felt
deeply unfair, and she has told herself that nothing has been done about it, that Kofi’s death was a
failure of the world to protect him, and that she is complicit in that failure.

These negative appraisals have shaped everything that followed. Because she believes she should have
been able to prevent the death, Aisha now blames herself repeatedly, going over the details of that
evening in her mind, searching for the moment she could have acted differently. This rumination is a
strategy she uses to feel in control, but it only deepens her conviction that she is fundamentally
flawed. She has also started picking arguments with colleagues and friends, creating drama that
distracts her from the trauma; she finds that when she is angry, she does not have to feel the grief
or the guilt. She avoids watching television programmes or films about sudden illness or death,
because even a reference to an asthma attack sends her into a spiral. The memory itself is
fragmented: she knows what happened but cannot find the words to describe it to anyone, and when she
tries, the image of Kofi on the floor feels like it is happening right now, not something that is
over. She often pushes the memory away before it can fully form, as if she can stop it from being
real.

This intrusive memory triggers her threat system directly. When something takes longer than
expected—a lab result, a colleague’s reply to an email—Aisha’s mind immediately assumes something
bad is happening. Her body reacts before she has time to think: her heart races, her palms sweat,
and she feels a surge of panic that she cannot control. This physical reaction then reinforces the
memory, because the sensations of fear and helplessness feel identical to what she felt when she
found Kofi. The threat also feeds back into her negative appraisals: the more her body alerts her to
danger, the more she believes she is the kind of person who attracts disaster, and the more she
feels the injustice of it all.

Sleep has become a battleground. She has trouble falling or staying asleep, because the moment she
lies down, her mind replays the evening, and her body tenses as if she is back in that moment. The
lack of sleep makes her more irritable, which fuels her tendency to seek conflict—a maladaptive
strategy that momentarily distracts her but leaves her feeling more isolated and more convinced that
she is a bad person. This cycle has tightened over the months: the more she blames herself, the more
she argues with others, and the more she pushes the memory away, the more vividly it returns when
she least expects it.

Aisha works in a field office where she analyses soil samples, a job that requires patience and
precision. But the delays inherent in her work—waiting for equipment to calibrate, for samples to
process—have become unbearable. She has started taking on only the most urgent tasks, avoiding any
project that requires extended waiting, because the uncertainty feels like a trap. Her supervisor
has noticed her withdrawal and has asked if she is okay, but Aisha has brushed it off, afraid that
if she explains, she will have to say the words she cannot find.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment at work last week: a colleague mentioned a
documentary about air quality and asthma, and Aisha felt her body react before her mind could catch
up. She excused herself to the bathroom and stood there shaking, convinced that something terrible
was about to happen. She realized she could not keep living this way—that her strategies for coping
were making the trauma more present, not less. She is most afraid that if she stops blaming herself,
she will have no way to make sense of Kofi’s death, and that without the anger and the vigilance,
she will collapse into the grief she has been running from. But she is also hoping, for the first
time, that there might be a way to remember her brother without reliving the moment she lost him.
