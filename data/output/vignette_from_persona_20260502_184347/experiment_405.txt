Kai is a 31-year-old judge, a role that demands composure, deliberation, and an almost forensic
attention to detail. Three years ago, he was driving home from a late session when a flash flood
swept his car off a rural road. He remembers it not as a continuous event, but as a series of
frozen, vivid snapshots: the wall of brown water appearing through the windshield, the crushing
pressure against the driver’s side door, the antiseptic sting of the river mixing with gasoline as
he struggled to free his seatbelt, and finally the roar of an ambulance siren as he lay on the
embankment, choking on silt. In the months that followed, Kai threw himself back into work,
convinced that if he simply didn’t think about the flood, it would fade. He suppressed the images
during the day, but at night, a specific trigger could undo all that effort. The first time it
happened was during a summer thunderstorm. As the rain began to fall in the same heavy, percussive
sheets as that night, the memory would return with full sensory force—he could smell the hospital
antiseptic from the emergency room, feel the cold water rising around his chest, and hear the
siren’s wail as if it were right outside his chambers. This was not a recollection; it was a
complete, terrifying relocation to the moment of the accident. The more the memories assaulted him,
the harder he tried to suppress them, but the effort itself left him feeling perpetually on edge.

Kai’s life began to narrow. He started avoiding going out after work, particularly if the sky looked
heavy, and he refused to relax at home, believing that letting his guard down was dangerous. He
would sit rigidly on his couch, scanning the weather alerts on his phone, his body braced for the
next emergency. This constant vigilance, in turn, made him acutely sensitive to any reminder of the
trauma. A passing ambulance on the street outside his courthouse would send him into a cold sweat,
and the mere whiff of a cleaning solution in the courthouse restroom could trigger the same visceral
flashback. The causal loop was tight: a trigger brought the memory, the memory demanded suppression,
and the suppression kept him in a state of high alert that made him even more vulnerable to the next
trigger. His wife noticed he had stopped laughing, stopped joining her for weekend errands. When she
asked why he never wanted to go to the park anymore, he said he just preferred the quiet of the
house, unwilling to admit that a cloudy sky felt like a personal threat.

What finally brought Kai to seek help was a moment of professional failure. During a routine
sentencing hearing, the defendant’s attorney mentioned a “flood of evidence,” and Kai felt the
courtroom dissolve. For several seconds, he was back in the car, the water rising, the siren
screaming. He lost the thread of the argument, stammered, and had to call a recess. The shame was
immediate and profound. He was terrified that this loss of control could derail his career—that the
very composure he had built his identity on was a fragile dam about to break. He came to therapy
hoping to learn how to contain the memories, to stop the triggers from ambushing him, but underneath
that, he was afraid of what would happen if he ever fully let himself remember.
