through the canyon where he had lived for twenty years. He had been home alone on a Tuesday
afternoon, reviewing a draft environmental impact report at his kitchen table, when the smoke alarm

days—but the smell that followed was not electrical. It was thick and sweet, like a campfire someone
had doused with gasoline. He walked to the window and saw the ridge behind his house glowing orange.
The evacuation order came over his phone thirty seconds later, and in the chaos of grabbing his
laptop and his go-bag, he lost his glasses, his car keys, and, for a long, terrifying moment, his
sense of which way the driveway faced. The fire did not reach his home—firefighters stopped it a
quarter-mile away—but the evacuation lasted three days, and when he returned, the air still tasted
of ash.

In the weeks that followed, Duc found himself scanning the horizon whenever he drove home from work,
checking for smoke that was not there. He began to avoid the canyon route entirely, taking a longer
highway that added twenty minutes to his commute. At the office, he started to notice that his hands
would tremble when a colleague walked into his cubicle without warning. Unpredictable situations—a
sudden meeting room booking, a fire drill, a child’s birthday party with a piñata that produced a
puff of confetti—made his chest tighten and his vision narrow. He had always been the reliable one
in his team, the analyst who caught the errors in other people’s spreadsheets. Now he found himself
double-checking his own work three or four times, certain he had missed something catastrophic. When
his supervisor asked why he had not submitted a routine quarterly report on time, Duc heard the
question as an accusation and spent the rest of the day convinced he was about to be fired. He told
himself that his reactions since the fire meant he was going crazy, that his mind had broken in a
way that could not be fixed. He had come to believe that he could not rely on himself anymore—that
his own judgment, the very thing his job depended on, was no longer trustworthy. What frightened him
most was the thought that if he let his guard down, even for a moment, he would miss the next sign
of danger and something far worse would happen. He became certain that he had to be especially
careful because you never knew what could happen next.

The connection between these fears and his triggers tightened into a loop he could not escape. A
minor headache—feeling physically unwell in any way—would send him into a spiral of checking his
body for symptoms, certain that the dizziness meant carbon monoxide or a stroke. That spiral would
confirm, in his mind, that he could not rely on himself to stay calm, which made him even more
vigilant, scanning for the next unpredictable threat. The smell of smoke from a neighbor’s grill, or
even the faint burnt-toast odor from the office toaster, would bring the canyon afternoon back so
vividly that he would have to excuse himself to the restroom and stand with his hands on the sink
until his breathing slowed. Each time he reacted that way, he felt more convinced that he was losing
his grip, and that conviction made the next trigger hit even harder. He stopped going to the grocery
store on weekends because the crowds felt unpredictable. He stopped driving his son to soccer
practice because a sudden lane change by another driver made him pull over and shake. His wife
noticed that he was quieter at dinner, that he flinched when she touched his shoulder from behind.

What finally brought Duc to seek help was a Tuesday afternoon exactly one year after the fire. He
was at his desk, and a maintenance worker began testing the fire alarms in the hallway. The sound
was shrill and sudden, and Duc dropped his coffee, stood up, and walked out of the building without
telling anyone. He stood in the parking lot for twenty minutes, unable to go back inside. He was
afraid that if he returned to his desk, he would have to admit to himself that he was no longer the
person he had been. He was more afraid, though, of staying the same.
