Malia is an 80-year-old grandmother and respected religious educator in her community, a woman who
has spent decades teaching scripture and guiding others through life’s trials. But the trials she
endured herself during the war in her home country, decades ago, have never settled into the past.
She remembers the day soldiers came through her village, the smell of damp earth and smoke thick in
the air as she hid with her children in a shallow ditch. She remembers the flames that consumed the
neighbor’s house, the heat on her face, and the moment a soldier’s hand grabbed her arm from
behind—unexpected, rough, and permanent in her mind. What haunts her most is not just what happened,
but what she cannot recall: entire stretches of that afternoon are simply gone, as if erased. The
memory that remains feels unreal, like a film she once watched about someone else’s life, yet it is
more vivid and real than any ordinary memory she has. She has come to believe that something about
her—some intrinsic fault—drew that soldier’s hand to her arm, that she was somehow responsible for
the humiliation she felt when he forced her to kneel in the mud. Her body, once a source of strength
through years of labor and prayer, now feels unreliable, a betrayer that trembles and freezes
without warning.

In the years since, Malia has built a quiet life in a small American city, teaching Sunday school
and caring for her grandchildren. But the triggers of that day have followed her. The smell of damp
earth when she gardens or the scent of a neighbor’s barbecue smoke can suddenly drop her back into
that ditch, her heart pounding and her breath shallow. She has learned to keep her mind occupied at
all times—listening to the radio constantly, reciting scripture under her breath, or mentally
listing her shopping items—because when her mind goes quiet, the alien memory rushes in with a force
that feels more real than the room she is standing in. The more she tries to push the memory away
through distraction, the more fragmented and intrusive it becomes, and the more she feels that her
body is not to be trusted, that it will betray her again. She now checks the locks on her doors and
windows three times before bed, and again when she wakes, because the act of checking gives her a
few moments of control over her environment. She has stopped accepting invitations to meet new
people, avoiding the church’s new members’ gatherings and declining coffee dates with other
grandmothers, because she is certain that if someone touched her arm unexpectedly—a friendly hand on

she still carries. The more she avoids, the more she is left alone with the memory, and the more the
memory confirms that she is fundamentally broken, that her body is a liability, that the fault lies
in her. The mental distraction and the lock-checking have become a full-time job, leaving her
exhausted and withdrawn, and her husband has begun to worry that she no longer seems like the woman
he married.

What brought Malia to seek help was a moment last month when a child in her Sunday school class,
reaching for a book, accidentally brushed her arm. She pulled away so sharply that the child began
to cry, and Malia sat frozen, unable to explain, filled with a shame that felt as fresh as the day
she knelt in the mud. She is afraid that she is becoming a stranger to her own family, and she is
hoping—though she does not quite believe it—that she might one day trust her own body again.
