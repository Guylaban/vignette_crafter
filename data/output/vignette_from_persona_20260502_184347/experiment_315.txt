Arthur is a 65-year-old married man, a prosecutor who has spent decades in the courtroom, but the
violence he encountered there was not the kind he was trained to argue against. Three years ago,
during a routine cross-examination, a defendant in a custody case—a man with a history of explosive
outbursts—lunged across the table, his face contorted with rage, and slammed Arthur’s head against
the wooden rail before bailiffs could intervene. The sound of the impact, the sudden
incomprehensible roar of the courtroom, and the sight of the man’s uniformed officers pulling him
away became seared into Arthur’s memory. What stayed with him most was not the physical pain but the
moral shock: he had believed in the order of the courtroom, in the unspoken rule that words, not
violence, decided fates. Now, that belief was gone.

In the months that followed, Arthur found himself unable to sit through hearings without his heart
hammering. The trigger was everywhere: a lawyer’s raised voice, the sharp click of a handcuff, the
sight of a police officer standing near the bench. But the most insidious trigger was the noise—the
overlapping, incomprehensible chatter of a busy courthouse hallway, a sound that now felt like a
prelude to chaos. When he heard it, his body tensed as if the attack were about to happen again. He
began scanning the room for exits, watching people’s hands, their eyes, their postures, looking for
the flicker of aggression that might precede another assault. This constant vigilance, he told
himself, was just being careful. But it left him exhausted, and at night, he lay awake replaying the
scene, his mind unable to shut off the loop.

To cope, Arthur developed a set of strategies that he believed would keep him safe. He started
putting on loud music or the TV as soon as he got home, drowning out the echoes of the courtroom
chatter that seemed to follow him. He also began asking his wife, Margaret, repeatedly, “Are you
sure I’m okay? Do I seem okay?”—a reassurance he needed to hear, though it never truly settled him.
And he checked his surroundings constantly, even at the grocery store, scanning for anyone who might
be a threat. But these strategies had an unintended effect. The loud music made him miss the sound
of a door opening or a step behind him, which only heightened his alertness. The reassurance-
seeking, he noticed, made Margaret worry more, which in turn made Arthur feel more fragile, more in
need of watching. And the constant scanning of his environment meant he was always finding something
to be wary of—a man muttering to himself, a group of teenagers laughing too loudly—which sent his
threat system into overdrive again. The very actions he took to calm himself became new triggers,
feeding the cycle.

His work suffered. As a prosecutor, he now avoided any case involving police testimony, requesting
reassignments to quieter, document-heavy files. He stopped attending the weekly team meetings where
colleagues debated cases, preferring to work alone in his office with the door closed. But even
there, the sound of footsteps in the hallway made him look up, his pulse quickening. He had come to
believe that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that the threat of violence was always latent,
waiting for a moment of inattention. This belief drove him to watch others even more closely—in the
elevator, on the street, in the courthouse cafeteria—looking for signs that someone might harm him.
He was certain that if he let his guard down, he would be caught off guard again.

The cycle tightened. The more he watched, the more he saw potential threats. The more he saw, the
more he needed to distract himself or seek reassurance. And the more he did those things, the more
his environment felt dangerous, because his strategies kept him from feeling truly safe. What
finally brought Arthur to seek help was a moment in court last week, when a young attorney raised
his voice during a heated objection. Arthur felt his chest seize, his vision tunnel, and he had to
excuse himself to the restroom, where he stood gripping the sink, trembling, for ten minutes. He
knew then that he could not continue this way. He is terrified of what will happen if he cannot stop
the cycle—he fears he will lose his career, his marriage, his sense of himself. But more than
anything, he is afraid of the next time he hears that incomprehensible chatter, and what it will do
to him.
