The event that cracked everything open happened three years ago, on a cold November night. Marcus
was driving his late shift through the south side, the bus nearly empty, when he saw the flashing
lights in his mirror. He pulled over, assuming it was a routine stop—maybe a passenger had
complained, or a tail light was out. But the officer who approached was already shouting, hand on
his holster, ordering him out of the seat with his hands up. Marcus complied instantly, but the
officer shoved him against the side of the bus, wrenched his arms behind his back, and held him
there for what felt like an eternity while another officer searched the cabin. It turned out to be a
case of mistaken identity—someone had reported a man matching his description, same jacket, same
build, driving a similar bus. They let him go with a curt apology, but Marcus never forgot the cold
metal of the handcuffs, the officer’s breath hot on his neck, the way his own heart hammered so hard
he thought it might stop. He went home that night and sat in the dark for hours, replaying every
second.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus found himself unable to shake the memory. It would surface
without warning—a sudden flash of the officer’s face, the sound of the handcuffs clicking shut—often
when he was least prepared, like while he was eating dinner or trying to fall asleep. The more it
intruded, the more he began to feel that the world itself had shifted. He had always considered his
neighborhood a place of belonging, but now he saw danger in every patrol car, every uniform. He
started to believe that people couldn’t be trusted, that the community he had once relied on was
just a fragile illusion. These thoughts made him pull away. He stopped attending the weekly block
meetings he had helped organize. He stopped greeting his neighbors. When a friend asked him to grab
a beer, Marcus made an excuse. He told himself it was safer to be alone.

Driving became a minefield. Every time a police cruiser appeared in his rearview mirror, Marcus’s
hands would tighten on the wheel, his jaw clenching. The irritability that followed was hard to
contain—he snapped at passengers for minor infractions, once shouting at an elderly woman who took
too long to find her fare card. His supervisor noticed the change, warning him about complaints.
Marcus knew he was losing control, but the anger felt like the only thing he could hold onto. To
manage the rising tension, he started wearing a baseball cap pulled low over his face during his
shifts, and he would cover his face with his hands whenever he passed a police station. He also
began avoiding people altogether, eating lunch alone in the bus depot rather than the crowded
cafeteria. At home, he would blast loud music or the TV until the walls vibrated, trying to drown
out the intrusive memories. The distraction worked for a few minutes, but it also made it harder to
concentrate on anything else—he found himself zoning out during his favorite shows, unable to follow
a plot.

The door-checking started around the same time. Every night, Marcus would get up three or four times
to make sure the locks were engaged, running his fingers along the chain, testing the deadbolt. He
knew it was irrational, but the ritual gave him a fleeting sense of control. The hypervigilance bled
into his sleep, leaving him exhausted. And the exhaustion fed his belief that the world was
fundamentally dangerous—after all, if he couldn't even feel safe in his own apartment, what hope was
there? This cycle tightened over months. The more he checked doors, the more he saw threats
everywhere; the more he saw threats, the more he retreated into isolation; the more isolated he
became, the more the intrusive memories returned, sharp and vivid. He started to lose track of why
he had once felt connected to anything at all. One afternoon, while driving his route, a police
cruiser pulled up alongside him at a red light. Marcus’s heart lurched. He pressed his hand over his
mouth, turned up the radio to full volume, and stared straight ahead until the light changed. The
memory of that November night washed over him like a wave, and he knew, in that moment, that he
could not keep living like this. He called a therapist the next morning, his voice barely above a
whisper. What he feared most was that he had already lost the person he used to be—the man who
trusted his neighbors, who waved at kids from his bus window, who believed the world was mostly
good. He was desperate to find his way back, but he had no idea where to start.
