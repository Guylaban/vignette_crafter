Raymond is a 52-year-old school principal who has spent nearly three decades building a career
around order, safety, and predictability. That foundation shattered eighteen months ago, during a
parent-teacher conference that turned violent. A father, angry about his son’s suspension, cornered
Raymond in his office after hours. The man was larger, younger, and had a cold, focused rage.
Raymond remembers the first punch landing on his jaw, the shock of the impact, the way his glasses
flew off and skittered across the floor. He remembers the man’s hand closing around his throat, the
pressure building in his ears, the desperate scrabbling of his own shoes against the carpet as he
tried to push back. What he cannot do, no matter how hard he tries, is deliberately recall the
sequence of events after that. The memory is there—he knows it is—but it feels like a locked drawer.
When he tries to open it deliberately, his mind goes blank, and only fragments remain: the grunt of
the man’s effort, the distant sound of a janitor’s key in the lock. The full narrative, the order of
blows, how long it lasted—all of it is inaccessible to conscious recall.

But the memory does not stay locked. It comes to him unbidden, usually at night or in moments of
quiet. It plays like a film he cannot stop, starting from the moment the man entered his office and
ending with the janitor pulling the attacker off. And when it plays, Raymond’s body responds as
though it is happening again. His shoulders hunch, his neck tenses, his hands curl into fists at his
sides. He finds himself in the same defensive posture, his back pressed against an imaginary wall,
his head tilted to protect his throat. These bodily reenactments are exhausting and
disorienting—they leave him feeling as though he has been physically assaulted all over again, even
if he has not moved from his chair.

The experience of these intrusive memories has fundamentally rewired how Raymond moves through the
world. Every time the film plays—every time his body snaps into that protective crouch—his sense of
safety erodes further. He has come to believe that danger is everywhere, waiting to spring. He is
perpetually on edge, startling at the slam of a classroom door or a student shouting in the hallway.
His startle response is so pronounced that colleagues have begun to notice; he jumps when someone
taps his shoulder, and his face flushes with shame afterward. More than anything, Raymond scans. He
scans every room he enters—meetings, the cafeteria, his own living room—for exits. He notes the
nearest door, the windows, the fire escape. He also scans for weapons. He notices the letter opener
on a desk, the heavy stapler, the pair of scissors left out in the art room. He cannot help it. His
eyes go to these objects automatically, and a cold calculation runs through his mind: *Could that
hurt me? How quickly could he reach it?*

This hypervigilance is worst at work, where he cannot avoid the setting of the assault. Every time
he walks into his office, he sees the spot on the carpet where he fell. He has rearranged the
furniture so his desk faces the door, but he still feels exposed. He has begun holding meetings in
the conference room instead, and he no longer stays late after school. His wife has noticed that he
has stopped going to the grocery store, preferring to send her instead; the crowded aisles and the
unpredictability of other shoppers feel unbearable. He has also stopped attending his son’s
basketball games, claiming exhaustion, but the real reason is the bleachers—he cannot sit with his
back to the crowd, and scanning the exits for two hours leaves him drained.

What finally brought Raymond to seek help was a moment of acute shame. Last week, during a fire
drill, he grabbed a teacher’s arm and pulled her toward the exit, his heart hammering, his voice
sharp with panic. She was startled and pulled away, and he saw the look on her face—fear of him. He
realized then that he was no longer the calm, steady principal his staff relied on. He was a man who
jumped at shadows, who saw weapons in paperweights, who could not remember the worst moment of his
life but could not stop reliving it. He is terrified that he will lose his job, that his marriage
will fray, that he will pass this vigilance on to his children. But what frightens him most is the
thought that this is permanent—that his brain has been permanently rewired to see threat everywhere,
and that the man who attacked him has taken not just that evening, but every safe moment that
follows.
