police were called; a restraining order was issued. But by then, the damage had taken root.

The memory of those nights had become a strange, fractured thing. Sometimes, when Layla was standing
at the stove at home, she would catch a whiff of stale coffee and industrial cleaner—the exact smell
of the restaurant lobby during those final shifts—and her stomach would drop before her mind had
even caught up. At other times, she could replay the entire sequence of events in her head—the face
at the window, the note fluttering onto her dashboard—and feel nothing at all, as though she were
watching a stranger in a movie. The story itself felt unreal, too extreme to have actually happened
to her, and this unreality only deepened her confusion about who she had become.

What frightened Layla most was the belief that if she allowed herself to think about the stalking in
any sustained way, she would unravel completely. She had come to feel that she no longer knew
herself; the woman who had once joked with coworkers and walked to her car without a backward glance
was gone, replaced by someone brittle and watchful. This sense of lost identity fed directly into
her vigilance. Because she could not trust her own mind to handle the memory, she began scanning the
world for danger, and the more she scanned, the more dangerous the world appeared. She studied every
face that came through the restaurant’s doors, reading body language for signs of fixation, certain
that anyone could be the next person to follow her home.

The checking rituals started gradually. At first, Layla simply made sure her apartment door was

living room to confirm the windows were bolted. She began waking during the night, heart pounding,
to listen for footsteps in the hallway. Each time she found nothing amiss, the relief was short-
lived, because the very act of checking reinforced her belief that she was unsafe. And that belief,
in turn, made the memories more vivid and more intrusive: she would be lying in the dark and
suddenly smell the coffee and cleaner again, or see the photograph in her mind’s eye, and the cycle
would tighten.

Her husband, a retired mechanic, grew frustrated. He would lock the door himself and tell her it was
done, but Layla could not stop. She had come to believe that people could not be trusted—not
strangers, not coworkers, not even the neighbors who waved hello. This mistrust bled into her work
life. She requested only day shifts, which meant fewer tips and more conflict with the schedule
manager. Her earnings dropped. When the manager asked why, she could not explain that she was
terrified of the dark now, that the late shift had become a landscape of potential threat.

What finally brought Layla to seek help was a morning in late October when she found herself
standing in her own kitchen, refusing to open the refrigerator door because she thought she heard
breathing on the other side. She stood there for ten minutes, paralyzed, before realizing that the
sound was only the old compressor. It was in that moment, with her hands shaking and her husband
calling her name from the next room, that she understood she could not keep living this way. She was
afraid of what she might become if she did not find a way out—more than that, she was afraid that
the woman she used to be was already gone for good.
