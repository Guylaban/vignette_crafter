Marcus is a 48-year-old shopowner who owns a small hardware store in a suburban town, a business he
inherited from his father and has run for over two decades. He is married, with two adult children
who live out of state. He came to therapy at his wife’s urging, after months of watching him
withdraw and grow increasingly volatile. The event that shattered Marcus’s world happened three
years ago, when his younger brother, David, was killed in a car accident. Marcus was not present,
but he was the one who received the call from the hospital, and he was the one who had to identify
the body. What stays with him most vividly is the sight of David in the morgue—the stillness, the
discoloration, the way his brother’s face looked wrong, like a wax version of itself. That image is
not a memory; it is a presence. It plays over and over in his mind, unbidden, like a film he cannot
turn off. The memory feels as though it is happening right now, not something that is over and done
with. The clarity of it is punishing: he can recall the fluorescent hum, the cold tile floor, the
exact weight of the sheet when he pulled it back. When that memory surges, it does so with such
force that Marcus finds himself reacting as if he is in immediate danger—his heart pounds, he
startles at the smallest sound, and a wave of fear and guilt washes over him so strong it leaves him
breathless. He has come to believe that if he lets himself think about David at all, the memory will
swallow him whole.

To manage this, Marcus developed a series of strategies that have, over time, made everything worse.
He smokes constantly now, a habit he had quit years ago, because the act of lighting a cigarette and
inhaling gives him something to do with his hands and his breath when the memory threatens to break
through. He avoids thinking about the event with a vigilance that is almost automatic: if a thought
about David starts to form, he mentally shuts it down, turning his attention to inventory counts or
customer complaints. He avoids anything that reminds him of grief—he stopped attending church
because the mention of prayers for the departed felt unbearable, and he drives three miles out of
his way to avoid the cemetery where David is buried. Lately, even casual conversation about someone
losing a loved one triggers him; he will abruptly leave the room or change the subject, his face
going tight. Because he is so often on edge, he jumps at ordinary sounds like a door closing or a
customer’s sudden movement behind him in the store, and these startle reactions in turn make the
memory flash more intensely, as if his body is telling his mind that danger is still present. The
more he smokes and avoids, the more the memory intrudes, and the more the memory intrudes, the more
he smokes and avoids. He has started to notice that his avoidance of grief reminders actually makes
him more sensitive to them—the mere sight of a funeral home sign on his drive home now feels like a
threat, and he will take a longer route to avoid it, which then reminds him of why he is avoiding
it, which brings the memory roaring back.

The consequences have been concrete. His wife has told him she feels like a stranger in her own
home; he no longer talks about David, and when she brings up her own grief, he walks away. At the
hardware store, he has snapped at customers who linger too long in conversation, and his staff have
learned not to mention anything personal. What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a moment last
week when his daughter called to tell him she was pregnant, and instead of feeling joy, he felt a
cold wave of terror that something would happen to her too. He hung up and sat in the dark of his
living room, chain-smoking, certain that he was losing his grip on the life he had left. He is
afraid that talking about David will break him, but he is more afraid of what he is becoming.
