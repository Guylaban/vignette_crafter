Before the accident, Priya had known herself as the kind of teacher who could quiet a rowdy
classroom with a look, who remembered every student’s name by the second week, who brought homemade
snacks for the debate team on competition days. She taught tenth-grade English in a suburban Chicago
high school, and she loved it—the smell of dry-erase markers, the sound of thirty voices discussing
a poem, the small triumphs when a struggling reader finished a chapter on their own. That version of
Priya ended on a rainy Tuesday in October, during a routine fire drill.

She had been hurrying her last-period class down the crowded stairwell when a ninth-grader near the
bottom tripped on a loose shoelace and fell forward. Priya, two steps above, felt the student’s
weight against her leg, and in the chaos of alarms and chatter, she did not see the younger boy
already sitting on the landing, recovering from his own stumble. She stepped back to brace herself,
and her heel came down hard on his outstretched hand. The crack was audible even over the alarm. The
boy—Marcus, a quiet kid who loved drawing dragons in the margins of his worksheets—screamed. Priya
saw his fingers bent at an angle that made her stomach drop. She had broken three of them. Surgery,
eight weeks of physical therapy, and a lingering nerve damage that meant Marcus could no longer grip
a pencil the way he used to. The school district launched an investigation; no one blamed her,
officially, but she blamed herself with a ferocity that surprised even her.

In the weeks that followed, Priya found she could not stop replaying the moment. The memory did not
feel like a memory at all. When she closed her eyes, she was back on that stairwell: the smell of
rain-soaked concrete and floor wax filled her nostrils, the sound of the alarm was a physical
pressure in her ears, and the image of Marcus’s hand—frozen, wrong—hung in her mind like a
photograph she could not look away from. It felt present, as if she could reach out and touch it.
She had come to believe that the accident happened because of the way she acted—because she had not
looked, had not thought, had been too focused on getting her class out the door. What frightened her
most was the growing certainty that she was no longer capable of doing the things that had defined
her: keeping students safe, staying calm under pressure, being the teacher everyone trusted. She
felt like a permanent outsider now, even in her own classroom, as though a glass wall had slid down
between her and everyone she had once belonged with.

The causal connections tightened around her like a vine. The memory of the stairwell was always
waiting, and it was triggered by things she could not avoid. When a colleague praised her for a
well-run lesson, the words landed wrong—she was certain they did not know what she had done, and the
praise felt like an accusation. Positive emotions, even a moment of quiet satisfaction after a
student understood a difficult concept, confused her; they seemed to belong to someone else, and
they inevitably summoned the image of Marcus’s hand. Talk about accidents—a mention of a car crash
on the morning news, a student’s story about a bike fall—sent her straight back to the stairwell.
And each time the memory surfaced, it confirmed her worst appraisals: *See? You are not capable. You
are an outsider now.* This loop made her hypervigilant. She began scanning her environment for any
potential hazard—a loose backpack strap, a wet patch on the floor, a child running in the
hallway—not to protect her students, but to avoid ever being in a position where she might cause
harm again.

Over the months, the avoidance became a second job. She stopped volunteering for hallway duty. She
asked for a classroom on the ground floor so she would never have to use the stairs. She avoided the
lunchroom during busy periods, fearing crowds where someone might trip. When her principal offered
her the role of grade-level lead—a promotion she had once wanted—she declined without explanation.
Her husband, Ravi, noticed she no longer wanted to cook together; the kitchen felt too small, too
many sharp objects, too much potential for a sudden movement that could go wrong. She started coming
home from work exhausted, not from teaching but from the constant vigilance.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a moment in class last week. A student dropped a heavy
textbook, and the sound made her flinch so violently that she knocked over a cup of coffee,
splashing it across her lesson plans. The class went quiet. She stood there, hands shaking, and
realized she could not keep living this way. She is terrified that therapy will confirm what she
already believes—that she is permanently broken, that she should never have been a teacher—but she
is also desperate to find a way back to herself.
