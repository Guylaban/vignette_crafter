Tariq still remembers the night the call came in. He was five years on the job, a paramedic in a
busy city, and he and his partner were dispatched to a multi-car collision on the interstate. What
he hadn't known until he arrived was that his younger brother, Amir, had been driving home from a
late shift. Tariq found him pinned in the wreckage, conscious, bleeding from a deep gash across his
scalp, his leg twisted under the dashboard. Tariq worked the scene, his hands moving on autopilot,
but what he saw—the open wound, the way his brother's eyes looked through him, the smell of gasoline
mixing with the antiseptic from the trauma kit—etched itself into him. He remembers the terror of
that moment, the same cold terror that now floods back whenever the memory surfaces, and it feels
completely real across all his senses at once. There are parts of that night he cannot piece
together no matter how hard he tries—the exact sequence of extrication, who cut the roof off the
car—but the image of Amir's face, the smell of the hospital corridor afterward, those are seared
into him.

In the months that followed, Tariq returned to work, but the job itself became a minefield. Every
visible injury on a patient—a laceration, a compound fracture—sends him straight back to that night.
He catches the antiseptic smell of the hospital on his uniform and his stomach tightens, his
concentration scattering like leaves in wind. He has come to believe that he has permanently changed
for the worse, that something essential in him broke that night and cannot be fixed. He is certain
he cannot rely on himself anymore, because if he could not protect his own brother, what good is he?
The thought that he failed to protect Amir—that he should have done more, should have gotten him out
faster—is a weight he carries constantly. These appraisals make the triggers worse: every time he
sees a victim's face, he thinks of Amir's face, and the cycle tightens.

When a patient comes in with visible wounds, Tariq's body reacts before his mind can catch up. He
flinches if a colleague touches his shoulder unexpectedly, and he finds himself scanning the
ambulance bay for a seat with his back to the wall, a position where no one can approach from
behind. This hypervigilance feeds the memory: the more on edge he feels, the more vividly the
sensory reliving returns. He can smell the antiseptic, hear his brother's labored breathing, feel
the weight of the trauma shears in his hand. The memory then reinforces his negative appraisals—he
remembers his hands shaking, remembers the delay, and tells himself again that he failed.

to think. But overworking means he is constantly exposed to triggers, and the suppressed thoughts
only push the memory down until it bursts back stronger. He tries hard not to think about the
trauma, but the effort of suppression exhausts him, and the memories intrude anyway, often at night
when he is alone. He has started avoiding going to work when he can, calling in sick, trading shifts

cannot handle the job, that he is broken, that he failed. The more he avoids, the more he believes
