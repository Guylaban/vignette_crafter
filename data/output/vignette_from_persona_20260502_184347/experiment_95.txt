Mai was forty-two, a project manager for a regional healthcare logistics firm, and married for
fifteen years. Two years ago, she was hospitalized for a sudden, severe septic infection following a
routine surgery. The illness nearly killed her; she spent ten days in the ICU, drifting in and out
of consciousness, tethered to monitors, lines, and a breathing tube. What she remembered most
vividly was not the pain but the cold—the relentless, sterile cold of the IV fluids entering her
arm, and the sharp, disinfectant smell of the room that seemed to cling to her skin even after she
was discharged.

Now, those sensory fragments had become landmines. A whiff of rubbing alcohol in a hallway could
stop her mid-sentence, flooding her with a wave of panic that felt like drowning. The cold air from
an air-conditioning vent in her office made her flinch, her mind instantly pulling her back to that
ICU bed. When she saw a colleague’s insulin pen on a desk, her stomach dropped, and she had to
excuse herself to the restroom. Each time a trigger hit, Mai felt the memory surge back—not as a
coherent story, but as a fractured, terrifying jolt. Some moments, like the doctor’s face when he
told her she might not survive, were excruciatingly clear; others, like the first three days, were a
complete, black void. The worst part was that sometimes she would feel the full, crushing emotional
intensity of that terror without any image at all—just a raw, disembodied dread that left her
shaking.

In response, Mai had developed a rigid set of habits. She avoided hospitals entirely, rescheduling
annual physicals and driving an extra twenty minutes to avoid passing the medical center where she
had been treated. At work, she monitored her own thoughts constantly, scanning for any hint of the
memory or the fear, trying to catch it before it could take hold. She also tried, almost ritually,
to erase the memory itself—telling herself it was over, it didn’t matter, it wasn’t real. But these
efforts backfired. The more she tried to push the memory away, the more the fragmented, unreal
quality of it bothered her. It felt so extreme, so unbelievable, that she sometimes wondered if it
had actually happened to her. This sense of unreality, in turn, made her feel like she was losing
her grip on reality, which only intensified her thought monitoring and her determination to suppress
it.

These strategies also fed a deeper, more corrosive set of beliefs. Mai had come to believe that her
life was destroyed by what happened—that she was no longer the capable, reliable person she had once
been. She was certain she had failed in her most important role: as a mother to her two teenage
children, who had been terrified while she was sick and whom she now felt unable to protect or even
fully engage with. Every cold draft or medical smell reminded her that her body was permanently
damaged, that she was fundamentally vulnerable. The triggers did not just provoke fear; they
confirmed her negative appraisals, reinforcing the idea that she was broken and that her life was
over.

The memories themselves strengthened these beliefs. When the story felt unreal, Mai would tell
herself that she must be exaggerating, that she was weak for not moving on—which deepened her sense
of failure. And when the emotional flashbacks hit without a clear image, she interpreted them as
proof that her mind was fundamentally broken. The cycle tightened: a cold gust in the parking lot
triggered a memory fragment; that fragment confirmed her belief that her body was ruined; that
belief made her scan her thoughts for danger; the scanning triggered a new memory; and the loop
began again. Her husband had started to notice her pulling away, and her children had stopped asking
her to drive them to activities because she always made excuses. At work, she had begun missing
deadlines, her mind constantly occupied with the effort of avoidance.

What finally brought Mai to a therapist’s office was a panic attack in the grocery store, triggered
by a shopper wearing a strong floral perfume that reminded her of the hospital’s hand soap. She had
fled the aisle, leaving her cart behind, and sat in her car for twenty minutes, unable to stop
trembling. She was terrified that she was going crazy, that the memory would eventually swallow her
whole. But she was also, quietly, hoping that someone might finally help her believe that it was
real—and that she could survive it.
