the city. The event that changed everything happened on a humid August night, when he was the first
responder to a van overturned on an industrial access road. A chemical container had ruptured in the
crash, and as he climbed through the wreckage to reach the driver, the sharp, acrid smell of alcohol
mixed with something industrial hit him so hard his eyes watered. Within seconds, his throat
tightened and his vision blurred. He dragged the driver out, but by the time backup arrived, Rajesh
was vomiting and struggling to breathe. He was decontaminated at the hospital and released, but the
smell never really left him.

In the weeks that followed, Rajesh found that the memory of that night would return without
warning—a flash of the overturned van, the chemical burn in his throat, the terror of not knowing if
he would pass out before he could get the driver clear. A sudden flicker of darkness in his
peripheral vision, like the shadow of the van’s interior, could bring the whole scene back, and when
it did, he would feel so overwhelmed by emotion that he had to stop whatever he was doing. The
terror he had felt in that moment returned just as vividly, as if he were back inside the wreckage.
He began to notice that the smell of alcohol—someone’s drink at a party, a spilled beer at a grocery
store—would instantly trigger the same visceral response, and he would find himself scanning his
surroundings for the source, his heart racing.

To cope, Rajesh started to avoid anything connected to medical work. He took leave from his
paramedic shift, stopped driving past the hospital where he had been treated, and refused to let his
wife check his blood pressure at home. He also began to keep himself constantly surrounded by
people, filling his evenings with visits to friends and family, even staying late at the local
community center just to avoid being alone with his thoughts. He had come to believe that if he had
a quiet moment, the memory would ambush him, and he would not be able to function. But the constant
socializing had a cost: his wife grew frustrated that he was never home, and he felt exhausted by
the effort of maintaining a cheerful front. Sometimes, while sitting in a crowded room, a whiff of
alcohol on someone’s breath would cut through the noise, and he would feel the memory surge up
again, unbidden. He would then ruminate for hours about how the accident could have been
prevented—if only he had checked the cargo manifest, if only he had waited for the hazmat team—and
this rumination would, in turn, make the memory feel more vivid and more terrifying, as if he were
reliving it all over again.

The cycle tightened over months. A trigger would bring the memory, the memory would drive him to
seek out company, but the company itself would sometimes expose him to the alcohol smell, which
would trigger the memory again. He started to avoid certain friends who drank, and he began to feel
trapped in his own home, because even the darkness of his bedroom at night could bring back the
image of the van’s interior. His wife noticed he was jumpy and distant, and she suggested he talk to
someone. What finally brought Rajesh to seek help was a moment at a family dinner: his brother-in-

manage this on his own. He is afraid that if he lets himself stop avoiding, the memory will destroy
him completely. But he is also tired—tired of the constant vigilance, the social performance, the
feeling that his own mind is a trap he cannot escape.
