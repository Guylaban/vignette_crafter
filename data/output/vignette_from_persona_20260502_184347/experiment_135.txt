The flood came at night. Kwame had been working a late shift at the grocery store, restocking
shelves, when the sirens began. He drove home through rain so thick the headlights were useless, and
by the time he reached his neighborhood, water was already lapping at the curbs. He made it inside,
grabbed his wife, and they climbed to the attic as the first floor filled. For six hours, they sat
in the dark, listening to the groan of the house, the slap of debris against the walls, and the
sound of things they owned being swallowed below. When rescue came at dawn, Kwame was shaking so
hard he could not speak. He had been certain the attic door would give way and they would drown in
the dark.

In the months that followed, that certainty did not fade. Kwame came to believe the world had
revealed its true nature that night—unpredictable, hostile, ready to destroy without warning. He
told himself he had to be on guard all the time now, that the disaster had permanently changed him
for the worse. These beliefs shaped everything. He began to scan every room before he could settle,
his eyes moving automatically to exits, to windows, to anything that might offer escape. At work, he
would position himself near the back door of the stockroom, even when it meant awkwardly maneuvering
around pallets. His wife noticed he no longer slept through the night; he lay awake, listening, his
body tense as if waiting for the next wave. He told her it was necessary—he could not afford to be
caught off guard again.

The memories themselves were like a film that played over and over. When Kwame remembered the flood,
he did not simply recall it; he felt the water rising around him again, smelled the wet plaster,
heard his own ragged breathing in the attic dark. The memory did not feel like something that was
over. It felt present, immediate, as if the flood was still happening and he was still trapped.
These episodes would seize him without warning—a sudden rain shower, the sound of a drain gurgling,
the smell of damp concrete—and he would be back in that attic, his heart hammering, his palms slick.
Each time it happened, it confirmed what he already believed: that he was damaged, that the world
was dangerous, that he could never let his guard down. So he stayed watchful. He kept near exits. He
distracted himself with anything—his phone, a conversation, a task—to keep the memories at bay.

But the vigilance fed the memories. The more he scanned for threat, the more his brain stayed locked
in that survival mode, and the more the flood memory surfaced, unbidden. And each time the memory
came, it reinforced his sense that he had to be hypervigilant, because danger was real and it had
nearly killed him. He began to check his surroundings constantly, even in his own home. He would
walk through the house before bed, checking the windows, the basement floor, the sump pump. His wife
asked him what he was looking for. He could not answer in a way that made sense to her.

The cycle tightened over a year. Kwame’s distraction strategies—keeping busy, focusing on small
tasks—helped in the moment but left him exhausted, and exhaustion made the memories harder to push
away. When they broke through, he felt even more convinced that he was broken, that the flood had
taken something from him he would never get back. That conviction made him watchful again. He
started having difficulty falling asleep because he felt he needed to stay alert, and the sleep
deprivation made his mood brittle and his concentration poor. At work, a coworker joked about the
weather, and Kwame snapped at him. He had to apologize, but he could not explain why the joke had
felt like a threat.

What finally brought him to seek help was a morning in early spring. He was in the break room at the
grocery store, and someone opened a can of soda. The hiss of carbonation—that sound, so
ordinary—sent him into a full-body freeze. For a moment, he was back in the attic, and the water was
rising. He dropped his coffee. His hands would not stop shaking. His manager asked if he was okay,
and Kwame said he was fine, but he was not fine. He was afraid of what would happen the next time a
sound caught him off guard. He was afraid of how small his world had become. He was afraid that the
man he had been before the flood was gone, and he did not know if he could find his way back.
