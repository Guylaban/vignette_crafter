fatally wounded beside her. She had not spoken of that night since leaving the field, and for the
first year home, she had managed to keep the memories at bay by throwing herself into her work.

But the memories did not stay buried. They surfaced as vivid pictures—the flash of the blast, the
silhouette of the wall against the firelight, the stillness of her colleague’s chest. These images
felt alien to her, as though they belonged to someone else’s life, yet they forced their way into
her mind without warning. The worst moments came during council meetings. A colleague would drop a
heavy binder on a table, and the sharp bang would send Elena’s heart racing, the mortar flash
flooding her vision before she could stop it. She had come to believe that the world was a
fundamentally dangerous place, and that she would never be able to feel normal emotions again—that
something inside her had been permanently broken by what she had seen. She was certain that if she

images away whenever they surfaced.

The triggers kept reinforcing her belief that danger was everywhere. A man breathing heavily on the
subway, the sound of a car backfiring, even the clatter of a dropped tray in the council

back into the memory, the images felt more alien, more like a film she was watching of someone
else’s terror. She found herself scanning every room for exits, her body tensing at the slightest
noise, and she began to avoid crowded spaces altogether. She stopped attending evening community
events, citing scheduling conflicts, and she asked her assistant to ensure her office door remained
closed at all times. The council members noticed her withdrawing, and her relationships with
colleagues grew strained; she had once been known for her warmth and collaboration, but now she was
distant and easily startled.

The memory itself fed her belief that the world was unsafe. Every time the images returned, she was
reminded of how helpless she had been, how fragile life was. And that belief, in turn, made her more
vigilant, more likely to notice heavy breathing or sudden bangs—she was always listening, always
waiting for the next explosion. What frightened her most was the growing sense that she was losing
herself, that the woman who had once led relief missions with steady hands was now a stranger who
jumped at shadows and could not sit through a committee meeting without her pulse pounding in her
ears.

this was something she could outrun. She was terrified that therapy would force her to relive the
night she had spent years trying to forget, but she was equally afraid of what she would become if
