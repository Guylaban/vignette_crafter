Leilani is a 19-year-old fisherwoman from a coastal community in the Pacific Islands, now living in
a small American town. She works on her uncle’s fishing boat, a job that demands long hours on the
water and constant vigilance. Eighteen months ago, she witnessed her cousin drown during a sudden
storm. They had been fishing together when the boat capsized. Leilani managed to grab a life ring,
but her cousin was pulled under by the current before she could reach him. She still remembers the
sound of his shouts, then the silence, and the way her own body froze—she could not move, could not
think, could not save him.

Since that day, Leilani has been haunted by the memory. It forces its way into her mind without
warning: the cold water, the panic, the guilt that floods her whenever she recalls the event. She
often feels the full emotional intensity of that moment—the terror, the helplessness—without any
clear image, just a wave of dread that leaves her shaking. When she hears footsteps behind her,
especially on the dock or in a quiet street, her body reacts before her mind can catch up. Her heart
pounds, her muscles tense, and she is certain something terrible is about to happen. The same thing
happens when she hears sounds that remind her of that day—the creak of rope, the slap of water
against a hull, the distant shout of a coworker. She has come to believe that her body keeps
reacting as if she is still in danger, even when she knows she is safe, and this frightens her
deeply.

Leilani also fears her own anger. She worries that if she lets herself feel the rage she sometimes
carries—rage at the storm, at herself, at the world—she will lose control and do something terrible.
This fear has led her to avoid sex with her boyfriend, because she is afraid that intimacy might
unlock something violent inside her. Instead, she spends long hours on her phone, scrolling through
games and social media, to keep her mind occupied. She works excessively, often staying on the boat
well past her shift, because being alone with her thoughts feels unbearable. When she is not
working, she is glued to a screen, avoiding the silence that invites the memory back in.

The more she works, the more exhausted she becomes, and the harder it is to concentrate. She has
difficulty focusing on simple tasks—sorting catch, reading a text—because her mind is always
scanning for threats. When something takes longer than expected, like a repair or a delivery, she
immediately assumes something bad is happening. Her uncle has noticed her jumpiness and her
withdrawal, but she has not told him why. She has become convinced that she failed to protect her
cousin, and that this failure defines her. This belief makes her more sensitive to triggers: every
footstep, every unexpected sound, feels like confirmation that danger is near and that she will fail
again.

The memory of that day feeds her guilt, and the guilt makes the memory return more often. She tries
to outrun it with work and screens, but the strategies backfire—the longer she avoids, the more the
memory intrudes when she finally stops. Her body stays on high alert, and each time her body reacts,
she is reminded of how powerless she felt, which deepens her belief that she cannot trust herself.
She has started to avoid the dock at certain times of day, when footsteps echo most, and she has
stopped walking home after dark. Her boyfriend has asked why she seems distant, but she cannot bring
herself to explain.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was a moment on the boat last week. A rope snapped, and
the sound sent her into a panic so intense that she dropped her knife and nearly fell overboard. Her
uncle grabbed her arm and asked what was wrong, and for the first time, she could not pretend it was
nothing. She is terrified that she will lose her job, or worse, that she will hurt someone if she
does not get control of herself. But what she hopes for, quietly, is that someone will tell her she
is not a monster—that the guilt and the fear do not mean she is dangerous, just that she is still
carrying what happened.
