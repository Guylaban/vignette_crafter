were fragmented, disconnected, and each time one surfaced, it brought a wave of guilt so intense he
could barely breathe. He was certain he should have done more, should have run faster, should have
somehow stopped the car. He told himself that the legal system had failed them both, that the driver
had faced no consequences, and that the institutions meant to protect his family had simply let them
down.

These appraisals fed directly into a constant, low-grade threat that Amir could not shake. Even when
friends or his therapist told him he was safe, he found it impossible to believe. He could not
concentrate on scripts or rehearsals; his mind was always scanning for danger. The sound of a child
crying, even on television, would send his heart racing and his thoughts spiraling back to the park.
Media coverage of similar accidents became unbearable, and he began to avoid the news entirely.

To manage this pervasive threat, Amir developed a set of strategies that, over time, reshaped his
life. He became a people pleaser, agreeing with whatever others wanted because disagreeing felt too
risky, as if any conflict might invite catastrophe. He could not bear to be alone in his
apartment—the silence amplified the guilt—so he started spending evenings at a friend’s house, often
staying until late. But crowds, too, became unbearable; shopping centres and public events felt like
traps, full of unpredictable sounds and movements. He began to restrict his outings to early

rehearsals, which reinforced his sense of failure and inaction. The guilt on recall grew sharper
