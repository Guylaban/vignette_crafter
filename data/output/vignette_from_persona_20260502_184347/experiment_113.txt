that coated everything. The tsunami had swept through his coastal village in the early morning,
tearing his wife from his grasp as they tried to climb to the roof. He had been an auditor for the

it—sometimes while driving, sometimes while sitting at his desk, the odor so vivid it made him gag.

story that had happened to someone else, a film he had watched rather than a life he had lived.

evening at home, the silence settling around him like a blanket—that was when the taste would rise
in his throat. He had learned to dread silence, because it meant something dangerous was about to
happen. So he kept the television on, always, even when he was not watching. He smoked constantly,
the ritual of lighting a cigarette and inhaling the sharp, familiar burn giving him something to
focus on besides the creeping sense of dread. He had stopped going to unfamiliar places because he
could not assess the danger; a new grocery store, a friend’s house in a different neighborhood—these
felt like traps. Even delays became omens. If the bus was late, he was certain an accident had
occurred. If a colleague was slow to respond to an email, he assumed the worst.

could not stop them. He felt dead inside, hollowed out, and this numbness was a kind of protection.

disaster. He did not talk about the tsunami with anyone; even a casual question about his past made
his chest tighten, so he changed the subject or excused himself. He had become certain that if he
allowed himself to feel happy or relaxed, the other shoe would drop. That feeling of happiness had
become a trigger in itself, a flicker of warmth that immediately sent a spike of cold fear through

waking up beside his wife, the sun streaming in, and how that perfect peace had been shattered. So
now, when he felt a moment of ease, he would reach for his cigarettes, or find a reason to be busy,

more potent. He began to see danger in the ordinary: a door that was slightly ajar, a distant siren,
the way the light fell across his living room floor at dusk. He had stopped going to the office gym

he made the call anyway, his hand shaking as he dialed, because the alternative, he knew, was to
drown again.
