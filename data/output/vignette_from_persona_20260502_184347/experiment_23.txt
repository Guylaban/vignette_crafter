The crash happened on a clear Tuesday afternoon. Leilani had just finished a consultation at the
private psychiatric hospital where she worked, and was driving home along a familiar coastal road. A
driver in the oncoming lane, later found to have suffered a medical emergency, swerved across the
median and struck her car head-on at nearly fifty miles per hour. The impact crushed the front of
her sedan and spun it into the guardrail. She remembers the shriek of metal, the smell of gasoline,
and the strange, detached thought that she was about to die. She was trapped in the wreckage for
nearly twenty minutes before emergency services cut her free. Physically, she sustained a fractured
sternum and deep bruising, but no lasting injury. Something else, however, had been broken.

In the months that followed, Leilani found she could not tell a clear story of what had happened,
even to herself. The sequence of events was confused; she could not put the memories of the crash in
the right order. Instead, something in her surroundings could instantly bring back the full memory
without warning—the squeal of brakes from a passing car, the sight of a sedan with a crumpled hood,
the scent of oil or gasoline. These moments would flood her with a visceral, wordless terror that
felt as real as the crash itself. Because she could not make coherent sense of the memory, she came
to believe that she had permanently changed for the worse. She began to doubt her own judgment. A
woman who had spent thirty years assessing risk and guiding others through crisis now felt she could
not trust herself to do the right thing. The world, once a place of professional competence and
order, now felt fundamentally dangerous.

Leilani began to avoid sleep. Lying in the dark, her mind would drift toward the crash, and she
would wake during the night to check whether she was safe, her heart hammering. She felt jumpy and
easily startled, and she noticed she felt unsafe unless her back was against a wall where she could
see the room. These vigilance habits, born of her belief that the world was dangerous, only
reinforced her sense of permanent damage. At work, she found herself avoiding the hospital parking
lot and taking long, circuitous routes to her office. She stopped attending professional conferences
that required highway driving. She also began to avoid sex, not because she had lost desire, but
because the physical vulnerability of intimacy felt intolerable. She tried hard to control her
emotions, believing that if she let herself feel the full weight of the terror, she would fall apart
entirely.

This emotional control, however, made her memory worse. By suppressing her feelings, she found the
memory fragments became more intrusive and less ordered. The more she tried to lock the crash away,
the more it ambushed her. And each time she was ambushed by a trigger—a car accident on the news, a
patient describing a near-miss in traffic—she felt her negative appraisals deepen. The world was
dangerous; she was permanently damaged; she could not trust herself. This loop tightened over
months. She became hypervigilant even on the safest days, scanning the road for erratic drivers,
flinching at sudden sounds. Her sleep became a battlefield. She would lie awake for hours, then wake
in a panic at 3 a.m., checking the locks, the windows, the silence.

The consequence was occupational. Leilani, once a respected clinician known for her steady hand,
began to cancel afternoon appointments if they required a drive through the city at rush hour. She
took a leave of absence, telling herself it was temporary. A year later, she had not returned. Her
divorce, years before, had left her living alone, and there was no one to notice how small her world
had become. What finally brought her to seek help was a single moment: she was walking to the
grocery store, and a child on a bicycle braked sharply on the sidewalk. The sound sent her to her
knees, gasping, convinced the crash was happening again. She sat on the curb and wept, and for the
first time, she allowed herself to think that perhaps she could not fix this on her own. She is
afraid of what the therapy will ask of her—afraid that reliving the crash will break her—but she is
more afraid of spending the rest of her life with her back to a wall.
