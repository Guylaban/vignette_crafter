It was a Tuesday evening in late October when Marco’s life fractured. He was a parole officer,
fourteen years into the job, and he’d been called to a routine home check on a client in a South
Side housing project. The client’s file was clean, the visit was supposed to take ten minutes. But
when Marco stepped into the hallway, three plainclothes officers from a joint task force mistook him
for the subject of a separate warrant. He was shoved face-first into the cinderblock wall, his arms
wrenched behind his back hard enough that his right shoulder popped. One officer knelt on his neck
while another screamed at him. Marco kept saying, “I’m a parole officer, I’m a parole officer,” but
his badge had fallen out of his pocket and they wouldn’t listen. It took seven minutes for a
supervisor to arrive and call them off. Seven minutes of his own colleagues—people who wore the same
city badge he did—pressing him into the floor while he gasped for air. He was never formally
apologized to. The department offered a brief internal memo. Nothing changed.

In the weeks that followed, Marco found he could only remember the event in jagged pieces—the cold
of the floor, the weight on his spine, but not the face of the officer who’d knelt on him. The
memory felt like a photograph torn in half; he knew the outline of what happened, but the details
kept slipping away. When he tried to tell his wife, the story came out flat and distant, as if he
were describing something that had happened to someone else. He found himself pushing the memory
away before it could fully surface, stopping himself mid-thought because the injustice of it burned
too hot. What happened to me was deeply unfair, he would think, and nothing has been done about it.
The institution that should have protected me let me down. That sense of betrayal—by officers he
would have once called brothers—lodged in his chest like a splinter.

The betrayal began to color everything. At work, when a parolee showed up late or lied about a drug
test, Marco felt a flash of rage that was out of proportion. People misbehaving, he realized, now
felt personal—a violation of an unwritten contract, the same kind of contract the officers had
broken. Even ordinary stress at the office—a backlogged file, a missed deadline—triggered the same
tightness in his throat. And the most potent trigger was his own body: when his heart started
racing, whether from coffee or a brisk walk, he would freeze, convinced he was about to be attacked
again. That pounding in his chest became a warning bell he couldn’t ignore.

He began scanning his surroundings constantly. Walking into the parole office each morning, he
checked every corner, every open door, every person who stood too still. Unusual silence—the kind
that fell over a hallway between conversations—made him certain something dangerous was about to
happen. He startled when a coworker tapped his shoulder from behind, and the startle response itself
made him feel ashamed and exposed, which only deepened his anger at the system that had turned him
into this. The threat he felt was not abstract; it lived in the quiet spaces, in the weight of his
own heartbeat, in every misbehaving client who reminded him that rules could be broken by anyone,
including the people who were supposed to enforce them.

The cycle tightened over months. Each time Marco felt a trigger—a racing heart, a silence, a
client’s lie—his mind jumped straight to the injustice of what had happened. That injustice, in
turn, made the memory harder to hold onto; he would try to recall the exact moment the officer’s
knee pressed into his neck, and the image would dissolve into a blur of anger and shame. The
incomplete picture left him vigilant, scanning for the parts of the story he could not retrieve, and
that vigilance made every silence feel like a trap. He started avoiding the South Side entirely,
taking longer routes to work, and he stopped attending the department’s annual law enforcement
appreciation dinner. His wife noticed he was quieter at home, that he flinched when she came up
behind him in the kitchen. He told her it was nothing.

What finally brought Marco to a therapist’s office was a Tuesday morning in the parole office, when
a new client—a young man with a clean record and a nervous smile—sat down across from him. The
client was polite, cooperative, everything Marco should have welcomed. But the man’s stillness
reminded Marco of the hallway before the officers grabbed him. Marco’s heart began to pound. He
excused himself, walked to the bathroom, and stood with his hands on the sink, trying to breathe. He
was terrified that he was going to lose his job. He was terrified that he had become someone who
could not tell the difference between a threat and a routine Tuesday. And he was most afraid that
the institution that had betrayed him had also broken something in him that could not be fixed.
