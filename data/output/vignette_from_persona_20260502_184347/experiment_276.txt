Elena is a 27-year-old HVAC technician who came to therapy after years of feeling that her life had
been hollowed out from the inside. Five years ago, while living in a small border town, she was
kidnapped and held for three days by men who tortured her for information she did not have. They
kept her in a windowless room, and one of her captors was a woman who would sit with her during the
worst moments, sometimes speaking softly, sometimes watching silently. Elena still remembers the
shame that flooded her when she looked at that woman’s face—the feeling that she was being seen at
her most broken, and that this was somehow her fault. That memory, when it returns now, feels like
it belongs to someone else, as though she is watching a stranger in a film, and yet the emotion is
so overwhelming that she has to stop whatever she is doing and sit down until it passes.

In the months after her escape, Elena moved to a new city and started her job as an HVAC technician,
working in commercial buildings and private homes. She thought she had left the worst behind. But
she soon noticed that she could not be alone with a female client or coworker without her heart
pounding. The presence of a woman in a closed space—a kitchen, a basement, a mechanical room—would
trigger a visceral sense of entrapment, and she would find herself checking the exits, the locks,
the windows, sometimes twice, sometimes three times. She began to wake during the night to check
that her apartment door was locked, and she would lie awake, listening, feeling jumpy at the
smallest creak. She had come to believe that you can never know who will harm you, and that she
could not trust herself to do the right thing in a dangerous moment. This certainty made her
hypervigilant, and the hypervigilance, in turn, confirmed for her that she was permanently changed
for the worse, that the damage was done and could not be undone.

Elena’s work began to suffer. She started avoiding jobs that required her to work alone with a
woman, making excuses or asking male colleagues to cover. When she did take such a job, the sight of
a visible injury on a client—a bruise, a bandage, a scar—would send her back into that room with her
captors, and she would feel the shame and overwhelm all over again. The more she avoided, the more
she believed that she could not handle these situations, and the more she checked doors and locks,
the more she felt that danger was everywhere. One afternoon, she was repairing a furnace in a small
basement laundry room when the homeowner, a woman with a healing cut on her forearm, came down to
check on her. Elena felt the walls close in, and she left the job unfinished, telling her supervisor
she had felt sick. She was certain that the woman had seen her panic, and that night she checked her
own door locks four times before she could sleep.

What brought Elena to therapy was a quiet, accumulating despair. She had begun to wonder if she
would ever feel normal again, and she was afraid that if she kept avoiding, she would lose her job
entirely. She was most afraid of the memory itself—that it would never stop feeling like it belonged
to someone else, and that she would never stop feeling ashamed of the person in that room. But she
was also, faintly, hoping that maybe she could learn to be alone with herself again, without the
constant checking, without the fear.
