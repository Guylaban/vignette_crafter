It was a Tuesday afternoon in late October when a convoy of black SUVs rolled past the ranger
station where Dev worked, and the sight of them—the clean, dark vehicles, the suggestion of uniforms
inside—sent him straight back to the room. He had been held for eleven days in that concrete cell,
blindfolded for most of it, fed only when his captors remembered. The men who kept him had worn
uniforms too, though never insignia he could identify. They had asked questions he could not answer,
and when he could not answer, they had broken his ribs and his left hand. Now, years later, a line
of cars on a park road could make him feel the ache in those bones again, could make him see the
bare bulb swinging overhead, the chipped paint on the wall, the way the light fell across the floor
in exactly three slivers. He had come to believe that the sight of a uniform, or even talk of the
military on the radio, meant that what happened to him was still happening—that he was still the
kind of person who got trapped and broken. Somebody else would not have gotten into this situation,
he was certain. Somebody else would have seen it coming.

The memory did not arrive as a story. It arrived as a series of frozen images: the door closing, the
first blow, the taste of blood, the silence afterward. Sometimes he could recall these moments and
feel nothing at all, as if he were watching a stranger on a screen. But more often, when the memory
came, so did the pain. His ribs would ache. His left hand would cramp. And he would feel again the
humiliation of begging, of being stripped, of being seen at his most helpless. That feeling of being
seen—of being exposed—had not left him. He had become hyperaware of his own visibility. On the
trail, when hikers glanced his way, he felt certain they could tell. They could see the weakness in
him, the thing that had happened, the stain of it. He started to avoid the busier sections of the
park, volunteering instead for the backcountry patrols where he might go hours without encountering
anyone. When he did run into visitors, he found himself agreeing with whatever they said—yes, the
trail is that way, yes, the weather will hold—even when he knew they were wrong. It was easier to
please them, to disappear into their approval, than to risk being seen as difficult or defiant. He
had learned that defiance got you hurt.

At night, alone in his cabin, the images came back unbidden. A glimpse of a uniform on a television
show. The crackle of a radio. The sudden movement of a deer bursting from the brush. That last one
was the worst. Any unexpected motion near him—a bird taking flight, a branch falling—and he would
flinch, his heart hammering, his body bracing for a blow that was not coming. Even when he told
himself he was safe, even when the forest was quiet and the stars were out, he could not make
himself believe it. The world felt dangerous in a way that reason could not touch. He found himself
controlling what little he could: his food. He began to restrict his eating, carefully measuring
portions, skipping meals, because the discipline of hunger made him feel less helpless. It was
something he could decide. It was something that belonged to him.

The revenge fantasies came less often now, but they still surfaced. He would imagine finding the men
who had held him, making them feel what he had felt. But the fantasies did not comfort him. They
only made him feel more isolated, more set apart from the ordinary life he saw other people living.
His partner, Priya, did not understand why he flinched when she touched him from behind, or why he
had stopped going to the grocery store when it was busy. She asked him what was wrong, and he told
her nothing. He had learned that telling the truth did not help. It only made people look at you
differently. And being looked at—being seen—was the thing he feared most.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a morning in the park when a school group arrived, and
one of the children wore a small camouflage hat. Dev saw the hat and froze. The images came: the
blindfold, the pain, the humiliation. He excused himself to the restroom and did not come out for
twenty minutes. That evening, he called Priya and told her he needed help. He was afraid that if he
did not, he would lose the job he loved, the relationship he was barely holding together, and the
last thread of himself. He was hoping, though he could not yet say it aloud, that someone might
finally show him how to stop seeing the room every time he opened his eyes.
