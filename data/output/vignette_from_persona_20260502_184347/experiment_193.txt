Margaret is a sixty-eight-year-old retired woman who lives with her husband in the suburban home
they bought forty years ago. Eighteen months ago, her thirty-nine-year-old son, David, died suddenly
of a massive coronary while shoveling snow from their driveway on a Sunday morning. Margaret was in
the kitchen making coffee when she heard the thud against the garage door. She found him on his
back, his face already gray, the shovel still in his hand. She did not know CPR, and by the time the
paramedics arrived, twenty-two minutes had passed. She has replayed those minutes countless times,
each time certain that if she had checked on him sooner, if she had known what to do, if she had
made him stop when she saw him out the window, she could have saved him. She had come to believe
that her failure to protect him was not a matter of bad luck but a fundamental flaw in her—something
essential she had lacked when it mattered most.

In the months that followed, Margaret found herself unable to sit still. The moment she stopped
moving, the thoughts would press in: the image of his face, the paramedic’s gentle hands on her arm,
the certainty that she should have been faster. She began filling every hour with tasks—deep-
cleaning closets that were already clean, reorganizing the pantry by expiration date, taking on
small handyman projects around the house that she had never attempted before. She believed that if
she kept moving, she could outrun the guilt, and she was certain that if she slowed down, the weight
of what she had failed to do would crush her. But busyness did not bring relief. Instead, her body
remained coiled, her shoulders tight, her jaw clenched even when she was alone in the house. She
could not relax, not even in her own living room, because some part of her was always waiting for
the next terrible thing to happen, and she was sure she would fail to prevent that too.

As weeks turned into months, Margaret stopped answering the phone when old friends called. She
declined invitations to coffee, to book club, to the neighborhood potluck. Seeing people she had
socialized with before David’s death became unbearable—not because she disliked them, but because
she was certain they saw her differently now, as the woman who had let her son die. She felt
isolated and set apart from others, and she had come to believe that she no longer belonged
anywhere, not even among people who had known her for decades. The more she withdrew, the more her
body stayed on alert, her heart racing at the sound of a phone ringing or a doorbell she was not
expecting, her mind scanning every room for signs of danger even though she knew, logically, that
she was safe. She had become convinced that her failure was visible, that others could see it in her
face, and that the only way to protect herself from their judgment was to stay away. But staying
away only deepened her sense that she was fundamentally different from everyone else, and that
difference felt like a threat she could not escape.

Margaret’s husband tried to coax her out of the house, but she resisted. She had begun to feel that
even he must see her differently now, that he must blame her in some quiet way he would never say
aloud. She avoided sitting with him in the evenings, afraid that if they talked too long, the
subject of David would come up, and she would have to hear the accusation she was sure was there.
Her body remained tense through the night, her sleep fractured, her muscles rigid even as she lay
beside him. The isolation she felt had become a kind of second skin, and the more she withdrew, the
more her body stayed locked in a state of readiness, as though danger were always just around the
corner.

What finally brought Margaret to seek help was a moment of quiet terror. She was alone in the house
on a Sunday morning, the same day of the week David had died, and she heard a thud from the garage.
It was only a box of old books her husband had dropped, but for ten seconds she could not breathe,
her heart pounding so hard she thought she might collapse. She realized then that she could not go
on this way, that her body and her mind had become a trap she could not escape. She is most afraid
that nothing will change, that she will spend the rest of her life feeling this way, and that the
guilt she carries will never lift. But she is also afraid of what it might mean to let it go.
