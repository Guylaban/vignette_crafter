Mara was sixty-seven years old, a widow for nearly a decade, and a database administrator for a mid-
sized logistics firm. She had lived alone in the same two-bedroom apartment for twenty years, a
place she kept meticulously ordered. The trauma that had reshaped her life happened forty years ago,
during her first marriage—a relationship she had never spoken about until recently. Her ex-husband,
a man she met in her twenties, had been physically abusive in ways she had spent decades trying to
forget. The worst incident came one night when he pinned her against the kitchen counter, his hand
around her throat, after she had asked him a question about money. She remembered the pressure, the
sound of her own breathing, the way the edge of the counter dug into her lower back. She had not
told anyone at the time, and after she left him, she told herself she had handled it. She had moved
on, remarried a kind man, raised a family. But after her second husband died, the old memories began
to surface with a vividness she could not control.

For the past two years, Mara had found herself unable to make eye contact with anyone—colleagues,
neighbors, even the cashier at the grocery store. It was not a conscious choice, exactly. When
someone looked directly at her, her gaze dropped to the floor, to her hands, to the pattern on the
linoleum. She had come to believe that there was something fundamentally wrong with her as a person,
that her reactions since the event—the flinching, the sleeplessness, the way she avoided being
touched—proved she was a lousy coper. She felt she did not know herself anymore. The woman who used
to laugh easily at office parties, who had once managed a team of seven, now spent her lunch breaks
in the stairwell, eating a sandwich alone. She was certain that if anyone saw her startle at a
sudden touch, or noticed her pulling away from a handshake, they would see the defect in her. So she
avoided those moments entirely.

In the evenings, she deliberately delayed sleep, staying up until two or three in the morning,
scrolling through old photographs or reorganizing her kitchen cabinets. She told herself she was not
tired, but the truth was that lying in the dark, with no distractions, brought the memory of that
kitchen counter and the pressure on her throat. The later she stayed awake, the more exhausted she
became, and the more convinced she was that she could not manage her own life. She began to see her
avoidance of eye contact and touch as further evidence that she was broken, that a normal person
would have gotten over this by now. This cycle tightened over the months. The more she avoided, the
more she felt she was failing. The more she felt she was failing, the more she withdrew.

At work, she had started arriving early and leaving late to avoid the bustle of the office during
peak hours. When a younger colleague once touched her shoulder to get her attention, Mara flinched
so hard she knocked a cup of pens off her desk. She spent the rest of the day apologizing, her face
hot, her hands trembling. That night, she stayed up until four, replaying the moment. She told
herself that a competent person, a strong person, would not have reacted that way. The next morning,
she began sitting with her desk angled away from the hallway so she could not see people
approaching.

What finally brought her to seek help was a small thing: her daughter had invited her to visit for
Thanksgiving, and Mara had said no. She loved her daughter. But the thought of the airport, of the
crowds, of the inevitable hugs from grandchildren she adored, filled her with a dread she could not
explain. She had told her daughter she was busy with work. After she hung up, she sat in her silent
apartment and realized she had not hugged anyone in over a year. She was terrified that she would
never feel like herself again, but she was equally terrified of what it might mean to try.
