it away, the world turned to noise. The aircraft took fire from the tree line. The man beside him, a
loader named Kele, took a round through the throat and collapsed against Thomas’s shoulder, his
blood warm and slick on Thomas’s cheek and hands. The helicopter yawed hard, the pilot screaming

the wet weight of Kele’s body, and the exact pitch of the rotors as they strained through the
evasion.

and his wife, Lani, has made it clear she will not watch him retire into the armchair she already
calls his “bunker.” But flying has become a kind of endurance. Every time he steps into the cockpit,
the sound of the engines—a low turbine whine that vibrates through the floor—can, without warning,
collapse the distance between present and past. He will be running through the pre-flight checklist
and suddenly he is back in the Huey, the rotor blades chopping the air, and the terror hits him in
the chest like a physical blow. The memory feels alien, as though it belongs to someone else, a
younger man who did things Thomas cannot reconcile with the man he is now, but the terror is his
own, and it is immediate. He has learned to grip the yoke harder, to breathe through it, but he
cannot control when it comes. A texture—the rough fabric of his uniform cuff against his wrist—can
do it. The time of day can do it. The hour before dawn, when the sky is that particular bruised
purple, is the worst. He has come to believe that the world is a dangerous place, and that this is
not paranoia but simple truth, because the world proved it to him once and has been proving it ever
since.
