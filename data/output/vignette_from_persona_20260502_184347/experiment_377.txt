breath was the constant, nauseating backdrop; the pain in his neck from the zip-ties and the rough
handling was a dull, permanent anchor to each moment of terror. When he was finally released in a
prisoner exchange, he told himself he was fine. He went home to his wife, returned to his desk, and
tried to file the story. But the story was filing him.

The memory does not wait for permission. It arrives as a complete, sensory film—the scrape of the
chair, the pressure of the blindfold, the smell of alcohol, the terror that was so absolute it felt
like dying. And because remembering brings back that same raw terror, his body reacts as if the
event is happening again, right now. This is why a sudden sound—a car backfiring, a dropped pan in
the kitchen—will send him diving for cover, his heart hammering, his neck seizing with the old
familiar pain. He has come to believe that he is permanently broken, that any unexpected noise is a
potential threat, that he must be superalert or he will be caught off guard. This hypervigilance has
become his default state, a constant scanning for danger that leaves him exhausted and irritable.

The most insidious loop, however, involves his wife. A simple, loving touch on the shoulder from
behind can trigger the same physical startle response as a guard’s hand grabbing his collar. The
neck pain, which he now associates directly with the captivity, flares up whenever he is tense, and
he finds himself bracing for a blow during a hug. He has begun to pull away from her, not because he
does not love her, but because the sensation of being touched has become so tightly wired to the
memory of being handled roughly that he cannot separate the two. His hypervigilance means he is
constantly monitoring for the next sound, the next touch, the next smell—and that constant state of
alert primes his memory to replay the worst moments, creating a cycle that tightens with every
passing week.
