Malakai Tui was fifty-one years old, a welder from a small coastal town in the Pacific Northwest,
when he was referred to therapy by his primary care physician. He came reluctantly, his broad
shoulders hunched in the waiting room chair, his hands calloused and restless. He had been married
to his wife, Leilani, for twenty-eight years, and they had two grown children who lived across the
state. For the past year, since the accident, he had been slowly disappearing from his own life.

The crash had happened on a rain-slicked highway in late November. Malakai had been driving home
from a double shift at the fabrication yard, exhausted, when a pickup truck ran a red light and
T-boned his sedan at full speed. He remembered the moment of impact with a clarity that felt like a
curse: the shriek of metal, the violent rotation of the car, the way his body was thrown sideways
against the seatbelt. He had been pinned for forty minutes before the fire crew cut him free, the
cab of his truck crumpled around him like a fist. He had not been seriously injured—a cracked rib,
deep bruising—but something in him had broken differently.

When memories of the crash came back to him, they arrived not as thoughts but as full-body
experiences. He would be standing at his workbench, torch in hand, and suddenly his chest would
tighten and his right shoulder would seize up, his body instinctively bracing for the impact that
had already happened a year ago. The physical pain in his ribs would flare without warning, sharp
and real, as though the steering wheel were still pressed against him. And with that pain came the
smell of gasoline and his own blood, the screech of bending metal, the blinding white of the
airbag—everything, all at once, so completely real that for a few seconds he would forget where he
actually was. These episodes were triggered by things he could not predict or control: the sound of
a car horn in traffic, the sight of a dented bumper in the lot at work, the faint smell of alcohol
on a customer's breath at the hardware store. Each trigger yanked him back into the moment of the
crash, and each time the memory returned, it seemed to sharpen new triggers he had never noticed
before. After a particularly vivid flashback in the grocery store parking lot, he began to hear the
screech of tires in every car that passed, and the smell of alcohol became unbearable—he could no
longer walk past the liquor aisle without his hands shaking.

The most powerful and destructive loop was this: the more the memory overwhelmed him, the more the
world filled with reminders. The crash had happened because another driver was drunk, and now every
time Malakai caught a whiff of beer or whiskey—from a neighbor's breath, from a can in a recycling
bin—his body would lock up, his ribs would ache, and the sound of crumpling metal would echo in his
ears. He began to believe that the world was full of hidden threats, that any car on the road could
be the one, that any smell could be a warning. He started driving only on back roads, then only
during daylight, then only if Leilani was in the passenger seat. He stopped going to the hardware
store. He stopped meeting his friends for weekend barbecues. At work, he asked to be moved to the
back of the shop, away from the roll-up doors where he could see the parking lot. His supervisor
noticed the change but said nothing. Malakai knew he was becoming unreliable, but the
alternative—getting behind the wheel on a busy road—felt like volunteering for death.

Leilani had begun to cry, quietly, when he told her he could not drive them to their daughter's
graduation. He had watched her wipe her eyes with the back of her hand and say nothing, and in that
silence he felt the shape of what he was losing. What frightened him most was not the memories
themselves but the certainty that they would never stop coming—that every sound, every smell, every
glint of headlights would always pull him back into that crumpled cab. He came to therapy because he
could not bear to see his wife cry again, and because he was terrified that if he did not find a way
out of this loop, he would lose everything he had left.
