Leila is a 77-year-old tutor who had lived in the same quiet neighborhood for over forty years. When
the war came to her city, she did what she had always done: she stayed, she taught, she kept the

walking home with groceries. The blast threw her against a wall. She remembers the ringing in her
ears, the dust that made the sky go brown, and the sight of a young man—one of her former
students—lying motionless near an overturned fruit cart. She had tried to reach him, but her legs
would not move. She remembers that immobility with a shame that has not faded.

In the weeks that followed, Leila found that remembering the event brought back the same terror she
had felt at the time, and worse—when she recalled it, she felt like it was happening right now.
Something in her surroundings could instantly bring back the full memory without warning: the smell
of overripe fruit, the sound of a car backfiring, the sight of a dust-covered window. She had come
to believe that if she thought about the event, she would not be able to handle it. That belief
drove her to avoid watching television programs or films about similar events, and she found herself
staying close to the exit in every room she entered—the classroom where she tutored, the waiting
room at the doctor’s office, even her own living room. She was certain that being trapped would undo
her completely.

But the avoidance did not bring relief. Instead, the more she stayed near exits and avoided
reminders, the more the memories returned without warning, as if her mind were punishing her for
trying to escape. She began to dwell on how she used to be before the event—the woman who had not
flinched at a loud noise, who had not scanned every room for an escape route. That woman, she
believed, was a strong person. The person she had become felt dead inside, hollowed out. She told
herself she was a weak person now, and that thought made her avoid the memories even more fiercely,
certain that confronting them would prove her weakness beyond any doubt. This cycle tightened over
months: the more she avoided, the more she felt numb and worthless; the more she felt numb and
worthless, the more the memories came back unbidden, each time with the same terror.

One consequence was concrete and measurable: she began to cancel tutoring sessions if they were held
in rooms without a clear exit, and she stopped taking new students whose homes she had not inspected
first. Her income shrank. Her social world narrowed. She told herself this was simply being careful,
but the explanation did not quiet the fear. What finally brought her to seek help was a morning in
late autumn when she walked into her usual tutoring space—a library study room she had used for
years—and found that the furniture had been rearranged, blocking the door. She turned around and
walked out without a word to the student waiting for her. She sat in her car for twenty minutes,
unable to stop trembling. She was most afraid that she would never feel like herself again, but what
she hoped for, quietly, was that someone might tell her she was not as weak as she believed.
