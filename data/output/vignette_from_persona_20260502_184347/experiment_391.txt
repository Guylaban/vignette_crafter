Kiet Nguyen was sixty years old, a fisherman who had worked the Gulf waters out of Biloxi,
Mississippi, since he was a teenager. He was married to Linh, his wife of thirty-eight years, and
they had raised three children together. The traumatic event had happened eighteen months ago, on a
calm October morning. Kiet and his younger brother, Bao, had been hauling in a net when a sudden
squall capsized their skiff. Kiet managed to grab a floating cooler and was rescued within hours,
but Bao’s body was never found. For weeks afterward, Kiet had searched the shoreline himself,
walking the mudflats at dawn, certain he would find his brother alive. He never did.

In the months that followed, Kiet began to change in ways that puzzled and frightened him. The first
thing he noticed was the physical reactivity: whenever he stepped onto the dock and smelled diesel
fuel and brine, his chest would tighten, his hands would shake, and a wave of nausea would roll
through him. He had come to believe that these bodily sensations meant he was losing control, that
something terrible was about to happen again. This belief—that his own body was a warning system he
could not ignore—had grown directly out of a deeper conviction he could not shake: that people could
not be trusted. He had trusted Bao to watch the weather that morning, and Bao was gone. Kiet now
believed that relying on anyone was a mistake, that the only person he could count on was himself,
and even that felt uncertain. This mistrust colored everything. When his son offered to help repair
the engine on the boat, Kiet refused, insisting he would do it alone, even though his hands ached
from arthritis. He had become certain that if he let anyone close, he would be responsible for
losing them too.

The physical reactions, in turn, fed back into his bleak view of the world. Each time his heart
pounded and his breath grew shallow on the water, Kiet would think, *See? This is proof. You cannot
trust yourself. You cannot trust anyone. You are still in danger.* The loop tightened: the more his
body reacted, the more he felt that the world was unsafe, and the more unsafe he felt, the more his
body reacted. He began to avoid the boat entirely, telling his wife he was taking a break, but the
real reason was that he could not bear the feeling of his own terror. He stayed home, sitting in the
dark living room, watching television with the sound off. The emotional numbness crept in slowly. He
noticed that when his granddaughter hugged him, he felt nothing—just a hollow pressure against his
chest. He had begun to say to himself, *I feel dead inside,* and the phrase no longer felt like an
exaggeration; it felt like a fact.

The survival guilt was a quieter, more persistent presence. It surfaced in the middle of the night,
when sleep would not come. Kiet would lie awake, replaying the moment the skiff tipped, wondering
why he had grabbed the cooler and not his brother. He had come to believe that he should have died
instead, that Bao had been the better man—kinder, more patient, with a young wife and a baby on the
way. These thoughts did not cause his physical reactivity directly, but they lived alongside it, a
separate, heavy weight he carried into every sleepless hour. The sleep problems themselves had
become a nightly ritual: he would fall asleep for an hour, then jolt awake with his heart racing,
certain he was back in the water. The anger came in flashes—at the weather, at the boat, at
himself—and then disappeared, leaving only the deadness behind.

What finally brought Kiet to seek help was a moment on the dock three weeks ago. His son had asked
him to come out for a short run, just to test the new engine. Kiet had walked to the edge of the
pier, looked at the water, and felt his legs give way. He sat down on the wooden planks, unable to
stand, unable to speak, while his son watched in confusion and then in fear. That night, Linh told
him quietly that she could not keep watching him disappear. Kiet agreed to see a counselor, though
what frightened him most was not the therapy itself—it was the possibility that after all this time,
he might still have to get back on that boat.
