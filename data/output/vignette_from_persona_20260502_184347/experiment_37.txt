Elena is a 53-year-old stock broker, married for twenty-eight years, who was mugged eighteen months
ago while walking from the train to her car after a late evening at the office. Two men had come out
of an alley, one pressing something hard against her ribs that she was certain was a gun, and they
had taken her bag, her phone, her wedding ring. She had frozen completely, said nothing, handed
everything over—a response that had probably saved her life, she knew, but one that had left her
with a shame so deep she could barely speak of it. What had stayed with her most was not the loss of
the ring, which she had eventually replaced, but the feeling of being utterly unprotected in a world
she had trusted to be orderly.

Elena had always believed that institutions worked—that the police, the security cameras in the
parking garage, the well-lit train platforms, the whole architecture of urban life, existed to keep
people like her safe. That belief had been her bedrock. Now, she felt it had been a lie. The people
or institutions that should have protected her, she had come to believe, had let her down. The world
is a dangerous place, she would think, scanning the faces on the train platform, and she meant it
not as an abstract philosophy but as a concrete, daily truth. She had begun to feel dead inside,
emotionally numb, as though the part of her that had trusted and hoped had been taken along with her
ring.

This new certainty about the world’s danger had made her hypervigilant. She could not help it. Every
sudden movement by another person put her immediately on guard. A man reaching into his pocket for a
phone, a teenager shifting his weight, a door swinging open unexpectedly—each one sent a jolt
through her. She jumped at ordinary sounds, the clatter of a dropped tray in the office cafeteria,
the hiss of the train doors, the scrape of a chair behind her. And underneath all of it was a
constant wash of fear and anger and shame, a roiling emotional state she could not name or control.

Because she believed the world was dangerous and because she was so constantly on guard, Elena had
developed a set of strategies that she knew, in her clearer moments, were not normal. She performed
mental rituals before leaving her desk at work—counting backwards from ten, repeating a phrase in
her head—to prevent bad things from happening again. She became overly compliant when she felt
threatened, agreeing with colleagues she disagreed with, apologizing to strangers who bumped into
her, nodding along with her husband’s plans even when she wanted to say no. And she constantly
scanned her environment for signs of threat, her eyes moving from face to face, from shadow to
shadow, never resting. These strategies, in turn, deepened her sense that the world was dangerous
and that she was powerless within it. The more she scanned, the more threats she saw; the more she
complied, the more she felt erased; the more she performed her rituals, the more convinced she
became that disaster was always just barely held at bay.

At work, this had become a concrete problem. Elena was a senior broker, a woman known for her sharp
negotiations and her refusal to be pushed around. Now, in client meetings, she found herself nodding
along to terms she would have challenged before, her mind racing with the need to escape the room.
She had taken to sitting with her back to the wall, her eyes fixed on the door, unable to focus on
the numbers in front of her. Her performance had slipped, and her junior colleagues had begun to
notice. She had stopped taking the train altogether, driving an extra hour each way to avoid the
parking garage where it had happened.

What finally brought Elena to seek help was a moment in her own kitchen. Her husband had come up
behind her to reach for a coffee mug, and she had screamed, spun around, and shoved him away with
both hands. He had stood there, shocked, the mug shattered on the floor between them, and she had
seen in his face that she was becoming someone he did not recognize. She was afraid of what she
might do next, and more afraid that nothing would ever feel safe again.
