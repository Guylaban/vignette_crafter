Meera is a 70-year-old photographer, a woman whose life’s work has been about framing moments,
capturing light, and telling stories through images. But the story she carries inside her is one she
has never been able to frame. When she was a child, growing up in a South Asian household where
discipline was delivered with a heavy hand, her primary caregiver—an uncle who lived with the
family—subjected her to years of physical abuse. It was not the kind of violence that left broken
bones, but it was relentless: slaps across the face that came without warning, a hand clamped over
her mouth to stifle her cries, and the cold, silent shame of being told she deserved it. She learned
early that the worst thing she could do was show what she felt. So she hid. She covered her face
with her hands, a reflex she still cannot control. She tried hard to control her emotions, pressing
them down like a lid on a boiling pot. And for decades, she told herself that none of it mattered,
that it was in the past.

Now, at 70, the past is no longer staying put. When Meera tries to deliberately remember what
happened, she finds the details slippery, hard to access, as if the memories are buried under layers
of fog. But they are not gone. When the memories do surface—triggered by something as ordinary as a
sudden movement near her face, or the sound of a door slamming—they bring back the same terror she
felt as a child. Her body reacts before her mind can catch up: her hands fly to her cheeks, her
breath catches, and she is flooded with an intense shame so sharp it feels physical. She has come to
believe that the people responsible for what happened—her uncle, now long dead, and the family
members who looked the other way—have faced no consequences. This belief has hardened into a
certainty that the world is fundamentally unjust, and that she, Meera, is the one who carries the
stain of it all. She no longer belongs anywhere. Not in her family, not in her community, not even
in her own skin.

This sense of not belonging has driven her to a kind of hypervigilance that she wears like a second
coat. She has to be on guard all the time, she tells herself, because if she lets her guard down,
something terrible will happen. That hypervigilance feeds directly into her maladaptive strategies.
She covers her face instinctively when anyone reaches toward her, even a friend offering a
comforting hand. She tries so hard to control her emotions that she has become emotionally numb in
many situations, but the effort itself is exhausting. And she spends long hours thinking about why
the event happened to her, turning it over and over, as if she might find an answer that will make
it stop hurting. These strategies, in turn, affect her memory. The more she tries to control her
emotions and avoid the shame, the harder it becomes to deliberately recall the details of the abuse.
Her mind has learned that remembering is dangerous, so it keeps the memories fragmented and
difficult to reach. But the terror and shame remain, leaking through in unbidden flashes.

Meera works as a photographer, and her daily environment has become a minefield. She does portrait
work, mostly for local families and small events. But she has started to refuse assignments that
require her to be in tight spaces or to direct people physically. She avoids working with large
groups of men, especially older men, because she cannot stop scanning their hands, their faces,
waiting for a sign. She has lost several clients because she cancelled at the last minute, unable to
explain why she could not go into a certain house or studio. Her income has dropped, and she has
begun to isolate herself from her few remaining friends. They do not understand why she flinches
when they touch her arm, or why she sometimes ends a phone call abruptly. She has come to believe
that they are better off without her.

What finally brought Meera to seek help was a moment in her own bathroom. She caught a glimpse of
herself in the mirror, her hands covering her face, and she did not recognize the woman staring
back. She realized she has spent her entire life hiding from a man who has been dead for thirty
years. She is terrified that she will never feel safe, never feel that she belongs anywhere. But she
is also, for the first time, willing to look at the photograph of her own life—not to frame it
perfectly, but to see what is really there.
