Raj is a 34-year-old delivery driver living in a Chicago suburb with his wife and two young
children. He came to therapy after his wife urged him, worried by how withdrawn he had become in the
months since a single terrible afternoon in late November. Raj had been making his usual route when
he rounded a blind corner in a residential area and struck a seven-year-old boy who had darted into
the street after a ball. The boy survived, but with a traumatic brain injury that required multiple
surgeries and left him with lasting deficits. Raj stopped the van immediately, called 911, and
stayed with the child until paramedics arrived, holding pressure on the wound as he had been taught
in a first-aid course years before. But none of that mattered to him now. What he remembered, what
he could not stop remembering, was the moment of impact itself—the thud, the impossible stillness of
the small body on the asphalt—and then the thought that followed: *I should have been paying more
attention. I should have seen him coming. I should have done something different.*

In the weeks after the accident, Raj found himself unable to stop replaying that moment. The memory
felt vivid and immediate, as though he were still in the driver's seat, still watching the boy step
off the curb. He would be sorting packages in the back of his van and suddenly feel the steering
wheel under his hands, hear the sound of the impact. At night, he woke in a cold sweat, convinced
for a split second that he had just hit someone again. His wife would find him sitting on the edge
of the bed, staring at the wall, and he would say only, "I can't stop thinking about it."

Raj had always been a careful driver, proud of his clean record and his ability to navigate the
narrow streets of the older neighborhoods on his route. Now he found himself gripping the wheel so
tightly that his hands ached by midday. He started taking longer routes to avoid the street where
the accident had happened, adding twenty minutes to his shift. He began scanning the sidewalks with
a hypervigilance that exhausted him, watching for children, for movement at the edges of his vision.
He had come to believe that he could not trust himself behind the wheel, that his own judgment had
failed at the critical moment and would fail again. Every school bus he passed, every playground he
drove by, every child walking home from school made his chest tighten and his breath go shallow. He
would pull over and wait until his heart stopped pounding, telling himself it was just a memory, it
was over, but the feeling never quite went away.

At home, Raj had become quiet and irritable. He stopped playing with his own children, afraid that
if he let himself feel too much joy, something terrible would happen again. He could not bear to
watch them run in the yard, could not hear the sound of a ball bouncing on the driveway without
flinching. His wife asked him why he seemed so distant, and he told her he was just tired from work,
but the truth was that he could not forgive himself. He believed, with a certainty that felt like a
physical weight in his chest, that he had failed to protect a child who had trusted the world to be
safe, and that this failure could not be undone. He would never forgive himself for not doing more
to stop what happened, even though rationally he knew there had been no time, no warning, no way to
see the boy before he appeared. The event happened because of the way he acted, he told himself.

Six months after the accident, Raj was still avoiding that street, still scanning for children,

had asked if something was wrong. Raj said nothing. But the pressure was building—at work, at home,
in his own mind—and when his wife found him one evening sitting in the dark in the parked van,

the thought that he might never feel normal again, that this guilt would be with him forever, and
that maybe—maybe—he deserved it.
