Jordan is a 75-year-old non-binary financial analyst from Oceania, widowed for the past six years,
who sought therapy after a lifetime of silent endurance finally gave way. The trauma that reshaped
their world occurred decades ago, during a work trip to a conference in a city they had never
visited before. A colleague, someone they had trusted, cornered them in a hotel room after a late
dinner. The assault was swift and violent, and Jordan remembers fragments—the sharp, metallic taste
of blood from a bitten lip, the sound of the man’s belt buckle clinking, the harsh whisper of his
breath. What they cannot do, even now, is assemble these pieces into a coherent story. The memory is
a jumble of sensory flashes—a copper taste that floods their mouth without warning, or a faint sound
of jingling metal that makes their stomach drop—but no clear narrative thread. For years, Jordan
told themselves it was just a bad night, something to forget. But forgetting was never possible.

The triggers that now govern Jordan’s life are specific and relentless. The sight of a police car,
even from a distance, sends a cold spike of dread through their chest. They had called the police
that night, but the responding officers had been dismissive, asking if they had been drinking,
implying the assault was their fault. That memory of authority’s betrayal is now fused with the
sound of a patrol siren. More pervasive still is the experience of being criticised or corrected. In
their work as a financial analyst, Jordan reviews complex portfolios, and when a younger colleague
points out a miscalculation, Jordan feels not professional correction but a visceral shame—a sense
of being exposed and worthless. They have come to believe that criticism is a sign that people see
them as fundamentally flawed, and that these reactions are proof they are going crazy. Each time a
manager suggests an adjustment, Jordan’s mind goes blank, and the taste of blood returns.

This cycle tightened over the years. Being criticised triggers a memory fragment—the sound of that
belt buckle, the taste of copper—and the memory, in turn, makes Jordan hypersensitive to any hint of
judgment. They now avoid meetings where feedback might be given, working late to submit reports by
email instead. The more they avoid, the more they feel they cannot rely on themselves. Jordan’s desk
is cluttered with old spreadsheets they cannot bring themselves to finish, and they have stopped
taking on new clients, convinced they will fail. Nothing in their life feels meaningful anymore; the
work that once gave them purpose now feels like a minefield. The loss of their spouse five years ago
stripped away the only person who knew the full story, and Jordan has not spoken of the assault
since. They are certain that if anyone at the office knew, they would be pitied or blamed.

The police remain an active threat in Jordan’s mind. Driving past a station or seeing an officer on
the street triggers a full sensory return—the taste of blood, the sound of that jingling belt—and
Jordan’s immediate response is to flee. They have changed their commute three times to avoid a
precinct. This avoidance reinforces their belief that they are out of control, that their reactions
are proof of madness. The weight of these connections is crushing: criticism triggers memory, memory
triggers fear of going crazy, fear of going crazy makes every correction feel like an accusation,
and every accusation loops back to the assault. Jordan now finds themselves checking the exits in
every room, monitoring the tone of every conversation, waiting for the moment someone will judge
them.

What finally brought Jordan to therapy was a moment of collapse. Two weeks ago, a junior analyst
politely questioned a number in Jordan’s quarterly report. Jordan froze, tasted blood, and excused
themselves to the bathroom, where they sat on the floor for twenty minutes, shaking. They realised
they could no longer do their job, and that the life they had built—the quiet, solitary routine of
numbers and spreadsheets—was no longer a sanctuary. They are afraid that therapy will confirm they
are beyond help, that they are indeed going crazy. But they are more afraid of spending the rest of
their years trapped in that hotel room, tasting metal, listening for a sound that never stops.
