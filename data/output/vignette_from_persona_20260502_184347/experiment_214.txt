decades. He had raised three generations of children for the same family in a quiet suburb, and the
parents spoke of him with a reverence usually reserved for grandparents. But for the past two years,
since the night his wife of forty years had struck him across the face during an argument about
money, Minh had been living inside a body that no longer felt like his own.

The event itself had lasted only seconds. He had been standing in their darkened bedroom, trying to
explain why he had given their daughter a small loan without asking her first. She had been tired,
he knew, and under pressure from her own mother’s illness. But when she swung, he had not seen it
coming. The impact knocked him sideways onto the bed, and for a long moment he lay there, staring at
the ceiling, feeling the blood trickle from his lip and the cold sweat break across his back. What
frightened him most was not the pain but the fact that his body had frozen completely—he could not
move, could not speak, could not even raise a hand to protect himself. The memory of that
helplessness, of being pinned to the mattress by his own terror, came back to him now whenever he
closed his eyes, and when it did, his limbs would lock into that same position, his breath would
stop, and the terror would flood through him as fresh as the night it happened. He would lie rigid
on the sofa, unable to call out, unable to move, until the spell passed.

Because the memory felt so alien—as if it had happened to someone else, a stranger, not to him, not
to the man who had spent his life soothing crying children and tucking them safely into bed—Minh had
come to believe that the only way to keep it at bay was to never let himself feel anything at all.
He began suppressing every flicker of anger, every flash of frustration, every moment of irritation.
When a toddler threw a tantrum at the park, he did not allow himself to feel the heat rise in his
chest; he simply smiled, waited, and moved on. When his wife spoke sharply to him at the dinner
table, he swallowed the words before they could form. He was certain that if he ever let even a
single angry thought surface, the memory would break through and drown him.

This strategy worked for a time, but it came at a cost. The more he clamped down on his emotions,
the more his body seemed to rebel. He became hypervigilant in ways that made no sense to him. A
sudden movement from a child running past would send him flinching backward, his heart hammering,
his hands flying up in front of his face. He would startle at the sound of a door closing, at the
rustle of a coat, at the creak of a floorboard. And when he tried to relax—sitting in the garden,
watching the birds, a cup of tea in his hands—he found he could not. His shoulders stayed tight, his
jaw clenched, his eyes scanning the periphery for something he could not name. He had come to
believe that danger was always nearby, even when he knew, logically, that he was safe.

The hypervigilance, in turn, fed the memory. Each time he startled, each time his body reacted as if
under threat, the image of that dark bedroom would flash before his eyes—the ceiling, the blood, the
paralysis—and the cycle would begin again. He began avoiding anything that could possibly be
stressful. He stopped taking the bus after dark. He declined invitations to family gatherings where
there might be tension. He told his employers he could no longer work late shifts, and when they
asked why, he said only that he was getting older and needed his rest. They accepted it, of course,
but Minh knew he was shrinking his world one small refusal at a time.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment of quiet crisis. He had been sitting in the park
with the youngest child, a two-year-old girl, when a jogger ran past him unexpectedly from behind.
Minh had screamed—a high, animal sound that frightened the child into tears. The mother had looked
at him with concern, and Minh had felt a shame so deep it hollowed him out. He could not keep living
like this. He did not know what he hoped for, only that he was terrified of what might happen if he
did not find a way out.
