Thanh was sixty years old, a hotel manager in a mid-sized city, when he finally sat in a clinician’s
office. For thirty-two years he had worked in the same industry, rising from front-desk clerk to
overseeing a full staff, and for thirty-two years he had prided himself on his composure. That
composure had shattered on a Tuesday afternoon three years ago, during what should have been a
routine inspection of a newly delivered cleaning solvent. A valve on the storage drum had been
improperly sealed, and when Thanh leaned in to check the label, a plume of caustic vapor shot
directly into his face. The taste—acrid, metallic, chemical—filled his mouth and throat instantly.
He remembered staggering backward, gasping, certain he was dying, his lungs seizing as the burn
spread down into his chest. He was rushed to the emergency room, treated for chemical inhalation,
and released after six hours. Medically, he was fine. But something in him had not come home from
that hospital.

In the weeks that followed, the memory of that taste began to intrude without warning. Thanh would
be standing at the front desk, reviewing a reservation, and suddenly the acrid flavor would flood
his mouth, and with it came the full sensation of the vapor—the heat, the panic, the certainty that
he was about to stop breathing. He would feel his heart race, his palms sweat, and for a few
terrible seconds he was back in that storage room. The memory forced its way in, unwanted, and he
could not push it away. He began to notice that his body felt constantly tense, braced for
something, his muscles tight even when he was sitting still. He started scanning the hallways of his
hotel for anything unusual—a strange smell, a loose pipe, a canister left in the open. He checked
his own body, too, pressing on his chest, feeling his pulse, looking for any sign that something was
wrong. The checking itself seemed to make the memories come back more sharply, as if the vigilance
invited the taste to return.

Thanh had come to believe something fundamental about himself: that he could not stop bad things
from happening. He had always been a careful man, a planner, someone who anticipated problems before
they arose. But the accident had happened anyway, and he had been powerless. That helplessness
settled into his bones. He began to feel that he no longer belonged anywhere—not at work, where the
cleaning supply closet now felt like a trap, and not at home, where his partner could not understand
why he flinched at the smell of bleach. His own body, which he had always trusted, now felt like a
source of betrayal. He could not trust it to stay calm, could not trust it not to react as if danger
were present when nothing was actually wrong. When a delivery was late, he assumed something
catastrophic had happened. When a guest complained about a strange odor in the hallway, his chest
tightened and he had to excuse himself.

The helplessness drove him to monitor his surroundings obsessively. The more he scanned, the more he
found reasons to be alert—a faint chemical smell from a distant floor, a bottle left unlabeled in a
storage room. Each discovery confirmed that danger was everywhere, that he could not let his guard
down. And each wave of hypervigilance seemed to trigger the memory again, the taste rising in his
throat, the reliving of that moment when his lungs seized. The reliving, in turn, reinforced his
belief that his body was unreliable, that he was always one wrong breath away from disaster. The
loop tightened. He began avoiding conversations where the event might come up, steering colleagues
away from any mention of chemicals or accidents. When a staff member asked about the new solvent
protocol, Thanh changed the subject and walked away quickly, his heart pounding.

His partner noticed that he had stopped cooking at home, because the smell of onions and oil
sometimes reminded him of something acrid, and he could not separate the two. He stopped going to
the hardware store, because the cleaning aisle made him dizzy. His world had narrowed. At work, he
still managed, but he had started delegating the supply inventory to an assistant, claiming he was
too busy. He was not too busy. He was afraid. What frightened him most was the feeling that the
memory was not a memory—it was happening right now, every time. He could taste it. He could feel it.
And he was certain that if he relaxed, if he stopped checking, if he let his guard down for even a
moment, the next exposure would kill him.

What finally brought Thanh to seek help was a panic attack in the hotel lobby last month. A guest
had dropped a glass bottle of cologne near the front desk, and the sharp, alcohol-based scent hit
Thanh from across the room. He had frozen, then bolted to the bathroom, where he stood over the
sink, gasping, tasting the vapor from three years ago. His assistant manager found him there, pale
and shaking. Thanh had been humiliated. He had always been the calm one, the one others relied on.
Now he was afraid he would lose his job, his relationship, his sense of who he was. He came to
therapy not entirely sure it would help, but certain that he could not go on like this. He hoped,
more than anything, to stop tasting that chemical burn every time he tried to breathe.
