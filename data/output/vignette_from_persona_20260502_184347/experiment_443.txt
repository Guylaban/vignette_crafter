Arthur is a 74-year-old tutor of European descent who lives with his long-term partner in a quiet
suburban neighborhood. He sought therapy nearly three years after a catastrophic flood tore through
his town, forcing him and his partner to flee their home in the middle of the night. The water rose
so quickly that they had only minutes to grab a few belongings, and Arthur remembers the sound of
the current ripping through the walls and the sharp, muddy smell of silt and gasoline that filled
the air as they waded chest-deep through their own living room. That smell, he says, still comes to
him without warning—sometimes while he’s driving, sometimes in the middle of a tutoring session—and
when it does, the memory of that night forces its way into his mind, vivid and unwanted. In those
moments, Arthur feels his chest tighten and his eyes dart to the exits, scanning the room for any
sign of rising water or collapsing structures, even though he knows logically he is safe.

What frightens Arthur most is not the memory itself but what it does to him. When the olfactory
recall hits, he often finds himself slipping into a strange, numb state where he imagines the flood
happened to someone else—a stranger he watched on the news, not himself. He had come to believe that
this detachment was a way to cope, but over time he noticed it made the memories more likely to
intrude later, as if his mind was punishing him for trying to escape. To prevent this cycle, Arthur
began to ruminate extensively about how the flood could have been prevented: if the town had built
higher levees, if they had left earlier, if he had insisted on moving to higher ground years ago.
This rumination became a nightly ritual, but it never brought relief; instead, it kept the event
fresh and threatening, making him feel constantly unsafe unless his back was against a wall where he
could see the entire room.

Arthur’s daily life as a tutor has become a minefield of triggers. He now avoids anything that could
be stressful, turning down students who seem demanding or rescheduling sessions if the weather
forecast mentions rain. When a student arrives late or a lesson runs over, Arthur becomes convinced
that something bad is happening—a delay, to him, signals imminent disaster. His partner has noticed
that he no longer enjoys their evening walks, because he spends the entire time scanning the
neighborhood for signs of danger: a blocked drain, a low-lying patch of ground, the sound of a
distant siren. She has tried to reassure him, but Arthur is certain that she does not understand the
stakes, and he has quietly withdrawn from their shared activities, preferring to stay home where he
can control his environment.

The causal loop that tightens around Arthur is most visible in his tutoring work. After a session
where a memory intrudes, he will ruminate for hours about the flood’s preventability, which only
amplifies his sense of threat. Then, feeling unsafe, he will cancel the next day’s appointments or
dissociate during a lesson, pretending the conversation is happening to someone else. This avoidance
and detachment, in turn, make the memories more likely to return, often with the accompanying smell
of mud and fuel. His partner recently told him that he seems like a ghost in his own life, and it
was this comment—and the quiet fear in her voice—that finally brought Arthur to seek help. He is
most afraid that he will never feel safe again, but he is also hoping, quietly, that therapy might
help him stop the smell from coming back.
