Amina is a forty-seven-year-old plumber who grew up in a household where her uncle, who lived with
the family for several years when she was a child, would hit her for what he called her “sass.” She
remembers the sound of his voice, low and suddenly quiet before he struck her, and the way he would
say her name—Amina—as a warning. The physical pain faded, but what stayed was the feeling she was
being punished because she was fundamentally bad. She came to believe that the event happened to her
because of the sort of person she was, that there was something wrong with her as a person, and that
this defect explained why she deserved what she got.

In her daily life as a plumber, Amina often works in people’s basements or crawl spaces, and she
sometimes hears a customer call out a name that sounds like hers. When she does, her heart drops. A
woman’s voice from another room, a man’s voice on the phone—any voice that carries that same quiet,
warning tone—can make her freeze. These triggers immediately feed her old appraisals: she feels
defective all over again, as if the sound itself proves she is the kind of person who invites
punishment. And because she already believes she is fundamentally wrong, she is hypervigilant for
those sounds, listening for them in every job, every conversation, so that she is primed to hear
them even when they are not there.

To manage this, Amina has developed strategies that feel protective but have only deepened her
isolation. She avoids being around people in authority or uniform—she will not work on commercial
buildings where a foreman or inspector might stop by, and she has turned down jobs where a client is
a police officer or a school principal. She also avoids anything that reminds her of her uncle: she
will not drive past his old neighborhood, she has stopped watching movies with characters named
Amina, and she has thrown away old photographs. She dwells on what other people have done to her,
replaying her uncle’s cruelty in her mind late at night, and this rumination reinforces her sense
that she is a marked person, someone who was chosen for abuse because she was already broken. These
strategies make her feel briefly safer, but they also confirm her negative appraisals. She thinks,
*If I have to avoid so much, there must really be something wrong with me.* And because she is
constantly avoiding, she never gives herself the chance to encounter a neutral or safe voice or
name, so her triggers remain potent and her appraisals unchallenged.

Over the months, this cycle has tightened. She has lost two regular plumbing contracts because she
refused to work on sites with uniformed security guards. Her husband has grown frustrated with her
refusal to attend family gatherings where her uncle’s name might come up. She feels that nothing in
her life feels meaningful anymore—she used to take pride in fixing pipes, in being reliable, but now
she just feels tired and ashamed. What finally brought her to seek help was a moment last week when
she was under a sink and heard a customer’s television playing a show with a character named Amina.
She dropped her wrench, crawled out, and said she had to leave. She sat in her truck for an hour,
shaking. She is afraid that she is losing her grip on her work and her marriage, and she is most
afraid that the therapist will confirm what she already suspects: that there is something
fundamentally wrong with her, and that nothing can fix it.
