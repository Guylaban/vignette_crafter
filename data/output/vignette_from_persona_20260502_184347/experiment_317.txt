The event that fractured Mateo’s world happened in the parking lot of a grocery store, two years
ago. He was thirty-eight then, a graphic designer in the suburbs of Chicago, married to a woman he
adored, and he had never had a problem with police. That evening, he had stopped for milk. A patrol
car pulled into the lot, lights off, no siren. Two officers got out and began shouting at a teenager
on the sidewalk. Mateo, walking toward his car, was caught in the periphery. He froze. One officer
turned, saw him, and in the space of a breath, Mateo was on the ground, his face pressed into the
asphalt, a knee in his back, a voice screaming at him to stay down. He was held for fifteen minutes.
They released him without charge. The officer who had knelt on him said nothing. Mateo drove home
with the taste of gravel in his mouth and a bruise that bloomed across his ribs for a week.

In the months that followed, Mateo began to change. He could not explain what had happened when his
wife asked; the words would tangle, his throat would close, and he would have to leave the room.
What frightened him most was how the memory returned without warning. A car door slamming in a
commercial could bring the full scene back, the weight on his back, the shouting, the smell of hot
asphalt, and he would be so overwhelmed by emotion that he had to stop whatever he was doing. He
began to avoid people, because being around anyone—especially strangers—made him feel too exposed.
He told himself he was just tired. But the world felt hollow. Nothing in his life felt meaningful
anymore, and he was certain that his life had been destroyed by what happened, that the injustice of
it had been ignored and nothing had been done to fix it. He could not relax, even at home. His body
stayed tense, always ready, and before he could feel comfortable anywhere, he would scan for exits,
running through escape routes in his head.

He coped by keeping his mind occupied all the time. He spent long hours on his phone, scrolling
through design feeds, playing puzzle games, clicking through articles he did not finish. The moment
he stopped, the memory would surface again, and the surge of helplessness would return. So he did
not stop. He worked late into the night at his desk, sketching logos he would never send, because
the quiet of the house was worse. The screen became his refuge, but it also kept the memory alive:
every time he put the phone down, the memory hit harder, as if it had been waiting. He began to
notice that even the sight of visible injuries or wounds on others—a bandage on a coworker’s hand, a
child’s scraped knee—could trigger the full memory, the same rush of heat and dread. And the
presence of police, even a patrol car passing on the highway, would make his heart pound and his
mind go blank, and then the memory would come, and he would be back on the ground.

Mateo’s wife, Sarah, began to notice that he was never present. He would sit beside her on the
couch, phone in hand, eyes fixed on a screen, his shoulders tight. She tried to talk to him. He said
he was fine. But he was not fine. He could not relax, and the more he could not relax, the more he
scanned for threats, and the more he scanned, the more the memory returned, unbidden, overwhelming.
The memory itself made his body go on alert, and the alertness made the memory more vivid. He began
to believe that the world was dangerous, that he had been singled out, that no one would listen. The
injustice of it gnawed at him, and that sense of being wronged made him feel even more unsafe, as if
the same thing could happen again at any moment. He started to see police where there were none, to
flinch at the sound of a car door, to feel the asphalt under his cheek in the middle of a work
meeting.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment three weeks ago. He was at the grocery store
again, the same chain, different parking lot. A man in a dark jacket walked past him. Mateo’s vision
narrowed. He dropped his basket, walked out, and sat in his car for forty minutes, shaking, unable
to drive. That night, Sarah told him she could not live like this anymore. She was not leaving, she
said, but she needed him to try. He is afraid that trying will mean talking about what happened, and
he is not sure he can finish the story out loud. But he is more afraid of losing her, and of the
life he once had. So he came.
