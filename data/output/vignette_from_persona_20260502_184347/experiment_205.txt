Nadia is a 55-year-old HVAC technician, a woman who has spent the last fifteen years rebuilding a
life in the United States after fleeing the chaos of war-torn Iraq. She is divorced, lives alone in
a small apartment, and finds solace in the mechanical predictability of heating and cooling
systems—things that either work or they don’t, with no hidden cruelty. But the world outside her
work van feels different. It is the silence that gets her first. An unusual quiet in a client’s
basement, the kind that settles before a storm, makes her heart pound with the certainty that
something terrible is about to happen. It was that same dead silence, just before the militia kicked
down her door, that marked the beginning of her flight.

The event itself is a fractured collage she cannot fully trust. She remembers the flood of soldiers,
the rough hands, the wet, gritty texture of mud and blood that clung to her clothes as she was
forced into the back of a truck. But the memory feels so extreme, so cinematic, that she sometimes
wonders if it really happened to her. The shame that surfaces when she recalls it is visceral—a hot,
crawling feeling that she should have done something different, been braver. When the memory returns
unbidden, it overwhelms her with such a wave of emotion that she has to stop what she is doing,
gripping the edge of a counter until it passes. These moments are often triggered by the most
ordinary things. A client’s leaky pipe, a flooded parking lot, or the unexpected feel of a damp,
rough towel can instantly catapult her back. The sight of water, especially large bodies of it, or
the specific texture of wet, gritty concrete, acts like a key turning a lock in her mind.

Because of this, Nadia has become a master of preemptive control. Everywhere she goes—a grocery
store, a new job site, even a friend’s house for dinner—she first identifies the exits. She is
constantly scanning her environment for signs of danger, her eyes flicking to doorways and windows,
cataloging potential hazards. She also finds herself trapped in a loop of rumination, dwelling on
what was done to her, replaying the faces of the men who took everything from her. This constant
vigilance and planning, however, do not make her feel safe. Instead, they keep the memory raw and
accessible. The act of scanning for threats often brings the memory of the truck to the forefront of
her mind, as if she is looking for the same danger she once fled. Planning an escape route reminds
her of the moment she had no escape, and the rumination feeds the shame, which in turn makes the
memory feel more real and more overwhelming. The very strategies she uses to feel secure are the
ones that keep her trapped in the past.

The threat she perceives is not always external. The silence itself feels dangerous, a precursor to
violence. But the memory is the source of the most acute fear. When a flashback suddenly hits—and it
does, with a terrifying sensory completeness—she feels as though she is back in that truck, the cold
metal against her skin, the smell of diesel and fear. These flashbacks are so vivid that they
confirm her worst appraisal: that the world is fundamentally unsafe, and that her past is not over,
but merely waiting to happen again. This perception of threat, in turn, makes the memory more
potent. The more she feels danger in a quiet room, the more the memory of the original threat
intrudes, proving to her that her fear is justified.

Her life has shrunk around these cycles. She has stopped taking jobs that involve basements with
poor drainage, and she avoids any route that takes her near the river. She has lost a few clients
because she will not enter a home without first walking the perimeter, a habit they find odd. She
has also stopped going to her niece’s house because the family has a swimming pool, and the sight of
the blue water makes her feel faint. Her divorce, years ago, was partly because her husband could
not understand why she would freeze at the sound of a motorcycle backfiring or why she needed to
sleep with the light on. She has come to believe that her mind is broken, that the memory is a flaw
she cannot fix.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment at work last month. She was repairing a boiler in
a school basement when a pipe burst, flooding the floor with a few inches of murky water. The
texture of the wet concrete under her boots, the sudden silence of the room as the water rose,
triggered a flashback so intense that she dropped her wrench and ran out of the building, leaving
her tools behind. Her boss told her she needed to “get it together” or find another line of work.
Now, sitting in the clinic, she is most afraid of losing her job, the one thing that gives her life
structure and purpose. She is also terrified that talking about the event will make it more real,
that she will finally have to admit that the memory is not a bad dream, but the truth. And she is
equally afraid that if she does not talk about it, she will keep running from silence and water
until she has nowhere left to go.
