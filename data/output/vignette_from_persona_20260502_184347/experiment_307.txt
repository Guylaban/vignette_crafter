Marco is a 25-year-old housekeeper who grew up in a small town in the Pacific Northwest. When he was
eight years old, his mother’s boyfriend, a man named Carl, began punishing him for small infractions
by locking him in a dark utility closet for hours at a time. The closet smelled of bleach and old
rags, and the floor was covered in a rough, industrial carpet that felt like sandpaper against his
skin. Carl would sometimes stand outside the door and listen, and if Marco cried or called out, the
punishment would be extended. The worst times were when Carl would open the door just enough to
shove a wet, gritty rag into Marco’s mouth to silence him, the texture of damp fiber and chemical
residue pressing against his tongue. This went on for nearly two years until his mother finally left
Carl, but by then the pattern was set.

Now, at 25, Marco lives alone in a small apartment and works long shifts cleaning hotel rooms. He
has come to believe that other people can see what happened to him just by looking at him—that there
is something in his posture or his eyes that gives it away. He feels like an object, not like a
person, and he is certain that you can never know who will harm you. What frightens him most is the
dark. Waking up in the middle of the night, even for a moment, sends a surge of panic through his
chest because the darkness feels like the closet, and he is instantly back there, the carpet rough
under his palms, the chemical smell filling his nose. The memory returns as a film that plays over
and over, and when it comes back it feels completely real across all his senses at once—he can taste
the rag, feel the grit, hear the silence. When the memory returns he feels so overwhelmed by emotion
that he has to stop whatever he is doing.

Marco has developed a set of strategies to manage these experiences, though they have come at a
cost. He has given up important responsibilities to stay safe or avoid triggers: he no longer visits
friends after dark, he stopped attending a weekly dinner his coworkers organized, and he recently
turned down a promotion that would have required evening shifts. He has also become careful never to
be alone with another person, especially in enclosed spaces, because he is convinced that solitude
with someone else is when danger strikes. When he does feel threatened or flooded, he drifts off
into a world of his own, a fantasy escape that lets him disappear mentally even if his body is still
present. But this escape often backfires: the more he drifts, the more the memory seems to slip in
through the gaps, and he finds himself replaying the film again, the loop tightening.

The causal connections between these experiences have become deeply entrenched. A trigger—like the
sound of a child crying in the hallway, which he hears sometimes at the hotel—immediately pulls the
memory forward, and the memory then reinforces his belief that he is exposed and vulnerable. The
memory also drives his maladaptive strategies: after a flashback, he will cancel plans, avoid the
lobby, or retreat into his fantasy world. But those strategies, in turn, make the memory more
intrusive. The more he avoids being alone with others, the more he replays the closet in his mind,
and the more he replays it, the more he feels like an object. The hypervigilance he now lives
with—being superalert or watchful or on guard at all times—means he is constantly scanning for
threats, and he has strong physical reactions when something reminds him of the experience. His
heart pounds when he hears a child cry, and sometimes he suddenly feels as if he is back in the
closet, acting as if the stressful experience were actually happening again.

The texture of damp fabric remains a potent trigger. At work, when he picks up a used towel or a wet
rag, his hand freezes and his stomach turns. He has started wearing gloves even for tasks that do
not require them, and he avoids the laundry room altogether, asking a coworker to cover that part of
his shift. The coworker has started to notice, and Marco has had to lie about a skin condition. He
knows this is fragile. He knows he is barely holding things together.

What finally brought him to seek help was a night last week when he woke up in the dark, fully
reliving the closet, and found himself on his hands and knees on his bedroom floor, gagging, with no
memory of how he got there. He is afraid that next time he will not come back. He is hoping that
someone can help him stop the film from playing.
