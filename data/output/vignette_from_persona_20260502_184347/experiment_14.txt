that reshaped her life happened on a convoy escort mission outside Mosul. An IED detonated beneath
the lead vehicle, and in the chaos that followed, a fuel line ruptured, spraying burning diesel
across the road. Elena had been in the second vehicle, and as she scrambled out to help, she felt
the heat on her skin—a specific, searing temperature that she would later recognize instantly
whenever she stood too close to a stove or felt the sun through a car window. Shouting erupted from
all sides, orders and screams blending together, and she remembers the flames climbing the side of
the overturned Humvee. But she cannot tell a clear story of what happened, even to herself; the
memory exists as fragments—the sound of a voice calling her name, the crackling, the sensation of
heat—rather than a coherent sequence. Something in her surroundings can instantly bring back the
full memory without warning: a flicker of a lighter, a sudden rise in temperature, someone raising
their voice in anger. When those memories come, she sometimes hears things in her head that she
heard during the frightening event—the shouting, the metallic clang of the blast—and her body feels
constantly tense and ready to react to danger. She has come to believe that there is something wrong
with her as a person, that she should have been able to prevent the attack, that she should have
been more prepared. What happened to her was deeply unfair, she thinks, and nothing has been done
about it; the military investigated and closed the case, but she carries the heat and the noise
every day.

In the years since, Elena has built her life around avoiding anything that could be stressful. She
works as a combat engineer supervisor on a base, but she has requested desk duty, away from the
training ranges where fuel and fire are present. She has learned to numb her feelings, because when
she lets herself feel, the memory rushes back—the heat, the shouting, the helplessness. She
constantly scans her environment for signs of threat, her eyes moving across rooms, checking exits,
watching people’s hands. This vigilance has become exhausting; she has difficulty concentrating on
paperwork, on conversations, on her husband’s stories about his day. Unusual silence makes her feel
something dangerous is about to happen, so she keeps the television on at home, or music playing,
because quiet feels like a trap. She has become certain that the world is a dangerous place, and
that she cannot let her guard down for a single moment. This belief feeds her avoidance: if she
stops scanning, something bad will happen; if she lets herself feel, she will fall apart. The more

fundamentally defective in her. The scanning also brings the triggers closer—she notices every
flicker of light, every raised voice, every shift in temperature, and each one pulls her back into
the memory. The memory itself makes her scan more, makes her numb harder, makes her feel more
defective, and makes the world feel more dangerous.

feeling like she is still in that convoy, still waiting for the next explosion.
