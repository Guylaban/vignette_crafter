Mai was seventy-four years old and had spent forty years writing code, but she could not debug the
night her daughter nearly died. The memory surfaced not as a sequence of events but as a single,
suffocating sensation: the smell of antiseptic and the hollow echo of a hospital corridor at three
in the morning. Her daughter, then six, had been admitted with a sudden, unexplained fever that
spiked to 105 degrees. Mai remembered standing in the doorway of the ICU, watching the nurses move
with practiced urgency, and feeling the world go silent—not the silence of peace, but the silence of
something terrible about to happen. She had not moved. She had not spoken. She had simply stood
there, frozen, as the doctors worked. And in the years since, that silence had become a trigger.
When the house fell quiet, when her husband was out and the refrigerator stopped humming, Mai’s
chest would tighten. She would smell the antiseptic again, feel the terror rise, and then, without a
clear image, the shame would follow—a hot, familiar wave. She had been wrong to stand there. She
should have done something. The event happened because of the way I acted, she told herself, and the
world was a place where bad things happened and you could not stop them.

These beliefs had grown roots. Mai had come to believe that silence itself was a warning. When her
office went quiet during a late-night deployment, she would feel her pulse quicken, her attention
narrowing to the hum of the server fans. She would become hyperalert, scanning for signs of danger,
and the hypervigilance would pull her back to that hospital room. The memory would return with full
emotional force—the terror, the shame—and the cycle would tighten. She had difficulty falling asleep
because she needed to stay alert. She would lie in bed, listening to the house settle, certain that
if she let her guard down, something would happen. Her husband had long since stopped asking why she
kept the television on all night.

At work, the car sounds in the parking lot became another trigger. Every engine starting was a
potential emergency, every sudden silence a prelude to bad news. She had begun arriving early and
leaving late, avoiding the parking lot during busy hours. Her colleagues noticed she no longer
joined the team for lunch. She said she was busy. She was not busy. She was scanning the hallway for
exits, for signs, for anything that might tell her when the next bad thing was coming. The world was
dangerous, and she could not stop it.

What finally brought Mai to therapy was not a crisis but a quiet evening. Her daughter, now forty,
had called to say she was pregnant. Mai had felt no joy. She had felt only dread—a cold certainty
that something would go wrong, that silence would return, that she would be helpless again. She had
not told her daughter. She had hung up and sat in the dark, listening to the house, waiting. She was
most afraid of what she might do if her granddaughter ever got sick. She was hoping someone could
teach her how to stop the loop—how to hear silence without seeing that doorway again.
