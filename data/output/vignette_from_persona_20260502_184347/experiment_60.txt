quite believe it happened to her. These pieces surface without warning—while she is folding laundry,
or in the middle of a patient’s rehabilitation session—and each time they do, Rosa’s chest tightens
and her eyes dart to the door.

In the decades since, Rosa has built a life around avoiding anything that could feel stressful. She
schedules her physical therapy appointments with rigid precision, leaving no gaps for unexpected
conversations with colleagues. She has come to believe that strangers approaching her—even a patient
who steps too close to adjust their brace—pose a threat she cannot name. At work, she always
positions herself so that her back is against a wall, her eyes sweeping the room. At night, she
wakes three or four times to check the locks, to listen for footsteps, to confirm that no one has
entered. The cleaning began as a small habit—wiping down her treatment table between patients—but it
has become something else: she now scrubs her kitchen counters for forty-five minutes each evening,
reorganizing the spice rack until it feels right, because the act of controlling her environment is
the only thing that quiets the alarm in her chest.

The connection between the memory fragments and her vigilance is something Rosa feels in her bones.

catch up: her shoulders tense, her breathing quickens, and she scans the room for an exit. That
sense of threat, in turn, drives her to clean more fiercely, to eat an entire bag of chips while
standing at the counter, to double-check the locks again. And the more she engages in these
routines, the more the memory surfaces: washing her hands for the third time might trigger the
fragment of her mother’s hand raised mid-swing, and the cycle tightens.

A few months ago, Rosa found herself avoiding a particular patient—an older woman with a loud
voice—because the sound triggered the sudden pieces of memory. She rescheduled the woman four times
before her supervisor asked if something was wrong. Rosa said she was fine, but she knew she was
not. What finally brought her to seek help was a night two weeks ago when she woke at 3 a.m.,
convinced she heard her mother’s footsteps in the hallway, and spent an hour scrubbing the bathroom
floor on her hands and knees. She is most afraid that the fragments will keep coming, that the
cleaning will take over her life completely, and that one day she will not be able to leave the
house at all. She hopes, quietly, that someone can help her feel safe in her own skin.
