Kai is a 42-year-old electrician of Oceania descent who lives with his partner in a quiet suburban
neighborhood. He came to therapy at his partner’s urging, after months of what he describes as
“falling apart” following a brutal assault in the parking lot of a job site six months ago. The
attack happened at dusk, around 6:15 PM, as he was packing his tools into his van. Two men cornered
him, demanding his wallet and phone. When he hesitated, one slammed a heavy metal door from a nearby
dumpster against his back, while the other punched him in the ribs. The memory that haunts him most
is not the full sequence but isolated, vivid snapshots: the sound of the door slamming, the dull
thud against his spine, the feeling of his knees buckling onto asphalt. He recalls these moments as
frozen pictures, each one distinct and incomplete, as if his mind has edited out the rest. What
remains is a fragmentary loop of guilt—he cannot shake the belief that he should have fought back,
should have seen it coming, and that the attack happened because of the sort of person he is:
someone too trusting, too slow, too weak.

In the weeks that followed, Kai began to notice that the time of day—late afternoon, the light
beginning to fade—would trigger a sudden, visceral shift. As the clock edged toward 6 PM, his chest
would tighten and his surroundings would feel unreal, as if he were watching himself from a
distance. That same dissociative feeling, coupled with the sound of a door opening or slamming at
work or at home, would send him straight into a state of hypervigilance. A colleague shutting a
cabinet too hard would make him flinch, and he would catch himself scanning the parking lot for
escape routes. These triggers also pulled him back into the fragmented memories: the slamming door,
the guilt, the incomplete picture of what exactly happened. And each time, the thought would surface
again: *This happened because of who I am.* Kai had come to believe that his character was
fundamentally flawed, that another man would have handled the situation differently, and that his
future was effectively over.

To manage the constant dread, Kai developed a set of habits that seemed to help in the moment but
only deepened his isolation. After work, he would retreat to the basement and spend hours scrolling
through his phone, playing games, or drinking beer until he felt numb. He avoided any television
show or film that involved violence or confrontation, because even fictional depictions would bring
the memory roaring back. These strategies worked briefly, but they also reinforced his sense of
hopelessness—each evening spent escaping reminded him that he was not getting better, that he was
stuck. His partner noticed he was drinking more and withdrawing from their shared routines, and they
argued about it, which only made Kai feel more convinced that he was a burden.

The threat system Kai now lives with is relentless. He startles violently at unexpected
movement—someone stepping into his peripheral vision at a hardware store will make him drop his
keys. He reacts with alarm when his partner touches him from behind, even gently. He has begun
avoiding unfamiliar places altogether, because he cannot assess the danger in advance. He sticks to
the same grocery store, the same route home, the same few job sites he knows. This avoidance has
cost him work; he turned down a lucrative contract that required traveling to a new part of the
city. The threat is not just external—it feeds into his memory, because every startle reminds him of
the assault, and every reminder reinforces his belief that he is broken. And the triggers themselves
seem to multiply: now, even the sound of a car door at dusk can set him off, and he finds himself
scanning for danger in his own backyard.

What finally brought Kai to seek help was a moment of clarity. Last week, his partner came home
unexpectedly and found him sitting in the dark basement at 6:15 PM, phone in hand, a half-empty
bottle of whiskey on the floor, staring at the wall. He had been there for three hours. When she
asked what was wrong, he could only say, “I don’t know how to stop this.” He is terrified that he
will never feel safe again, that the assault has permanently reshaped who he is, and that his
relationship will not survive his retreat into avoidance. But beneath that fear, there is a fragile
hope: that if he can understand why his mind keeps pulling him back to those frozen moments, he
might finally find a way out.
