ago. He lives alone in a small apartment above the shop where he works, and he has built a quiet
life around the precision of his craft—measuring, cutting, stitching—work that demands focus and
steadiness. That life shattered on a Tuesday afternoon two years ago, when a man walked into a busy
market square in the neighborhood where Kwame had gone to buy fabric. Kwame was standing at a stall,
examining a bolt of indigo cloth, when he heard the first shot. Then another. Then screaming, bodies
dropping, the wet sound of feet slipping on concrete slick with blood. He remembers crouching behind
a metal cart, his heart pounding so hard he could not hear his own thoughts. He remembers a woman
next to him clutching her child, the child’s face frozen in a silent wail. And then he remembers
nothing for a stretch—a gap that still haunts him, a black interval he cannot retrieve no matter how
hard he tries. What comes after that gap is worse: the film that plays over and over in his mind,
the same images on a loop—the cart, the child, the blood—but sometimes, when he tries to recall it,
he feels nothing at all, as if it happened to someone else, and that numbness frightens him more
than the fear did.

In the months that followed, Kwame began to change in ways he did not fully recognize at first. He
started to believe that he was weak—that a stronger man would have done something, would have helped
someone, would not have frozen behind a cart. He became certain that he no longer belonged anywhere,
not in the market, not in his shop, not even in the small circle of friends he used to see on
weekends. He began to doubt his own judgment: if he could not trust himself to act in a moment of
crisis, how could he trust himself to make even simple decisions? This self-doubt seeped into
everything. He stopped making plans—holidays, birthdays, even the next month's rent felt impossible
to think about. He found himself picking arguments with customers over small things, a misplaced
measurement or a late payment, because the heat of conflict, the sharp back-and-forth, drowned out
the film in his head for a few minutes. And when he was alone, he escaped into his phone, scrolling
through games and videos for hours, losing entire evenings to the glow of the screen, because the
silence of his apartment was worse than the noise of the marketplace.

Kwame would scan for exits—the back door, the window, the gap under the counter. He could not work

entrance, always watching, always ready. He felt unsafe, constantly on guard, his body braced for a
sound that might be a gunshot. This hypervigilance drained him, and it also fed his belief that he
was broken—that a normal person did not live like this, checking doorways and flinching at the ring
of a bell. The worse his self-doubt grew, the more he avoided plans and picked fights, and the more
he avoided, the more the film played, and the more the film played, the more he scanned and watched
and feared. He came to believe that he was trapped in a loop he could not break.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was a small thing, but it broke something open. A customer

stammered, made an excuse, and watched the customer leave in confusion. That night, sitting on his
floor with his back against the wall, he realized he had not made a single plan for the future in
two years. He had not visited his mother, not called his brother, not even thought about what he

came to the clinic hoping that someone could help him remember the missing part of that

he is weak, and that he does not belong anywhere anymore.
