federal plaza. He had been walking back from a coffee shop, the morning still quiet, when the air
turned inside out. What he remembered best was not the blast itself but the seconds after: the
ringing silence, the dust falling like snow, a woman’s shoe lying on its side a few feet away. He
had crouched behind a concrete planter, and later, when the paramedics came, he had helped them
carry a man whose leg was wrong. He could not tell a clear story of what happened, even to himself.

a narrative. They hung in his mind like photographs without captions, more real than any ordinary
memory, so vivid that sometimes, walking into his office, he would feel the grit of concrete on his
palms and know, with absolute certainty, that it was happening right now.

In the months that followed, Marcus returned to work. The courthouse was three blocks from the
plaza, and he passed the barricades every morning without looking at them. What he could not avoid
was the smell of alcohol. A colleague came back from lunch one day, and the faint sweetness on his
breath hit Marcus like a wall. He felt his chest lock. The colleague had been drinking—just a beer,
he said later—but in that moment Marcus was certain that he should have known, should have stopped
it. He had not done enough. He had not protected anyone. The guilt was a weight he carried into

knew, rationally, that there was nothing he could have done. The feeling did not listen to reason.

in a trial gave him a straightforward account, and Marcus found himself searching for the lie, the
hidden thing. People are not what they seem, he thought, and the thought felt like a law of physics.

pressing in.
