Anh Tran is a 51-year-old tax inspector who has been married for twenty-three years. Four years ago,
his wife, in a fit of rage during an argument about their teenage son’s school performance, struck
him repeatedly across the face and chest with a metal cooking spoon. He had frozen, too stunned to
defend himself, and the beating had continued until their son intervened. In the months that
followed, Anh found himself unable to recall the exact sequence of that night—the memory arrived
only in sudden, fragmentary flashes: the glint of the spoon, the sound of his own gasping, the look
of contempt on his wife’s face. He could not remember how he had ended up on the kitchen floor or
what had been said afterward.

These fragments forced their way into his mind without warning, often when he was simply sitting at
his desk reviewing tax returns. The unpredictability of these intrusions made him feel like an
object, a thing that things happened to, rather than a person who could act. He had come to believe
that nothing good could happen to him anymore, that he had been betrayed by the one person who was
supposed to protect him. This sense of betrayal and dehumanization made him feel constantly on edge;
he began to react as if he were in danger even when nothing was actually wrong, jumping at the sound
of a colleague’s footsteps.

The discomfort of feeling trapped in his own home—where his wife’s voice, her presence in the next

against a wall where he could see the room. At work, he began frequently looking behind him to check
if someone was following him, a habit his colleagues noticed but did not comment on. The exhaustion
of this constant vigilance made him feel very tired, and that fatigue itself became a trigger: when
he was tired, the memory fragments came more easily, and his negative appraisals—that he was
nothing, that he had been betrayed—felt more true.

He had come to believe that his own exhaustion was a sign that he was failing, that he was weak, and
this belief made the unpredictability of his life feel even more threatening. The more he felt like
an object, the more he avoided unpredictable situations—he stopped attending his son’s basketball
games, stopped visiting his own family on weekends, stopped going to the grocery store unless it was
at a precise time when he knew it would be empty. At work, he took on only the most routine audits,
refusing any case that required negotiating with taxpayers in person, because he could not tolerate
the unpredictability of what they might say.

betrayal, the helplessness—reinforced his sense of being an object. This made him check behind him

him to seek help was a moment at his desk last month: he had been reviewing a routine audit, felt a
wave of exhaustion, and then found himself on the floor, his hands over his head, with no memory of

that if he does not get control of this, he will lose his job, but what frightens him most is the
thought that his wife might be right—that he is, after all, just an object, and objects do not
deserve to feel safe.
