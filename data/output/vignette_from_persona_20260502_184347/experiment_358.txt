Arthur is a 67-year-old married man who worked for decades as a school principal, a role he loved
for its order and predictability. That life ended on a rainy Tuesday evening eight months ago, when
a driver ran a red light and T-boned Arthur’s sedan at an intersection. The impact spun his car into
a utility pole. He remembers the shriek of twisting metal, the sensation of his body being thrown
sideways against the seatbelt, and then the silence before the ringing in his ears began. He was
trapped for nearly twenty minutes before firefighters cut him free. Physically, he recovered; the
bruises faded, the cracked ribs healed. But something else did not.

Now, a car braking too hard on the street outside his home will send Arthur’s heart racing, and he
finds himself suddenly back in that spinning vehicle, the smell of gasoline sharp in his nostrils.
He cannot put the event into words—when his wife asks what happened, he just shakes his head,
because the memory is not a story he can tell; it is a film that plays over and over, and when it
plays, he feels like it is happening right now. He has come to believe that he is permanently
changed for the worse, that the world is a fundamentally dangerous place where catastrophe waits at
every intersection. What frightens him most is the certainty that somebody else—a better man, a
stronger man—would not have gotten into this situation in the first place.

This conviction drives almost everything Arthur does. Because the world is dangerous, he scans for
threats constantly. When something takes longer than expected—a delayed staff meeting, a slow
elevator—he immediately assumes something bad is happening, that the universe is about to spring
another trap. He cannot relax, even at home, which he knows is safe; his body stays braced for
impact. The trouble falling asleep began within weeks of the crash and has only worsened, because
the moment he closes his eyes, the film starts playing again. The more he relives the event, the
more convinced he becomes that he is broken, and the more dangerous the world feels.

Arthur has developed strategies to manage this unbearable state, though each one makes things worse.
He forces himself to think only positive thoughts, a mental effort that leaves him exhausted and
hollow, and which secretly confirms to him that he must be deeply damaged if he has to work this
hard just to feel normal. He has also started eating excessively in the evenings, a habit that has
added fifteen pounds and now makes him feel ashamed and out of control. The most consequential
strategy, however, is his avoidance of work. As a principal, his days were filled with sudden loud
noises—slamming doors, fire drills, a child shouting in the hallway—and every shout, every bang,
every unexpected crash yanks him back into the crash. He began taking sick days, then medical leave.
He has not set foot in the school in six weeks. His assistant principal calls with questions; Arthur
stares at the phone and lets it ring. Each day he does not go in, he tells himself that a real
principal would have handled this, that he is a coward, that he has permanently changed for the
worse. The world is dangerous, he is broken, and the only way to survive is to stay still, to stay
quiet, to stay home.

But staying home has not helped. In the silence, his mind replays the crash. The more he replays it,
the more he feels its immediacy, and the more he feels the world closing in. A delivery truck
backfiring on his street sends him to the floor. His wife found him there last week, and she
insisted he call someone. Arthur is here now because he is afraid of what he is becoming—a man who
cannot work, cannot sleep, cannot even answer his own front door without his chest seizing. He is
hoping, though he barely dares to say it aloud, that someone can help him find a way back to the
person he used to be.
