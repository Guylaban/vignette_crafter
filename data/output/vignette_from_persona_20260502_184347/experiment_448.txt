become a battlefield. She had been a university student, studying social work, when soldiers came to
her campus one afternoon. They rounded up students in the courtyard, shouting orders in Spanish that
cracked through the air like whips. María remembered the smell of alcohol on the breath of the
commanding officer as he leaned close to her face, the way his uniform seemed to fill her entire
field of vision, and the sound of his voice—sharp, absolute, leaving no room for refusal. She had
done exactly what she was told, standing frozen while they searched the dormitories, and later that
night, she fled across the border with nothing but the clothes on her back. Now, twelve years later,
she worked as a prison guard in a state correctional facility, a job she had taken because it
offered stability and a steady paycheck for her family. But every shift brought her back to that
courtyard.

tighten before she even understood why. When a supervisor gave her a direct order—even a routine

body. And whenever she saw a corrections officer in full uniform, especially one with a stern

activated something inside her. The smell of alcohol, the sound of an order, the sight of a
uniform—each one told her, with the force of a physical blow, that the world was still dangerous,
that people in authority could not be trusted, that she was no longer the person she had been before
that afternoon. She had come to believe that the world was a dangerous place, that people were not
what they seemed, and that somewhere along the way, she had lost herself entirely. And because she
believed this, she began to scan for danger constantly—a flicker of movement in her peripheral
vision made her flinch, and she would find herself reacting as if she were under threat even in the
prison’s quiet hallways, her heart hammering for no reason she could name. She always chose her seat
in the break room with her back to the wall, facing the door, so she could see anyone approaching.

whatever she was doing, her hands trembling, her vision blurring. These vivid, detailed
recollections reinforced everything she already feared. They proved to her that the world was
dangerous, that she could not trust anyone, that she had been fundamentally changed. And so she

her and made her feel even more unsafe. She would then put on loud music in her car to block it out,

What finally brought María to seek help was a moment in the prison cafeteria. A new officer, a young

would happen if she did not—afraid she would lose her job, afraid she would lose herself completely,
afraid that the world she had tried so hard to escape had followed her here, and that it would never
let her go.
