old unit onto a dolly—a heavy, awkward load—and lost his grip. The unit tipped, and in the seconds
it took Marco to realize what was happening, it had already struck the man in the head. The sound
was sickening and wet. The boy screamed. Marco’s hands went numb as he knelt beside the man, calling
911, pressing a towel to the wound, but he knew even then that he had not done enough. The man
survived, but with lasting neurological damage. Marco has not forgiven himself.

In the months that followed, Marco found himself replaying that moment in fragments—not as a
coherent memory, but as a series of sensory intrusions that felt as though they belonged to someone
else. He would be driving home from a job and suddenly smell the hot metal of the condenser and the
cut grass of that backyard, and he would think, *That isn’t mine. That didn’t happen to me.* But the
smell was real, and his heart would slam against his ribs before he could stop it. He had come to
believe that his own body was fundamentally unreliable—that his hands, which he had always counted

anything that might put him in a position to fail again. He stopped taking jobs that involved
working near other people. He turned down calls from families with children. He began to avoid dates
and anniversaries—the anniversary of the accident, his own birthday, holidays—because each one felt
like a trap waiting to spring. He would lie in bed on those days, refusing to move, certain that if
he did anything at all, he would cause another disaster.
