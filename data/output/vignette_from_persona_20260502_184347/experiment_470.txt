Meera was thirty-two years old, a chaplain who had once found her calling in the quiet spaces of a
hospital, sitting with the dying and the frightened. That was before the night she was taken. She
had been volunteering with a humanitarian aid convoy in a conflict zone, a brief mission she had
undertaken with her faith community. Armed men in military uniforms had stopped the vehicle, pulled
her out, and held her for three days. She remembered the cold concrete floor, the zip ties biting
into her wrists, the sound of boots on the stairs, and the questions shouted in a language she
barely understood. She was released eventually, dumped near a checkpoint, but her body remembered
what her mind could not fully speak.

In the months that followed, Meera returned to her work in the United States, but something had
fractured. The memory of the event had become a strange, wordless weight. She knew what had
happened—she could see the concrete, feel the restraints—but when she tried to tell anyone, the
words evaporated. She would open her mouth and find only fragments: the sensation of a hand on her
shoulder, the echo of a door slamming. She could not explain clearly. In sessions with a therapist,
she would start a sentence and then lose the thread entirely, the story dissolving into a tightness
in her chest. This inability to speak about it made her feel trapped all over again, and she began
to hurt herself physically to stop the thoughts from spiraling. A sharp pinch on her forearm, a nail
dug into her palm—the pain was a language her body understood, a way to interrupt the silent film
playing behind her eyes.

The memory also sharpened the world into a landscape of threats. Every sound became a trigger. The
clatter of a metal tray in the hospital cafeteria sent her heart racing; the low hum of a male voice
around a corner made her freeze. The feeling of being physically restrained, even in a crowded
elevator or during a routine medical exam, would flood her with panic. And uniforms—police,
security, even a delivery driver in a dark jacket—were the worst. She would cross the street to
avoid them, her throat closing. The memory, though wordless, seemed to have mapped every sensory
detail onto her present life, turning the everyday into a minefield.

Because she could not find the words, and because the triggers felt relentless, Meera began to
withdraw. She stopped going to the hospital chapel, where the long, open hallway to the sanctuary
made her feel exposed and vulnerable. She stopped returning calls from the friends she had known
before the event; they wanted to ask how she was, and she knew she could not answer. Her avoidance
became her only strategy. She took a leave of absence from work, then resigned. She stopped leaving
her apartment except at dusk, when the streets were quiet. But the avoidance itself fed back into
the memory. The more she hid, the more the event dominated her mind. She would spend hours staring
at the wall, the same images playing on a loop—the zip ties, the boots, the door—and she could not
find the words to push them away. The silence she created only amplified the noise inside.

Her ex-husband, from whom she had divorced two years before the trauma, had once said she was too
intense. Now she was simply absent. She had no one to tell that she had started cutting her forearm
again, a small, secret ritual that gave her a few minutes of quiet. She told herself it was the only
way to stop the memory from taking over, but she also knew, in a distant way, that the memory was
what drove her to it.

What finally brought Meera to seek help was a panic attack in a grocery store. A man in a security
uniform walked past her, and she dropped to the floor, gasping, unable to speak. A stranger called
an ambulance. In the emergency room, a doctor asked her what had happened, and Meera opened her
mouth, but the words were gone. She was terrified that she would never find them again. She was also
terrified that if she did, she would fall apart entirely.
