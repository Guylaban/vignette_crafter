The attack happened just before dawn, in the alley behind her studio. Aisha, then seventy-three, had
been unloading clay from the back of her van, the way she had done a thousand times before. She
heard nothing until the arm was around her throat and the voice, low and close to her ear, told her
not to scream. She did not scream. She went rigid, her hands still gripping the bag of clay, and she
felt the cold point of a blade press against her kidney. In the moments that followed, the world
narrowed to the smell of the man’s sweat, the weight of his chest against her back, and the
terrible, vivid clarity of every brick in the wall she was staring at. He took her wallet, her
phone, and the small pot she had just finished—a wedding vase she had spent three days on—and then
he was gone. She stood in the alley for a long time, still holding the clay, unable to move.

For weeks afterward, Aisha could not stop seeing it. The memory forced its way into her mind without
warning: the cold point of the knife, the smell of sweat, the bricks. When she remembered it—and she
tried very hard not to, because she was certain that if she let herself think about the event she
would not be able to handle it—she felt as though it was happening right now. Her body would seize
up, her palms would sweat, and she would be back in that alley, unable to move. The memories were
mostly pictures or images, sharp and wordless, and they came most often when she was alone in her
house at night.

She had come to believe that the world was a dangerous place, and that belief seeped into
everything. It was not a thought she articulated; it was a feeling that lived in her chest, a
constant hum of vigilance. She began to listen for sounds that might mean someone was entering her
home—the creak of a floorboard, the rattle of a window, the distant sound of a car door closing. She
woke during the night to check whether she was safe, her heart hammering before her eyes were even
open. She found she was unable to relax even when she knew she was in a safe place, sitting in her
living room with the door locked, the chain on, and a chair wedged under the handle. Her body kept
reacting as if she was still in danger even when she was safe, and she hated it—she hated the way
her hands would tremble for no reason, the way her breath would catch at a sudden noise.

The checking became a ritual. She checked the locks on the front door and the back door three times
before she could sit down. She checked the windows in every room, even the ones on the second floor.
She kept checking mirrors, too—not because she expected to see someone behind her, but because she
could not help it. She would glance at the reflection in the hallway mirror, then look away, then
look again, as if she needed to confirm that no one was there. The checking did not make her feel
safe; it made her feel more convinced that she was not safe, because why would she need to check if
there was no danger? But she could not stop. She was certain that if she stopped, something terrible
would happen.

The pottery studio became a problem. She could not bear to walk past the alley, so she stopped
taking the van to the back door. She parked on the street and carried the clay through the front, a
longer and harder journey for her seventy-eight-year-old body. She stopped working late, even though
she had always preferred the quiet of the evening. She stopped taking commissions for wedding vases,
because the shape of them reminded her of the one that had been stolen. Her income dropped, and she
began to feel isolated, cut off from the rhythm of her work that had sustained her for fifty years.

What frightened her most was the way her own mind seemed to be against her. The more she tried to
suppress the thoughts, the more the memories forced their way in. And each time they did, they
reinforced her belief that the world was dangerous, that her body was unreliable, that she could not
handle the truth of what had happened. The loop tightened. She checked the locks again. She looked
in the mirror. She stayed awake, listening.

It was her daughter who finally convinced her to come. Aisha had not told her about the checking or
the waking or the fear that sat in her chest like a stone. But her daughter had noticed that she had
stopped making pots, and that was how Aisha knew something was wrong. She is here now because she is
afraid of what she will become if she does not find a way out, and because she is even more afraid
of what will happen if she lets herself remember.
