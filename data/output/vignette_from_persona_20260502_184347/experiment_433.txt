Nadia was sixty-three years old, a single woman who had worked as an auditor for the same firm for
nearly thirty years. The trauma that had reshaped her life had happened two years ago, on a weekend
hike in a state park she had visited dozens of times before. She had been walking a familiar trail
with her younger brother, Elias, when he stumbled on a loose rock near a cliff edge and fell thirty
feet onto the boulders below. Nadia had scrambled down to him, but he was already gone—his body
broken, his eyes open and still. She had sat beside him for hours, unable to reach a phone,
surrounded by the sound of wind in the pines and the smell of damp earth, until another hiker found
them.

In the months that followed, Nadia’s mind became a trap she could not escape. The memory of that
afternoon did not behave like a normal memory. Something in her surroundings—the creak of a door,
the particular shade of green in a houseplant, the scent of wet soil after rain—could instantly
bring back the full sensory experience of sitting beside Elias, feeling the cold seeping through her
clothes, seeing the unnatural stillness of his chest. When the memory came, it felt completely real
across all her senses at once, and she would feel the same terror she had felt in that moment, as if
she were still there, still helpless.

She had come to believe that her body was her enemy. Her heart would pound, her hands would shake,
and she would break into a cold sweat even when she was sitting safely in her own apartment, and
this physical reaction only deepened her sense that she could not stop bad things from happening to
her. That feeling of helplessness began to color everything. She lost faith in the idea that the
world was orderly or that people could be trusted to keep each other safe. Every reminder of
victims—a news report about an accident, a hospital show on television, a colleague mentioning a
funeral—would drag her back into that helpless state, and the more helpless she felt, the more
hypervigilant she became. She found herself watching doorways, scanning parking lots, checking the
locks on her apartment three and four times before bed. She had trouble falling asleep because her
mind would not stop running through scenarios of disaster.

Nadia’s job as an auditor required her to visit different client offices each quarter, but she had
begun to dread these assignments. She avoided unfamiliar places because she felt she could not
assess the danger in them—she did not know the exits, the stairwells, the people. When her
supervisor asked her to cover a new site across town, she made an excuse about a scheduling
conflict. The avoidance felt necessary, but it also meant that her world was shrinking. She spent
most of her time at home or at her regular office, and even there, she avoided the supply closet and
the break room because both were small, windowless, and locked from the inside. Being in a locked or
confined room had become unbearable. The sensation of a door clicking shut behind her was enough to
make her chest tighten, and that tightening would summon the memory of sitting trapped beside her
brother, unable to leave, unable to help.

The most difficult trigger was being in nature. She had stopped driving past the state park
entirely, but even a tree-lined street or a patch of woodland visible from a bus window could pull
her back into the full reliving of that afternoon. She would see the moss on the rocks, hear the
wind, feel the dampness of the ground through her jeans. The terror would come back, and with it the
thought that her body was betraying her again, that she was still in danger even though she was
blocks away from anything resembling a cliff.

What frightened her most was the unpredictability. She never knew when the memory would surface. A
locked door, a patch of green, a passing mention of someone who had been hurt—anything could trigger
it. And each time, the helplessness deepened. She had come to feel that no matter what she did, bad
things would happen to the people she loved, and there was nothing she could do to stop them. This
belief made her watchful, always scanning for threat, and the more she watched, the more she found
reasons to be afraid.

She finally sought help because she had started canceling visits with her remaining family members.
Her niece had asked her to come for dinner, and Nadia had said no without explanation. That night,
lying awake at three in the morning, she had realized she was afraid of loving anyone else, because
loving someone meant losing them. She did not know if therapy could change that. But she was tired
of being trapped in her own life, and she had come because she was afraid that if she did not, she
would end up completely alone.
