Aisha, a 71-year-old woman of Middle Eastern descent, still remembers the exact sound of the
shattering glass. It was a Tuesday afternoon in late autumn, and she had been working as a nanny for
a family in a quiet suburban neighborhood. She was pushing the youngest child, a two-year-old boy
named Leo, on the swing in the backyard when she heard the screech of tires and then a sickening
crunch from the street out front. She told the older children to stay put and ran around the side of
the house. What she saw was a crumpled bicycle, a delivery driver standing over a motionless body,
and the frantic, helpless screaming of a woman who had been walking her dog. The man on the ground
was a neighbor she had waved to that morning. He died at the scene. In the weeks that followed,
Aisha could not stop replaying the image of his hand, twitching once on the asphalt before going
still. She had not been able to do anything. She had not even known CPR. She told herself she should
have run faster, should have been there sooner, should have somehow prevented it. The thought that
she was inadequate, that she had failed to protect anyone, took root.

That sense of failure—that the people responsible had faced no consequences, that she herself was
somehow lacking—began to shape everything she did. She started to believe that if she had been a
better person, a more capable person, she would have known what to do. Because she felt so
inadequate, she began to go along with whatever others wanted, especially at work. When the mother
of the children she cared for asked her to take on extra hours or change her schedule, Aisha never
said no. She was terrified that if she displeased anyone, she would be seen as incompetent or,
worse, that her hesitation would put someone else in danger. This people-pleasing was a strategy to
feel safe, a way to control a world that now seemed full of unpredictable threats.

The world itself had become a minefield. Aisha found herself watching other people closely for signs
that they might harm her, scanning the faces of strangers in the grocery store, looking for the
flicker of aggression she now expected. She frequently looked behind her to check if someone was
following, a habit that made her feel constantly hunted. This hypervigilance was exhausting, but it
felt necessary. She was certain that danger was everywhere, and that the only way to stay safe was
to be on guard. This constant threat monitoring fed directly back into her negative appraisals.
Every time she caught herself checking behind her, she felt more afraid, and that fear confirmed her
belief that she was too weak to handle the world. The more she watched for danger, the more she
became convinced of her own inadequacy.

To manage the overwhelming fear, Aisha began to avoid the place where the accident happened. She
took a different bus route to work, adding forty minutes to her commute. She also tried hard not to
think about the trauma, pushing the images away whenever they surfaced, but they always came back,
especially at night. These maladaptive strategies—the avoidance, the suppression, the constant
pleasing of others—only deepened her sense of failure. She told herself that a strong person would
not have to avoid anything, that a capable person could just think about the event and move on. Her
inability to do so felt like proof of her brokenness. The harder she tried to suppress the memories,
the more inadequate she felt, and the more she believed she had failed.

The consequences of this cycle became visible over the months. She lost her job with the family

made her chronically late. She was now working for a different family, but the pattern was
repeating: she never said no to any request, and she was perpetually exhausted from the
hypervigilance. Her husband, a retired mechanic, noticed she had stopped going to their weekly
community gatherings. She told him she was just tired, but what frightened her most was the feeling
that she was shrinking, becoming smaller and smaller, unable to trust herself or the world around
her. What finally brought her to seek help was the morning she found herself standing in her own

was watching her. She stood there for ten minutes, her heart pounding, before she realized it was

that therapy would only confirm what she already believed: that she was too far gone, that she had
failed, and that she would never feel safe again.
