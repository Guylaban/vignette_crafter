Adjoa was seventy-one when it happened, though she often feels like she has been seventy-seven her
entire life since. She had been a politician for decades, a woman known for her steady voice and her
refusal to be intimidated, first in her home country and then in the United States after she and her
husband emigrated. The man who beat her was not a stranger; he was her lover, a man she had trusted
with the keys to her apartment and the passwords to her phone. The night it ended, he had grown
quiet during dinner, and she had mistaken his silence for tenderness. When she reached for his hand,
he struck her across the face with such force that she tasted blood before she felt the pain. She
remembers the wet sound, the sting, the way her glasses flew across the room, but the minutes after
that are a blur of broken images—a shoe on the carpet, the glint of a lamp, her own voice saying
something she cannot recall. She has never been able to piece together how she got from the dining
table to the bathroom floor, and that missing gap haunts her more than the blows themselves. Every
time she tries to complete the memory, a wall slams down inside her, and she pushes it away before
she can see what comes next.

In the months that followed, Adjoa found herself scanning rooms before she entered them, her eyes
darting to corners and doorways. She began to notice the way certain people raised their voices in
meetings, even calmly, and her chest would tighten before she could stop it. A colleague’s raised
eyebrow during a committee vote sent a spike of alarm through her that made no sense in the moment
but felt utterly real. She came to believe that she had been permanently damaged by that night, that
something essential in her had broken and could not be repaired. She told herself she would never
feel normal emotions again, that the part of her that had once laughed easily and trusted freely was
gone for good. This conviction made her hypervigilant: if she could not trust her own feelings, she
had to rely on constant scanning for danger, and so she began checking behind her as she walked to
her car, especially at dusk, when the shadows lengthened and the world felt less safe. She found
herself startling at the sound of a door closing too sharply, her heart hammering for minutes
afterward, and each time she felt that surge of fear, the memory of that night pushed closer to the
surface, demanding to be finished.

The weight of her mistrust grew heavy. She had believed, before the assault, that people were
fundamentally decent; now she was certain they could not be trusted, and this belief colored every
interaction. When a young aide approached her with a concern, Adjoa found herself bracing for an
attack, reading threat into the aide’s earnest tone. These moments of perceived danger triggered the
memory of her partner’s quiet anger before the first blow, and the guilt would rise—a hot, shameful
wave—because she had not seen it coming, because she had loved him, because she had stayed. She
began to avoid physical proximity whenever possible, stepping back from handshakes and standing at
the far end of conference tables. In her office, she kept her desk angled so that no one could
approach from behind, and she flinched when a staff member touched her shoulder to get her
attention. The sight of blood, even a paper cut, sent her reeling into a state of high alert, her
mind racing with fragments of that incomplete memory.

Her husband, a gentle man who had never raised a hand to her, noticed the changes. She stopped
wanting to walk with him in the evening, claiming fatigue. She stopped wanting to be touched at all.
He asked what was wrong, and she could not tell him—not because she did not trust him, but because
she was afraid that speaking the words aloud would make the damage permanent in a way she could not
undo. So she withdrew further, and the loneliness of that withdrawal confirmed what she already
believed: that she was changed for the worse, that she would never feel normal again. At night,
alone in the dark, she lay rigid, listening to every creak of the house, certain that someone was
coming. She felt the guilt press down on her chest, and she pushed the memory away again,
incomplete, unfinished, always waiting.

What finally brought Adjoa to seek help was a moment in a public hearing when a witness raised his
voice in frustration—not at her, not even loudly—and she felt her vision narrow and her breath stop.
She excused herself, walked to the restroom, and stood gripping the sink, staring at her own
reflection. She was afraid that she was losing the ability to do her job, the one thing she had
always been sure of. She was afraid that the missing pieces of that night would never come back, and
that she would spend the rest of her life jumping at shadows, waiting for a blow that had already
landed.
