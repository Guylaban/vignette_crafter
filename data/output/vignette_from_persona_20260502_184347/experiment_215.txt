finally opened the door, the boy was still gripping the scissors, and in the split second that he
lunged forward—not at Marcus, but toward the window—Marcus grabbed his arm. The boy’s wrist twisted.
The scissors went in. Elijah survived, but the damage to his hand was permanent. Marcus had been the
one holding the arm.

In the weeks that followed, the memory did not behave like a normal memory. It arrived as a full
sensory reliving—the squeak of the closet door, the smell of dust and cleaning fluid, the heat of
Elijah’s wrist in his palm, the wet sound of the blade entering flesh. The memory felt like it was
happening right now rather than something that was over, and when it came, Marcus would find himself
breathless, his heart slamming, his palms slick, his body responding as though he were back in that
hallway. He could not find words to describe what had happened; when his wife asked, he would go
silent, the scene playing behind his eyes while his mouth refused to form sentences.

The memory fed a growing sense of threat. Marcus began to have difficulty falling asleep because he
needed to stay alert—he would lie rigid in the dark, listening for footsteps, for the creak of a
door, certain that something terrible was about to happen. Strong negative feelings—shame, guilt, a
hot, useless anger at himself—flooded him whenever the memory surfaced, and they did not fade
quickly. He had strong physical reactions when something reminded him of the stressful experience:
the smell of a certain cleaning product could send him into a sweating, trembling state within
seconds.

thought: The event happened because of the way I acted. He had come to believe that he could not
trust himself to do the right thing, that his judgment was fundamentally broken. His life had been
destroyed by the trauma; he could no longer see a future that included the work he loved.
