At forty-six, Leilani had spent most of her adult life as a touring musician, her world built around
the steady rhythm of breath through a flute, the press of a crowd’s warmth, and the quiet hum of a
stage before a performance. That world collapsed two years ago, during a routine physical exam when
a doctor mentioned an irregularity in her lung function. Within weeks, she was diagnosed with a
life-threatening pulmonary embolism, and the sensation that defined her life—the easy, unnoticed
flow of air—became a source of terror. She remembers the exact moment in the hospital when she first
felt she could not get enough oxygen, the way her chest tightened and her mind went blank with the
certainty that she was dying. The medical team stabilized her, but something in Leilani never came
back.

In the months that followed, she began to notice that her body reacted before her mind could catch
up. She would be walking to the grocery store, and without any conscious thought, her heart would
hammer and her throat would close—the same suffocating feeling from that hospital bed. It was only
afterward that she realized she had smelled a faint antiseptic or heard a distant machine beep. This
bodily vigilance bled into her sleep; she would wake several times a night, not from a nightmare,
but from a jolt of alertness, her first waking thought a frantic check that she was still breathing.
She came to believe that only a handful of places were truly safe: her own bedroom, a specific
corner of her couch, and the back porch where the air felt open. Anywhere else, she was certain,
danger could ambush her.

These constant warnings from her body fed a deepening sense of inadequacy. Leilani began to tell
herself that her reactions since the event proved she was a lousy coper. She could not trust herself
to handle even a mild cold without spiraling into panic, and she started to doubt every decision she
made about her health. What frightened her most was the thought that she might not do the right
thing if another crisis came—that her own fear would paralyze her when she needed to act. And so,
because she believed she was fundamentally inadequate, she began to punish herself. She told herself
she deserved to suffer for what had happened, as if the illness were a moral failing. She also
caught herself thinking, again and again, about how different life would have been if she had never
gone to that exam, if she had ignored the cough, if she had stayed on stage and never walked into
that room.

Her avoidance became a rigid structure. She stopped going to hospitals entirely, canceling follow-up
appointments and refusing even a blood pressure check at the pharmacy. The sight of an ambulance on
the street sent her into a cold sweat, and she would cross to the other side of the road. Most
consequentially for her career, she stopped performing in any venue that had a medical station or a
paramedic on standby—which, as she learned, was nearly every concert hall she had ever played. She
turned down gigs, then stopped answering calls from her agent altogether. Her husband, a guitarist
who had toured with her for years, found himself playing solo sets while Leilani sat at home, her
flute case gathering dust in the corner.

The cycle tightened as her threat system kept her on high alert. Because she was always waking to
check her safety, and because her body reacted before she could think, she became exquisitely
sensitive to any physical sensation that mimicked breathlessness. A flight of stairs, a brisk walk,
even a yawn could trigger the full cascade of panic. And each time it happened, she told herself
again that she was a bad coper, that she could not be trusted, that she deserved the suffering. Her
punishment thinking grew more insistent, and her counterfactual reveries—what if she had never been
examined—became a daily ritual, a way to mentally escape a present she found unbearable.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was a simple moment: her husband came home from a gig, set
down his guitar, and told her he had been offered a residency in another state. She saw the hope in
his eyes, and she realized she could not follow him if he took it, because she no longer felt safe
anywhere but this house. The thought of losing him—of losing the last person who made her feel
anchored—was more terrifying than facing her own fear. In her first session, she sat with her hands
clenched in her lap and said, quietly, “I just want to be able to breathe again. Not because I’m
dying. Just because I’m alive.”
