Amina is a 20-year-old pharmacy student who came to the United States from East Africa three years
ago to study. She lives with her boyfriend, a quiet and supportive man she met during her first year
of college. For the past eighteen months, she has worked part-time as a pharmacist in a busy
downtown clinic, a job she initially loved for its precision and order. Now, she is barely holding
it together.

The event that changed everything happened two years ago, during a visit home to see her family.
Political violence had been escalating in her region, and one evening, armed men stormed her
village. They separated the men from the women, and Amina was taken with several other young women
to a compound near a river. For three days, she was held in a small room that flooded each night
when the rains came. The water rose to her chest, and she was forced to remain standing, pinned
against a wall, while men shouted at her in a language she barely understood. She watched two other
women be dragged out and never return. On the third night, she managed to escape by slipping through
a broken window as the guards argued among themselves. She fled through the floodwaters, certain she
would be shot in the back. She made it to a relative’s house and eventually back to the United
States, but she left behind everything she knew.

Now, water is everywhere. A glass of water on her nightstand makes her stomach clench. Walking past
a fountain in the clinic plaza, she feels her throat tighten and her legs go weak. The worst is the
rain. When she hears it start, she immediately checks the windows, then the door, then the windows
again. She has come to believe that if she does not stay alert, something terrible will happen
again. Arguments with her boyfriend—over something as small as whose turn it is to do the
dishes—send her into a spiral of panic. She cannot bear being told what to do, even gently. When he
asks her to take out the trash, she feels her skin prickle with a cold dread, as though she is being
forced into a corner. These triggers have reshaped her daily life. She has started working only day
shifts, because the dark and the quiet of the evening clinic make her feel too exposed. She avoids
walking past the small pond in the park near her apartment, taking a longer route that adds fifteen
minutes to her commute. And she cannot bring herself to watch any movie or news report that shows
water, flooding, or crowds.

The memories themselves are fragmented. Amina can recall the sound of the rain on the tin roof, the
cold weight of the water against her ribs, the shouting—but she cannot put the whole event into
words. When she tries to tell her boyfriend what happened, the sentences dissolve. She feels her
body lock into the same position she was in during those nights: back straight, arms pinned at her
sides, chest tight. In those moments, she is not in her apartment anymore; she is back in that room.
This disorientation terrifies her, and she has learned to push the memories away the moment they
surface. She tells herself that if she lets herself think about it, she will fall apart completely
and never recover. She also carries a deep, unshakable guilt for surviving when the other women did
not. She believes, on some level, that she should not have escaped, that she should have done
something to save them. This guilt makes her feel that she deserves to suffer, and she sometimes
catches herself thinking that the fear and sleeplessness are a kind of punishment she has earned.

Because of this, she is always on guard. She scans every room she enters for exits, noting the
nearest door, the windows, the fire escape. She cannot fall asleep until she has checked the locks
three times and positioned herself so she can see the bedroom door. Her sleep is shallow and broken,
and she often wakes in the early hours with her heart pounding, certain she has heard shouting. This
constant vigilance has made her jumpy at work. When a patient raises their voice in frustration, she
freezes, her mind blank, and she has to excuse herself to the bathroom to breathe. She has begun to
avoid the pharmacy’s break room because it has a large window that looks out onto a courtyard with a
decorative fountain. She eats her lunch at her desk instead, facing the wall.

The cycle feeds itself. The more she avoids—the dreams, the people who remind her of the compound,
the conversations about her past—the more fragmented and intrusive the memories become. The less she
sleeps, the more hypervigilant she feels. The more she scans for danger, the more she notices
potential threats, and the more convinced she becomes that she is not safe. She has started to
believe that she can never let her guard down, not even for a moment. Arguments with her boyfriend
have become more frequent, not because he is unkind, but because any hint of pressure or demand
triggers a wave of fear that she cannot explain to him. She has stopped going out with friends, and
she has begun to cancel shifts when the weather forecast predicts rain.

What finally brought her to seek help was a panic attack last week, in the middle of the pharmacy,
during a minor disagreement with a coworker about a prescription. She felt the room tilt, her vision
narrowed, and she had to sit down on the floor behind the counter. Her supervisor found her there,
shaking and unable to speak. She was sent home early, and that night, she told her boyfriend she
could not go on like this. She is most afraid that if she starts to talk about what happened, she
will never stop, or worse, that she will remember something she has blocked out and be destroyed by
it. But she is also tired—tired of the scanning, the avoiding, the guilt. She wants to feel like
herself again, though she is not sure who that is anymore.
