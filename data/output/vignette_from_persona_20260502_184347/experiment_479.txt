without violence. The younger officer shoved her against the hood of her own truck before she could

that way in her life. The incident lasted less than two minutes before the older officer waved it
off, apologized, and told her to go home. But something in her had locked into place.

In the months that followed, Mara found she could not stop replaying that moment. The memory was not
vague or fading; it was photographic, almost unbearably clear. She could see the grain of the
asphalt beneath her cheek, the scuff mark on the officer’s boot, the way the sunlight caught the
edge of his badge. When she remembered it—and she remembered it many times a day—she felt the
pressure of his arm on her back again, the heat of the hood, the helpless fury rising in her throat.

stayed on the boat until well past dark. She told herself that keeping busy was the only way to stay
sane, and for a while it worked. The more hours she spent hauling gear or reviewing logs, the less

finally sat down at her kitchen table, the memory would surge back with renewed force, as though her
mind had been saving it up. She would find herself going over and over why it had happened to

captain who commanded respect, who never doubted her right to stand her ground. That woman felt like
a stranger now.

too, unspoken, pressing against the inside of her ribs like the edge of a badge.
