Alicia is a 43-year-old chef who lives with her partner in a quiet suburban town. Two years ago, she
was diagnosed with a rare and aggressive form of bacterial endocarditis, a life-threatening
infection that attacked her heart valves. The illness began with what she thought was a bad flu —
fevers, chills, exhaustion — but within days she was in the ICU, septic and struggling to breathe.
She remembers the exact moment a doctor stood at the foot of her bed and told her she might not
survive the night. The memory returns to her now in full sensory reliving: the antiseptic smell, the
fluorescent hum, the cold plastic of the oxygen mask pressing against her face, and the sound of her
own heartbeat — fast, irregular, terrifying — as if it were happening all over again. The experience
was so extreme that sometimes, even now, she finds it hard to believe it actually happened to her.
When the memory surfaces, what overwhelms her most is not fear but a profound, crushing grief —
grief for the body she used to have, for the life she took for granted, for the woman who could work
a twelve-hour shift without a second thought.

In the months after her discharge, Alicia tried to return to her life. She went back to the
restaurant kitchen, but something had shifted irreversibly. Her body kept reacting as if she were
still in danger, even when she knew she was safe. A sudden clatter of pots, the hiss of a steam
valve, the sight of a coworker’s flushed face — any of these could trigger a flashback that left her
disoriented and shaking, her heart pounding before her mind had even registered the trigger. She
became convinced that her body had been permanently damaged by what happened, that she would never
be able to feel normal emotions again. This belief drove her to avoid anything that might contradict
it. She began to numb her feelings deliberately, pushing away both sadness and joy. She started
staying up until very late at night, delaying sleep because the moment she closed her eyes, the
sensory memories flooded back — the beeping monitors, the IV line tugging at her arm, the doctor’s
voice saying she might not make it. She also began avoiding positive events: she turned down
invitations from friends, stopped celebrating holidays, and refused to let her partner plan a
birthday dinner. What frightened her most was the idea that feeling happy or hopeful would only make
the next crash harder. If she let herself feel good, she reasoned, the grief would be even more
unbearable when it inevitably returned.

Alicia’s inability to relax became a constant companion. Even at home, in her own bed, with her
partner beside her, she lay rigid and alert, scanning her body for any sign of danger — a skipped
heartbeat, a flutter in her chest, a moment of dizziness. She had come to believe that her body was
an enemy, something that could betray her at any moment. This hypervigilance fed directly back into
her memory: every time she noticed a physical sensation, it triggered a flashback, and every
flashback reinforced her belief that she was still fundamentally unsafe. She stopped going to the
doctor for routine checkups because the sight of a stethoscope or the smell of antiseptic would send
her into a panic. The avoidance, in turn, deepened her conviction that her body was broken and that
she could never recover.

At work, her behavior changed noticeably. She used to thrive in the chaos of the dinner rush, but
now she found herself retreating to the walk-in cooler whenever the noise became too much. She would
stand among the cold shelves, breathing slowly, trying to convince herself that she was not back in
that hospital bed. Her partner noticed she had stopped laughing, stopped cooking at home, stopped
planning for the future. When he tried to talk to her about it, she shut down, unable to explain why
she felt so disconnected from everything she had once loved.

What finally brought Alicia to seek help was a moment of sudden clarity during a quiet Tuesday
shift. A delivery driver dropped a crate of tomatoes, and the sound — a wet, heavy thud — sent her
into a full flashback in the middle of the kitchen. She collapsed to her knees, gasping, certain she
was dying. Her sous chef called an ambulance, but by the time the paramedics arrived, Alicia’s
vitals were normal. She was not having a heart attack. She was having a panic attack. But in that
moment, lying on the cold kitchen floor surrounded by her coworkers, she realized she could not
continue like this. She was terrified that her body would fail her again, but she was equally
terrified of the life she was living — a half-life, hollowed out by fear and avoidance. She came to
therapy hoping to feel something real again, even if it meant facing the grief she had been running
from for two years.
