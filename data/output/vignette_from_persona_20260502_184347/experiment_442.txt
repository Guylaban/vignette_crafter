a war-torn region. His most traumatic memory is of a night when an airstrike hit a school he had
converted into a shelter. He had been distributing food when the building collapsed around him. He

later, he is a politician in a Midwestern city, and that smell still finds him. A passing car’s
exhaust, a neighbor’s grill, the faint tang of smoke from a distant fire—each one can send him back
to that moment. When that happens, the memory returns in fragmented pieces: the sound of a wall
groaning, a child’s shoe in the rubble, the heat on his face. He feels the same terror he felt then,
so overwhelming that he has to stop whatever he is doing.

Amir has learned to manage these intrusions. When he talks to strangers at fundraising dinners or
town halls, he notices his heart racing. He tells himself to stay calm, to control his emotions, and
he distracts himself by focusing on the next person in line. But the effort is exhausting. He has
thrown himself into work, taking on extra committees and late-night meetings, because keeping his
mind occupied keeps the memories at bay. Yet the silence of an empty office after hours feels
dangerous to him. He has come to believe that unusual silence means something terrible is about to
happen. He constantly scans his surroundings for signs of danger—a door left ajar, a shadow in the
hallway, a car idling too long outside the building. Before leaving his house each morning, he
repeatedly checks that the doors and windows are locked, sometimes three or four times.

The causal loops have tightened over the years. When a trigger—like a stranger’s comment about
military action—brings the memory rushing back, Amir’s sense of threat spikes. He becomes
hypervigilant, scanning the room for exits, and his need to control his emotions intensifies. He
will excuse himself, go to the bathroom, and breathe slowly until the feeling passes. But the very
act of controlling his emotions, of pushing the memory down, seems to make the next intrusion more
vivid. The more he distracts himself, the more the memory returns in disconnected, terrifying
pieces. And each time he checks a lock or scans a crowd, he is reminded of the fragility of safety,
which in turn makes the memory feel more present and more dangerous.
