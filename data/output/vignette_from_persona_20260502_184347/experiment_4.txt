Amir is a nineteen-year-old environmental scientist who was physically abused by his father
throughout his childhood. He cannot remember a time before the beatings, only the sharp,
unpredictable escalation—a raised voice, a slammed hand on a table—that would precede a blow. He
learned to read the subtle shifts in his father’s face: the tightening of the jaw, the narrowing of
the eyes, the slight tilt of the head that meant he was about to be struck. That hypervigilance
never left him. Now, as an adult, he scans every room he enters for an exit, and he always chooses a
seat with his back to a wall. He studies the faces and body language of everyone around him,
searching for the same warning signs he once relied on to survive.

Amir works in a small field laboratory with a team of five. The work is precise and collaborative,
and disagreements are common—about sampling protocols, data interpretation, shift schedules. For
Amir, every argument feels like the first step toward violence. When a colleague raises their voice
in frustration, his heart pounds and his vision narrows. He begins to scan their face for the
familiar signs of impending attack. The raised voice itself becomes a trigger, but so do the crying
of a coworker after a difficult call, or the unexpected touch of a hand on his shoulder when he is
bent over a microscope. Each of these moments activates the same threat system he developed as a
child.

In response, Amir has developed a set of strategies that he believes keep him safe. He avoids any
situation that might involve disagreement or confrontation, even when his professional opinion is
needed. He agrees to protocols he knows are flawed rather than risk a debate. He avoids being alone
in the lab or his apartment, because when he is alone, his mind replays the old scenes, and the
threat feels closer. Most of all, he avoids other people. He has stopped joining his team for lunch,
and he leaves the lab as soon as his shift ends, walking home quickly with his head down, scanning
the street for anyone who might approach him.

These strategies, however, have a paradoxical effect. By avoiding people, he has fewer opportunities
to test his predictions about safety, so his belief that others are dangerous remains unchallenged.
More concretely, his colleagues have noticed his withdrawal. When he avoids a team meeting, they
talk about his absence, and one of them—frustrated by his silence on a project—raises her voice at
him the next day. That argument, in turn, becomes a new trigger, reinforcing his sense that
confrontation is dangerous. His avoidance creates the very conditions that produce the triggers he
fears.

The most dominant loop in Amir’s life is this: a trigger—a raised voice, a crying coworker, an
unexpected touch—activates his threat system. He scans faces, positions himself with his back to a
wall, and feels unsafe until he can control his environment. This threat state then drives him to
avoid the people and situations that might produce the trigger again. But his avoidance, by
isolating him and creating social friction, makes it more likely that someone will confront him or
express frustration, which becomes a new trigger. The cycle tightens around him. He has come to
believe that he cannot trust anyone, that safety is a temporary condition he must constantly manage,
and that any moment of relaxation will be punished.

What finally brought Amir to seek help was a moment at work two weeks ago. A senior scientist,
unaware of Amir’s history, placed a hand on his shoulder to congratulate him on a successful
analysis. Amir reacted by flinching violently, knocking over a tray of samples. The silence in the
lab afterward was unbearable. He went home that night and told his wife he could not keep living
this way. He is most afraid that therapy will ask him to stop avoiding—that he will have to sit with
the threat, to let people touch him, to endure arguments without fleeing. But he is also afraid of
what will happen if he does not change. He is hoping, quietly, that someone can teach him a
different way to read a face.
