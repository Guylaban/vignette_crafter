surviving a period of torture at the hands of local gang members who suspected him of informing to
the police. For three days, he was held in a concrete room, beaten, and threatened with death while

neighborhood, forced him to watch as they hurt the man, and then told Jorge that the same would
happen to everyone he loved if he ever spoke to authorities again. After his release, Jorge fled
north, leaving behind his wife and young daughter; the divorce came two years later, when the
distance and his silence became too much for the marriage to bear.

Now Jorge works as a maid in a chain hotel, cleaning rooms on a floor where he rarely has to
interact with guests. The work is solitary, and that suits him. But certain things have become
nearly impossible to manage. When he sees a dark sedan in the parking lot that resembles the car his
captors used, his chest tightens and he feels a surge of panic that forces him to retreat into the
stairwell until the car is gone. He has also stopped watching television entirely after a news
segment about gang violence in Central America sent him into a shaking, tearful state that lasted
hours. Even positive emotions have become dangerous: a few months ago, a guest smiled warmly at him
in the hallway and thanked him for his work, and Jorge felt a confusing wave of dread that he could
not explain, only that the warmth felt like a trap, like kindness that might precede betrayal. That
evening, he smoked three cigarettes in a row on the fire escape, trying to calm the sense that
something bad was about to happen.

Jorge's sleep is broken. He wakes multiple times during the night, often sitting up in bed and
scanning the room for threats before he can orient himself to where he is. If his roommate brushes
against him accidentally in the dark, Jorge flinches violently, his heart pounding. He has come to
believe that strangers approaching him—even a guest asking for directions or a coworker walking
toward him in the break room—carry some hidden threat, and he has begun avoiding eye contact
entirely, keeping his head down as a way to stay invisible. These moments of fear, triggered by a
car or a face or a sudden touch, feed directly into his vigilance: each time he startles, he becomes
more convinced that danger is everywhere, and that he must stay alert or be caught off guard again.

daily. He avoids anyone in uniform—police officers, security guards, even hotel maintenance workers
in their blue shirts—because the authority they represent reminds him of the power his captors held
over him. He will change his route through the hotel to avoid passing the front desk if a security
guard is standing there. If a coworker brings up anything related to violence or crime, Jorge
quickly changes the subject or walks away, because being reminded of the event by others feels like
being dragged back into that room. The smoking has become a near-constant habit, a way to create a
small pocket of control when the world feels unsafe. But these strategies have a cost: the more he
avoids, the more he interprets his own behavior as proof that the world is dangerous. Each time he
retreats from a car or a uniform, the relief is temporary, and the fear settles deeper. He has
started to notice that his avoidance actually makes triggers appear more frequently—now every sedan
in the lot catches his eye, every uniformed figure seems to be walking toward him, and even a
guest's friendly smile feels like a warning sign he must decode.

The cycle has tightened over the past year. Jorge's manager has commented on his jumpiness, and his
roommate has asked why he smokes so much. Jorge has no answer that makes sense to them. What finally
brought him to the clinic was a morning two weeks ago when a guest—a middle-aged man in a dark
suit—approached him in the hallway and asked for extra towels. Jorge froze, dropped his cart, and
ran to the supply closet, where he stayed for twenty minutes, shaking and unable to breathe. After
that, he could not go back to work. He is afraid that he is losing his mind, and he is even more
afraid that if he does not find a way to stop the fear, he will lose the only job he has, and then
there will be nothing left.
