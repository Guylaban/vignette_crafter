Kofi is a 22-year-old school principal, a position he earned young through fierce determination to
build a life far from the home he grew up in. The traumatic event was not a single explosion but a
climate—years of physical abuse from his father, a man whose punishments were unpredictable,
escalating from a backhand across the mouth to a closed-fist blow to the ribs for a grade that was
not an A, for a chore left undone, for speaking too loudly at the dinner table. What stayed with
Kofi was not a clear narrative of any one beating but the texture of those years: the specific smell
of his father’s aftershave before a beating began, the way the floorboards creaked in a particular
pattern as his father approached his bedroom door. When he tries to deliberately recall what
happened, he finds the memory difficult to access, as if a wall slides down. Instead, the memories
come in disconnected, fragmented pieces—the glint of a belt buckle, the sensation of his own spit
tasting like blood—that feel more real and vivid than memories of ordinary things like his
graduation or his first day as a principal.

A few months ago, his girlfriend, Amara, bought a frame for a photo of them at a school event. When
she set it on the nightstand, Kofi saw the glass catch the light in a certain way. In that instant,
the fragmented piece was upon him: not the full memory, but the feeling of being small, pinned, the
glint of a buckle. The memory flooded in, feeling more real than the room he was standing in, and
with it came the old, familiar belief that he has permanently changed for the worse, that there is
something broken inside him that cannot be fixed. He pulled away from Amara and spent the rest of
the evening in the garage, certain that if he stayed in the room, he would not be able to control
his anger and would do something terrible—though he does not know what that terrible thing would be.
This fear of his own rage has grown into a conviction that he simply cannot stop bad things from
happening to him, that his own body is a ticking bomb.

The weight of these appraisals now shapes his daily life. Because he believes he is permanently
damaged and dangerous, he has begun to scan every situation for signs of unpredictability. At work,
as principal, he finds himself hypervigilant during assemblies or fire drills—any situation without
a rigid script. A child running in the hallway, a sudden loud noise from the boiler room, these
unpredictable moments now send a jolt through him, and in that jolt, the fragmented memories return:
the creak of the floorboard, the glint of the buckle. The memory then reinforces his belief that he
cannot stop bad things from happening, that he is still that helpless child, and the cycle tightens.

Kofi has started avoiding old photos altogether. He has asked Amara to keep them in a drawer. He
also dreads waking up from nightmares, because the dream residue—that lingering feeling of his
father’s presence—acts as a trigger, pulling him into a fragmented memory before his eyes are even
open. He has stopped sleeping in the same bed as Amara on those nights, telling her he has a
headache. The consequence is that she has started to ask, gently, if he is angry with her. He cannot
explain that he is not angry with her—he is terrified of himself.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a moment last week during a parent-teacher conference. A
father raised his voice, not at Kofi, but at a teacher. Kofi felt his vision narrow, his hands go
cold, and the fragmented piece of the buckle glint came back so vividly he had to excuse himself and
vomit in the bathroom. He is now sitting in the therapist’s office, afraid that he will be asked to
remember everything, but even more afraid that if he does not, he will lose Amara, or his job, or do
something terrible that he cannot undo.
