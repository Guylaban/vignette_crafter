escalating violence in the city where he had been serving a small, tight-knit Jewish community. He
was thirty-nine then, a young rabbi with a wife and two young children, and he had been warned that
his congregation was a target. One evening, as he walked home from evening prayers, a group of men
cornered him in an alley. One of them pressed the cold, hard muzzle of a pistol against the back of
his neck. They shouted at him in a language he understood only too well, their voices raw with
hatred. He was beaten, robbed, and left bleeding on the ground. What stayed with him, more than the
pain of the blows, was the absolute, paralyzing certainty that he was about to die, and the feeling
of that weapon’s metal against his skin. He survived, but the family fled the country within the
month, resettling in a quiet American suburb where he eventually rebuilt his life and his
congregation.

For years, David managed. He told himself the past was the past. But the triggers, when they began
to surface, were unignorable. A sudden, unexpected touch on his shoulder from a congregant after a
service would make him flinch so violently that he would knock over a chair. The sight of a long,
heavy flashlight in a hardware store, or a thick wooden rolling pin in a kitchen, would send a jolt
of cold dread through his chest. The worst, however, was shouting. A raised voice—even a child
yelling in play, even a spirited debate during a Torah study session—would make his heart hammer and
his vision narrow. In those moments, he would tell himself he deserved to suffer for what had
happened, a punishing thought that felt like an old, familiar payment for his own perceived failure
to have prevented the attack. To cope, he began to mentally recite the week’s parsha, or count the
panes of glass in the sanctuary window, anything to distract himself from the rising panic.

These moments of being triggered also began to corrode his sense of himself. He would think, with a
kind of grim certainty, that somebody else—a braver man, a stronger rabbi—would have stopped the
event from happening. This belief that he was fundamentally inadequate hardened into a deeper
conviction: he could not rely on himself. His own body, he came to feel, was an enemy, reacting as
if he were still in that alley even when he was safe in his own study. This feeling of betrayal by
his own flesh and mind made him hypervigilant. He found it difficult to concentrate during services,
his attention constantly scanning the exits and the faces in the back rows. He would choose his seat
in the synagogue’s social hall based entirely on how safe and protected he felt, always with his
back to a wall and a clear view of the door. The only place where the vigilance ever truly quieted
was in the small, windowless storage room off his office, a space he had come to think of as his
safe zone.

The perceived threat in the world around him began to feed back into his experience of the triggers.
Because he was always scanning for danger, a sudden touch felt not just surprising, but
confirmatory—proof that danger was everywhere. Because he could not concentrate, he missed the
subtle cues of a congregant’s approach, making every unexpected encounter a shock. The constant
state of threat also drove his maladaptive strategies deeper. He began to avoid emotional intimacy
with his wife, Rachel, pulling away from her embraces and deflecting her concerned questions. He was
certain that if she knew the depth of his fear, she would see him as the weak man he believed
himself to be. This avoidance, in turn, only reinforced his negative appraisals. Each time he
withdrew from her, he silently proved to himself that he was broken, that he could not rely on
himself to be a proper husband. The punishing thoughts grew louder. The cycle tightened.

What finally brought David to seek help was a single, small incident. A young man in his
congregation, trying to get his attention during a noisy Kiddush, placed a hand on his back and
shouted his name over the din. David spun around, his face pale, his hands shaking, and shouted
back, “Don’t touch me!” The room fell silent. He saw the hurt and confusion in the young man’s face,
and the pity in the eyes of his board members. That night, Rachel told him, quietly, that she could
not live like this anymore—not with his distance, not with his fear. He is now sitting in a
therapist’s office, terrified that he is beyond help, but more terrified of losing the life he has
spent twenty-three years trying to hold together. He is most afraid of what he might discover about
himself if he looks too closely at the past.
