Leilani he had fallen off a ladder. The shame was immediate and absolute. He had come to believe
that what happened had made him less of a man, and that if anyone knew—especially his wife—she would
see him as weak, as broken, as someone who had let it happen.

In the weeks that followed, the world began to narrow. The first thing he noticed was the alcohol
smell. A single whiff of beer on a client’s breath would send his stomach into a cold knot, and he
would find himself standing rigid on the edge of a roof, his hands shaking around a nail gun. Then
came the motorcycle noise. A Harley rumbling past at a traffic light would hit him like a punch—the
low, vibrating growl was the same sound that had filled the truck cab just before the man’s hand had
clamped over his mouth. Manaia began to flinch at the television, too. A news report about a sexual
assault trial, even one involving a stranger and a woman, would flood him with a heat so intense he
had to leave the room. These triggers did not just disturb him; they pulled the memory forward.
Sometimes, when the motorcycle passed, the whole scene would snap into his mind with a clarity that
felt more real than the roof he was standing on—the smell of the vinyl seat, the pressure of the
man’s knee against his thigh, the exact pitch of the man’s laugh. At other times, the emotional
intensity would arrive without any image at all: a wave of grief so sudden and total that he would
have to sit down, his eyes burning, unable to explain to his foreman why he had stopped working.

To manage this, Manaia developed a set of habits that he told himself were just discipline. He
started restricting his eating, controlling every meal with precision—the same three foods at the
same times every day—because the structure gave him a feeling of order in a world that felt
unmoored. He also began to avoid any physical stress that mimicked the sensations of the assault: he
stopped lifting weights at the gym, he refused to run, and on the job he would hand off the heaviest
bundles of shingles to younger men, claiming his back was sore. The thing that consumed most of his
energy, however, was keeping busy. He would take on extra shifts, volunteer for the dirtiest jobs,
and spend his evenings fixing things around the house until his hands ached. He told himself he was
being productive, but the real reason was that stillness felt dangerous. When he stopped, the grief
came. And when the grief came, he found himself scanning his environment for the next
trigger—listening for the rumble of an engine, checking the breath of the man next to him at the
hardware store, bracing himself for a news headline. The busier he stayed, the less often the memory
surfaced. But the strategy had a cost. The constant vigilance exhausted him, and the grief, when it
finally broke through, was worse each time, because he had been holding it at bay for so long. He
had also begun to withdraw from Leilani. She wanted to go out for dinner, but the restaurant might
have a bar, and the smell of alcohol was everywhere. He would say he was tired, and then lie awake
for hours, listening to the house settle, waiting for the next wave to hit.

What finally brought Manaia to a therapist’s office was a morning in late July. He had been on a
roof, the sun already brutal at eight a.m., and a delivery truck had backfired in the street below.

breath gone, his hands pressed flat against the shingles. His foreman had called his name three

something bad had happened to him years ago, and that he could not stop thinking about it. She held

sound of an engine that was waiting for him every day.
