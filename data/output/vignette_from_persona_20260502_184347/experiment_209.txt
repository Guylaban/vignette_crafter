Leilani began to feel the weight of it most acutely at dusk. The same time of day, around six in the
evening, her chest would tighten and her vision would swim. She would feel dizzy, lightheaded, as
though the floor were tilting. When that dizziness hit at dusk, she stopped what she was doing and
reached for her phone to buy something online, because the feeling of the floor tilting was
unbearable unless she drowned it out with a purchase. Each time dusk arrived and the dizziness rose,
she thought to herself that anyone else would have been over this by now, and the thought confirmed
what she already feared — that she was a lousy coper, fundamentally inadequate. She had come to
believe that her reactions since that night proved she was a lousy coper, that she was fundamentally
inadequate for not being able to shake it off. Each time the memory broke through — the door

broken, and that this proved something permanent about her. That belief — that she was a lousy coper

not accept. When she felt that dizziness rising, she was certain she was losing her mind, that she
was going crazy. It was in those moments, standing behind her register as the light faded, that she
would find herself reaching for her phone and buying something online—a new kitchen gadget she did
not need, a dress she would never wear. The spending was a way to distract herself, to feel a brief
flood of something other than fear.

As the weeks passed, Leilani began avoiding the store at closing time, asking her part-time employee
to handle the last hour. She avoided the anniversary of the event entirely, taking a flight to visit
her sister on the mainland, telling herself it was just a vacation. Not talking about it, not
telling the story to anyone, had kept the memory sealed and untouched — she had thought about not
thinking about it every day for months, and that effort had preserved it exactly as it was. She had
learned that if she kept moving, kept the television on, or bought something online, the memory
couldn't get a foothold — stillness was the enemy, and activity was the only way to keep the
fragments at bay. But the effort of keeping the event at a distance had done the opposite — the
memory remained sealed and untouched, and when it did surface, it came back with the same raw,
fragmented force as the first week, as though no time had passed at all. Not talking about it, not
telling the story to anyone, had kept the memory in its original form — unprocessed, unsoftened,
exactly as vivid as the night it happened. But the memory refused to stay buried. The story felt
unreal to her, too extreme to have actually happened, and yet she could not put it into words when
her sister asked. The event existed only as a series of fragments—the door, the hand, the floor—and
she could not string them together into a coherent narrative. This inability to talk about it
reinforced her sense that she was a bad coper, that anyone else would have been able to move on.
Because the memory came back in those vivid, wordless fragments, she began to see threads of it
everywhere—in the way a customer's hand moved near a counter, in the creak of a door opening, in the
particular shade of fluorescent light that reminded her of that evening.

The dizziness itself had become a trigger. Whenever she felt lightheaded—from standing up too fast,
from the heat—it pulled her back into that moment on the tile floor, and the memory would surge up,
vivid and wordless. She had begun to believe that being watched made it worse. If a customer
lingered near the counter, or if she noticed a security camera in a shop window, her stomach would
drop. She would leave the store abruptly, telling her employee she felt unwell, and walk home with
her head down. The avoidance made her feel temporarily safer, but it also deepened her conviction
that she could not handle normal life, that she was falling apart. Each time she asked her employee
to close the store or walked out of a shop, she added another piece of evidence to the case against
herself — that she was too fragile to live an ordinary life, that she needed crutches other people
didn't. Three months of avoiding the store at dusk had told her something she couldn't argue with:
that she must be more fragile than other people, or she would have been able to close up like she
used to. And the places she now avoided — the store at dusk, the grocery store aisle, any shop with
a security camera — had themselves become reminders; the long list of what she could no longer do
was its own daily map of what had happened that Tuesday evening. The long way she walked home to
avoid the shop with the security camera, the routes she no longer took, the hours she could no
longer work — all of it was a daily geography of the event, each detour a fresh reminder of what she
was running from. Each place she avoided became a trigger in itself — the long way she walked home
every evening was its own daily acknowledgement of what had happened that Tuesday. Each time she
asked her employee to close the store or walked out of a shop when she felt watched, she added
another piece of evidence to the case against herself — that she was too fragile to live an ordinary
life, that she needed crutches other people didn't. The spending, too, had a cost: her credit card
bills were climbing, and she had started hiding packages from her daughter, who visited on weekends.

By the time she sought help, Leilani could no longer ignore the pattern. She had stopped going to
the store altogether for three days, claiming she had the flu. What finally brought her to the
clinic was a single moment: she had felt dizzy in the grocery store, and for a split second, she had
been certain she was about to be thrown to the ground again. She left her cart in the aisle and
walked out. She was afraid of what she might do next—how much further she would retreat, how much
more she would spend—and she was desperate for the memory to stop feeling so unreal, so that she
could finally believe it was over.
