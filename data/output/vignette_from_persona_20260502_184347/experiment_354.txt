ignited beneath her car. The flames came from the dashboard vents first—a sudden, acrid smell of

her face, the way her hands flew up to cover her eyes, the certainty that she was about to die. She
escaped with only minor burns, but the smell never left her. Three years later, that burnt odor
still arrives without warning—a whiff from a passing truck, the scorch of a toaster, the ghost of
smoke in a winter coat—and Mara is right back in the driver’s seat, heart hammering, palms pressed
to her cheeks as if the flames are still there.

At first, she tried to manage it by force. Whenever the smell came, she would tell herself it was
fine, she was safe, she needed to think only positive thoughts. She would push the memory down and
reach for food instead—whole sleeves of crackers, a second dinner, anything to fill the space the
fear left hollow. But the reframing never held, and the eating only made her feel more out of
control. She started covering her face with her hands whenever she caught a burnt scent, a reflexive
gesture she could not stop, even in synagogue. A congregant once touched her shoulder to ask if she
was alright, and Mara flinched so violently she knocked a stack of prayer books to the floor. The
startle response was humiliating. She became irritable, snapping at staff members for small things,
and she noticed herself taking risks she never would have before—driving too fast, walking through
parking lots at night without looking—as if daring the world to try again.

become—jumpy, brittle, hiding behind her hands—was unrecognisable. She began to believe her own
flesh had turned against her, that it kept sounding alarms long after the danger was gone, and that
there was no one who could understand. She felt isolated from her congregation, from her colleagues,
from the woman she used to be. That isolation only sharpened her vigilance. Every flicker of a
lighter, every whiff of smoke, every unexpected touch became a fresh confirmation that she was still
unsafe. She would wake from nightmares drenched in sweat, the smell of burnt plastic clinging to the
air of her bedroom, and lie there certain that the fire was coming again. The dreams left a residue
that followed her through the day, a low hum of dread that made her scan every room for exits and
flinch at the hiss of a radiator.

The cycle tightened. The more she covered her face, the more she felt weak and ashamed. The more she
ate to soothe herself, the more she felt her body had betrayed her. The more irritable she became,
the more she pushed people away, and the more isolated she felt, the more hypervigilant she grew.

letting anyone sit too close during services. Her congregation noticed. They asked if she was ill.
She said she was fine. She forced herself to think only positive thoughts. And then she went home
and ate until her stomach hurt, haunted by a smell that was not there.

what will happen if she stops covering her face, if she lets herself feel the fear without forcing
it away. But she is more terrified of spending the rest of her life hiding from a smell that no one
