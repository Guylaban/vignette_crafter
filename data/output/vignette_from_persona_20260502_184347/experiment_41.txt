space Kofi had entered willingly, believing they were meeting to discuss a collaborative research
project. The door clicked shut automatically behind him, and within moments, the conversation

still as his mind scrambled to make sense of what was happening. Afterwards, he dressed in silence,
walked out of the room, and flew home the next morning without telling anyone.

In the weeks that followed, Kofi found himself unable to sit in his own office at the community
mental health center where he worked. The room had a door that locked from the inside, and every
time he closed it to begin a session, he felt his chest tighten and his breathing grow shallow. He
began leaving the door slightly ajar, then propping it open with a chair, then eventually asking a
colleague if he could use a shared space instead. He told himself it was about ventilation, about
needing more light. But he knew the truth: being in a locked or confined room made him feel like he
was back in that hotel, and he had come to believe that he could not stop bad things from happening
to him, that his body and his will were not his own. This sense of helplessness became the lens
through which he saw everything. He felt that others could see what had happened to him just by
looking at him, as though the assault was written on his skin. He started avoiding eye contact with
male clients, especially those who reminded him of the colleague, and he began scheduling his
sessions so that he was never alone in the building with another man. What frightened him most was
the certainty that he had no future—that the life he had been building, the career he had worked
for, was already over.

These negative appraisals fed directly into a state of constant threat. Kofi could not relax, even
in places he logically knew were safe. At home, he slept on the couch with his back against the
armrest, facing the door, because he felt unsafe unless his back was against a wall where he could
see the room. He had difficulty falling asleep because he needed to stay alert, his ears tuned to
every creak and footstep. The vigilance was exhausting, but he could not let it go. He was certain
that if he let his guard down, something else would happen. And this vigilance, in turn, reinforced
his belief that he was fundamentally exposed and helpless. The more he scanned for danger, the more
dangerous the world felt. He began to withdraw from social life entirely, declining invitations from
friends and canceling plans at the last minute. His supervisor noticed that he seemed distracted and
asked if everything was all right. Kofi said he was tired, and left it at that.

The threat also intensified his sensitivity to triggers. One afternoon, a female colleague mentioned
a news article about sexual violence in the workplace, and Kofi felt his stomach drop. He excused
himself to the restroom and stood with his hands on the sink, breathing slowly, trying to push the
memory back down. Comments on sexual trauma cues had become unbearable. They pulled him into the
moment of the assault with a vividness that felt like reliving it. He also found himself
increasingly uncomfortable being alone with men, even those he had known for years. He started
timing his arrivals and departures to avoid walking through the corridor at the same time as male
coworkers. He knew this was changing how he worked, how he lived, but he could not see a way out. He
had begun to believe that the assault had not only happened to him but had also revealed something
true about the world—that safety was an illusion, and that he would never feel it again.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a session with a new client, a man who spoke in a soft,
measured tone that reminded him of the colleague. Kofi felt his hands go cold, his mind go blank,
and for a full thirty seconds he could not speak. He ended the session early and sat in his car in
the parking lot, shaking. He realized he could not keep doing this—could not keep pretending that
the door was open for ventilation, could not keep arranging his life around a terror he refused to
name. He was most afraid that therapy would not work, that the hopelessness was correct, that his
future really was gone. But he was also afraid of what it might mean if he started to feel safe
again—whether that would mean forgiving, or forgetting, or betraying what had happened. He came to
the first session wanting to know if it was possible to live without the wall at his back, and not
knowing if he was ready to find out.
