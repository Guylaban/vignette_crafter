Sister Margaret had spent forty-two years in the convent, most of them teaching third grade at St.
Catherine’s, but nothing in her life had prepared her for the morning Sister Anne collapsed in the
garden. It was late October, and they had been pruning the rose bushes together, Margaret holding
the clippers while Anne reached for a tangled branch. The sound Anne made was not a cry but a soft
exhale, and then she was on the ground, her face the color of old linen. Margaret knelt beside her,
fumbling for a pulse, and in that moment she knew—knew with a certainty that felt like judgment—that
she had done everything wrong. She had hesitated before calling for help, had wasted precious
seconds trying to remember the number for the parish office instead of dialing 911 immediately. The
ambulance came, but by the time they reached the hospital, Anne had suffered a massive stroke. She
survived, but she would never speak again, never walk, never tend her roses. Margaret replayed those
seconds endlessly, and each time she reached the same conclusion: the event happened because of the
way she acted. She had failed to protect someone she loved, and that failure meant something
fundamental about her—she could not rely on herself. These thoughts burrowed into her like
splinters, and the more she tried to pull them out, the deeper they went.

The memory of that morning was both brutally clear and strangely fragmented. Some moments—the feel
of Anne’s wrist under her fingers, the exact shade of yellow on the fallen rose petals—were so vivid
they felt like photographs she could hold. But other moments were entirely blank: she could not
remember dialing the phone, could not remember what she said to the dispatcher, could not remember
who drove her to the hospital. The gaps terrified her because they confirmed what she already
believed: her mind was unreliable, just as her actions had been. And then there were the times the
memory came without warning. A flash of yellow in a parishioner’s garden, the scent of damp earth
after rain, the sound of a woman’s soft laugh—any of these could drop her back into that garden, her
hands shaking, Anne’s body still on the ground. Each time it happened, she felt the old certainty
return: you failed, you failed, you failed.

Margaret began to avoid the garden entirely, taking a long route around the convent to the back door
so she would not have to see the rose bushes. She stopped walking past the hospital on her errands,
even if it meant adding twenty minutes to her trip. But the avoidance spread further. Anything that
reminded her of Anne—a particular hymn, the smell of lavender soap, the sight of someone
pruning—became a trigger she had to navigate. She began to perform small mental rituals, counting
silently to seven whenever she passed a certain corner, repeating a prayer under her breath whenever
an intrusive image surfaced, because she had come to believe that if she did not, something worse
would happen. She knew, in the rational part of her mind, that these rituals made no sense, but the
fear that followed each unbidden memory was so sharp that the rituals felt like the only thing
standing between her and disaster. And each time she performed one, she saw more clearly how broken
she was, how incapable of trusting herself, and this only deepened her conviction that she had
failed Anne because of some flaw at her core.

The rituals and avoidance did not stop the memories; they seemed to sharpen them. The more she tried
not to think about that morning, the more clearly she saw Anne’s face, the more the blank spots in
her memory felt like accusations. She found herself unable to concentrate during evening prayers,
her mind snagging on the same loop of images and self-blame. Her community noticed her distance, but
she could not explain it—what would she say? That she had killed Sister Anne through her own
incompetence? That she could not be trusted with anything important anymore? The silence itself
became another ritual, another way of trying to control a world that felt dangerous.

What finally brought her to seek help was a Sunday in February, when she was asked to lead the
congregation in a prayer for the sick. She opened her mouth and could not speak. The words were
there, but her throat closed, and all she could see was Anne’s face on the hospital pillow. She fled
the chapel, and later that evening, she called the diocesan counseling line. She was afraid—afraid
that talking about it would make the memories worse, afraid that she would be judged as she judged
herself—but she was more afraid of what she was becoming. She hoped, though she could barely admit
it, that someone might show her a way out of the loop she had been running for months, that there
might be a version of herself who could look at a rose bush and feel nothing but peace.
