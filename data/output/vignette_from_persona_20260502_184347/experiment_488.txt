against the industrial dryer. He shouted at her, his voice filling the small, windowless space,
demanding she do things she did not want to do. Leila remembers the pressure of his hand on her
shoulder, the smell of bleach from a tipped bottle, and then nothing—a blank space where the next
minutes should be. She remembers the flash of pain in her ribs as she was shoved against the
machine, and then she was on the floor, the door open, the man gone. The memory is a series of
sharp, disconnected images: his red face, the white of his knuckles, the pain in her side. She
cannot stitch them into a single, coherent story, and the gaps feel more threatening than the
fragments she does hold.

In the weeks that followed, Leila found herself unable to stop thinking about the moments she did
remember. The physical pain would return without warning—a sharp stitch in her ribs when she bent to
pick up a dropped towel, or a phantom pressure on her shoulder when she reached into a closet. These
sensations were the most vivid parts of the memory, and they would trigger a wave of fear that felt
as immediate as the day it happened. The memory, incomplete as it was, had become a trap. The
partial clarity of the shouting and the pain made her feel like the event was still happening, and
she began to notice that certain things would pull her back into it: the sound of a raised voice in
the hallway, a door slamming, a client telling her to clean a spot she had missed. Being told what
to do, even politely, felt like a violation. The triggers were everywhere.

Because the memories were so fragmentary, Leila started to feel that she had to be constantly alert
to fill in the blanks. She could not trust her own recollection, so she began scanning her
surroundings for any sign of danger, any clue that might warn her before another assault. She would
listen for footsteps in the hallway, for the creak of a door, for the sound of a key turning in a
lock. At work, she could not concentrate on her tasks. She would stand in a living room, sponge in
hand, straining to hear if anyone was approaching, and she would lose track of what she was
cleaning. The constant scanning for danger made the triggers more potent, not less. Every shout she
heard, every command from a resident, felt like a confirmation that she was not safe. The threat she
was looking for seemed to find her.

Over time, Leila’s vigilance became a cycle she could not escape. She would hear a raised voice in
the next apartment, and the memory of the shouting would flash in her mind, incomplete but
terrifying. That memory would send her back into scanning mode, her heart pounding as she listened
for more sounds. The more she scanned, the more she noticed—a door that was left ajar, a man walking
too close to her in the stairwell—and each new trigger fed the sense of threat. She began to avoid
the laundry room entirely, even though it meant carrying heavy bags of linens to a laundromat three

father had a loud voice. Her income shrank, and her husband began to ask why she was turning down
work. Leila could not explain it in a way that made sense to him. She just knew that every time she
felt forced into something, every time she heard shouting, every time she saw a news report about
another woman being attacked, she felt the pain in her ribs and the terror of the blank spaces in
her mind.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning last month when she could not leave her bedroom.

of someone entering the apartment, her body braced for a blow that did not come. She is afraid of
how small her world has become, and she is terrified that if she tries to remember the missing parts
of the assault, she will find something even worse waiting there.
