the restaurant where he worked. The assault happened on a Tuesday night, after a late shift. A

locked the door. Marcus had not screamed. He had not fought. He had stood there, frozen, as the
world he had built around himself—his kitchen, his reputation, his sense of being a man who could
handle anything—collapsed into a single, unbearable moment.

manager, he told the police, he told his ex-wife when she called to check on him. The words came out
smooth and practiced, as if he were reciting a recipe. But privately, something was wrong. He could
not put the memories of the event in the right order. He would recall the click of the lock, then
the feel of the cold metal shelf against his back, then the sound of his own breath—but not the
sequence that connected them. And every time he tried to force the memories into a timeline, he
found himself pushing the memory away before he could fully recall it. It was like grabbing at
smoke. His mind would skate to the edge of the image and then veer off, leaving him frustrated and
hollow.

What Marcus could not shake was the feeling that he was no longer capable of doing the things that
defined him. He had always been the one who could read a kitchen, anticipate every order, keep
twelve stations running smoothly. Now, when a ticket came in during a rush, his hands would pause.
He would stare at the paper, and a voice inside him would whisper: *You couldn't even protect
yourself. What makes you think you can run a line?* He felt dirty or contaminated because of what
happened, as if the walk-in cooler had left a residue on his skin that no shower could wash off. He
began to believe that his life had been destroyed by the trauma. Not just altered—destroyed. The
future he had imagined, the quiet pride of being a chef who had earned his place, felt like a
photograph of someone else.

the assault and, almost immediately, the memory would slip into a fog of shame. *I didn't fight. I
didn't even yell.* The sequence would blur, and he would push the whole thing away, unable to hold
it in his mind. The more he told himself he was contaminated, the less he could bear to let the
memory surface fully. It was as if his own story had become toxic to him.

Marcus adjusted his life around this new reality. He stopped cooking at home. He stopped
experimenting with new dishes. At work, he avoided the walk-in cooler entirely, sending junior staff
to fetch what he needed. He stopped eating with the team after service, claiming he had paperwork to
do. He began to drink more, a glass of wine that became two, then three, after every shift. His
hands, once steady and quick, now trembled slightly when he plated a dish. His sous-chef noticed.
His manager noticed. Marcus told them he was fine.

What finally brought him to seek help was a night three weeks ago when a ticket came in for a dish
he had invented—a seared scallop with a citrus beurre blanc that had been his signature for years.

sat in the fridge behind him, but he could not make himself turn around. He walked out of the
kitchen instead, took off his apron, and sat in the alley behind the restaurant until the cold air
stopped his hands from shaking.

He is afraid that if he lets himself remember the event fully, he will not survive it. He is hoping,
quietly, that someone can show him how to hold it without being destroyed.
