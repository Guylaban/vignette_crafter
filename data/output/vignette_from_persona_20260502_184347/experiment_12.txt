Elena, a 59-year-old project manager, had spent thirty years coordinating complex timelines and
teams, but nothing in her professional life had prepared her for the three days she spent locked in
a supply closet. It happened during a routine site visit to a subcontractor’s warehouse. A
disgruntled former employee, armed and erratic, had herded her and two colleagues into a windowless
room barely six feet square. The door was bolted from the outside. Elena remembers the sound of the
metal latch sliding home, the absence of light, the pressure of another body against her shoulder,
and the way the ceiling seemed to lower with each hour. She could not sit, could not stand fully,
could not open the door. There were no options. She was trapped.

In the months that followed, Elena returned to work, but the world had changed shape. What
frightened her most was that she could no longer trust the feeling of safety. At home, in her own
apartment, she would find herself standing with her back to a wall, scanning the room for exits. She
was unable to relax even when she knew rationally that no one was coming. Her neck, which had been
wrenched during the hours of standing hunched, throbbed with a dull, persistent ache, and that pain
became a signal. When her neck tightened during a long meeting or while driving in traffic, she
would feel a sudden rush of alertness, as though the walls were closing in. A locked conference room
door at work, even one she had closed herself for privacy, would make her chest seize. She had come
to believe that you can never know who will harm you, and that belief lived in her body before her
mind could catch up.

Elena began to dwell on what she had done wrong. She went over and over the details of that
afternoon—should she have refused the site visit, should she have noticed the man’s mood earlier,
should she have fought instead of frozen. She told herself, sometimes, that the event had not really
been that bad, that she was overreacting, that other people had survived worse. But the self-blame
rumination only deepened her conviction that she was now fundamentally broken. She used to be a
happy person, she thought, but now she was always miserable. She felt dead inside, hollowed out, as
though the woman who had once planned vacations and laughed at dinner parties had been left in that
closet.

The more she told herself it was not that bad, the more she needed to make sense of it, and the more
she made sense of it, the more dangerous the world seemed. She began to react as if she were in
danger even when nothing was actually wrong—a sudden noise in the hallway, a colleague’s hand on her
shoulder, the click of a door latch. Each false alarm reinforced her vigilance. She could not relax
even when she knew she was safe, because she was no longer sure she knew what safe felt like.

Her project manager role became a minefield. She started avoiding meetings in rooms without windows,
and when she could not avoid them, she sat nearest the door, her neck rigid, her attention split
between the agenda and the latch. She began leaving work early, claiming headaches, and her team
noticed her distance. She stopped accepting new assignments that required site visits. She told
herself she was being careful, but the avoidance fed the numbness, and the numbness fed the fear.
She was certain that if she let her guard down, something would happen again. The cycle tightened
until she could not remember a single hour in which she did not feel watched or trapped.

What finally brought Elena to seek help was a morning at home when she could not bring herself to
unlock the bathroom door. She stood inside, hand on the lock, and felt the old panic rise—not
because she was locked in, but because she was locking herself in. She called a therapist that
afternoon. What frightened her most was the thought that she might never feel the way she used to,
that the happy person she remembered was gone and would not come back. But she was also, somewhere
beneath the vigilance, hoping that maybe she was wrong.
