At fifty-six, Leo had spent over three decades as a professional dancer, first with a contemporary
company and later as a freelance choreographer and instructor. The accident happened during a
rehearsal for a small, experimental piece. He had been demonstrating a lift, a partner work sequence
he had performed hundreds of times, when his partner, a younger dancer named Elena, lost her
balance. He tried to catch her, but his hand missed her wrist and caught her shoulder at an odd
angle, and she fell hard, her head striking the edge of the wooden stage. He heard the sound of the
impact. There was a lot of blood. The paramedics came, and at the hospital they said she had a
fractured skull and a subdural hematoma. She survived, but she had to stop dancing. Her career was
over.

In the weeks that followed, Leo could not stop replaying the moment — the sound, the angle of her
body, the way his hand had failed. He became convinced that he should have done more, that he had
failed in his most important role: to protect his partner, to keep her safe. He told himself that a
real dancer, a real man, would have caught her. He would never forgive himself for not doing more to
stop what happened, even though he knew, logically, that it had been a split-second mistake. These
thoughts came most powerfully when he heard certain songs — the track they had been rehearsing to,
or any music with a similar rhythm. The melody would start in a coffee shop or a grocery store, and
he would feel the guilt rise up in his chest, hot and immediate, as if the accident were happening
again. The guilt, in turn, made him more alert to those sounds; he began scanning for them, bracing
himself before entering any room with a speaker.

He tried hard not to think about the trauma. He suppressed the images, pushed the thoughts away, but
the effort left him exhausted and on edge. He could not relax, even at home in his own living room,
where he knew he was safe. He had difficulty falling asleep because he felt he needed to stay alert,
as if something bad might happen if he let his guard down. His concentration suffered; he would lose
the thread of a conversation or find himself staring at a page without reading it. He began to avoid
going anywhere near the theater where the accident had happened, taking a longer route to the studio
where he still taught, a route that added twenty minutes to his commute. He dwelled on how he used
to be before the event — the dancer who trusted his body, who moved through crowded rehearsal spaces
with ease. Now, crowded spaces felt dangerous, overwhelming; he could not predict where people would
be, and he found himself flinching when someone moved too quickly near him. This hypervigilance only
made the crowded studio feel more threatening, and he would arrive already tense, scanning the room
for potential hazards.

The cycle tightened over months. His guilt told him he was a failure, and so he watched for signs
that he might fail again — a student losing balance, a dancer stumbling, a car in the parking lot
moving too fast. He had come to believe that if he was not constantly vigilant, he would cause
another injury, and that belief drove his avoidance and his suppression, which left him more
agitated and more sensitive to any trigger. He stopped accepting new choreography commissions. He
turned down a teaching residency. His wife told him he seemed distant, that he was not sleeping,
that he was always on edge. He knew she was right, but he did not know how to stop. What finally
brought him to seek help was a moment in class: a student wobbled during a turn, and Leo froze. He
could not speak. He stood there, heart pounding, for what felt like a full minute, while the class
watched him. Afterward, he went home and told his wife he needed to see someone. He was most afraid
that he would never trust his own body again, and that he had already lost the person he used to be.
