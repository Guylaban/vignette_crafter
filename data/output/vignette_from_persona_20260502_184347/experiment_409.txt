Samuel is a 63-year-old journalist, married, and originally from West Africa. He came to therapy at
his wife’s urging, though he admits he has been struggling for months. The event that haunts him is
not his own injury but the slow, incomprehensible death of his younger brother, Kwame, in a hospital
in Accra two years ago. Samuel had flown home when Kwame’s condition worsened after a routine
surgery turned septic. For three days, Samuel sat by the bed, listening to the doctors and nurses
speak rapidly in Twi and English, their words blurring into a frantic, incomprehensible chatter that
he could not parse. He held Kwame’s hand as his brother’s breathing became shallow, and in the final
hour, a metallic, coppery taste filled Samuel’s own mouth—a taste that returns to him now, unbidden,
whenever a memory surfaces. What devastates Samuel most is that he cannot tell the story properly.
When his wife asks what happened, or when a colleague offers condolences, Samuel opens his mouth,
but the thread vanishes. He knows exactly what he saw and felt, but the words scatter like startled
birds. He loses the thread, trails off, and is left standing there, mute, as if the event itself has
stolen his language.

The triggers are everywhere, and each one pulls him back into that hospital room. Talking about the
future—his editor mentioning an upcoming assignment, his wife discussing retirement plans—now feels
unbearable. Samuel has come to believe that planning ahead is a kind of arrogance, a tempting of
fate, because Kwame had been planning for his own recovery just days before he died. Similarly,
overhearing conversations about loss, whether in the newsroom or at a dinner party, makes Samuel’s
chest tighten. But the most potent trigger is incomprehensible chatter. In the newsroom, when two
reporters speak over each other in rapid bursts, or when he walks through a crowded market and hears
overlapping languages he cannot quite catch, the taste of metal floods his mouth. That taste is the
first sign that the memory is coming. The chatter does not just remind him of the hospital; it
*causes* the memory to break through. He will be standing at his desk, the noise around him rising,
and suddenly he is back in that sterile room, Kwame’s hand in his, the doctors’ words dissolving
into sound.

This has reshaped his daily life. Samuel now avoids the newsroom’s open-plan floor, preferring to
work from a quiet corner where he can control the noise. He has stopped accepting assignments that
require interviews in crowded spaces. His editor has noticed, and Samuel has already been passed
over for a major feature. He tells himself it is temporary, but he is also certain that if he cannot
explain what happened, no one will understand why he has changed. What frightens him most is the
growing silence between him and his wife. She wants to talk about their future, about travel, about
grandchildren, but every time she brings it up, Samuel feels the taste rise, and he cannot answer.
He came to therapy because he is afraid that if this continues, he will lose her too—and he cannot
find the words to tell her why.
