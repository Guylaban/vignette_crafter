had come into her store twice before, looking at rings he never bought—stepped out from the alley
beside her building. He grabbed her wrist, twisted it until she dropped her keys, and pressed
something hard against her ribs. She remembered the smell of his jacket, damp and metallic, and the
way his voice was almost gentle when he told her not to scream. What she could not remember, no
matter how hard she tried, was how she got from the alley to the ground. There was a gap there, a
complete blank, and then a sudden fragment: the concrete against her cheek, the sound of his
footsteps running away. She had pushed the memory back so many times that it arrived now in pieces,
unbidden—a flash of his hand on her arm, a wave of nausea that came from nowhere, and then nothing
again.

In the weeks that followed, Lena found herself unable to shake the feeling that she was
contaminated. She showered twice a day, sometimes three times, but the sensation of his grip
lingered on her skin. She told herself that her reaction was proof of weakness—that a stronger
person would have fought back, would have screamed, would not have frozen the way she did. This
belief that she was a lousy coper became a kind of background hum, and it made the world feel more
dangerous. If she could not trust herself to handle a threat, then every stranger approaching her on
the sidewalk was a potential catastrophe. She began to scan for exits as soon as she entered any
room—the coffee shop, the post office, even her own apartment—and she felt safe only in the narrow
space behind her workbench at the shop, where she could keep her back to the wall and her eyes on
the door.

The triggers were everywhere. Seeing the offender was the worst—she had spotted him once, three
blocks from her shop, and her stomach had turned inside out. She had ducked into a doorway and
waited, trembling, until she was sure he was gone. But even without seeing him, the nausea came when
she least expected it: a customer talking about someone losing a loved one, a news report about a
robbery, the smell of damp wool on a rainy day. Each time her stomach lurched, the memory fragments
surged back—the pressure of his hand, the blank space in her mind—and each time, she felt more
convinced that she was falling apart. The nausea itself became a signal that danger was near, and so
she started watching for it, bracing herself, which only made the world feel more saturated with
threat.

Her scanning for exits became automatic, but it also kept the memory alive. Every time she checked a
doorway or mapped an escape route, she was reminded of the alley, of how she had not seen him
coming. The threat monitoring fed the intrusions, and the intrusions fed the threat monitoring,
until Lena could not tell which came first. She started avoiding the street where her shop was
located, taking a longer route to work that added twenty minutes to her commute. She stopped going
to the grocery store after dark. She told her friends she was too busy to meet for coffee, because
the cafe had too many corners and too many strangers and she could not breathe until she was back
behind her workbench, where the only things that mattered were the weight of a stone and the steady
pressure of her tools.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning in early January, when she found herself unable
to unlock her shop door. She stood on the sidewalk, keys in hand, and the nausea rose so fast that
she vomited into the gutter. A customer asked if she was okay, and Lena could not answer. She was
certain that she was broken, that her reactions had proven she was a lousy coper, but she was also
terrified that if she did not find a way out of this cycle, she would lose the only place she still
felt even remotely safe. She came to therapy because she was desperate to feel clean again, and
because she could no longer bear the weight of scanning every room for a threat that might never
come—but that she could not stop looking for.
