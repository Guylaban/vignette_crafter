accountant. It was a Tuesday evening, still light out, and she had just crossed the parking lot of
her apartment complex when a man came up fast behind her. She never saw his face, only felt his hand
clamp over her mouth and the hard shove of his other hand against her back, forcing her to the
ground. Her knee hit the concrete. She tasted blood from where she had bitten her cheek. In the
seconds that followed, he took her purse and was gone, but what stayed with her was not the loss of
her wallet or her phone. What stayed was the feeling of his body against hers, the pressure of his
hand, and the taste of iron in her mouth that she could still summon months later, as if her tongue
remembered what her mind wished it could forget.

In the weeks after, Leilani began to notice the world differently. She had always considered herself
a practical person, but now she found herself scanning every street corner before she turned it, her
shoulders tensing at the sound of footsteps behind her. She could not trust safety, even when her
husband assured her their neighborhood was fine. The world, she had come to believe, was a genuinely
dangerous place, and she was certain the mugging had happened because of the sort of person she
was—someone who had not been paying attention, who had let her guard down. She began to ruminate on
what she had done wrong, replaying the evening in her mind: she should have taken the main road, she
should have carried pepper spray, she should have run. These thoughts were relentless, and they made
the memory feel more real and more unreal at the same time. Sometimes, when the memory returned, her
body would lock into the same position she had been in on the ground, her knee bent, her mouth
pressed shut, and she would think, *This cannot have happened to me.*

The triggers became impossible to avoid. Footsteps behind her, even a coworker approaching her desk
from behind, sent her heart racing. She began to deliberately dissociate, trying to blank out or
leave her body when these moments occurred, because the feeling of being touched without warning was
unbearable. She also started to ruminate about how the event could have been prevented, spinning out
scenarios in her head for hours. This rumination only deepened her belief that she was to blame, and
that the world was unsafe, which in turn made her even more vigilant. She found she could not relax,
even at home in her locked apartment, because she was always listening, always waiting. Her husband
noticed she had stopped sitting with her back to the door at restaurants, and that she flinched when
he touched her shoulder from behind. She had explained it away, but she knew the truth: she was
afraid.

The cycle tightened. When something took longer than expected—a delayed train, a late reply from her
husband—she assumed something bad was happening. This sense of threat fed directly back into her
memories, making them feel more vivid and more intrusive. She would taste the blood again, feel the
concrete scrape her knee, and then she would ruminate further, trying to understand what she had
done wrong. The rumination, in turn, kept her threat system on high alert. She could not escape the
loop. At work, she began to take the stairs instead of the elevator, because the enclosed space and
the sudden stop made her feel trapped. She avoided the parking lot after dark, leaving early or

concentrate on spreadsheets, her mind drifting back to the taste of blood and the pressure of a hand

an entire Saturday sitting rigidly on the couch, unable to let him leave the room to get the mail.

living the rest of her life this way.
