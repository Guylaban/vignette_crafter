in a small convent in a working-class neighborhood of Chicago, when a man detonated a backpack bomb
inside the parish hall during a community dinner. She had been serving soup near the back of the
room. The blast threw her against a concrete pillar, and she landed among overturned tables, her
left arm gashed open, blood pooling around her on the tile floor. In the chaos she tried to stand
but slipped in the blood, and for what felt like minutes she could not find her bearings—the
screams, the smoke, the wet red spreading beneath her hands. She pressed her palm against her own
wound to stop the bleeding, and the warmth of it, the stickiness, became seared into her body’s
memory.

In the weeks that followed, she found she could not recall the event deliberately. When she tried to
think back, the sequence was fractured, slippery—she could get only fragments, like a film with
missing reels. But the memory came anyway, without warning. Often it arrived in the middle of the
night: she would wake from a nightmare drenched in sweat, the smell of smoke in her nostrils, the
sound of the blast still ringing in her ears, and for a terrifying moment she was back on that
floor. She had come to believe that the memory was not a memory at all but something alive,
something that could ambush her. And when it ambushed her, it arrived fully—she could feel the grit
of the tile against her cheek, hear the screaming, smell the metallic tang of blood. It was not a
recollection; it was a return.

The physical pain in her left arm, where the scar tissue still ached on damp days, became a trigger.
The moment she felt that familiar dull throb, her mind would lurch back to the parish hall. She
would see the blood on her hands, and the fear would rise up in her chest—hot, suffocating, a terror
that felt indistinguishable from the moment itself. She began to notice that even the sight of
blood, anywhere—a cut on someone’s finger, a spot on a tissue—could send her reeling. The triggers
fed the memory, and the memory fed the fear, and the fear made her hypervigilant to any sign of
danger.

She started scanning every room she entered. Before she could settle anywhere, she had to locate the
exits, trace the paths to the doors, note whether they were unlocked or blocked. She only felt safe
in a very limited number of places: her own small cell in the convent, the chapel during early
morning Mass when it was empty. Anywhere else felt like a trap. Crowded places became unbearable.
She stopped going to the grocery store, stopped attending community events, stopped walking through
the neighborhood market. She told herself it was sensible—if something happened, she needed to know
how to get out. But the escape-route planning became a compulsion, a ritual she could not skip
without feeling her stomach knot with dread.

She also found herself going over and over the details of the bombing in her mind, trying to make
sense of it. Why had he chosen that night? Could she have seen something, done something? She would
replay the minutes before the blast, searching for a warning she must have missed. The overanalysing
felt necessary, as though if she could just find the missing piece, the memory would stop ambushing
her. But it never did. The more she analysed, the more vivid the memory became, and the more afraid
she grew.

She withdrew from intimacy entirely. The other sisters in the convent noticed she had become
distant, closed off. When one of them tried to comfort her, touched her shoulder, María Elena felt a
flash of panic—not of the person, but of the vulnerability. Being close meant being exposed, and
being exposed meant being hurt. She had come to believe that the only safe person was herself, and
even that felt fragile.

Months passed. She continued her duties mechanically—prayer, cleaning, cooking—but she lived inside
a narrowing circle. The pain in her arm became a daily sentinel, and with it the fear, and with the
fear the need to scan the room, and with the scanning the exhaustion. She was sleeping poorly, the
nightmares leaving her hollow. What finally brought her to seek help was a moment in the chapel: she
was kneeling before the altar, and she felt the ache in her scar, and suddenly she was back on the
floor of the parish hall, and she could not breathe. She stayed there, frozen, for several minutes.
Afterward, she called the diocese health line. She was afraid of what was happening to her, but more
than that, she was afraid that if she did not stop the cycle, the memory would eventually swallow
everything else.
