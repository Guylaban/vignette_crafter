It started with the bicycle. For three months in the winter of her thirty-second year, a man had
followed Elena home from her graphic design studio. He would appear at the same intersection,
coasting silently behind her on a dark bike, always keeping just enough distance that she could
never be sure. The first time, she told herself it was coincidence. The second time, she changed her
route. The third time, she turned around and shouted at him, and he smiled and pedaled away. She
never saw his face clearly, but she remembered the shape of his silhouette against the streetlights,
the way his tires hummed on the asphalt. The police took a report but nothing ever came of it; the
case went cold, and the man was never identified. That was when the belief first settled into her
bones: the people responsible had faced no consequences, and the world was a dangerous place.

Now, ten months later, Elena cannot hear the whir of bicycle spokes without her chest tightening.
The trigger of physical proximity—anyone on a bike who comes too close—makes her heart race as if
the man is right behind her again. She has come to believe that people are not what they seem, that
a stranger’s smile can hide a predator, and this distrust colors every outing. When she walks to the
coffee shop near her apartment, she finds herself scanning every parked car, every doorway, her gaze
flicking to the curb for the gleam of a bicycle frame. The threat feels constant, even when nothing
is wrong: she often reacts as if she is in danger, her startle response so sharp that a neighbor’s
friendly wave from a passing car makes her jump. She is certain that the world is dangerous, and
this belief makes her hyperaware of every possible escape route before she feels comfortable
anywhere. She always identifies the nearest exit before sitting down in a café, and she checks her
surroundings constantly, a habit that has become automatic.

These maladaptive strategies, in turn, reinforce her negative appraisals. The more she scans and
plans, the more she proves to herself that danger is everywhere—why else would she need to be so
vigilant? Her checking makes her notice more potential threats, like the bicycle locked to a rack
outside her studio, which triggers a fresh wave of anxiety. And because she always stays near an
exit, she never lingers long enough to feel safe, which keeps her threat system on high alert. The
cycle tightens: a trigger—say, a cyclist passing too close—floods her with fear, and she immediately
scans for escape routes, which reminds her that the world is unsafe, which makes her more vigilant,
which leads her to notice another cyclist a block away, and the loop begins again.

At work, she has started taking the bus instead of riding her own bike, and she sits with her back
to the wall, her eyes on the door. Her husband has noticed she no longer wants to walk to the
farmers’ market on weekends, but she cannot explain why without sounding paranoid. What she feels is
a quiet certainty: the man who followed her is out there, unpunished, and the world remains a
dangerous place. She came to therapy because she is exhausted—tired of the constant scanning, the
jumpiness, the way her own mind turns every stranger into a threat. What she fears most is that this
will never stop, that she will spend the rest of her life looking over her shoulder. But she also
hopes, faintly, that someone can help her see the world differently.
