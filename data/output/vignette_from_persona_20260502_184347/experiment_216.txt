been hiding under the kitchen table, her small hands pressed over her ears, but she could still hear
her father’s voice rising and falling like a storm, and her mother’s quieter, broken sounds. What
she remembered most vividly now, at thirty-nine, was not the shouting itself but the air—thick with
the sharp, metallic tang of her father’s sweat as he loomed over her mother, and the way that smell
seemed to cling to everything for days afterward. When she tried to deliberately recall the details
of that night, the memory was stubbornly fragmented, slipping away like water through her fingers,
leaving only a series of vivid, silent images: the crack in the linoleum floor, the blue of her
mother’s housecoat, the way the light from the television flickered across the wall. What came
easiest was the grief—a wave of it that would crash over her without warning, so heavy and complete
that she sometimes felt she was drowning in the sorrow of a child she no longer was.

Sofia became a detective because she believed that if she could understand the worst of what people
did to each other, she could keep herself safe. But the job had only deepened the certainty that had
taken root under that kitchen table: that the world was a place where danger could erupt from the
most ordinary moments, and that she was the sort of person who attracted it. The event happened to
me because of the sort of person I am, she had come to believe, a conviction that settled into her
bones like a low-grade fever. She had begun to doubt her own instincts—how could she trust that she
would do the right thing when she had not been able to protect her mother, had not even known how to
run? Her reactions since that night felt like proof that she was going crazy: the way her heart
would hammer at the sound of a raised voice in the precinct bullpen, the way she would freeze when a
suspect’s sweat-soaked shirt was bagged as evidence, the way she flinched when a colleague clapped a
hand on her shoulder in congratulation.

These triggers—the shout, the smell, the touch—did not merely startle her. They sent her spiraling
into a state of raw, animal alertness. She would wake in the middle of the night, her body rigid,
scanning the darkness of her bedroom for threats that were not there. At work, she always chose her
desk with her back to the wall, and in meetings she sat closest to the door, her eyes tracking every
movement in the room. An unexpected movement nearby—a detective rising quickly from his chair, a
door swinging open too fast—would make her react as if she were already under attack, her hand going
to her hip where her service weapon rested. The world had become a series of ambushes waiting to
happen, and she was always, always on watch.

The only way Sofia had found to stop the cascade was to hurt herself. When the images of her
mother’s face, pale and streaked with tears, pressed too insistently into her mind, or when the
grief threatened to pull her under, she would dig her fingernails into her forearm until the pain
cut through the memory. The physical sensation was the only thing that could anchor her back in the
present, a harsh reset that silenced the loop of shame and fear. But this strategy came at a cost.

bed, she would go stiff, and the look on his face—hurt, confused—only confirmed her belief that she
was broken, that she could not do anything right.

The memories, when they came, were not something she chose. They arrived as unbidden pictures, and
each one brought with it the old grief and the old accusation: this happened because of who you are.
That belief, in turn, made her more sensitive to the triggers around her—a man’s laugh in a coffee
shop, the smell of sweat on a crowded subway car—so that she was constantly scanning, constantly
waiting for the next assault on her senses. And each time she flinched or froze or hurt herself to
escape, she proved to herself again that she was not in control, that she was, in fact, going crazy.
The cycle tightened until her whole life had become a series of small, invisible battles fought in
the space between one breath and the next.

What finally brought Sofia to seek help was not the fear or the grief or the shame. It was the
