Mai is a 49-year-old divorced woman of Southeastern Asian descent who works in IT support for a mid-
sized logistics company. Eight years ago, during a tour as a combat medic in a conflict zone, her
convoy was struck by an improvised explosive device. The vehicle ignited, and she spent the
following hours pulling wounded soldiers from the wreckage as fuel and rubber burned around her. She
remembers the smell of charred flesh and melted wiring more vividly than the gunfire that followed.
That burnt odor has never fully left her; it surfaces without warning—when a coworker microwaves
popcorn too long, when she passes a construction site where asphalt is being laid, or when the
office printer overheats and emits a faint acrid scent. Each time, her chest tightens, her palms
sweat, and she feels a sudden, overwhelming need to scan the room for threats, certain that
something terrible is about to happen again.

In the months after her discharge, Mai tried to resume a normal life. She took a job in IT support,
reasoning that a cubicle and a headset would keep her safe from the unpredictability of the outside
world. But the workplace itself became a landscape of hidden dangers. The ventilation system
sometimes carried a burnt smell from the break room, and the distant thrum of a helicopter passing
overhead during her shift would freeze her hands over the keyboard, her heart hammering as she
stared at the monitor unseeing. She began to feel threatened by colleagues who approached her desk
without warning, interpreting their casual greetings as potential ambushes. She started checking
behind her as she walked to the restroom, convinced someone was following her. The physical
reactivity—the racing pulse, the shallow breathing—became a constant companion, and she grew
terrified of her own body's alarms, which she could no longer distinguish from real danger.

To manage this, Mai developed a set of strategies that made sense to her in the moment. She stopped
talking about the event with anyone, refusing to discuss her service record or the nightmares that
still woke her. She avoided situations that might stir strong emotions—birthday parties, team
meetings, even phone calls with her adult daughter—because she had learned that feeling anything
intensely could trigger a cascade of memories. When the thoughts became unbearable, she hurt herself
physically, digging her fingernails into her forearm or pressing a paperclip into her palm until the
sharp pain cut through the intrusive images. These acts of self-harm became her most reliable way to
stop the spiral, but they also left her feeling more isolated and more convinced that she was broken
beyond repair.

Over time, the very strategies she used to protect herself began to backfire. By avoiding emotional
situations and refusing to talk about what happened, Mai narrowed her world considerably. She
stopped attending the monthly veterans' group her therapist had once recommended. She declined
invitations from coworkers. Her daughter stopped calling as frequently, interpreting Mai's distance
as rejection. The more she withdrew, the more the triggers seemed to multiply. A helicopter sound on
the news became unbearable; the smell of burnt toast in the morning sent her to her knees. She found
herself scanning every parking lot, every hallway, every grocery aisle. The threat monitoring
intensified, and with it, the need to hurt herself to regain control. Each time she checked behind
her, she was reinforcing the belief that danger was imminent, and each time she hurt herself, she
was teaching her brain that the only way out of fear was through pain.

What finally brought Mai to seek help was a moment at work three weeks ago. A colleague dropped a
hot glue gun on her desk, and the smell of melting adhesive hit her before she could prepare. She
fled to the bathroom, locked herself in a stall, and pressed the sharp edge of her key into her
wrist hard enough to draw blood. A supervisor found her shaking on the floor. The company offered
her a leave of absence and a referral to a trauma specialist. Mai accepted because she was
terrified—not of the memories themselves anymore, but of what she might do to herself the next time
she couldn't escape. She hopes that talking to someone might finally make the world feel less
dangerous, but she is also certain that nothing can undo what she saw or smelled that day.
