Valley, taking a detour to avoid highway construction, when she rounded a curve and saw a pickup
truck swerve into a roadside encampment of Arab families—migrant farmworkers, she later learned. The

thrown clear; a woman was not. Elena stopped, got out, tried to help, but there was nothing she
could do. The woman died in front of her. For weeks afterward, Elena could not stop turning it over
in her mind: why had she taken that detour? Why had she been the one to see it? Somebody else would
not have gotten into this situation, she told herself. Somebody more alert, more capable, would have
been elsewhere, would have known what to do.

Three years later, Elena is a data scientist at a tech firm in San Francisco, and the memory has not
faded. It feels more real than ordinary things—more vivid than her wedding day, sharper than her
daughter’s first words. She can hear the woman’s last breath, a wet, ragged sound that sometimes
plays in her head unbidden. The memories are triggered without warning: a news report about tensions
in the Middle East, the smell of a barbecue, even the sight of a flame on a stove. When these
triggers hit, Elena feels as though she is back at the roadside, her hands shaking, her heart
pounding, her mind insisting the event is happening again. She has come to believe that she failed
to protect that woman, that she should have done something more, and that this failure defines her.
What frightens her most is when she finds herself thinking that her life is not worth living
anymore—a thought that arrives quietly, like a door clicking shut.

Elena’s response to these intrusions has been to work. She throws herself into her job with a
ferocity that her colleagues admire but her husband finds alarming. She takes on extra projects,
stays late, works weekends. She does this because being alone with her thoughts is unbearable. When
she is not working, the memories crowd in, and the threat feels immediate: strangers approaching her
on the street seem dangerous, and she checks the locks on her apartment door three times before bed.

then the cycle tightens. The more she works to avoid the memories, the more they intrude. The more

Valley entirely, and she avoids Arab neighborhoods in the city, though she is ashamed of this. Her
husband has noticed she is distant, irritable, and has started sleeping in the guest room.

What finally brought Elena to seek help was a moment at work last month. She was leading a team
meeting when a colleague mentioned a news story about a fire in an Arab village overseas. Elena felt
the room dissolve, heard that wet breath, and excused herself to the bathroom, where she sat on the
floor for twenty minutes, convinced she was losing her mind. The next day, she made an appointment.
She is terrified that the memories will never stop, that she will always be this hollow, but she is
also hoping, quietly, that someone can help her find a way to live with what she saw.
