Marcus is a 28-year-old social media manager who came to therapy at his girlfriend’s urging, though
he admitted he didn’t expect it to help. He had served a single deployment in Afghanistan five years
earlier as a vehicle mechanic, and the event that reshaped his life happened on a routine supply
run. An IED detonated under the lead Humvee, and Marcus, in the vehicle behind, was first on the
scene. He remembers the heat, the smell of fuel, and the sight of his sergeant’s leg—torn open, bone
visible, blood pooling in the dirt. He helped pull the wounded out, but what stayed with him was not
the chaos; it was the quiet afterward, the way the sergeant’s eyes looked past him, and the feeling
that something in Marcus had snapped and could not be rethreaded.

In the months after returning home, Marcus found himself unable to shake the sense that he no longer
belonged among civilians. He had come to believe that he was permanently an outsider now, that the
deployment had carved a line between who he was and who everyone else remained. Somebody else would
not have gotten into that situation, he told himself—a better soldier would have seen the signs,
avoided the route, saved the sergeant. These thoughts sat inside him like a low hum, and they grew
louder whenever he saw visible injuries or wounds on others. A cyclist with a scraped arm, a child
with a bandaged knee—each image yanked him back to the sergeant’s leg, and with it the certainty
that he had permanently changed for the worse. He began to notice that simply driving with people,
even his girlfriend in the passenger seat, made his chest tighten and his palms sweat; he would scan
intersections for debris, for anything out of place, and he often insisted on taking routes he knew
were clear. Helicopter sounds, even from a news report on television, sent him into a state of
hypervigilance that lasted for hours.

Because his memories of the event came in disconnected pieces that did not fit together—the heat,
then the bone, then the quiet, with no clear sequence—Marcus felt as though he was reliving
fragments rather than remembering a story. The memory was so extreme it was hard to believe it had
actually happened to him, and yet sometimes he felt the full emotional intensity of the event
without any clear image, a wave of terror that rose from nowhere and left him shaking. These
intrusions made him feel weak, and he began to rehearse what to say before any interaction, running
conversations through his mind so that he would not be caught off guard. He also started avoiding
military, police uniforms or weapons, crossing the street when he saw a cop or a soldier, because
the sight triggered a cascade of memory and shame. Situations that put his body under
stress—exercise, hot weather, even carrying groceries upstairs—he avoided too, because the physical
sensation of strain felt too close to the panic he had felt that day.

The threat monitoring that followed was relentless. Marcus had difficulty falling asleep because he
needed to stay alert; he would lie awake listening for footsteps, for helicopters, for anything that
did not belong. He had strong physical reactions when something reminded him of the stressful
experience—his heart would pound, his vision would narrow, and he would feel a desperate need to
escape. He reacted with alarm when someone touched him unexpectedly, once elbowing his girlfriend in
the ribs when she put a hand on his shoulder from behind. He knew it was not rational, but he was
certain that if he let his guard down, something terrible would happen again. The more he scanned
for danger, the more he noticed triggers—a distant engine, a stranger’s scar, a news clip about the
military—and the more he avoided, the more fragmented and unreal his memories became, because he
never let himself sit with them long enough to form a coherent story. He rehearsed conversations to
control how others saw him, but the rehearsing itself reminded him of how broken he was, and that
fed the belief that he was permanently damaged. Each avoidance deepened his sense of being an
outsider, and each intrusion sharpened his vigilance.

What finally brought Marcus to therapy was not his own distress but a concrete consequence: his
girlfriend had told him she could not keep living with a man who flinched when she touched him and
who planned every conversation like a combat operation. He was afraid that if he stopped avoiding,
the memories would overwhelm him, and that if he let himself feel the full weight of what happened,
he would never come back. But he was also afraid of losing her. So he sat in the chair, jaw tight,
and said, “I don’t know if this can fix me. But I’m tired of being afraid of everything.”
