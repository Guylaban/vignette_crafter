Raj, a 50-year-old South Asian animator living in a Chicago suburb, had always prided himself on his
steady nerves, a quality he relied on daily while crafting intricate frames for children’s cartoons.
That steadiness shattered three years ago, when his seven-year-old son, Arjun, was diagnosed with a
rare, aggressive form of leukemia. For six months, Raj lived in the hospital, sleeping on a pull-out
chair beside Arjun’s bed, the antiseptic smell of disinfectant and the soft beep of monitors
becoming the soundtrack of his life. The worst moment came during a bone marrow biopsy, when Arjun,
terrified and in pain, had looked at Raj and whispered, “Daddy, are you going to let them hurt me?”
Raj had held his son’s hand, his own heart pounding so violently he thought he might die, as the
doctor inserted the needle. He had felt utterly powerless, convinced that his presence was not
protecting Arjun but merely witnessing his suffering. After a successful stem cell transplant, Arjun
recovered fully, but Raj did not.

Now, three years later, that powerless feeling has calcified into a pervasive dread that governs his
daily life. The most immediate trigger is sleeping alone. Whenever his wife travels for work, Raj
finds himself unable to fall asleep in their bed without her. The silence of the empty house, the
darkness, the absence of another heartbeat—it all feels like the hospital room at night, when the
only sound was Arjun’s labored breathing. On those nights, he lies awake, his mind racing with the
worst-case rehearsal that has become his primary coping strategy. He mentally replays every possible
catastrophe: a delayed diagnosis, a sudden infection, a relapse that no one catches in time. He
rehearses these scenarios not to prepare, but because he has come to believe that if he does not
imagine the worst, the worst will actually happen. This rumination, however, only deepens his
hypervigilance. The more he rehearses disaster, the more sensitive he becomes to any potential
trigger. A whiff of antiseptic from a cleaning cart at his animation studio is enough to send him
into a cold sweat, his thoughts instantly leaping to the hospital corridor. A routine dental
cleaning becomes an ordeal; the smell of the latex gloves and the sight of the needle make him
nauseous, and he finds himself asking the dentist repeatedly, “Are you sure this is safe? Are you
sure I’m okay?” This reassurance seeking has become a compulsion—he texts his wife multiple times a
day, asking if she has checked on Arjun, if the school called, if everything is fine. She has
started to sigh when she sees his name on her phone, a small, guilty sound that cuts him deeply.

The cycle has tightened into a self-reinforcing loop. The triggers—sleeping alone, medical smells,
the memory of the biopsy—activate his maladaptive strategies, and those strategies, in turn, make
the triggers more potent. After a sleepless night spent rehearsing a hospital relapse, Raj is so on
edge that the faint smell of rubbing alcohol in a colleague’s cubicle sends him into a full panic
attack. He now avoids the studio’s break room because it sometimes smells of cleaning fluid, and he
has started working from home twice a week, a decision that has strained his relationship with his
director, who has noticed his absences. He has also begun ruminating about revenge—not against a
person, but against the abstract idea of fate, the universe, or the doctors who he irrationally
blames for not warning him sooner. These vengeful thoughts give him a fleeting sense of control, but
they only exhaust him further, making him more irritable and more prone to scanning his environment
for threats. He has come to believe that his vigilance is the only thing keeping his family safe,
and that if he relaxes for even a moment, something terrible will happen.

What finally brought Raj to seek help was a moment of occupational consequence. Last week, while
animating a cheerful scene of a child laughing, he found himself unable to press the keys. His hands
were shaking, and all he could see was Arjun’s face during the biopsy. His supervisor pulled him
aside and asked if he was okay, and Raj lied, saying he had the flu. Driving home, he realized he
had lost the ability to trust his own mind. He is most afraid that his anxiety will cost him his
job, but what he hopes for, more than anything, is to sleep through the night without seeing the
hospital room.
