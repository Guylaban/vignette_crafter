alienation from her own memory had been there from the start, and it had only deepened.

In the weeks that followed, Amara noticed that the nausea would return without warning. She would be
standing at the whiteboard in a student’s home, explaining a math problem, and suddenly the familiar
queasiness would rise in her throat, triggered by nothing she could name. It was the uncertainty
that frightened her most—not knowing when the feeling would hit, or why. She had come to believe
that the event had permanently damaged her, that she was fundamentally changed for the worse, and
that she could not handle even the slightest upset anymore. When she heard a child crying in the
next room during a tutoring session, the sound would land in her chest like a stone, and she would
think, *See? You are broken. You cannot even tolerate a normal sound.* The crying did not directly
remind her of the assault, but it activated something raw, and that raw feeling confirmed her worst
appraisal of herself.

Because she was certain she could not cope, Amara began to avoid anything that might stir emotion.
She stopped accepting invitations from friends to go out for coffee or dinner, because those
situations might make her feel happy or hopeful, and hope felt like a trap—a false promise that she
could return to who she used to be. She also avoided people who brought up the event. When David’s
sister once asked gently, “How are you doing with everything?” Amara cut the conversation short and
stopped taking her calls. The avoidance worked in the moment, but it had a cost. The more she pushed
reminders away, the more the memory felt like an intruder that did not belong to her, and the more
alien it felt, the more she blamed herself for not being able to simply move on. She would lie awake
at night, replaying the evening in fragments, and tell herself, *If I had just left earlier. If I
had not trusted him. This is my fault.* That self-blame, in turn, made her feel even less capable of
handling her own life, and so she withdrew further.

The cycle tightened over months. Every time she avoided a positive event, the memory felt more
foreign and more terrifying. And every time she tried to think about what happened, the terror would
flood back—the same visceral panic she had felt on that couch—and she would shut down, convinced
that if she let herself think about it fully, she would not be able to handle it. Her work as a
tutor became a minefield. She began scheduling sessions only with older students, because younger
children cried more often, and the sound of crying, even a toddler’s tantrum in a neighboring
apartment, would send her stomach into freefall and reignite the belief that she was permanently
ruined. She started canceling sessions last minute when she felt nauseous, and her income suffered.
David noticed she was pulling away, but when he asked what was wrong, she could not find the
words—the memory simply would not come out in language, only in waves of sickness and terror.

What finally brought Amara to seek help was a morning in late October. She had been scheduled to
tutor a ten-year-old boy, but as she drove toward his house, her stomach turned, and she pulled over

she had become—a person who could not hear a child cry, who could not feel hope without suspicion,
who blamed herself for something that still did not feel like her own story. She wanted to believe
there was a way out, but she was terrified that the permanent damage she felt was real.
