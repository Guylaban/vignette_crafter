Amir was thirty-four when it began—a slow, creeping presence that he first noticed outside the
grocery store near his station. At first, he dismissed it: a man in a dark sedan who seemed to
appear wherever Amir went. But over weeks, the sightings became impossible to ignore. The sedan was
there when he left the firehouse at odd hours, when he biked home along the river path, when he
stopped for coffee. The man never approached, never spoke, but he watched. Amir reported it to the
police twice. They found nothing. The stalking lasted four months before the man simply stopped
appearing. By then, the damage was done. Amir, now thirty-seven, sat in a therapist’s office with
his hands clasped tightly, unable to explain what had happened without feeling his chest tighten and
his words dissolve into silence.

The triggers were everywhere. Physical locations—the grocery store, the river path, the corner where
the sedan had once idled—could send him reeling. His bicycle, once a source of freedom, now sat
untouched in the garage; the sight of it brought the full memory crashing back without warning, a
wave of helplessness and fear that left him breathless. When the memory returned, he felt so
overwhelmed by emotion that he had to stop whatever he was doing, frozen mid-step. At the firehouse,
the sound of the alarm—loud, unexpected—made him flinch and scan for exits, his eyes darting to the
doors and windows before he could even register the reason. Amir had come to believe that his
reactions proved he was permanently changed for the worse, that the man in the sedan had broken
something inside him that could never be repaired. What frightened him most was the thought that he
was going crazy—that his hypervigilance, his inability to describe what happened, his constant sense
of being watched were signs of unraveling.

Amir watched his colleagues closely, studying their faces for signs of threat, certain that danger
could come from anyone. He watched other people too—on the street, in the station, even at home with
his wife—looking for the subtle cues that had once been invisible. The more he scanned, the more he
found reasons to be alert. A loud noise, a stranger’s prolonged glance, a car that slowed near him:
each triggered the memory, and each memory reinforced his belief that he was weak, that a real
firefighter would have handled it, would have confronted the man, would not have been reduced to
this. The cycle tightened. Because he believed he was weak, he avoided the river path and the
grocery store, but those same avoidances made the triggers feel larger, more dangerous. The memory
of the sedan idling outside his home returned when he least expected it—in a quiet moment, in a
familiar street—and each return deepened his conviction that he had been permanently damaged. He
could not find words to describe what happened; the story felt stuck in his throat, a jumble of
images and sensations that defied language.

Amir’s wife had noticed. She asked him why he no longer biked, why he flinched at the firehouse
alarm, why he checked the locks three times before bed. He told her he was fine. But he was not
fine. The threat monitoring had become a constant hum beneath his thoughts: the scanning of exits,
the watching of others, the tension in his shoulders at every unexpected sound. The more he
monitored, the more he felt he was going crazy, and the more he felt he was going crazy, the more he
scanned. His work suffered. He had missed two shifts because he could not bring himself to enter the
station after a routine call near the grocery store. His captain had pulled him aside, concerned.
Amir had said nothing.

What finally brought him to seek help was a morning in late October. He was driving to work when a
black sedan pulled up beside him at a red light. The driver was a stranger—a woman on her phone—but
for a split second Amir’s mind went blank with terror. The memory surged, overwhelming, and he
pulled over, shaking, unable to continue. He sat on the curb and called his wife. She drove him to
the clinic. Now, in the therapist’s office, Amir was terrified that admitting any of this would
confirm that he was broken beyond repair. But he was also terrified of staying the same. He wanted
to get back on his bicycle. He wanted to stop flinching. He wanted to believe that the man in the
sedan had not taken everything.
