together, and that fragmentation has become its own source of dread.

In the months since, Leilani has come to believe she no longer belongs anywhere. She used to be a
happy person, but now she is always miserable. Nothing in her life feels meaningful anymore. She is
a professor of marine biology, and her lectures once felt like a calling; now she stands before her
students and feels like a ghost performing a script. What frightens her most is the certainty that
she has been permanently changed—that the woman who loved her work, her husband, her morning coffee
on the porch, is gone. She is certain she will never get her back.

This sense of being hollowed out has made her hypervigilant in ways she cannot control. She wakes
during the night to check whether she is safe, her hand reaching for the lamp before her brain
catches up. During the day, she is superalert, watchful, on guard—scanning parking lots for trucks,
flinching at the sound of a horn. She has come to believe that the world is fundamentally unsafe,
and that she must be ready at all times. This vigilance, in turn, deepens her sense of alienation.
The more she watches for danger, the more she feels like a stranger in her own life.

husband what happened, she loses the thread and cannot finish, and this failure convinces her that

the blanks with fear. She has begun to experience flashbacks—suddenly feeling or acting as if the

that has been hit makes her stomach drop.

overwhelmed, as though she is watching herself from a distance.

is lost, and each moment of feeling lost makes her scan the world more desperately for threats. She
wakes in the night to check if she is safe, and finds only the dark. She used to be a happy person.
