Adwoa is a 38-year-old Ghanaian-American woman who works as a commercial fisherman on a small boat
out of a coastal town in Maryland. The trauma that shattered her life did not happen to her
directly, but to her younger brother, Kofi, who was visiting her for the summer. One evening, as
they drove back from the dock in her old sedan, a driver ran a red light and T-boned the passenger
side at full speed. Kofi, in the passenger seat, suffered catastrophic internal injuries and died
before the paramedics could free him from the wreckage. Adwoa walked away with a bruised rib and a
deep, unshakeable conviction that she had killed her brother. She had been the one driving. She had
been the one who chose to take that route. She had not seen the other car coming, had not swerved,
had not done anything. In the months that followed, she became certain that the event happened
because of the way she acted—her failure to anticipate the danger, her slow reflexes. She told
herself she should have protected him and she failed, and she has not forgiven herself for not doing
more to stop what happened.

The anniversaries of the event hit her hardest. Every year, as the date approaches, she begins to
feel it in her body before she even names it—her heart pounds, her chest tightens, and she breaks
into a cold sweat at the mere thought of the date. These physical reactions are so strong that she
often has to leave the dinner table or excuse herself from a conversation. Any talk about car
accidents, even in passing, triggers the same visceral response: a surge of fear and horror that
leaves her shaking. She cannot look at images of cars that have been hit by accident, which seem to
appear everywhere—on the news, in magazines, even in roadside debris. What frightens her most is the
anger that follows. She becomes irritable, snapping at her husband for small things, and has had
angry outbursts that she later regrets. She has come to believe that her strong negative
feelings—the guilt, the shame, the rage—are proof that she is a dangerous person, someone who cannot
be trusted behind a wheel.

To manage this overwhelming threat, Adwoa has developed a set of habits that she knows are not
healthy but feels powerless to stop. She stays up until very late at night, often past three in the
morning, because the moment she closes her eyes she sees Kofi’s face in the shattered window. She
dwells on how the event could have been prevented, replaying the intersection, the timing of the
light, the angle of the other car, searching for the exact moment she could have done something
different. This rumination only deepens her guilt and reinforces the belief that she failed to
protect him. When the rumination becomes unbearable, she spends money impulsively—online shopping
for things she does not need, buying expensive fishing gear she never uses, ordering takeout she
cannot afford. The spending distracts her for a few hours, but it also leaves her with a new layer
of shame and financial strain, which she then interprets as further evidence of her poor judgment.
The cycle tightens: the more she ruminates, the more she needs to distract herself; the more she
spends, the more she blames herself; the more she stays awake, the more exhausted she is the next
day, which makes her more irritable and reactive to any reminder of the crash.

Her occupation as a fisherman means she spends long hours on the water, often alone or with a small
crew. The boat is one of the few places she feels safe, because there are no cars, no intersections,
no red lights. But even there, the triggers find her. A crew member mentions a recent accident on
the highway, and the conversation derails her for the rest of the shift. She becomes quiet,
withdrawn, and then snaps at the man for no reason. She has learned to avoid the dock on the
anniversary of the crash, calling in sick even though she knows it costs her wages. Her husband has
noticed the late nights, the credit card bills, the short temper, and has asked her to talk to
someone. She finally agreed to come to therapy because she is terrified that her anger will cost her
her marriage, and because she cannot stop the loop of guilt and spending and sleeplessness. What she
is most afraid of is that if she stops the rumination, she will forget Kofi entirely—and that would
be the ultimate betrayal.
