finished a shift at the community counseling center where she worked, and was walking toward the bus

nearby park, and she noticed a police cruiser idling at the corner before she reached the shelter.

cruiser appeared, and a third, and within seconds officers were converging on a young man near the

and shoved her back, telling her to move along, and in that moment her body became a stranger to
her. She felt her knees give, then a hand on the back of her neck pushing her down, and for a few
seconds she was certain she was going to be arrested or worse. It lasted maybe two minutes. The
young man was handcuffed and driven away. Meera was left standing on the sidewalk, shaking, the
smell of soil and exhaust thick in her throat.

In the weeks that followed, the memory did not behave like a normal memory. Sometimes she could
recall the sequence of events with perfect clarity but felt nothing—a hollow, clinical replay, as if
she were watching a stranger on a screen. Other times, without warning, the memory would return in
full sensory force: the weight of the officer’s hand, the grit of the pavement against her cheek,

hands pressed flat against an invisible ground, her breath shallow and fast. These episodes left her
disoriented and afraid. She began to avoid the bus altogether, walking the two miles to work even
when it rained, because the moment she stepped onto a bus the smell of the floor—that mixture of
dirt and rubber—would trigger a full reenactment. She also stopped seeing friends from before the
event, people who had known her as easygoing and unafraid. She could not explain to them why she
flinched at a raised voice or why she had started covering her face with her hands whenever a police
car passed. She felt contaminated by what had happened, as if the dirt on the pavement had seeped

seemed, that anyone—a neighbor, a client, a stranger on the street—could turn dangerous without
warning. This distrust reshaped her world. She began studying faces and body language obsessively,
scanning for threat before she could even think about what she was doing. Her body reacted before
her mind, and she had learned to trust that physical alarm more than anything else.

skin. She was a counselor, trained to help others process trauma, but she could not process her own.
