Arthur is a 68-year-old school counselor, married for over forty years, who came to see me at the
urging of his wife, Sarah. He had always been a steady, gentle presence in their home and at the
middle school where he worked, but over the past year, he had become someone she no longer
recognized. The change, he told me quietly, began on a Tuesday afternoon fourteen months ago, when
he was leaving a parent-teacher conference after hours. A man he had never seen before was waiting
in the darkened hallway, and before Arthur could speak, the man slammed him against a row of
lockers, his forearm pressed hard against Arthur’s throat. Arthur remembers the smell of the man’s
breath—sharp, sour alcohol—and the bright, chemical tang of floor cleaner from a nearby janitor’s
closet. He remembers the lockers digging into his back, the sound of his own glasses skittering
across the linoleum, and then nothing else until he was on the ground, the man gone, the hallway
silent. What stays with him is not a continuous story but a handful of frozen moments: the pressure
on his throat, the flash of a silver belt buckle, the smell of alcohol and antiseptic, and the
terror that was so total it felt like his body had stopped existing.

In the months that followed, those moments began to surface without warning. He would be walking
through the grocery store, and a man in a worn jacket would pass too close, and suddenly Arthur
would be back in that hallway, the terror flooding his chest before he could even register what had
happened. The most potent triggers were not visual but olfactory. A whiff of rubbing alcohol from a
medical drama on television, the sharp scent of hand sanitizer in the school nurse’s office, or the
faint, sweet smell of whiskey on a colleague’s breath at a retirement party—each of these could drop
him, without transition, into one of those snapshot memories. The smell of alcohol was the worst,
because it was the smell of the man’s breath, and whenever Arthur caught it, the memory of the
forearm against his throat would arrive as a single, vivid image, bringing with it the same raw
terror he had felt in that moment. He began to avoid people, a strategy that made sense to him even
as it shrank his world. He stopped lingering in the staff room after school, he took a different
route to his car to avoid the hallway where it happened, and he learned to scan every room he
entered for exits and for men who might be drinking. The more he avoided, the more his world
contracted, and the more the triggers seemed to multiply. A patient in a waiting room with a faint
smell of beer on his clothes, a student whose parent reeked of mouthwash and wine at a
conference—each encounter felt like an ambush, and each ambush deepened his certainty that other
people were dangerous, that closeness itself was a risk he could no longer afford.

This belief bled into his marriage. Sarah had always been the person he turned to, but now, when she
reached for his hand in the car or tried to talk to him about her day, he felt a spike of something
close to panic. Being emotionally close to her meant being vulnerable, and vulnerability felt like
lying on the hallway floor with a stranger’s forearm on his throat. He began to dissociate
deliberately during their conversations, focusing on a spot on the wall and letting his mind go
blank, leaving his body while she talked. He knew this hurt her, but the alternative—staying
present, feeling the terror that intimacy now carried—felt impossible. Sarah’s hurt became another
trigger: her quiet withdrawal, the way she stopped reaching for his hand, reminded him of the
isolation of that hallway, and the cycle tightened.

The memory of the assault remained fragmented, each piece arriving without context and vanishing
just as quickly. Arthur could not tell the story of what happened in a straight line; he could only
describe the pictures in his head and the terror that came with them. He came to see me because

hollow resignation, that he had become a stranger in his own life. What frightened him most was the
possibility that he might never feel safe again, that the smell of alcohol and the pressure of a
hand on his throat would always be waiting, just beneath the surface of his ordinary days.
