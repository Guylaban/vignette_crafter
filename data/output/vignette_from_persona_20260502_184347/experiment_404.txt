memories were most likely to surface when she was riding the bus to work, the lurch and stop of the
vehicle pulling her back to the truck bed, or when certain conversation topics arose among
coworkers: jokes about the supervisor, talk about overtime, any mention of the packing shed. The
memories made her feel as though she was back there, and she would become flooded with fear and

work, and then more, and she started mentally rehearsing worst-case scenarios before every
shift—imagining the supervisor cornering her again, imagining what she would do, how she would
scream, where she would run. She told herself that if she prepared for the worst, she would not be
caught off guard again.

But the rehearsals only made the memories come more often. The more she imagined the worst, the more
the real memory intruded, vivid and fresh. And the drinking, she noticed, made her drowsy and
careless the next day, which meant she was less alert, and then the memories would hit her harder,
without warning. She began to avoid other people entirely. She stopped sitting with coworkers at
lunch, stopped riding the bus with them, and instead walked two miles each way to the fields,
leaving before dawn and returning after dark. She came to believe that she could not rely on
herself—that she had failed to protect herself once, and would fail again. She felt like an
outsider, permanently set apart from the other women who laughed and talked and seemed untouched by

they knew, they would see her as damaged or complicit.

there, the fear would spike, and that spike would trigger another intrusive memory—the hand, the
diesel, the weight—and the cycle would begin again. Her husband noticed she was jumpy and withdrawn,
