Kwame is a 19-year-old UX designer living in a mid-sized American city. He was born in Ghana and
came to the United States with his family when he was twelve. He is single and lives alone in a
small apartment near the tech incubator where he works. He sought therapy six months after the end
of a stalking episode that lasted nearly four months.

The stalking began when a former coworker, a woman in her late twenties named Elena, started sending
him long, rambling emails after he declined her invitation to coffee. At first, Kwame ignored them.
But the messages escalated quickly: dozens of texts per night, voicemails at 2 a.m., and finally,
appearances outside his office building and his apartment. She would wait in the lobby, or stand
across the street, watching. He changed his route to work three times, but she always found him. The
police took a report but said there was not enough evidence for a restraining order. The worst night
came when he found her sitting on the steps of his building, holding a photograph of the two of them
she had taken without his knowledge. He moved out of his apartment the next day and stayed with a
friend for two weeks. Elena was eventually arrested for trespassing at his office, but by then Kwame
had already resigned from the job he loved.

In the months that followed, Kwame came to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous. He
had always considered himself a careful person, but now he saw threats everywhere. He stopped going
to coffee shops, avoided crowded sidewalks, and began checking the locks on his door three times
before bed. He was certain that if he let his guard down for even a moment, something terrible would
happen. He told his therapist, "I have to be especially careful because you never know what can
happen next." This constant vigilance exhausted him, but he could not imagine relaxing it. He also
blamed himself for the stalking. In quiet moments, he replayed the initial interaction with Elena,
wondering if he had been too friendly, too polite, too accommodating. He told himself that the event
happened because of the way he acted, and that if he had been firmer from the start, none of it
would have unfolded. This self-blame sat heavily in his chest, separate from the fear but adding its
own weight.

At work, Kwame’s vigilance affected his performance. He sat with his back to the wall in every
meeting, and he could not focus when colleagues approached his desk from behind. He began working
from home as often as possible, telling his manager he was more productive in a quiet space. In
truth, he was afraid of who might walk through the office door. He had stopped going to the gym,
stopped seeing friends, and stopped dating. He felt trapped in a small, watchful life.

What finally brought Kwame to therapy was a panic attack in a grocery store. A woman in the produce
aisle had been staring at him—or he thought she was staring—and he had frozen, heart hammering,
unable to move until she walked away. He realized he could not keep living this way. He was afraid
of the world, and he was afraid of himself for having been, as he saw it, the cause of his own
suffering. He wanted to stop checking locks, stop scanning every face, stop replaying that first
conversation. He wanted to feel safe, but he did not know what that would even feel like anymore.
