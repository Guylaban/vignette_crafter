Kwame was seventy-nine years old when he finally walked into a clinic, a wiry man whose hands had
spent decades stacking boxes in a warehouse, and whose mind had spent far longer trying to outrun a
single night. He was born in a small village in Ghana, and when he was seven years old, he had
watched his father beat his mother until she stopped moving. He could not remember the sound of her
voice that night, or the color of the dress she wore. What he remembered, or rather what he felt,
was a terror that arrived without warning, without an image attached, like a wave that knocked the
air from his lungs. When he tried to recall the event deliberately, the pieces would not fit
together—a shard of moonlight through a window, the smell of palm wine on his father’s breath, the
thud of a body hitting packed earth—but no whole story, no sequence. He had learned not to try.

For decades, Kwame had managed by keeping himself surrounded. He worked double shifts, stayed late
to help unload trucks, and then drove straight to a friend’s house or a bar where he knew people. He
kept himself constantly surrounded by people so that he was never alone with his thoughts. The quiet
of his own apartment was unbearable; there, the emotion without image would rise up, and he would
feel the full intensity of that childhood terror as if it were happening, even though he could see
nothing. So he avoided quiet. He avoided nature, too—parks, wooded trails, even the small garden
behind his building—because the stillness of trees and open air reminded him of the field behind his
family’s compound where he had run that night, hiding until dawn. More than anything, he avoided the
smell of alcohol. A coworker’s beer at a warehouse barbecue, a stranger’s breath on the bus—these
could send him into a cold sweat, his chest tightening, his hands shaking.

When the smell hit him, or when a door slammed in the warehouse office and the sound cracked through
him like a whip, Kwame’s first instinct was to drown it. He drank alcohol, took medication he bought
from a friend, or used whatever he could find to blunt the edge. The drinking did not stop the
memories, though; it loosened them, and late at night, after a few beers, the fragmented pieces
would surface more vividly, the disconnected images spinning faster. He had come to believe that if
he stopped moving, if he stopped drinking, the terror would swallow him whole. So he picked
arguments, too. He would snap at a coworker over a misplaced pallet, or start a fight with his ex-
wife on the phone over nothing, because the rush of anger was simpler than the rush of fear. The
conflict distracted him, gave him something to hold onto, but it also left him isolated. His
children stopped calling as often. His supervisor put him on warning.

The cycle tightened over the months. A door slamming at work would trigger a surge of fear, and
Kwame would head to the bar after his shift, drinking until the edges softened. But the drinking
made the memory more intrusive, and the next morning he would be raw and irritable, picking a fight
with anyone nearby, which in turn kept him out of the warehouse—avoiding the triggers he knew were
waiting. He had come to believe that he was weak, that other men had survived worse and moved on,
and that his inability to let go of a childhood night meant something was fundamentally broken in
him. What frightened him most was the feeling itself: the terror without a picture, the way his body
could be hijacked by something he could not even see.

What finally brought Kwame to the clinic was his supervisor’s ultimatum—one more incident, and he
was out—and the quiet realization that he had spent seventy-nine years running from a night he could
not even properly remember. He sat in the intake chair, his hands on his knees, and said, “I just
want to stop feeling it. I don’t even know what I’m afraid of anymore.”
