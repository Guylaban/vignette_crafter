Leilani was twenty years old, a recent bride and an interior designer just starting to build her
client list, when the man she had married began to hurt her. It started with a shove in the kitchen,
then a slap that sent her into the wall, then a night when he pinned her down in their dark bedroom
and told her she was nothing without him. She left after that night, but the leaving did not end
anything. The memory of his hands, his voice in the dark, the way she had frozen—these things
stayed. And when she tried to tell her mother what happened, the words fell apart. She would start a
sentence and then lose the thread, the room going blurry, and she would stop talking altogether. She
felt the guilt like a stone in her chest—she had chosen him, she had stayed too long, she had let it
happen. Sometimes, without warning, the full wave of terror would wash over her while she was
driving or folding laundry, and there was no image attached, just the body feeling everything again.

She had come to believe that her life was destroyed. She told herself she no longer belonged
anywhere—not with her family, who looked at her with pity, and not in the world of other couples,
who seemed untouched. People could not be trusted. This belief sat beneath everything. It made her
watchful. She walked into a room and scanned every face, every exit, her body constantly tense and
ready. She checked behind her when she walked to her car, certain someone was following. When she
saw a man who resembled her ex-husband—same build, same jaw—her heart slammed and her throat closed.
She had to get out of the store, out of the street, anywhere away.

Being alone with any man now felt impossible. She had stopped meeting new clients in their homes
unless another woman was present, and even then she kept the door open. Loud noises made her flinch
so hard she dropped things. She avoided the dark altogether—she slept with every light on, and if a
bulb burned out she would stay in the living room until morning. She had come to believe that the
world was full of threat, and that she had to be ready. This vigilance exhausted her, but it also
felt necessary. She was certain that if she let her guard down, something terrible would happen
again.

The rumination was constant. She blamed herself for what happened, replaying the moments she could
have left, the signs she should have seen. This self-blame fed the guilt that rose when she
remembered the event, and the guilt in turn made her want to avoid her own mind. She could not be
alone in a room for very long—her thoughts would circle, and the emotion would spike without
warning, and she would call her sister or just walk outside. But being alone in the dark was worse,
because the memory of that night would surface without an image, just the pressure of his weight and
the smell of his breath, and then the guilt would hit again.

The avoidance shaped her life. She turned down a project that required evening site visits. She
stopped going to a coffee shop she had loved because a man there had a voice like her ex-husband's.
Each time she avoided, she confirmed to herself that she could not handle the world, that her life
was narrowed and ruined. The mistrust deepened. She began to believe that her husband had shown her
who people really were, and that she had been a fool to think otherwise.

What brought her to therapy was a moment at work. A contractor dropped a metal ladder in the
hallway, and the sound sent her into a panic so severe she hid in a supply closet for twenty
minutes, shaking. Her boss found her and asked if she was safe. She said yes, but she was not sure
she believed it. She was most afraid that she would never feel safe again, that the guilt and the
hypervigilance would never stop, that the man who hurt her had taken more than she had realized. But
she was also, quietly, hoping that someone could help her find a way back to herself—to the person
who had not yet learned to be afraid.
