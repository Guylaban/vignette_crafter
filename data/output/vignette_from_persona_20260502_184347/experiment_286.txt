The flood came at night. Mei Lin, then forty-seven, had been asleep when the sirens started, a low
wail that seemed to come from everywhere at once. She remembered the water rising with terrifying
speed, the cold against her ankles, then her knees. She had grabbed her husband’s hand and they had
pushed through the dark, waist-deep water, the sound of things breaking and people screaming. What
stayed with her most, though, was not the noise but the silence afterward—the sudden, absolute quiet
when the rain stopped and the water stilled, and she stood in the dark, not knowing if anyone else
was alive. That silence had become a trapdoor in her mind.

Now, five years later, Mei Lin worked as a librarian at a quiet branch in the suburbs. The job
should have been safe, but the library’s long, still aisles and the hush of patrons reading had
become unbearable. A sudden silence—a lull in conversation, the moment between turning pages—could
drop her straight back into that water. The memory came without warning, triggered by something as
simple as a patron walking away and leaving an empty, quiet space. She would feel the cold, hear the
distant scream, and the grief would hit her like a physical blow, so overwhelming she sometimes
gasped out loud. In those moments, she had learned to deliberately leave her body, to blank out
until the wave passed, because staying present felt impossible.

The dark was worse. Waking up in the middle of the night, the bedroom pitch-black, she felt the
terror surge before she was even fully conscious. Her heart would pound, and she would scan the
corners of the room, searching for signs of danger. She had become hypervigilant at night, lying
rigid, her ears straining for any sound that might signal the water returning. This constant
watchfulness meant she rarely slept deeply, and when she did, she was often jolted awake by a memory
triggered by the very darkness around her. The threat she felt at night made her avoid going to bed
until she was exhausted, and even then, she insisted on a small nightlight—a habit that embarrassed
her but felt non-negotiable.

During the day, Mei Lin had developed a set of quiet rituals. She always chose a seat with her back
to a wall, preferably near an exit. In the library break room, she would sit at the far end of the
table, her chair positioned so she could see both doors. She knew this was odd, but the feeling of
being unprotected was worse. This constant scanning for danger meant she was never fully relaxed.
She noticed that when she was on high alert—when a colleague mentioned a storm, or when the lights
flickered—her mind would involuntarily leap to the memory of the flood, and then the grief would
follow, and she would need to dissociate to get through the rest of her shift. The threat she felt
was not just a feeling; it actively summoned the very memories she was trying to escape.

The avoidance had narrowed her life considerably. She had stopped exercising, because the sound of
her own heavy breathing reminded her of gasping for air in the dark water. She avoided being in
circumstances that reminded her of the event, which increasingly meant avoiding the dark, avoiding
quiet places, and avoiding anyone who had been through the flood. She had stopped attending
community meetings and had pulled away from neighbors who had also survived, because their presence
was itself a trigger. Each time she avoided something, she felt a brief relief, but soon after, the
triggers seemed to multiply. The more she dissociated, the more easily the memories slipped through,
unbidden, and the more vigilant she became, waiting for the next one.

What finally brought Mei Lin to her therapist’s office was a morning at the library. A child had
been reading aloud, and then stopped, and in the sudden silence, Mei Lin had felt the memory crash
over her so completely that she had stood frozen in the stacks, unable to move or speak for nearly a
minute. She had excused herself to the bathroom and deliberately blanked out, but afterward, she
could not shake the feeling that she was losing control. She was afraid that one day, she would not
be able to come back. What she hoped for, more than anything, was to sit in a quiet room without
being pulled under.
