She remembered the exact shade of the waiting room carpet—a dull, industrial gray—and the way the
fluorescent lights hummed as the oncologist spoke words that seemed to come from a great distance:
*survival rate*, *aggressive protocol*, *immediate admission*. For six months, Malia lived in that
hospital room. She learned the beep of every monitor, the weight of Tama’s body gone thin against
her chest during transfusions, the way the nurses’ faces would tighten just before delivering bad
news. She stopped sleeping. She stopped eating unless someone put food in her hand. She became a
machine of vigilance, calibrated to the smallest change in her son’s breathing, the slightest shift
in the machines that kept him alive. When Tama finally went into remission, Malia thought she could
go home and resume her life. Instead, she found that she had brought the hospital with her.

As a network engineer, Malia spent her days in a data center where the air was cool and the servers
hummed in a steady, predictable rhythm. She had always found comfort in the order of cables and
protocols—things that behaved as they were supposed to. But now, the hum of the cooling fans
sometimes sounded like the ventilator that had breathed for Tama during his worst night, and she
would freeze at her workstation, her heart slamming against her ribs. The smallest unexpected
movement—a colleague rounding a corner too quickly, a cable slipping from its rack—would send a jolt
through her body, her hands flying up as if to ward off a blow. She had started to feel physical
reactions to these moments: a rush of heat, a trembling in her legs, a sensation of choking. She
told herself it was just stress, just the memory of those months, but her body did not believe her.

Arguments had become unbearable. When a colleague pushed back on a proposed system change, Malia
felt the familiar tightening in her chest—the same feeling she had had when a doctor hesitated
before answering a question about Tama’s counts. She had come to believe that any conflict, any
raised voice, was a sign that something was about to go terribly wrong. She began to avoid people
she knew well—her sister, her ex-husband, even the neighbors who had brought meals during the
treatment—because their faces reminded her of the fear she had seen in their eyes when they visited
the hospital. Being around them made her feel exposed, as if they could see the panic she worked so
hard to hide. What frightened her most was that she no longer felt safe in her own home. At night,
she would check the locks three times, then lie awake listening for the sound of a beeping monitor
that was not there. She had become hyperalert to the ordinary sounds of her own house: the creak of
a floorboard, the hum of the refrigerator, the distant siren that might mean an ambulance was
carrying someone else’s child to the hospital.

The connection between these experiences had become a closed loop she could not escape. A trigger—an
argument, a medical appointment, the sight of a familiar face—would set off a cascade of physical
reactivity and hypervigilance, and that state of high alert made her more sensitive to the next
trigger, so that even a minor disagreement felt like a crisis. Last month, she had to have a routine
blood draw for a work physical, and the sight of the needle sent her into a full-blown panic attack.
She fled the clinic and did not go back. She had started to believe that the only way to survive was
to stay small, stay quiet, stay away from anyone who might remind her of what she had almost lost.
But that strategy had its own cost. She had stopped taking calls from her sister, stopped attending
team meetings in person, stopped going to the grocery store after a chance encounter with a former
nurse left her shaking in the frozen foods aisle for twenty minutes.

What finally brought Malia to seek help was a small thing, or so it seemed: her supervisor mentioned
that she had been passed over for a promotion because her “interpersonal availability” was in
question. The word *unavailable* echoed in her mind for days. She realized that she had become a
ghost in her own life, present only in the spaces where no one could reach her. She was terrified
that if she let her guard down, even for a moment, something terrible would happen again—but she was
beginning to understand that the vigilance itself was destroying her. She came to therapy hoping to
find a way back to the person she had been before the diagnosis, though she was not sure that person
still existed. What she feared most was that the answer might be no.
