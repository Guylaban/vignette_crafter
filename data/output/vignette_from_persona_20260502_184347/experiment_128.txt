Layla, an 80-year-old widow and retired social worker of Middle Eastern descent, still lives in the
apartment she shared with her late husband. The trauma that reshaped her life was not a single
violent moment but a slow, agonizing ordeal: her son’s childhood leukemia diagnosis and the two
years of treatment that followed. He survived, now a healthy man in his fifties, but for Layla, the
event never ended. She can still feel the full, suffocating weight of the grief she felt standing in
the hospital corridor, the antiseptic smell so thick it seemed to coat her tongue, even though no
clear image of that moment comes to mind—only the emotion, raw and undimmed, as if her body had
forgotten the picture but kept the feeling. The memory forces its way into her mind when she least
expects it, often triggered by something as simple as the scent of rubbing alcohol in a doctor’s
office or a passing comment on the news about someone losing a loved one. When this happens, the
overwhelming grief she felt back then returns, and she is left feeling helpless, certain that she
has permanently changed for the worse, that the woman she was before the illness is gone forever.

These triggers do more than just bring the memory back; they also reinforce her deepest negative
appraisals. Every time she smells a hospital or sees a needle, she is flooded not only with grief
but with the conviction that she acted in ways that go against who she is—that she was too
frightened, too weak, too consumed by her own panic to be the mother her son needed. She had come to
believe that she cannot stop bad things from happening, and each triggered episode only deepens that
belief, making her feel even more helpless and damaged. This appraisal, in turn, makes her
hypervigilant to any potential trigger in her environment, scanning for the next olfactory or visual
cue that might confirm her worst fears about herself. She now avoids going out unless absolutely
necessary, and when she does, she takes circuitous routes to steer clear of the hospital district.
She has also learned to suppress the thoughts when they intrude, trying to push them out of her
mind, though this effort often leaves her feeling more exhausted and more convinced of her own
permanent damage. The maladaptive strategy of avoidance—refusing to see a doctor for her own aches
and pains, canceling appointments, ignoring routine health screenings—has only deepened her sense of
helplessness, creating a loop where her actions confirm her worst appraisals. The memory itself,
when it intrudes, strengthens her belief that she is irreparably broken, and that belief makes the
next memory more likely to surface, as she remains on constant alert for any sign of failure.

The most dominant connection in her experience is the one she feels tightening every day: the memory
triggers her negative appraisals, and those appraisals trigger the memory in turn, a closed circuit
with no exit. She will be sitting quietly, and the grief will rise unbidden; then the thought
follows, *I failed him, I am not the same person, I am helpless*, and that thought alone is enough
to summon the memory again, as if her mind is proving her right. She has come to believe that she is
a permanent victim of her own past, and this belief has cost her. She no longer visits her son’s
family as often, fearing that a stray comment about a sick friend or a child’s fever will pull her
back into that hospital room. Her daughter-in-law has noticed, and Layla knows she has hurt them,
but the shame of that only adds to her sense of permanent damage.

What finally brought her to seek help was not the grief itself, but the realization that she had not
been to a doctor in four years, even as her own body began to ache. She is terrified of what she
might find, but she is also terrified of what she has become: a woman who cannot bear to walk past a
hospital, who cannot talk about loss without collapsing, who has let her own trauma slowly erase her
from her family’s life. She is hoping, more than anything, to find a way to feel like herself
again—though she is not sure she remembers who that is.
