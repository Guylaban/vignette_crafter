Mara is a 27-year-old non-binary special education teacher who works with elementary-aged children
in a self-contained classroom. They came to therapy three months after the end of a six-month
stalking campaign by a former colleague who had been reassigned to a different school. The stalking
began subtly—notes left on Mara’s desk, small objects moved in their classroom—then escalated to
late-night phone calls, the colleague appearing outside Mara’s apartment building, and finally a
confrontation in the school parking lot where the colleague followed Mara to their car, whispering
threats through the driver-side window. Mara reported the behavior to administration, but the
colleague was only reassigned, not fired, and Mara continued to see them at district meetings. The
police were involved only once, after Mara found a handwritten letter tucked under their windshield
wiper that read, “I know your route home.”

What stays with Mara most is not a single image but a series of fragments: the sound of footsteps
accelerating behind them in the parking lot, the smell of the colleague’s cologne, the sight of
their own hand shaking as they fumbled for the car keys. Some moments are impossibly clear—the
colleague’s face reflected in the window, the exact shade of the sky—while other parts are
completely blank, like the drive home that night or what they said to the police officer. The memory
feels like a puzzle with missing pieces, and Mara has spent hours trying to fill in the gaps,
replaying the parking lot scene over and over, wondering what they could have done differently, how
they might have prevented it entirely.

Now, whenever Mara hears footsteps behind them—in the school hallway, on the sidewalk, in the
grocery store—their mind immediately returns to those fragmented, incomplete images. The sound of
footsteps is the most potent trigger; it sends Mara’s thoughts spiraling back into the gaps of the
memory, trying to reconstruct what happened, trying to find the missing pieces. A single footstep in
a quiet hallway is enough to make Mara’s chest tighten and their vision narrow, as if they are back
in that parking lot, fumbling for keys, the footsteps getting closer.

Because the triggers feel so unpredictable, Mara has developed a set of strategies to manage the
fear. They have stopped relaxing entirely—no music in the car, no podcasts, no sitting with their
back to a door. Being still feels dangerous, as if lowering their guard invites the threat to
return. Instead, Mara constantly scans their environment: every corner they turn, every car that
passes, every person who walks too close. At work, they check the hallway before leaving their
classroom, they stand with their back to the wall during fire drills, and they have memorized every
exit route in the building. These strategies have become a full-time job, and they are exhausting,
but Mara is certain that if they stop scanning, if they let themselves relax, something bad will
happen.

The scanning itself has become a trigger. The more Mara checks their surroundings, the more they
notice ambiguous signs—a figure lingering near the playground, a car that seems to be driving slowly
past the school—and each one sends a fresh jolt of fear through their body. The checking also makes
the memory worse. Every time Mara scans and finds nothing, the relief is short-lived, and the
fragmented images come back stronger: the footsteps, the letter, the face in the window. Mara has
begun to wonder if they are going crazy, because the memory feels less real each time they try to
hold it, yet the fear is sharper than ever.

The hypervigilance has bled into every part of Mara’s life. They cannot sit through a staff meeting
without flinching every time someone shifts in their seat. A sudden movement from a student—a child
reaching for a pencil, a hand raised too quickly—makes Mara’s heart pound and their vision tunnel.
They know this is not rational, but their body reacts before their mind can catch up. The threat
feels constant, as if danger is hiding in every sudden motion, every unexpected sound.

cannot tolerate the feeling of being outside after dark. Mara has stopped attending school events,

old apartment. The avoidance has shrunk their world, but it feels necessary. What brought Mara to
therapy was a moment in the classroom last week: a child ran up behind Mara to show them a drawing,

shaking, unable to speak, and realized that the strategies they had built to keep themselves safe
were now hurting the children they were supposed to protect. They are terrified that they will never
feel safe again, but they are equally terrified of what they might become if they keep living this
