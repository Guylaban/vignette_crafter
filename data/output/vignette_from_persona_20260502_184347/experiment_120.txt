adult daughter after months of progressive withdrawal from his work and social life. Eight months
ago, he was driving home from a client meeting on a rain-slicked highway when a delivery truck
hydroplaned across the median and struck his car head-on. He remembers the sound of tearing metal
and the sensation of his own body being thrown sideways against the door—then the long, strange
quiet afterward, broken by the hiss of steam from the crumpled engine. He spent three days in the
hospital with a fractured pelvis and a concussion, but the physical injuries healed faster than the
ones he could not name.

Since the accident, Mark has found himself unable to drive past the stretch of highway where it
happened without his chest tightening and his palms going slick on the steering wheel. He avoids
that route entirely now, taking a longer back road even when he is late for appointments. Car sounds
have become unbearable: the rumble of a diesel engine in a parking garage, the screech of brakes at
a stoplight—each one sends a jolt through him, and he catches himself scanning the road ahead for
trucks the way a soldier might scan for snipers. He has also stopped watching the evening news
because local traffic reports sometimes include accident footage, and he cannot tolerate the sight
of a car with its front end caved in. Last month, a colleague mentioned a minor fender-bender during
a team meeting, and Mark excused himself to the restroom, where he stood gripping the sink until his
hands stopped shaking.

What troubles Mark most, though, is not the fear itself but what it has done to his sense of who he
is. He used to pride himself on his composure, his ability to walk into any client's home and calmly
reimagine their space. Now he feels like a stranger in his own body. "I can no longer trust my own
body," he told me during our first session, his voice flat. He described a moment two weeks ago when
he reached to pick up a coffee mug and his hand trembled so badly he had to set it down. He had come
to believe that the accident permanently damaged something inside him—not just bones or muscles, but
the fundamental reliability of his own reflexes, his own judgment. He feels like he doesn't know
himself anymore. The man who used to navigate complex renovations with steady hands has been
replaced by someone who startles at the sound of a delivery van and avoids highway exits. "I have
permanently changed for the worse," he said, and the words hung in the air as though he were
pronouncing a verdict.

His work has suffered. He turned down a major hotel lobby project because the site was adjacent to a
busy intersection. He finds himself canceling site visits at the last minute, claiming illness, when
the route involves a road where he might encounter heavy truck traffic. His daughter, a graduate
student in social work, was the one who finally insisted he seek help. She told him, gently, that
she missed the father who used to laugh easily and plan weekend carpentry projects. Mark sat in my
office, his hands folded in his lap, and said he was afraid of what he might find out about himself
if he kept going this way—but also afraid of what it would mean if therapy could not fix him.
