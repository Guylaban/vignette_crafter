Marcus was thirty-two years old, a combat pilot who had spent years training to stay calm under
fire, to trust his wingmen with his life. That trust had never been tested in the air. It was tested
on the ground, at a squadron party two years ago, when a fellow officer he had known for years
followed him into a quiet hallway and assaulted him. Marcus had frozen. He had not fought back. He
had not said a word afterward. What stayed with him, more than the physical memory, was the
sickening realization that someone he had shared a ready room with, someone he had briefed with
before missions, had done that. He had not told anyone. He had simply gone back to flying, convinced
that if he kept moving, the whole thing would dissolve.

It did not dissolve. In the months that followed, Marcus found himself scanning the faces of his
fellow officers during pre-flight briefings, watching for something he could not name. A particular
way a man held his gaze too long, or the way someone laughed a little too loudly in the
hallway—these moments snagged his attention and held it. He had come to believe that the world was
full of people who would betray you, that his own judgment of character was fundamentally broken,
and that his failure to react during the assault proved he was a lousy coper, someone who could not
be counted on. These thoughts were not abstract. They surfaced every time a door closed behind him
in the hangar, every time a colleague walked up behind his chair without warning. In those moments,
his certainty that he was weak and that others were dangerous fused into something immediate and
physical.

The triggers were everywhere now. A man walking too close behind him in the PX. A hand on his
shoulder that was not expected. Waking from a nightmare—always the same hallway, the same moment of
freezing—and lying in the dark, listening for sounds that might mean someone was entering his home.
He had taken to checking the locks on his apartment door three times before bed, and he slept with a
small knife on the nightstand, though he was ashamed of it. The sound of a dropped clipboard in the
hangar could make him flinch so violently that another pilot once asked if he was okay. Marcus had
laughed it off, but the question burrowed into him. See, he thought. You can’t even handle a noise.

He began to avoid the squadron lounge. He stopped going to the gym at the same time as the others,
shifting his schedule to late evenings when the place was empty. He told himself it was about focus,
about getting more done. But what he was really doing was managing the unpredictable—the sudden
appearance of someone in his peripheral vision, the unexpected greeting from behind. At work, he
stayed in his cockpit as long as possible before and after flights, the only place where the space
was his and the threat was known. His crew chief noticed and said nothing, but the silence between
them grew heavier.

The loop tightened. Every time he jumped at a sound, he told himself he was falling apart. Every
time he felt that flash of vigilance in his own home, he concluded that the assault had broken
something in him permanently. And these conclusions, in turn, made him more watchful, more ready to
see threat in ordinary faces. He had lost his faith in people entirely. He no longer believed in
loyalty. He believed that everyone was capable of doing what that officer had done, and that he was
too weak to stop them.

What finally brought Marcus to the clinic was not the nightmares or the startle response or the
isolation. It was a moment in the briefing room, six weeks ago, when his commanding officer asked
him to lead a two-ship formation with a new wingman. The request was routine. But Marcus felt his
chest lock, his vision narrow. He saw the new pilot’s face, young and eager and unknown, and he
thought: I don’t trust him. I can’t fly with someone I don’t trust. He had excused himself to the
bathroom and stood there, gripping the sink, breathing in shallow gasps. He knew then that the thing
he had been trying to outrun was now sitting in the cockpit with him. He was afraid he was going to
get himself or someone else killed. He made the appointment that afternoon. He was terrified that
the therapist would confirm what he already believed—that he was broken, that he could not be
fixed—but he was more terrified of what would happen if he kept flying alone.
