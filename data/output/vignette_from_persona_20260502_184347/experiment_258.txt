Ravi is a 52-year-old South Asian man who works as a maid in a suburban hotel. He is divorced and
lives alone in a small apartment. His PCL-5 score is 51, indicating severe posttraumatic symptoms.
The traumatic event occurred six months ago, while he was cleaning a guest room. A heavy dresser had
been pulled away from the wall, and when Ravi tried to push it back, it tipped forward, pinning a
housekeeping supervisor who had entered the room behind him. The supervisor suffered a fractured
pelvis and a concussion. Ravi remembers the event as a series of frozen snapshots: the dresser
tilting, the supervisor’s face, the sound of her body hitting the floor, the silence afterward. Each
moment is a picture in his mind, and he can recall it with unbearable clarity.

When the memory surfaces, it comes without warning—a sudden, full return that leaves him breathless
and trembling. He might be vacuuming a hallway and the angle of light through a window will trigger
the image of the dresser’s edge. Or he will hear a sharp sound in the hotel—a door slamming, a cart
dropping—and the memory crashes back, bringing with it the same helplessness and shock he felt in
that moment. But what comes next, always, is rage. When Ravi remembers the event, he feels intense
rage—not at the supervisor, but at himself. The rage is hot and immediate, and it feeds directly
into how he appraises what happened. He has come to believe that the event happened because of the
way he acted, that he should have known better, that he should have checked the dresser’s balance
before pushing it. This belief has taken root deeply: he violated his own values. He sees himself as
a careful, conscientious person, someone who takes pride in doing his job well. Now he cannot
reconcile that identity with what he did. He acted in ways that go against who he is, and that
thought hollows him out. Nothing in his life feels meaningful anymore. His work, which once gave him
a sense of purpose and structure, now feels like a stage for failure.

Over the weeks following the incident, Ravi began to avoid certain tasks. He would ask coworkers to
handle rooms that required moving furniture, and when that was not possible, he would rush through
the job, his heart pounding, his hands shaking. He stopped taking breaks in the staff lounge because
the supervisor sometimes came through there on crutches. He stopped talking to his colleagues about
anything personal, afraid they might ask about the accident or, worse, judge him. He began to work
longer hours, often staying late to clean rooms no one else wanted, as if punishing himself could
make the memory quieter. It did not. The memory and the appraisals had become a loop: the snapshot
returned, the rage flared, the self-blame deepened, and meaning drained further from each day.

Ravi has not told anyone the full extent of what he feels. He is certain that if people knew what he
thought about himself, they would agree with his judgment. What finally brought him to seek help was
a moment three weeks ago when he was cleaning a room and saw a small, unsteady table near a window.
He stopped moving. He stood there for several minutes, unable to approach it, his mind filling with
images of the dresser, the supervisor’s face, the sound. He left the room, told his manager he was
sick, and went home. That night, he could not sleep. He lay in the dark, replaying the event,
feeling the rage and the shame, and for the first time, he thought, *I cannot keep living like
this.* He is afraid of what therapy might uncover—that he is truly to blame, that there is no way to
undo what he did—but he is more afraid of staying where he is, trapped in the pictures that will not
stop coming.
