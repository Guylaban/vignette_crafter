worked toward since she was eighteen, but the woman who sat in the chair looked more like someone
bracing for a storm than someone who commanded a vessel. She was twenty-three, single, Southeast
Asian American, and she spoke in short, clipped sentences, her eyes scanning the room the way she
scanned the radar at night. The traumatic event was not a single moment but a childhood that

house was quiet, when she was already in bed. She learned to sleep with one eye open, to listen for
the creak of the floorboards, to map every shadow in her room. The darkness itself became a signal:
something bad was about to happen.

maritime training, into the discipline of navigation, into the bright, fluorescent-lit world of a
ship’s bridge. But the fear had not left. It had only learned to attach to new things. Now, as a
captain, she found herself unable to sleep without a light on. Nighttime, or waking up in the dark,
sent a spike of cold terror through her chest. Her body went tense, her muscles coiled, ready to
react to danger that was not there. She had come to believe that the world was a dangerous place,
that danger could come from anywhere, that she was somehow defective, broken in a way that other
people were not. She felt isolated from her crew, set apart by something she could not name. When
she drove with other people in the car, she felt trapped, her eyes darting to the rearview mirror,
her hands gripping the wheel, her mind racing through escape routes before the car even left the
dock. She always identified escape routes before she felt comfortable anywhere—every restaurant,
every meeting room, every hallway on her own ship. Being alone with a male crew member, even for a
routine briefing, made her stomach clench. She would keep the door open, position herself near the
exit, and feel the threat rise in her throat like bile. These triggers did not just happen to her;
they confirmed what she already believed. The darkness, the car, the man—each one was proof that the
world was unsafe, that she was right to be afraid. And because she believed the world was dangerous,
she saw danger everywhere. Every shadow became a threat. Every closed door became a trap. The cycle
tightened.

Over the months, she began to avoid situations that might trigger her. She stopped accepting rides
from colleagues, even when it was practical. She insisted on driving herself, even when exhausted.
She rearranged her watch schedule to avoid being alone on the bridge with certain male officers,
inventing reasons that sounded professional but felt hollow. She stopped going to social gatherings
after dark. The isolation she already felt deepened. Her crew noticed. They called her cold,
distant, unfriendly. She did not correct them. What she felt was not coldness; it was a constant,
humming readiness for violence. Her body was on alert all the time. She had strong negative
feelings—fear, shame, anger—that rose without warning, sometimes in the middle of a calm
conversation. She could not explain them. She thought there was something fundamentally wrong with
her, that she was defective, that other people did not live like this. The threat she felt made the
world seem more dangerous, and the dangerous world made her feel more threatened. She was trapped
inside a logic that never let go.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment on the bridge, three weeks ago. It was night. The
sea was calm. She was alone with a male deckhand who was simply reviewing the log. He did nothing
wrong. But her body went into full alarm. She felt her heart pound, her breath shorten, her vision
narrow. She had to excuse herself to the bathroom, where she stood shaking over the sink, staring at
her own face in the mirror. She realized she could not keep living this way. She was afraid of the
dark, afraid of her own crew, afraid of the job she had worked her whole life to earn. She came to
therapy because she wanted to stop running. But what frightened her most was the possibility that
she was right—that the world really was that dangerous, that she really was that broken, and that no
amount of talking would ever make the darkness feel safe again.
