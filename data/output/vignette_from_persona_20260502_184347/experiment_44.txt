path, now pinned beneath the trunk. Graham ran down the slope, screaming for help on his radio, but
by the time the medics arrived, the man’s leg was crushed beyond saving. In the weeks that followed,
Graham could not stop replaying the moment the tree began to fall—not just the image, but the
sensation of his own arms holding the saw, the vibration in his palms, the exact angle of his
stance. When the memory surfaced, his body would lock into that same posture: shoulders braced,
hands gripping an invisible handle, heart pounding as if the saw were still running. What frightened
him most was the rage that followed, a white-hot fury at the hiker for being there, at the wind, at
himself—rage that felt as fresh as the day it happened, and that left him ashamed and hollow.

The physical pain in his lower back, where he had strained himself running down the hill, became a
trigger he could not escape. Every time he bent to lift a log or climbed into his truck, the ache
would flare, and the thought would come unbidden: *This is because of what I did.* He had come to
believe that there was something fundamentally wrong with him—some intrinsic fault that had caused
the accident, some hidden carelessness he could not see but others could. He told his wife, Marie,
that he could no longer trust himself to make even simple decisions. "I can't rely on myself
anymore," he said one night, staring at the kitchen floor. He stopped volunteering for the fire
crew, stopped leading trail maintenance. He felt he was no longer capable of the work that had
defined him for thirty years.

county knew what he had done. He found he could not relax, even at home in his own living room. He
sat with his back to the wall, facing the door, listening for footsteps. The threat felt constant,

vivid. The more he scanned for danger, the more his mind threw him back to the moment of impact—the
sound, the angle of the sun, the way the saw's chain had been slick with sap. And each memory

had occurred, and took longer routes to avoid any trailhead where hikers might be present. Physical
proximity to other people, especially strangers on the logging roads, sent his heart racing. He
stopped eating lunch in the communal break room, preferring to sit alone in his truck. His
supervisor noticed and asked if something was wrong. Graham said he was fine, but he knew he was
not. The grief would hit him without warning—not just for the hiker, whose name he had learned was

accident had revealed a truth about himself he had never seen before, and that truth could not be
undone.

What finally brought Graham to seek help was a morning in late March, when he found himself standing
in the middle of a clear-cut, unable to remember how he had gotten there, his hands shaking, his

afraid of was that she would confirm what he already suspected—that he was broken in a way that
could not be fixed. But he was also, quietly, hoping that she might tell him otherwise.
