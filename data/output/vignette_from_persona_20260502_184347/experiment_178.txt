Kai was twenty-four when the relationship ended the way it did. He had been a reporter for a local
paper then, still living with his partner in a small apartment near the bus line he took to work.
The abuse had built slowly—criticism, then isolation, then the first time he was shoved against the
kitchen counter so hard his ribs met the edge. He remembered the sound more than the words. What
stayed with him was the feeling of being pinned, of his body refusing to move even when the door was
open. Years later, when he thought about it—which he tried not to—the memory did not feel like
something that had ended. It arrived with the same pressure in his chest, the same ache along his
left ribs, the same wave of heat that made him stop whatever he was doing and grip the edge of a
desk or a table until it passed. The physical pain would return exactly where it had been, and the
emotion that came with it was so overwhelming he sometimes had to leave the room.

Kai worked as a journalist for a city paper now, and the job required him to take the bus to
interviews and to sit across from sources he had never met. He had not anticipated how much that
would cost him. Every time a bus ran late, he felt a cold certainty that something terrible was
happening—that the driver had been attacked, that a passenger had a weapon, that the delay was a
sign of danger he could not see. He had learned, over the months, that he could not trust his own
sense of safety. Even when colleagues told him the bus was just stuck in traffic, he found it
impossible to believe them. What frightened him most was the unpredictability of other people. He
had come to believe that you could never really know who would harm you, and that trust was a
mistake he was not going to make again. The world had become a place where any man, alone with him
in a room or on a train, might turn into a threat. He avoided being alone with men whenever
possible. He had stopped taking the bus entirely after the third panic attack, and now he walked
forty minutes to work each way, rain or shine.

The walking helped in one way, but it did not touch the rest of it. At night, he woke several times
to check the lock on his door, to scan the room, to confirm that nothing had changed. He had begun
to restrict what he ate, skipping meals when he felt the familiar tightness in his chest, because
eating felt like a vulnerability he could not afford. He never allowed himself to feel or express
anger. He had learned, during the relationship, that anger was dangerous—that it would be used
against him, that it meant he was the problem. So he turned it inward instead. He dwelled on what he
had done wrong, replaying conversations and arguments until he was certain that the abuse had been
his fault somehow, that he was inadequate, that he had failed to be the person his partner needed.
He had come to believe that his own inadequacy was the reason he had been hurt, and that belief made
the world feel dangerous in a way that was constant and personal. Every delay, every closed door,
every man who looked at him too long felt like confirmation that he was right to be afraid.

His girlfriend noticed. She had asked him, gently, why he would not let her drive him to work, why
he flinched when she touched his shoulder from behind. He told her it was nothing. He had stopped
telling her the truth because the truth made him feel weak, and weakness was what had gotten him
hurt in the first place. The cycle had tightened over the past year without him naming it: a memory
would surface, and he would feel the old helplessness, and that helplessness would convince him that
he was the one at fault, and that conviction would make the world seem more threatening, and the
threat would send him back to the memory. He was exhausted. He was losing weight. He had turned down
two assignments that required interviews with men he did not know, and his editor had started to ask
questions.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment on the street last week. A man walking behind him
had matched his pace for half a block, and Kai had frozen, his ribs aching, his breath gone, certain
that he was about to be hurt again. The man had passed him without a word. But Kai had stood there
for five minutes, shaking, and he had realized that he could not keep living this way. He was afraid
of what he might become if he did not find a way out. He was also afraid that there was no way
out—that he was broken in a way that could not be fixed. But he made the appointment anyway, and he
showed up, and that was the first thing he had done in years that felt like it might be a step
toward something else.
