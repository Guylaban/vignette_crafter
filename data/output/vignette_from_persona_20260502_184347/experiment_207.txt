Leo is a 49-year-old plumber, married for twenty-two years, who lives with his wife in a split-level
house in a suburban Ohio town. He came to therapy at his wife’s urging, after she found him pacing
the basement at 3 a.m. for the fourth night in a row, unable to explain why. His score on the PCL-5
was 72, reflecting severe posttraumatic symptoms.

The event that shattered Leo’s sense of safety happened three years ago, in the garage of his own
home. His partner at the time—a man he had been seeing secretly for eight months—had come over after
a fight. Leo had tried to end the relationship. What followed was a sustained physical assault: he
was shoved against the workbench, punched in the ribs, and then, as he lay on the concrete floor,
his partner pressed a lit cigarette into the back of his neck. Leo remembers the smell of his own
burnt skin mixing with gasoline fumes from the lawnmower. He did not call the police. He told
himself it was his fault for getting involved, for not seeing it coming, for being weak. He never
told his wife the full story.

In the months that followed, Leo found himself unable to go near the garage. He started parking his
van on the street and entering the house through the front door, even in rain or snow. He would
leave tools in the backyard rather than return them to the garage. This avoidance of the event site
became a fixed part of his routine, and he never questioned it—he simply could not bear the thought
of that concrete floor, the smell of gasoline.

The triggers that now governed his daily life were specific and potent. A burnt smell—from a
toaster, a neighbor’s barbecue, even the faint scorch of a hair dryer—would stop him mid-sentence,
his chest tightening. More disruptive were confrontations. As a plumber, Leo regularly dealt with
angry homeowners, frustrated by backed-up drains or burst pipes. When a customer raised their voice,
even calmly, Leo felt his throat close. The most difficult trigger was any expression of anger from
another person, even if it was not directed at him. He had come to believe that anger meant
danger—that any raised voice or sharp tone was the beginning of violence.

These triggers drove Leo into a set of maladaptive strategies that hollowed out his life. When a
customer expressed frustration, Leo would nod quickly, agree to anything, and leave the job early,
losing income. He avoided thinking about the assault entirely; if a memory surfaced while he was
driving between jobs, he would turn on talk radio at full volume and force his mind to a blank. At
night, he could not bring himself to go to bed. He would stay up until very late, watching
television or scrolling through his phone, because he had learned that if he went to sleep, he would
dream of the garage. This delay of sleep became a nightly ritual, and he would often not lie down
until 3 or 4 a.m., then wake exhausted at 6.

The pattern tightened over two years. The burnt smell from a faulty water heater on a job site would
send him into a panic, and afterward he would cancel his next appointment and drive home, where he
would sit in the dark living room until his wife returned. He had come to interpret his own racing
heart as proof that the world was unsafe, that danger was imminent. His wife noticed he had stopped
going to family gatherings; he could not tolerate his brother-in-law’s loud voice, even when the man
was joking. Leo had lost three regular clients because he would not return calls after a
confrontation.

What finally brought Leo to seek help was a moment last month when his wife asked him to pick up a
tool from the garage. He stood at the side door for fifteen minutes, unable to open it, and then
walked away. She found him in the kitchen, crying silently. He is most afraid that if he lets
himself remember the assault fully, he will fall apart completely. But he is also, for the first
time, hoping that someone can show him a way out of the garage.
