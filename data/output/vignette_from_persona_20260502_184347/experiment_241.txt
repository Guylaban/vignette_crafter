career built on precision and command. The trauma that now defined his life happened not at sea, but

a shell struck a building they were using as a staging point. Eduardo was knocked unconscious by the
blast. When he came to, he saw a young woman, a local interpreter he’d been working with for weeks,
pinned under rubble. He tried to lift the beam, but it was too heavy. He had to leave her to save
the rest of the team. He still hears her voice calling his name over the radio static, and sees the
red dust settling on her outstretched hand. The memory comes back with a visceral, full-sensory
reality—the smell of cordite and wet concrete, the percussive shockwave in his chest, the taste of
grit in his mouth. He can feel the exact strain in his arms as he tried to lift that beam. Other
times, he can recount the entire event in a flat, dispassionate tone, as if it happened to a
stranger, leaving him feeling hollow and numb afterward.

about himself: he was a weak person. This belief had hardened into a fixed idea. He was convinced he
would never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened, and he felt a crushing,
personal failure for not protecting the people under his command. This sense of weakness and failure
directly fed the intrusive quality of the memory. When he remembered the event, he did not just see

the image of the interpreter’s face flashed into his mind—often triggered by the sound of a

inaction. The memory itself was the primary, punishing evidence that he was, indeed, weak. He had
come to believe that if he were a stronger person, he would have found a way, and the fact that he
hadn’t was proof he was worthless.

that day. At home with his partner, he grew quiet and withdrawn, often sitting in the dark in the
living room after she had gone to bed, listening to the radio static on a low band, as if trying to
hear a voice that would never come again. The clinical picture had become a tightening loop: the

triggered the full memory of the event, which in turn confirmed his belief. He had begun to monitor

that someone could help him forgive himself—or at least, help him stop feeling like a stranger in
his own life.
