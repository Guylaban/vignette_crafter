Kai is a 24-year-old painter who lives with his girlfriend in a small apartment on the outskirts of
a midsized city. He came to therapy at her urging, after months of what she described as him "living
like a ghost." The event that fractured his life happened eighteen months ago, when he was still
living with his previous partner, a man named Derek. What began as verbal jabs during arguments
escalated one night into Derek shoving him against a wall, then pinning him to the floor with a knee
on his chest while screaming inches from his face. Kai remembers the pressure in his ribs, the spit
on his cheek, and then nothing—a complete blank where the next several minutes should be. Other
moments from that night, like the sound of a lamp shattering or the sight of his own hand gripping
the carpet, are burned into his mind with excruciating clarity, but he cannot arrange them in any
order that makes sense. The gaps terrify him more than the images do, because he is certain that
what he cannot remember must be the worst part.

In the months that followed, Kai found himself unable to shake the feeling that he was fundamentally
the kind of person this happens to. He had always struggled with a quiet sense of inadequacy, but
now it crystallized into a fixed belief: the event happened because of who he was, and that meant he
no longer belonged anywhere—not in his old life, not in his new relationship, not even in his own
body. This conviction colored everything. When his girlfriend would ask him a simple question, he
would feel a spike of dread, certain she was about to see through him. He began to notice that any
moment of uncertainty—a text left unread, a delay in her response—triggered a cascade of unwanted
thoughts about Derek finding him again. The uncertainty itself became a trigger, and soon he was
scanning for exits the moment he entered any room, listening for footsteps at the door, and
interpreting any delay as evidence that something terrible was unfolding just out of sight. The more
he believed he was inadequate and did not belong, the more he watched for threats, and the more he
watched, the more triggers he found.

The triggers, in turn, fed back into his memory. A sudden silence in the apartment would drop him
straight back into that night, reliving the pressure on his chest as if it were happening right
then. The reliving episodes were disorienting and fragmented, and each one deepened his confusion
about what had actually happened, which made him feel even more broken and worthless. He started
avoiding anything that reminded him of Derek—certain streets, the time of evening when the attack
had occurred, even the smell of a particular cologne. He also began to freeze completely when he
felt threatened, unable to move or speak, which only reinforced his sense of inadequacy. These
strategies, meant to protect him, actually made things worse: avoiding triggers meant he never
tested his fears, so the memories stayed raw and unpredictable, and freezing left him feeling
humiliated and even more convinced that he was the sort of person who deserved what happened.

The cycle tightened. Every time he froze, the memory of the attack felt more vivid and more out of
his control. Every time he scanned for exits, he noticed new potential threats, which triggered more
intrusive thoughts, which sent him back into the fragmented reliving. He began to monitor for sounds
of someone entering the apartment obsessively, and this hypervigilance made him irritable and
exhausted. His girlfriend started to feel like she was walking on eggshells, and Kai, noticing her
hesitation, interpreted it as proof that he no longer belonged in this relationship either. The
strategies that were supposed to keep him safe were, in his mind, the very things that confirmed he
was broken.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at work. He was painting a ceiling in a client's
living room when the homeowner, a large man, walked in without warning. Kai dropped his roller,
scrambled down the ladder, and stood frozen in the corner, unable to speak or move for nearly a
minute. The man was apologetic, confused, and eventually concerned. Kai drove home shaking, and that
night he told his girlfriend he could not keep living this way. He is afraid that he will never feel
safe again, and that the person he was before the attack is gone for good. But beneath that, in the
quietest part of himself, he is most afraid that his worst appraisal is correct—that the event
happened because of who he is, and that no amount of therapy can change that.
