Kwame Asare is a 46-year-old curriculum designer, married and living in a quiet suburb of Atlanta.
He was born in Ghana and came to the United States for graduate school, building a life he thought
was secure. That changed on a Tuesday evening in late October, two years ago. He was driving home
from a late meeting, taking his usual route through a neighborhood that had recently seen a string
of break-ins. A patrol car pulled him over for a broken taillight. Within seconds, the stop
escalated. The officer drew his weapon, shouting commands Kwame could barely process. He remembers
the floodlight in his eyes, the cold asphalt against his cheek, the weight of a knee on his back. He
was handcuffed and held for twenty minutes before the officer returned to the squad car and issued a
warning. He drove home in silence, his hands still shaking on the wheel.

In the weeks that followed, Kwame found himself unable to stop scanning. He would catch himself
checking his rearview mirror three, four times on a short drive. Every police cruiser he saw made
his chest tighten and his palms go slick with sweat. He began avoiding certain streets altogether,
taking longer routes that felt safer. The hypervigilance bled into his work as a curriculum
designer. Sitting in open-plan offices, he positioned himself with his back to the wall, his gaze
drifting to the door every few minutes. His colleagues noticed he had become edgy, easily startled
by sudden noises—a dropped binder, a raised voice in the hallway. He told himself he was just being
careful, that it was reasonable, but the vigilance was exhausting and it never stopped.

What frightened Kwame most was not the memory itself, but what happened when it returned. Sometimes
it came without warning—a flash of blue light, the crackle of a police radio—and his body would
react before his mind could catch up. His heart would hammer, his breath would shorten, and he would
feel a wave of helplessness so intense he had to stop whatever he was doing. The emotion was raw and
wordless; he could not describe what he felt, only that it overwhelmed him. At other times, the full
emotional weight of the event would wash over him without any clear image or memory attached—just
the terror, the shame, the sense of being utterly powerless, rising from nowhere. He would sit at
his desk, frozen, unable to speak, waiting for it to pass.

The hypervigilance made the memories more frequent. Every time he flinched at a siren or tensed at a
loud noise, he was thrown back into that moment on the asphalt. The more watchful he became, the
more triggers he found, and the more the memory intruded—raw, fragmented, impossible to articulate.
A single sentence from a colleague about a traffic stop could send him spiraling into that wordless
state for the rest of the afternoon. He began to avoid conversations about policing, about safety,
about anything that might touch the edges of what happened. At home, he grew withdrawn. His wife
noticed he no longer wanted to drive at night, that he would flinch when she touched his shoulder
from behind. He could not explain why. He could not find the words.

Kwame came to therapy because the cycle had become untenable. His work was suffering—he had missed
two deadlines, and his supervisor had expressed concern. He was sleeping poorly, waking with his
heart racing. What he most feared was that he would never feel safe again, that the vigilance and
the wordless dread would become permanent. He was hoping, quietly, that someone could help him find
a way to put the event into words, and to stop the endless loop of watching and remembering.
