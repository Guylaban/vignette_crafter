grab her by the wrists, shake her, or push her into walls during arguments. The worst episode
happened on a rainy Tuesday night in December. She had come home late from a deadline at the studio,
and Carlos accused her of lying about where she had been. When she tried to walk past him to the
bathroom, he grabbed her by the hair and slammed her head against the doorframe. She remembered the
sound more than the pain—a hollow crack that seemed to come from inside her skull. After that night,
she stopped fighting back. She would go still and quiet when he was angry, hoping that if she didn't
move or speak, he would stop sooner. That stillness, that silence, is what haunts her now more than
the bruises.

Nadia believes she has permanently changed for the worse. She told her intake clinician that she

headphones on, working on the same shot for hours because she cannot stop second-guessing her
choices. She is certain that the event happened because of the way she acted—specifically, because

and compliant, is not the woman she recognizes. She told her therapist, "I stayed. I let him do that

with the person she became in that apartment, and that fracture feels like a kind of moral injury
she carries alone.

because she cannot stand the sound of raised voices in a crowded room—it makes her chest tighten and
her vision blur. She has told no one at work about Carlos. She is afraid that if they knew, they
would see her the way she sees herself: weak, foolish, permanently damaged. She has come to therapy

and a life that no longer belong to her. She is most afraid that she will never feel like herself
