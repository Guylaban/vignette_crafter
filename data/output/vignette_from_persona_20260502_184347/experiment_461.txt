Tran was 77 years old, a retired firefighter who had spent decades rushing toward danger when others
fled. But the danger he carried now was not one he had chosen. It had found him in the spring of
1975, in a small coastal city he had called home. He was a young man then, not yet a firefighter,
just a civilian caught in the final chaotic weeks of the war. He remembered the day the shelling
grew close, the way the ground shuddered, and the sharp, percussive blast that threw him against a
wall. He had been walking home; the next thing he knew, he was on his back, ears ringing, dust and
blood in his mouth. The memory was not vague—it was brutally clear and detailed, every sound and
smell preserved as if it had happened yesterday. Yet sometimes, when the memory surfaced unbidden,
forcing its way into his mind during quiet moments, it felt like it belonged to someone else, like
he was watching a stranger’s nightmare.

Decades later, Tran lived with his wife in a quiet suburban house. But the war had never really
ended for him. A distant ambulance siren, the sudden slam of a car door, the crack of a fallen
branch—any of these could trigger the memory. Hearing a name that sounded like someone he had known
from that time would make his stomach clench. The worst was an unexpected bang: a firework, a
backfiring truck, a dropped tray at the grocery store. In that instant, the memory would flood back,
vivid and disorienting, and with it came a crushing sense of helplessness. He had come to believe
that if he let himself think about the event, he would not be able to handle it—that he would fall
apart completely. This fear made him avoid his own thoughts, and it also made him feel hollow. He
sometimes caught himself thinking that somebody else would not have gotten into that situation, that
a stronger or smarter person would have avoided the blast. At his worst, he felt like an object, not
a person—just a body that had been in the wrong place, a thing that had been acted upon.

To manage this, Tran had developed a set of careful strategies. He avoided going to sleep in the
dark, leaving a small lamp on because the darkness felt too much like the moment after the
explosion, when the world had gone black and silent. He constantly scanned his environment for signs
of threat, his eyes moving from car to doorway to rooftop whenever he left the house. This vigilance
was exhausting, but he was certain that if he stopped watching, something terrible would happen. The
more the memory intruded, the more he scanned; the more he scanned, the more he imagined getting
revenge on the people responsible, a fantasy that gave him a fleeting sense of control. But the
fantasy also deepened his feeling of being alienated from himself. He had come to believe that his
own anger proved he was not a good person, that a normal man would have moved on.

The cycle wore him down. His wife noticed he no longer wanted to visit their grandson, who lived
near a busy road where ambulances often passed. Tran said he was just tired. He had stopped
attending the weekly firehouse gatherings with his old crew, because someone there might mention a
name from the past, or a story might drift toward war. His world had grown small, and he had grown
quiet. What finally brought him to seek help was a moment in the hardware store, when a display of
metal shelving collapsed with a deafening clatter. Tran had dropped to the floor, heart pounding,
and could not get up for several minutes. A young employee helped him to his feet, and Tran saw the
concern in the man’s face. He felt a deep shame, and for the first time, he wondered if his
strategies were not protecting him but imprisoning him. He was afraid of what the therapy might
unearth, but he was more afraid of living the rest of his life on the floor of a hardware store,
waiting for the next bang.
