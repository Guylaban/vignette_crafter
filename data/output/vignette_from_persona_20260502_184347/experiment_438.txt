Samira is a 59-year-old police officer of Middle Eastern descent, married for over thirty years, who
had built her career on a quiet competence that let her move through the city’s roughest precincts
without incident. That changed one evening eighteen months ago when she responded to a domestic
disturbance call that turned into an ambush. Two men, high on methamphetamine, cornered her in a
narrow hallway. One of them struck her in the chest with a crowbar while the other screamed
obscenities in her face. She remembers the sound of the metal hitting her sternum, the smell of
sweat and stale beer, and the terrifying clarity of knowing she was going to die. She fired her
service weapon and survived, but something inside her did not.

Now, in her daily life, Samira’s heart races at the slightest provocation—a car horn, a raised
voice, even the thud of her own pulse when she climbs stairs. That pounding in her chest, which she
once dismissed as normal adrenaline, now feels like a betrayal. She has come to believe that her
body is her enemy, that it keeps reacting as if she is still in that hallway even when she is
objectively safe. When her heart speeds up during roll call or while driving with a partner, the
memory of the attack floods back with unbearable clarity: she hears the crowbar’s impact, she feels
the terror of that moment as if it were happening right then. The memory is so vivid, so detailed,
that she sometimes catches herself checking her surroundings for the two men, certain they have
somehow followed her home.

Samira’s belief that she cannot rely on other people has grown into a hardened conviction. She used
to trust her fellow officers implicitly, but now she is certain that no one would have her back.
That thought crystallized during the internal investigation, when the department’s response felt
dismissive—what happened to her was deeply unfair, she believes, and nothing has been done about it.
She has cut off all contact with friends and family, isolating herself completely. She tells herself
it is easier this way, that she does not have to explain why she flinches when someone touches her
shoulder or why she cannot sit with her back to a door. In truth, she is terrified that if she lets
anyone close, they will see how broken she has become.

The hypervigilance is exhausting. Every person she passes on the street is a potential threat; she
scans their hands, their eyes, their posture, always looking for the tell that precedes violence.
She has also begun monitoring her own thoughts obsessively, checking for signs that she is losing
her grip on reality. When a memory intrudes—and they intrude often, triggered by a siren or a raised
voice or the feel of a steering wheel under her hands—she immediately tries to push it away, but
that only makes the images more persistent. The more she tries to control her mind, the more the
memories return, and each return brings the same raw terror she felt in that hallway.

Driving with people has become nearly impossible. The last time she rode with a colleague, her heart
started racing, and she became convinced that she was about to have a panic attack right there in
the passenger seat. She made an excuse and has not accepted a ride since. She drives alone now,
always taking the same routes, avoiding the street where it happened. But avoidance has its own
cost: her sergeant has noticed her refusal to partner up, and there have been quiet conversations
about her fitness for duty. Samira knows she is on thin ice, but she cannot explain why she would
rather face a disciplinary hearing than sit next to another officer for a ten-minute drive.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment last week when she was alone in her patrol car,
stopped at a red light. Her heart started pounding for no reason—no siren, no violence, just the
idle rumble of the engine. The memory of the crowbar hitting her chest came so vividly that she felt
the impact again, and for a few seconds she could not breathe. She pulled over and sat there,
shaking, and realized that if she did not do something, she would lose her job, her marriage, maybe
her mind. She is terrified that talking about it will make the memories worse, but she is more
terrified of what will happen if she does not.
