been standing near the dessert table, holding a plate of her famous pecan pie, when the old wooden
beams gave way with a sound like a cannon shot. She remembered the smell of splintered pine and
spilled wine, the way a woman’s scream seemed to come from everywhere at once. For a long moment,
she was pinned beneath a fallen beam, her cheek pressed against the cold linoleum, her left arm
twisted beneath her. She could hear people shouting, could feel the weight of the debris on her
back, and what she remembered most vividly—what she could still see, years later, as though it were
a photograph projected onto the inside of her eyelids—was the pattern of the ceiling tiles above
her, a grid of tiny holes, just before everything went dark. She had been an artist all her life, a
painter of landscapes and still lifes, and that image had burned itself into her visual memory with
a permanence she could not shake.

In the months that followed, Martha found that the memory came with a physical reenactment she could
not control. Whenever the recollection surfaced, her body would stiffen into that same pinned
position, her left arm pressing against her ribs, her cheek tilting toward an invisible floor. And
with the memory came a crushing guilt: she had been the one to suggest the venue, had insisted that
the community center was perfect for the dinner. She had convinced her husband, who had been
reluctant to attend, and he had been standing only a few feet away when the beam fell. He had
survived, but the guilt had not faded with time. She had come to believe that her judgment was
fundamentally flawed, that the world was a place where ceilings fell without warning, where her own
choices could bring harm to the people she loved. Her beliefs about how the world works had been
completely destroyed. She was not sure her life was worth living anymore, and she had begun to feel
that she would never be able to feel normal emotions again.

Martha worked from a studio in her home, a converted garage where she had painted for forty years.

her palette when a creak from the roof would make her freeze, her heart hammering, her eyes fixed on
the ceiling. She had started listening for sounds that might mean someone was entering her home,
even during the day, even when she knew she was alone. Sleep had become fragmented; she would lie
awake in the dark, tracking the groan of the house settling, certain that the ceiling was about to
give way. Her therapist had noted that Martha’s irritability had grown—she snapped at neighbors who
knocked unexpectedly, and she had ended a phone call with her daughter by shouting, then hanging up,

even a hand on her shoulder from a well-meaning friend, made her flinch and pull away, because her
body remembered the weight of the beam. And crying—she had started to cry at unexpected moments, and
the sight of her own tears in the mirror would trigger the memory of the woman’s scream, and she
would feel the guilt wash over her again, and she would become convinced that she would never feel
normal emotions, which only made her cry harder.

She had developed a set of strategies to manage the chaos. She tried hard not to think about the
trauma, pushing the images away whenever they began to surface, but the suppression required
constant effort. She kept her mind occupied all the time—she listened to audiobooks while she
painted, she watched television while she ate, she played word games on her phone while she waited
for water to boil. She avoided news reports about building collapses or natural disasters, changing
the channel or leaving the room if a story came on. But the avoidance had narrowed her world. She no
longer visited restaurants with high ceilings. She had stopped going to the grocery store because
the overhead lights, suspended on wires, reminded her of the community center. Her daughter had
started doing her shopping, and Martha was grateful, but she was also ashamed.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning in late November, three years after the
collapse. She had been standing in her studio, brush in hand, staring at a blank canvas, when she
heard a car backfire in the street. The sound was distant and ordinary, but her body had reacted as
though the ceiling were falling again. She had dropped the brush, had pressed herself against the

shaking, unable to pick up the brush. She was afraid that she was losing the ability to paint, the
