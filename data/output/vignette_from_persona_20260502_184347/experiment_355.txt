Amina was twenty years old when she returned from her deployment, though she often felt as though
she had left a different person behind. She had served as a counselor embedded with a medical unit,
her role to provide psychological first aid to soldiers in the field. During a routine convoy, their
vehicle was ambushed. The helicopter that had been escorting them was struck and crashed in a
fireball less than fifty meters from where she was pinned behind an overturned truck. She had
watched the burning wreckage, listened to the rotors whine and then choke into silence, and felt the
heat on her face as she tried to crawl toward the wounded. She had not been physically injured, but
something in her had broken that day. Now, a year later, she was living alone in a small apartment,
working a desk job at a community health center, and struggling to understand why she could not
simply move on.

The first thing that began to change was her sleep. She could not bear total darkness anymore; the
blackness of her bedroom felt too much like the moment after the explosion, when the smoke had
blotted out everything. She left a lamp on every night, but even then, she would wake with a jolt at
the sound of a passing car, her mind instantly translating it into the thrum of rotor blades.
Helicopters were rare in her city, but when one flew overhead, she would freeze at her desk, her
heart slamming against her ribs. The sound was a direct wire back to that afternoon, and she had
come to believe that her reaction proved something was fundamentally wrong with her. She began to
think that the reason she had been in that convoy, the reason the helicopter had been hit, was
because of some intrinsic fault in her—a flaw that had drawn disaster toward her. Somebody else, she
was certain, would not have gotten into this situation; somebody braver or better would have been
stationed elsewhere, would have avoided the ambush entirely.

These thoughts grew louder each time she was triggered. She started avoiding anything that might
remind her of the event. She stopped watching television altogether, because a single scene of
conflict or a news report about military operations could send her spiraling. She deleted her social
media accounts because she could not bear to see posts from old friends who still served, or even
from civilians who posted casually about their lives, as if the world were safe. She had stopped
returning calls from the people she had socialized with before her deployment, because speaking to
them meant explaining why she was no longer the person they remembered. She was certain she was no
longer capable of doing the things that had once defined her—offering comfort, staying calm under
pressure, being a reliable presence for others. The more she isolated herself, the more convinced
she became that she had lost those qualities forever. The isolation itself became proof of her
failure.

As weeks turned into months, her avoidance tightened its grip. She stopped even considering new
friendships or relationships, because starting something new meant eventually having to explain her
past, and she could not imagine anyone understanding. The fewer people she saw, the more the silence
of her apartment amplified every sound. A car backfiring on the street would send her heart racing,
and she would be right back in the hot, smoky air of the ambush. The triggers came more frequently
now, because she had narrowed her world so much that any unexpected noise felt like an intrusion.
The cycle was self-reinforcing: the more she avoided, the more vulnerable she felt, and the more
vulnerable she felt, the more she avoided.

What finally brought her to seek help was a comment from a client at the health center. The client,
an older woman, had mentioned her own family in passing—a simple remark about a daughter's
wedding—and Amina had felt a wave of nausea so sudden she had to excuse herself to the bathroom. The
word "family" had undone her, because she had not spoken to her own mother in months, too ashamed to
admit what she had become. She sat on the bathroom floor, trembling, and realized she could not keep
living this way. She was afraid of the darkness, afraid of the sounds, afraid of herself. She hoped,
more than anything, that there was a way to stop feeling like she was still trapped in that burning
field.
