damp earth and smoke, the sudden flurry of birds from the trees, and the way her own body froze
while her mind screamed at her to run. For months afterward, she could not speak of it without
losing the thread mid-sentence, her thoughts scattering like those birds, and when she did try, the
memory felt as though it belonged to someone else—a stranger’s nightmare she had borrowed. What
stayed with her most was the rage: a hot, wordless fury that rose without warning when she recalled
the helplessness of that moment, a rage that made her want to break something, to feel something
concrete and physical.

Back home in the city, Mai threw herself into her work as an urban planner, filling every hour with
site visits, community meetings, and late nights drafting zoning reports. She kept her mind occupied
all the time, because the moment she stopped, the memory would surge back—the shouting, the birds,
the smell of wet earth—and she would feel the rage again, burning in her chest. To contain it, she
began pushing the thoughts away the instant they surfaced, a mental shove she performed dozens of
times a day. But the suppression only seemed to make the memories more fragmented and alien, as if
she were trying to hold water in her fists. On bad nights, when the rage pressed too hard against
her skin, she would lock herself in the bathroom and press the tip of a blade into her forearm, just
deep enough to feel the sting, to replace the diffuse fury with something sharp and manageable. The
physical pain quieted the memory for a while, but it also left her more alert, more attuned to
threat—she found herself scanning the edges of her vision, startled by the sudden movement of a
colleague reaching for a stapler, unable to fall asleep because her body insisted on staying
watchful.

This hypervigilance, in turn, made the memories feel more vivid and intrusive. After a sleepless
night, she would catch a glimpse of a park with tall grass and hear the echo of shouting from a
neighbor’s argument, and the scene would flood back with such force that for a disorienting second
she was back in the compound. The triggers multiplied: old photographs she found while cleaning, the
sound of raised voices in a café, even the quiet of a nature trail she had once loved. She began
avoiding these things—she deleted photos, she left restaurants when a table argued, she stopped
hiking altogether—but the avoidance only deepened her sense that the world was full of hidden
dangers, and that she had to stay one step ahead. Her boyfriend noticed she no longer wanted to go
for weekend walks, and she told him she was just tired from work. What she did not tell him was that
she spent her evenings sitting rigidly on the couch, listening for footsteps on the stairs, certain
that something—she did not know what—was about to come through the door.

The cycle tightened over months. The more she suppressed and distracted and hurt herself, the more
sensitive she became to triggers, and the more the memories intruded, alien and rage-filled. She
began to believe that she could never let her guard down, that the world was fundamentally unsafe,
