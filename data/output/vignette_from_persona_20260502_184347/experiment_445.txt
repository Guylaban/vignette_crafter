Rajesh, a 44-year-old single man of South Asian descent and a chef by trade, still feels the weight
of the journey that brought him here. He had fled a village torn apart by ethnic violence, a night
he can only describe in fragments: the acrid smell of smoke, the wet thud of boots on mud, and the
sight of his younger brother crumpling beside him. He cannot find the words for what happened—the
memory is a series of visceral images and sounds, not a coherent story. When he tries, he often
feels nothing, recounting the facts with a hollow numbness, as if it happened to a stranger, but
other times, a wave of overwhelming grief crashes over him, a grief that feels as fresh as the day
it happened. He has come to believe he has been permanently changed for the worse, and he is haunted
by a punishing guilt: he will never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened, and he
feels a deep, corrosive guilt for surviving when his brother and so many others did not.

This guilt is the engine of his waking life. It has convinced him that he is a coward, a judgment
that makes him hypervigilant in his daily world. As a chef in a busy downtown kitchen, Rajesh is
constantly on guard, scanning for threats. The clatter of a metal pot dropping on the tile floor can
make him flinch and freeze, his heart hammering as he searches for the nearest exit. He has
difficulty concentrating on chopping vegetables or reading a ticket because his mind is always half-
focused on escape route planning; he cannot feel comfortable in any room until he has identified
every possible way out. This constant state of alertness, in turn, makes his memory more
intrusive—the more he scans for danger, the more the smells and sounds of that night return
unbidden, especially the smell of diesel and smoke.

His triggers are specific and potent. The wail of an ambulance siren on the street outside his
apartment can paralyze him. A customer at the restaurant discussing a car accident will make his
stomach turn. Most difficult of all is seeing a visible injury or wound on another person—a cut on a
line cook's hand or a customer's bandaged arm—which instantly transports him back to the sight of
his brother. Each of these moments spikes his sense of threat, and to manage that fear, he has
developed rigid maladaptive strategies. He avoids any area of the city he cannot visually survey,
refusing to walk through open plazas or parks where he feels exposed. He also avoids any place where
he might see a police officer or someone in military uniform, crossing the street or ducking into a
shop to avoid them. In the kitchen, when a manager raises their voice in the heat of service, Rajesh
does not argue or defend himself; instead, he becomes overly compliant, nodding and apologizing
excessively, a fawning response that leaves him feeling even more ashamed and weak afterward.

This very avoidance feeds back into his guilt. Each time he ducks away from a uniform or scurries
through a back alley to avoid an open space, he tells himself that he is still that same powerless
person who could not act. His fawning at work leads him to take on extra shifts he does not want,
and then he berates himself for his cowardice, reinforcing the belief that he is permanently
damaged. The cycle tightens: his threat-driven hypervigilance makes him more sensitive to triggers,
and his avoidance of those triggers means he never learns that he is safe, so the memories remain
raw and unprocessed. He has become isolated, turning down invitations from coworkers to eat at
outdoor patios, and his manager has noticed his jumpiness, though Rajesh has never explained why.
What finally brought him to seek help was a panic attack in the walk-in cooler after a delivery
driver walked in with a bloody hand from a box cutter. He sat on the floor among the crates, unable
to breathe, and knew he could no longer manage this alone. He is terrified that therapy will only
confirm what he already suspects—that he is broken beyond repair—but he is even more afraid of
spending the rest of his life hiding from ambulances and his own reflection.
