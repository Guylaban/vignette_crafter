Sofia was thirty-nine years old, a staff sergeant in the Army, and had spent fourteen years
believing she could handle anything. That belief ended during the hurricane. She had been stationed
at a coastal base when the storm surge came through, and for six hours she was pinned in a collapsed
supply building with water rising to her chest, debris battering the metal walls, and the sound of
the roof tearing away in sheets. She remembered the way the air pressure changed, how her ears
popped, and then the roar—a sound she had never heard before and could not forget. She had held onto
a steel beam, certain she was going to die, and had screamed for help that did not come until dawn.

In the months that followed, Sofia did not talk about it. She returned to duty, went through the
motions, and told herself she was fine. But she was not fine. The first time it rained hard—a summer
thunderstorm that rolled in fast—she found herself standing in the middle of her kitchen, heart
pounding, unable to remember walking there. Her husband, Marco, asked if she was okay, and she
snapped at him for no reason she could explain. What frightened her most was the feeling that she
was not really there, that she had become something hollow. She had come to believe that her
reactions since the storm proved she was a lousy coper, that other people would have handled it
better, that something inside her had broken and could not be fixed. She felt like an object, not
like a person, going through the motions of her life without actually living it.

The triggers multiplied. Any weather that reminded her of that night—the same heavy humidity, the
same gray-green sky, the same pressure drop before a storm—would send her into a state of
hypervigilance that she could not control. She began scanning her surroundings constantly, checking
for escape routes every time she walked into a room, whether it was the mess hall, the PX, or her
own living room. Loud noises were the worst. A truck backfiring in the motor pool would make her
jump so violently that her commanding officer noticed and asked if she was getting enough sleep. She
started to avoid being outside when the sky looked uncertain, and she began to dread the sound of
wind. Even the presence of a police car on the road made her chest tighten, though she could not say
why—it was authority, she supposed, the feeling of being helpless and waiting for someone to save
her.

The causal connections tightened into a loop she could not escape. Each time a trigger appeared—the
rumble of thunder, the crack of a door slamming—it activated her threat system, and she would
startle, scan, plan escape routes. But the threat itself then reinforced her negative appraisals:
see how jumpy I am, see how scared, I really am a lousy coper. And those appraisals, in turn, made
her more sensitive to triggers. She began to believe that she could not stop bad things from
happening to her, that the world was fundamentally unsafe, and that belief made her scan for danger
even more. She would find herself checking the weather forecast obsessively, and then feel ashamed
for doing so. The more she felt like an object, the more she avoided situations where she might feel
exposed—she stopped going to the gym on base because the echo of weights dropping sounded too much
like debris hitting metal.

At work, her performance did not suffer visibly, but she had stopped volunteering for field
exercises. She found reasons to stay in the office, to keep her back to a wall, to keep the door
open. Marco had started sleeping in the guest room because she would wake up gasping, and she could
not explain to him what she was afraid of. What finally brought her to the clinic was a drill. A
simulated explosion during a training exercise—a controlled blast, she knew it was controlled—sent
her into a full panic. She locked herself in a supply closet and could not come out until a fellow
soldier found her twenty minutes later, shaking and crying. She was certain she would be discharged,
that her career was over, that everyone would see her for what she really was. But what frightened
her most, sitting in the waiting room, was the thought that nothing would change. She was hoping,
barely, that someone could tell her how to stop feeling like a ghost in her own body.
