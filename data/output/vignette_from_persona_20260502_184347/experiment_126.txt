Maya was forty-seven years old, a divorced South Asian woman who had served as a pastor in a small,
multi-ethnic congregation for over a decade. Three years ago, during a humanitarian trip to a
conflict zone, she had been taken by armed men who accused her of espionage. For six days, they held
her in a concrete room, interrogating her with methods she could still sometimes taste—a bitter,
metallic film that rose in her throat when she least expected it. They had forced her to drink from
a rusty canteen, and now, any hint of that mineral tang, even from tap water on a hot day, could
send her reeling. She had been beaten, deprived of sleep, and made to kneel for hours on a cold
floor while they shouted questions she could not answer. What she remembered came in disconnected
pieces: the crack of a radio, the shadow of a boot, the sensation of her own tears on her face. Some
moments were excruciatingly clear—the exact pattern of a ceiling crack above her—while entire hours
were blank, as if erased.

Back home, Maya tried not to think about it. She told herself that if she let the memories in, she
would not be able to handle them, that she would fall apart and never recover. This belief grew
stronger each time a trigger caught her off guard. A parishioner’s story about travel, the clink of
a metal cup during coffee hour, or even someone simply asking about her trip could bring the
metallic taste back, and with it, the shame of having survived when others had not. She had come to
believe that she was no longer capable of the work that had once defined her—the pastoral
counseling, the steady presence she offered others. What future could there be for someone who could
not even trust her own body? Her body reacted before her mind did: a sudden sweat, a racing heart, a
feeling of being watched, all before she had consciously registered any danger. She was
hypervigilant in crowds, scanning for threatening faces, certain that even when others told her she
was safe, she could not believe it.

To manage this, Maya began restricting her eating with strict discipline, controlling every meal as
a way to feel in control of something. She also tried hard to suppress thoughts of the trauma,
pushing them down with prayer or frantic activity. But these strategies only made things worse. The
more she pushed memories away, the more they surfaced in fragments—intrusive, guilt-laden,
disconnected. When she did remember, she felt intense guilt: why had she been released when others
were not? Why had she not fought harder? The guilt made the memories more vivid, more threatening,
and the cycle tightened. Each time she suppressed a thought, the next trigger hit harder, and each
time a trigger arrived, her hopelessness deepened. She began to avoid talking about the event
entirely, even with her closest colleagues. When a deacon asked gently about her health, Maya
changed the subject, her throat tight with unshed tears.

The threat she felt was constant, and it fed her negative beliefs. Being hyperalert made her certain
she was in danger, which confirmed that she could not trust safety, which made her more vigilant.
This vigilance, in turn, made her more sensitive to triggers—the taste of water, the sound of a door
slamming, the sight of a certain uniform in a crowd. She started arriving late to services to avoid
the bustle of the greeting line. She stopped visiting the hospital, afraid of the smells and the
helplessness. Her congregation noticed her withdrawal, and she began to receive concerned calls,
which she did not return. She felt trapped: the very people she had once helped now felt like
witnesses to her failure.

What finally brought Maya to seek help was a Sunday morning when a child in the congregation began
to cry during a hymn. The sound—high, broken, persistent—hit her like a physical blow. She excused
herself to the vestry, locked the door, and stood trembling, the metallic taste flooding her mouth,
fragments of memory crowding her vision: the crying of a woman in the next cell, her own tears, the
cold floor. She knew then that she could not continue this way. She was most afraid that therapy
would force her to remember everything, that she would not survive the full truth. But she was also
afraid of losing her congregation, her calling, herself. She wanted to hope, but hope felt like a
luxury she no longer had.
