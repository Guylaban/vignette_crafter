Elena had always been the one people called when things went wrong. A paramedic for thirty-two
years, she had walked into car wrecks, shootings, and house fires with a calm that her younger
colleagues called supernatural. But nothing had prepared her for the night she became the patient.
During a routine transfer of a restrained prisoner, the man had broken free, and before anyone could
react, he had her at knifepoint in the back of the rig. For four hours, he kept her pinned in that
metal box, driving through side roads, his breath hot against her neck, whispering about what he
would do if she screamed. She had stayed silent. That silence had saved her life.

In the weeks that followed, Elena found herself unable to trust the quiet. Unusual silence in her
own living room—the kind that used to feel peaceful—now made her chest tighten. She felt certain
something dangerous was about to happen. Even inside her home, she was on guard, her eyes scanning
doorways and windows as if the threat might materialize from the stillness. She had come to believe
that the world was a dangerous place, a conviction that felt less like a thought and more like a
fact written into her bones. What frightened her most was the feeling that she couldn't stop bad
things from happening to her. That helplessness, she feared, meant she was going crazy.

The memory of that night was not like other memories. It forced its way into her mind when she did
not want it—while she was cooking, while she was trying to fall asleep, while she was driving to
work. And when it came, the story felt so extreme it was hard to believe it had actually happened to
her. But the disbelief did not protect her. On recall, she became so overwhelmed by emotion that she
had to stop whatever she was doing, her hands trembling, her breath shallow. The more she told
herself the world was dangerous and she was helpless, the more the memory erupted, vivid and
uninvited. And each time the memory surfaced, it reinforced her belief that she was broken and the
world was unsafe—a loop that tightened with every intrusion.

Elena began checking behind her whenever she walked anywhere, frequently looking over her shoulder
to see if someone was following. She stopped taking the back stairwell at the station. She avoided
the supply closet where the rig keys were kept because it reminded her of being trapped. At work,
she started requesting only day shifts, and she made excuses to avoid the ambulance bay after dark.
Her colleagues noticed. They asked if she was okay. She said she was fine. But she was not sleeping,
and her husband had started sleeping in the guest room because she woke him with her tossing and her
sharp, sudden gasps.

What finally brought Elena to seek help was a moment she could not explain away. Last month, during
a quiet afternoon shift, a patient's family member had simply stood in the doorway of the exam room
without speaking. He was not threatening. He was just still. And Elena had frozen. Her heart had
pounded so hard she thought she might pass out. She had excused herself and walked to the bathroom,
where she stood gripping the sink, staring at her own face in the mirror, certain that she was
losing her mind. She was terrified of what might happen next time—afraid that her own body would
betray her in front of her crew, or worse, in front of a patient who needed her. She wanted to stop
feeling like the world was a trap. She wanted to believe she could be safe again. But right now, she
was not sure that was possible.
