twelve years. She had been on the line late that afternoon, helping a customer with a billing error,
when the first explosion shook the building. She remembered the lights flickering, the muffled thud

bomb had detonated in the plaza two blocks away, part of a coordinated attack on the city’s
financial district. But what she could not put into words, what she could never seem to describe no
matter how hard she tried, was the feeling of the air itself turning wrong. She knew what had
happened—she had seen the smoke, heard the sirens, watched her coworkers scramble under their
desks—but when she tried to tell her daughter about it, the words would not come. There were parts
of that afternoon she simply could not retrieve, no matter how many times she turned it over in her
mind: the minutes between the first blast and the second were a blank, a gap she could not fill.

In the weeks that followed, Therese began to notice that certain things pulled her back into that
confusion. Talking about the event, even in passing, left her feeling hollow and disoriented, as
though the story belonged to someone else. She found herself avoiding the route that took her past
the plaza, taking a longer bus that added forty minutes to her commute. When the anniversary of the
attack approached, she called in sick for three days, unable to explain to her supervisor why she
could not face the date on the calendar. And though she had always been close with her daughter, she
began to pull away from their weekly phone calls, finding that the effort of emotional closeness—of
having to listen, to respond, to care—felt like a demand she could no longer meet. Each time she
avoided a conversation or a location, the memory of the event seemed to grow more fragmented, more
slippery, less anchored to anything she could say out loud. The gaps in her recollection widened,
and the things she could remember—the smell of dust, the pressure in her chest—felt more like
physical sensations than stories.

The triggers did not just shape her behavior; they reshaped how she understood herself. A customer

come to believe that she had no future worth planning for, that her most important role in life—as a

failed at the one thing that mattered.

neck throbbing, and thought about what she was most afraid of: that there was no story she could
tell that would make sense of any of it, and that the gaps in her memory were not gaps at all, but
proof that she had never really been there at all.
