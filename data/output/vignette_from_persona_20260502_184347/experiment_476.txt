Kofi is a 34-year-old parole officer who has been with his girlfriend for three years, though the
relationship has grown distant in recent months. Three years ago, his ex-partner, a woman he had
lived with for two years, became violent during an argument. She pinned him against the wall, her
forearm pressed across his throat; he could smell the antiseptic hand sanitizer she had just used at
her nursing job. She screamed that he was worthless, that no one would ever believe him. He
remembers the taste of blood from his bitten lip, and even now, when the memory returns, he can
taste that metallic saltiness as though it were fresh. The sound of her voice—the exact pitch and
rhythm of her threats—sometimes plays in his head without warning, and the memory does not feel like
something from the past; it feels as though he is back in that moment, pinned and helpless, his
airway closing.

Kofi works in a downtown parole office, where he meets with formerly incarcerated men in a small,
windowless room. The smell of the building’s industrial disinfectant hits him every time he walks
through the door, and his body immediately goes on alert. His shoulders tighten, his jaw clenches,
and he begins scanning the hallway for any man who might be standing too close or looking at him too
long. He watches their hands, their posture, their eyes. When a parolee holds his gaze a second
longer than expected, Kofi feels the memory surge—the taste of blood, the sound of her voice—and he
becomes convinced that the man is about to attack. What happened to Kofi was deeply unfair, he
believes, and nothing has been done about it; his ex-partner faced no consequences, and he has come
to feel that his life has been destroyed by what she did. There are days when he is not sure his
life is worth living anymore.

These appraisals drive much of Kofi’s behavior. Because he believes his life is already ruined, he
has stopped pushing back against small provocations at work. He avoids any situation that might
involve disagreement or confrontation, even when a parolee is clearly violating a condition of
release. He has also begun avoiding eye contact with everyone—colleagues, strangers, his
girlfriend—because looking someone in the eye feels like an invitation to be seen, and being seen
feels dangerous. He pushes away positive emotions, too: when his girlfriend tries to hold his hand
or tell him she loves him, he pulls back, because feeling good feels unsafe, as though any moment of
softness will be punished.

The constant threat monitoring feeds back into itself. Kofi’s body is perpetually tense, ready to
react, and this hypervigilance means he notices every potential danger—a man walking behind him in
the parking lot, a stranger’s lingering glance. Each perceived threat triggers the memory again, and
each memory reinforces his belief that the world is unsafe and that his life has been stolen. His
avoidance strategies, in turn, make the memories more intrusive: by dodging eye contact and
conflict, he never tests whether his fears are realistic, so the memory retains its power to feel
present and real. And by avoiding positive feelings, he starves himself of any experience that might
counter his negative appraisals.

The cycle tightened over months. Kofi began arriving late to work, claiming traffic, because the
antiseptic smell in the lobby was unbearable. He stopped attending team meetings, citing caseload
pressure, because sitting in a room full of men made his skin crawl. His supervisor noticed his
avoidance of conflict and assigned him fewer high-risk cases, which Kofi interpreted as proof that
he was failing. At home, his girlfriend stopped reaching for him; she told him he felt like a
stranger. What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a moment in the parole office when a client—a
large man with a quiet voice—said, “You okay, man?” and Kofi froze, tasted blood in his mouth, and
could not speak for nearly a minute. He realized he could no longer do his job. He is afraid that
therapy will not work, that he is too broken, but he is also afraid of what will happen if he does
not try.
