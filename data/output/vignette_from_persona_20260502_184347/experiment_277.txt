Kofi is a 47-year-old park ranger who lives alone in a small cabin on the outskirts of a national
forest in the American South. He was born in Ghana and came to the United States in his twenties,
but for the past several years he has worked in a vast, wooded preserve. The trauma that shattered
his life occurred six years ago, during a visit to his home village during a period of civil
conflict. He was caught in a militia attack that swept through his family’s compound. He survived by
hiding in a dry brush thicket as flames consumed the structures around him, listening to the screams
of neighbors and the crackle of fire. He emerged to find bodies and rubble, including his cousin’s
body near the compound gate. He has not returned to Ghana since.

The memory of the fire is seared into Kofi’s senses. Now, in his daily work as a ranger, he is
surrounded by the very landscape that echoes that day—the dry brush, the smell of pine, the sight of
smoke from a distant campfire. Every time he patrols a wooded trail and the wind shifts, carrying
the scent of burning wood, his chest tightens and his mind floods with images of flames. These
triggers have become unbearable. He has begun to avoid entire sections of the park, taking longer
routes that keep him far from areas where visitors might build fires. When a supervisor criticized
him last month for taking an inefficient patrol path, Kofi felt a surge of panic that made no sense
to him—the tone of correction felt like a condemnation, a reminder that he had failed to protect
others. That evening, he drank heavily, a habit that has grown from a single beer after work to a
bottle of whiskey most nights.

The guilt sits inside him like a stone. He believes he should have done more, that his survival was
a kind of betrayal. When he sees a fire, or even hears a raised voice, the thought returns: *You are
not the one who should be here.* This appraisal feeds into a constant low-level vigilance. He cannot
relax, even in his own locked cabin. He lies awake at night, listening for sounds that are not
there, his mind racing with imagined threats. When a ranger meeting runs long, he becomes convinced
something terrible is happening—a fire, an attack—and he has to check his phone repeatedly. He has
come to believe that any delay or criticism is a sign of impending danger, and this hypervigilance
only deepens his isolation. He stopped answering calls from his sister in Accra, because hearing her
voice made the guilt unbearable—she would ask about the village, and he could not speak of it.

Kofi’s isolation is almost total. He has not seen a friend in over a year. He avoids any reminder of
military or police presence, which means he has stopped visiting the ranger station on days when the
county sheriff’s deputies are there. He once walked out of a grocery store because a customer wore a
camouflage jacket. This avoidance, in turn, strengthens his belief that he is fundamentally
different from others, that he is set apart by what he did—or did not do. He has told no one the
full story of that day. The more he isolates, the more the triggers multiply: a flicker of sunlight
through leaves becomes the flash of fire; a distant shout becomes a warning. Each time, the threat
feels real, and each time, he reaches for the bottle or retreats further into the woods.

He has come to therapy only because his employer mandated a fitness-for-duty evaluation after a
hiker reported him for appearing disoriented and agitated near a controlled burn site. Kofi is
afraid that he will lose his job, the only thing that still gives his life structure. But what
frightens him most is the thought of being forced to talk about what happened, because he is certain
that if he speaks the words aloud, the guilt will finally crush him.
