felt like a fragile shell around something that had cracked three years ago. The trauma itself was a
single, impossible morning during monsoon season in his hometown in Thailand, where he had been
visiting family. He had woken to the sound of water, not rain but a deep, groaning rush, and then
the wall of his bedroom had simply given way. He remembered the taste of silt and gasoline flooding
his mouth as he was swept through the dark, the way his ribs had scraped against a submerged
doorframe, and the absolute silence after the water stopped rising, when he was alone on a roof,
watching the brown surface swallow everything he had known. That taste, that specific, chemical
grit, still arrived without warning. Sometimes he would be brushing his teeth and his mouth would
fill with it, a phantom sensation so real he would have to spit and rinse repeatedly. Other times,
the emotion would hit him first—a wave of pure, paralyzing dread—and only minutes later would he
realize he had been standing frozen in his own kitchen, with no clear image of the flood in his mind
at all.

In the months that followed, Kai moved to the United States for university, telling himself he was
starting over. But the memory was so extreme, so far outside the realm of normal experience, that it
often felt like it had happened to someone else. He could narrate the facts of that morning like a
story, but the story itself felt unreal, like a film he had watched rather than a life he had lived.
This disconnect only deepened his sense that he no longer belonged anywhere. He saw his American
classmates worrying about deadlines and relationships, and he felt like an alien observing a species
whose concerns he could no longer share. He had come to believe that people were not what they
seemed—that beneath every friendly surface lay the same capacity for chaos and abandonment that the
flood had revealed. He kept his distance, and his isolation became a kind of self-fulfilling
prophecy.

The physical symptoms started about a year after the flood. A persistent, gnawing pain in his neck
would settle in during the afternoon, and with it would come a general feeling of being physically
unwell, as though his body were bracing for impact. These somatic triggers were the most insidious,
because they had no obvious cause. He would be sitting at his desk, wireframing a new app interface,
and the ache would bloom at the base of his skull. The moment he noticed it, the memory would surge
back—not the visual scene, but the full-body terror, the taste of gasoline, the knowledge that he
was about to die. This made him feel even more isolated, more certain that he was fundamentally
broken, and that certainty made his body tighten further, which made the neck pain worse. The loop
was seamless and exhausting.

His work as a UX designer, which required long hours of focused screen time, became a daily
battleground. He had begun to avoid any project that involved water imagery or blue color schemes,
and he had quietly asked his manager to be moved away from a team working on a travel app that
featured beach destinations. He told no one why. The avoidance felt necessary, a way to keep the
world small and predictable, but it also meant he was taking fewer assignments, falling behind on
deadlines, and eating lunch alone at his desk to avoid the communal kitchen where someone might have
left a glass of water on the counter. What frightened him most was not the memory itself, but the
feeling of being permanently untethered—of having a life that looked normal on the outside while,
inside, a single ache in his neck could send him back to that roof. He finally scheduled an
appointment with a therapist after he spent an entire Saturday lying on his bathroom floor, unable
to move, because the taste of silt had returned and he could not make it stop. He was hoping, more
than anything, to feel like he belonged to his own life again.
