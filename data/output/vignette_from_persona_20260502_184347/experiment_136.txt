years ago, in the parking garage of the courthouse where she often consulted. A man she had helped

the concrete wall. She remembers the smell of stale coffee on his breath, the rough texture of the
wall against her scalp, and the way his thumb pressed into the hollow of her throat just below her
right ear. He let her go after a few seconds, but those seconds have never ended.

the case, a news report about a similar incident—would trigger a vivid, unwanted replay. More
troubling, she discovered that specific body parts could also pull her back: the sight of a man's
thumb, or the curve of a jawline, or the particular way a hand could form a grip. When she sees
these things, the memory rushes in before she can stop it, and she feels a surge of intense rage so
powerful it frightens her. At other times, she recalls the event but feels nothing—a strange, hollow
numbness, as if she is watching a stranger in a film. She has come to believe that if she lets
herself think about it fully, she will not be able to handle it, so she pushes the memory away
before it can complete itself, leaving her with fragments that feel unfinished and threatening.

Marlene has begun to see her own inadequacy everywhere. She used to consider herself a capable,
happy person, but now she is certain that she is fundamentally inadequate—that she failed to protect
herself, that she should have seen it coming, that she is no longer the person she was. This belief
in her own inadequacy has made her miserable, and she has lost the sense of happiness she once took
for granted. Because she believes she cannot cope with the memory, she has started to restrict her
eating, finding that an empty stomach gives her a sense of control she otherwise lacks. She also
avoids exercise, because the physical sensations of exertion—the pounding heart, the shortness of
breath—remind her too much of the moment of the attack, and she cannot bear that. These strategies,
however, only reinforce her sense of inadequacy: she sees herself as someone who cannot eat
normally, who cannot move her body, who is diminished.

Her belief that she is inadequate also makes her hypervigilant to potential triggers. She now scans
every room she enters for threats, and when she sits down, she always places her back against a wall
so she can see the entire space. This constant scanning means she notices more body parts, more
potential reminders, and so the triggers multiply. The act of sitting with her back to the wall
itself becomes a reminder of the attack, because she is always aware of why she is doing it.

The memory of the assault, when it surfaces, strengthens her belief that she is inadequate. She
replays the moment and thinks, *I should have fought back. I should have screamed. I am weak.* This
negative appraisal then makes the memory more accessible, because she is constantly monitoring
herself for signs of that weakness, and the memory is the ultimate proof of it. The cycle tightens:
the memory makes her feel inadequate, which makes her avoid eating and exercise, which makes her
feel more inadequate, which makes her scan for triggers, which brings the memory back.

At work, Marlene now avoids talking about the event with anyone, and she has stopped taking cases
that involve personal violence. She used to be the go-to analyst for assault cases; now she requests
only property crimes or fraud. Her colleagues have noticed, and she has felt their unspoken
questions. She has also stopped talking about the future with her husband. When he mentions
retirement plans or trips they might take, she feels a cold dread, as if planning ahead is an
invitation for disaster. She cannot imagine a future that includes happiness, because she no longer
believes she deserves one.

What finally brought Marlene to seek help was a moment in the grocery store. She saw a man reaching
for a jar on a high shelf, his thumb pressed against the glass in a way that mirrored the pressure
on her throat. She dropped her basket and walked out, leaving the cart in the aisle. She stood in
the parking lot, shaking, and realized she could not keep living this way. She is terrified that
therapy will force her to remember the event fully, and she is certain she will not survive that.
But she is also exhausted, and she has begun to wonder, for the first time, if there might be
another way to live.
