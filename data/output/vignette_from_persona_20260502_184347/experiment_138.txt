Mai was thirty-one, a journalist who had spent years covering city council meetings and human-
interest features for a regional newspaper in the Pacific Northwest. She was divorced, living alone
in a small apartment whose windows she kept curtained even during the day. Eighteen months ago, her
younger brother, Anh, had died suddenly of a pulmonary embolism at twenty-eight. He had been
healthy, or so everyone believed. Mai had been at her desk when her mother called; she remembered
the way the phone felt like a foreign object in her hand, the way her editor’s voice came from far
away when she tried to explain why she had to leave.

In the weeks after the funeral, Mai returned to work because she did not know what else to do. But
something had shifted. The newsroom, once familiar, now felt charged with an invisible threat. She
found herself scanning the faces of her colleagues during morning meetings, watching for signs of
distress or illness. When an editor mentioned a colleague’s recent hospital visit for a routine
procedure, Mai’s stomach tightened and she could not focus for the rest of the afternoon. She began
arriving early to avoid the crowded elevator, certain that if she were trapped in close quarters
with someone who looked pale or tired, she would not be able to breathe.

Her apartment became a kind of fortress. She checked the locks three times before bed, not because
she feared an intruder, but because she had come to believe that safety was an illusion—that
disaster could enter through any unnoticed gap. She could not relax even when she knew she was in a
safe place. Sitting on her couch, scrolling through her phone, she would suddenly feel her heart
race, her palms damp, as if some unseen danger were approaching. She was superalert and watchful, on
guard against a threat she could not name. The feeling was strongest at night, when the silence of
the apartment seemed to amplify her own breathing, and she would lie awake, listening.

Mai carried strong negative feelings that she could not shake. There was guilt: she had not called
Anh the week before he died, had been too busy with a deadline. There was anger: at the doctors who
had missed the clot, at the universe for taking him, at herself for not being able to move on. And
there was fear—a constant, low-level fear that something else terrible was about to happen, that she
would lose someone else, that her own body might betray her the way Anh’s had.

Over the months, her vigilance became a habit she could not break. At work, she avoided the
breakroom where people gathered to talk about their weekends, because she did not want to hear about
vacations or illnesses or anything that reminded her that life continued while Anh’s had stopped.
She stopped meeting friends for coffee; the effort of pretending to be normal was too great. Her
editor noticed her distance, the way she flinched when someone touched her shoulder, the way she
declined assignments that required travel. Mai told her she was fine, just tired.

What finally brought Mai to seek help was a moment in the grocery store. A child coughed behind her,
and she dropped her basket, her vision tunneling, her chest so tight she thought she might collapse.
She stood in the aisle, trembling, while a stranger asked if she was okay. She could not explain
what had happened. She only knew that she could not go on like this—that the hypervigilance, the
inability to relax, the constant tide of fear and guilt and anger had worn her down until she no
longer recognized herself. She was afraid that she was losing her mind, and she was afraid that this
was just how her life would be now. But she made an appointment with a therapist, and she showed up.
