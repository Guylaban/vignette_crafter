The accident happened during a routine weld on a structural beam. Maya, thirty-eight, had been a
welder for over a decade, her hands steady and her eye precise. That afternoon, a colleague had
walked into her work zone just as she struck the arc. The flash caught him square in the eyes; he
screamed, stumbled backward, and fell from the scaffolding, breaking his arm and wrist. Maya
remembered the sound of his cry, the smell of burning metal mixed with something else, and the taste
of copper flooding her mouth—a taste that still returned without warning, thick and metallic on her
tongue, even months later. What frightened her most was that she could only recall fragments: the
arc, the scream, the fall. The moments before and after were gone, leaving a hole she could not
fill.

That incomplete picture haunted her. When the taste came back, often at night as she lay in bed, her
mind would race to fill the gaps with worse possibilities—what if she had seen him, what if she had
called out, what if she had done something differently. She would dwell on what she had done wrong,
replaying the same imagined corrections until her chest tightened and her heart pounded. These
physical reactions—the racing pulse, the sweat, the sense of suffocation—made the memory feel even
more present, as if she were back under the helmet, the arc flaring. She had trouble falling asleep
because she dreaded the taste arriving, and when she did sleep, she woke exhausted, her
concentration frayed at work. Her supervisor had noticed her missing simple steps on the weld line,
and she had started taking longer breaks, avoiding the bay where the accident had occurred.

Maya began to avoid her colleagues altogether. She ate lunch alone in her truck, clocked in and out
without speaking, and took shifts that minimized contact. She was certain they blamed her—she blamed
herself, after all—and she could not bear to see the man she had hurt or hear his name. Thinking
about the event made the guilt surge, so she tried to push it away, but the more she avoided, the
more the memory intruded, the taste arriving at unpredictable moments: while driving, while
showering, while staring at a weld seam. Each time, the guilt hit her like a physical blow, and she
would retreat further, telling herself she was dangerous, that she did not deserve to be around
other people.

Over weeks, the cycle tightened. The memory triggered the physical threat response; the threat
response made the memory feel more vivid and more real; her avoidance strategies kept her isolated
and unable to test her belief that everyone saw her as a careless monster. She stopped answering
calls from friends, missed her niece’s birthday, and began drinking a glass of wine each night to
quiet her mind before bed. The drinking helped her fall asleep but left her groggy, and her
concentration worsened. At work, she nearly dropped a torch on her foot, and her foreman pulled her
aside, concerned. Maya finally sought help because she was terrified she would hurt someone
again—not from carelessness, but from the constant distraction and exhaustion that had taken over
her life. She hoped, more than anything, to find a way to remember the accident fully and
accurately, so she could finally know what she had done, and stop being afraid of herself.
