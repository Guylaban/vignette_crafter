but nothing had prepared him for the night his wife, Mai, died. They had been driving home from a
dinner celebrating their thirty-fifth anniversary when a truck ran a red light and T-boned the
passenger side. Thanh had been conscious through the entire rescue, pinned behind the wheel,
watching paramedics work on Mai as she slipped away. He could still hear the sound of the impact—a
wet, metallic crunch that played in his head at random moments, often when he least expected it. But
worse than the sound was the fragmentation: the memory came in jagged pieces that refused to form a
coherent story. He would remember the smell of gasoline, then the paramedic’s voice saying “she’s
not breathing,” then the cold of the glass against his cheek—but never in sequence, never as a
single narrative he could hold onto. This disjointed quality made him feel as though he had not
truly processed what happened, and he had come to believe that if he could not remember it properly,
he must be failing Mai somehow.

Because the memory felt broken and unreliable, Thanh found himself scanning every room he entered
for potential threats. In the courthouse hallways, he watched other attorneys’ hands, certain

hypervigilance exhausted him, and by evening he would eat large portions of whatever was in the
fridge—not from hunger, but to quiet the restless feeling that something bad was about to happen
again. The overeating became a nightly ritual, a way to distract himself from the images that
surfaced when he tried to sleep. Yet the distraction was short-lived: after eating, he would often
remember the paramedic’s face, or the way Mai’s hand had felt limp in his, and the grief would wash
