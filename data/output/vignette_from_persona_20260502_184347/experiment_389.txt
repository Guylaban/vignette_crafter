Mira is a 35-year-old physical therapist, married for eight years, who came to therapy three months
after a violent incident with her husband, Leo, during an argument that escalated when he pinned her
against the kitchen counter and held a hot pan against her forearm until she stopped struggling. She
had never seen that look in his eyes before, and she had frozen, apologizing, convinced that if she
had just not raised her voice about the finances, he would not have lost control. When she tries to
tell anyone what happened, she loses the thread—her sentences collapse, and she gives up,
overwhelmed by a wave of heat and shame that makes her feel as though she is back in that kitchen,
the skin on her arm burning, her voice trapped in her throat. The memory does not feel like a story
she tells; it feels like a thing that keeps happening to her, and when it returns, she has to stop
whatever she is doing, her heart racing, her palms slick, unable to speak for several minutes.

In the weeks that followed, Mira became acutely attentive to the temperature of surfaces—she
flinched when she touched a warm coffee cup, and she began wearing long sleeves even in summer,
partly to cover the fading scar, partly because the sensation of fabric against her arm felt safer
than the possibility of an unexpected warmth. She also noticed that she could not tolerate being
told what to do. At work, when a patient insisted she adjust their therapy in a way she knew was
wrong, she felt a cold fury rise in her chest, and she would comply mechanically, then later dwell
for hours on how she should have refused. She had come to believe that the abuse happened because of
the way she acted—that she had been too confrontational, too loud—and that there was something
fundamentally defective in her, a brokenness that Leo had merely exposed. This belief made her feel
permanently humiliated, as though everyone who looked at her could see that she had been overpowered
in her own home, and she began scanning the faces of male patients and colleagues, watching for the
subtle signs of anger she had missed before.

Mira found herself caught in a loop she could not name. The more she dwelled on how she used to be
before the event—confident, decisive, someone who laughed easily—the more vigilant she became for
dangerous people, especially men with a certain tightening around the jaw. That vigilance itself
became a trigger: when she noticed a patient’s hand clench on a therapy table, her stomach dropped,
and she felt again the helplessness of being forced to stay still while the pan pressed down. She
would then spend the rest of the day replaying the argument in her mind, trying to find the exact
moment she could have prevented it, as though if she just thought hard enough, she could rewrite the
past and restore the person she used to be. This prevention rumination made her hypervigilance
worse, because she was now watching not only for danger but for her own failure to see it coming,
and every near-miss—a raised voice in the grocery store, a door slammed by a neighbor—felt like
proof that she was still the person who had let it happen.

Her avoidance was subtle but costly. She stopped cooking with pans, asking her husband to handle all
hot meals, and she began taking the stairs at work to avoid the crowded elevator where someone might
brush against her arm. She declined invitations to dinner with friends, terrified that someone would
ask about the scar or that a well-meaning hug would send her spiraling. Her marriage became a
careful choreography of silence: she did not bring up the incident, and Leo did not either, and she
began to believe that if she could just be a better wife—quieter, more accommodating—she could
prevent any recurrence. What brought her to therapy was not the memory itself but the growing dread
that she was becoming someone she did not recognize: a woman who flinched at her own husband’s
touch, who could not finish a sentence about her own life, who was certain that the humiliation she
carried was visible to everyone. She was afraid that if she did not find a way out of this, she
would lose the last part of herself that still believed she deserved to feel safe.
