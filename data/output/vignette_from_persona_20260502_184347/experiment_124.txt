The drowning happened on a Saturday afternoon in late August, at a crowded lake beach north of the
city. Marcus had been twenty-two, still in his final year of pharmacy school, and his younger
brother Koa had just turned nineteen. They had gone with friends to celebrate. Marcus remembered the
heat, the way the air felt thick and wet, the sound of music from a portable speaker. He remembered
Koa laughing, then wading out past the buoys, then the moment someone pointed and said, *is he
okay?* The next hour was a blur he could never reassemble into a clear story—he could only recall
fragments: a lifeguard shouting, the strange stillness of the water, the way his own legs would not
move. Koa did not survive. The official report said a sudden cramp, possibly alcohol-related; there
had been a cooler of beer on the beach. Marcus had not touched alcohol since.

In the months that followed, Marcus found himself unable to hold the event in his mind as a coherent
narrative. When he tried to remember, he would either feel nothing at all—a flat, hollow numbness,
as if the memory belonged to someone else—or he would be struck by a wave of guilt so sharp it
doubled him over. He should have been watching. He should have said something when Koa went out past
the buoys. He should have been a better older brother. These thoughts came without warning,
triggered by things that seemed unrelated: the smell of beer on a customer’s breath at the pharmacy,
the sight of a funeral procession from the store window, the sound of a group of people laughing too
loudly. Each time, the memory would surge back—fragmented, unbearable—and Marcus would feel his body
tense as if he were still standing on that beach, helpless.

He worked at a chain pharmacy in a strip mall, and the job had become a daily minefield. The smell
of alcohol was the worst: a patient would lean across the counter, and the faint sour note on their
breath would send Marcus’s stomach into freefall. His body would react before his mind caught up—his
heart pounding, his palms slick, his shoulders locked—and he would realize, seconds later, that he
was not in danger at all. Nothing was wrong. But his body stayed on alert, tense and ready, as if
the threat were still there. He had come to believe that his life had been destroyed by that
afternoon, that the person he used to be—someone who laughed easily, who felt hopeful about the
future—was gone forever. Nothing felt meaningful anymore. He went to work, he came home, he stared
at the wall. He had stopped seeing his friends because their sympathy made him feel worse, and
because being around them meant the possibility of someone mentioning Koa, or of a drink being
opened, or of a joke being made that felt too light for a world that had taken his brother.

The cycle tightened. The more Marcus believed his life was ruined, the more vigilant his body
became, scanning every room for signs of danger. And the more his body stayed on alert, the more
convinced he grew that he was broken, that he would never feel safe or happy again. The guilt, too,
fed the vigilance: if he let his guard down, he reasoned, he might fail someone else the way he had
failed Koa. The smells and the grief-soaked places he avoided—funeral homes, cemeteries, the liquor
aisle at the grocery store—only grew more powerful in his absence, because he never gave himself the
chance to learn they were not dangerous. He had stopped visiting his parents because their house
still had Koa’s room, and because his mother cried every time she saw him. He told himself he was
protecting them. He knew, somewhere underneath, that it was himself he was protecting.

What finally brought Marcus to therapy was a panic attack in the pharmacy break room, triggered by a
coworker’s perfume that smelled, inexplicably, of lake water. He had called in sick the next day,
and the day after that, and he had realized he could not keep living this way—half-present, waiting
for the next trigger, carrying a guilt that would not fade. He was afraid that talking about Koa
would make it worse. He was also afraid, for the first time in two years, that staying silent might
be worse still.
