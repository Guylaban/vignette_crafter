María was 78 years old, a retired librarian who had spent decades organizing the quiet chaos of a
public library, but the order she once imposed on shelves now eluded her own mind. She had fled her
home country in Latin America decades ago, a refugee from political violence, but the event that
haunted her most was a single night at a military checkpoint. Soldiers had pulled her from a bus,
forced her to kneel on gravel as they searched her belongings, and held a gun to her head while
shouting accusations. She could still smell the diesel fumes from their truck and the sour sweat of
the man who pressed the metal against her temple. That odor would surface without warning—while she
walked through the produce aisle of a supermarket, or when her husband turned on the gas stove—and
the guilt would follow, sharp and immediate. She blamed herself for ever boarding that bus, for not
hiding in the countryside like her cousin had advised. The memory came back in fragments she could
not put into words, a jumble of sensations and shame that left her breathless.

In the weeks and months after her resettlement, María found that the smells could ambush her at any
moment, and each time they did, her body tensed as if she were back on that gravel road. Her heart
would pound, and she would scan the room for exits, certain that danger was near. The hypervigilance
became a constant companion: she was superalert in crowds, watchful of any man in uniform, and she
could only feel safe in her own home, and even then, only in the living room with the curtains
drawn. This state of alert drained her concentration. She would sit down to read a novel—a lifelong
pleasure—and find herself staring at the same paragraph for twenty minutes, unable to track the
words because her mind was listening for footsteps.

To escape this, María threw herself into busyness. She worked hard at keeping occupied: she
reorganized her kitchen cabinets alphabetically, volunteered at the local church’s food pantry three
days a week, and took on the task of cataloging her husband’s coin collection. The busyness was a
shield. If she was moving, she reasoned, the memories could not catch her. But the strategy
backfired. The exhaustion from constant activity made her more vulnerable, and when she finally sat
down, the smells and the guilt would rush in with renewed force. She would go over and over why the
event had happened to her, replaying her own decisions, repeatedly blaming herself for not fighting
back or for not staying silent. These ruminations made the memory feel more vivid, more present, and
soon she would be back in the hypervigilant state, her heart racing, her eyes darting.

The cycle tightened over the years. The more she ruminated, the more the memory intruded. The more
it intruded, the more she scanned for threats. The more she scanned, the more she needed to stay
busy. And the busier she was, the less she slept, and the less she slept, the more the olfactory
recalls surfaced. She stopped going to the library entirely, even though it had been her sanctuary.
The smell of old books, she discovered, was too close to the smell of the diesel fumes that had
clung to the soldiers’ uniforms. Her husband tried to reassure her, but she could not explain what
she could not put into words—the event lived in her body, not in language.

What finally brought María to seek help was a moment in the grocery store. A delivery truck idled
near the entrance, and the exhaust hit her nostrils. She froze in the aisle, dropped her basket, and
wept. The store manager called her husband, who drove her home in silence. That night, she told him
she could not keep living like this—always on guard, always guilty, always running from a smell. She
was afraid that her mind was breaking, but she was more afraid that the memory would never let her
go. She came to therapy hoping, for the first time in decades, to find words for what she could not
say.
