Leilani is a thirty-nine-year-old park ranger who grew up in a coastal village in Oceania before
moving to the United States in her twenties. For nearly a decade, she worked as a relief coordinator
for an international humanitarian organization, and it was during a deployment to a conflict zone
that her life fractured. She had been assigned to a small medical outpost near a contested city, and
one afternoon, as she helped unload supplies from a convoy, the compound was struck by mortar fire.
In the chaos that followed, she found herself pinned beneath debris, unable to move, while a
colleague beside her—a man named Tomas—was killed instantly. She remembers the smell of his cologne
mixing with dust and smoke, and she remembers the way his body was lifted and thrown by the blast.
What she cannot remember, no matter how hard she tries, is how she got out. There are gaps in her
memory that feel like swallowed seconds—she cannot tell a clear story of what happened, even to
herself. Some moments are agonizingly clear: the sound of the whistle before the mortar, the weight
of the metal across her legs, the cologne. Others are blank, and when she tries to explain what
happened to anyone, she loses the thread and cannot finish.

In the months after, Leilani left humanitarian work entirely and took a job as a park ranger in a
quiet national forest, believing that open space and solitude would help her heal. Instead, the
triggers followed her. The memory of the event comes to her mind suddenly, often without warning,
and it is almost always brought on by the smell of a specific perfume or cologne—any scent that
reminds her, even faintly, of Tomas. She has also found that physical affection, such as hugs from
her husband, can set off the memory, because the closeness of another body reminds her of being
pinned and helpless. Each time this happens, she feels the trauma surge back, and she has come to
believe that she cannot trust her own mind. She believes she acted in ways that go against who she
is—she had frozen, she had not been able to help Tomas, and she had survived when he did not—and
this belief has grown into a deeper conviction that she no longer belongs anywhere, that her life is
not worth living anymore. These negative appraisals make the triggers feel even more potent, because
each intrusion confirms to her that she is broken and that she does not deserve to feel safe.

Leilani has developed a set of maladaptive strategies that she uses to manage the overwhelming fear.

happened will only make the gaps in her memory worse, and she avoids places of worship or religious
gatherings, because the silence there reminds her of the stillness of Tomas's body. She is
hypervigilant towards people, scanning every visitor to the park for signs of danger, and she has
become acutely attuned to sudden movements by others, which put her immediately on guard. This
hypervigilance, in turn, reinforces her memory problems, because when she is constantly scanning for
threat, she cannot settle her mind enough to form a coherent narrative of what happened. The
hypervigilance also triggers the memory itself, because a sudden movement can feel like the blast,
and suddenly she is back in the compound. To manage this, she avoids unfamiliar places entirely,
because she cannot assess the danger there, and she has stopped going to town for groceries, asking
her husband to do the shopping instead. This avoidance has deepened her sense of isolation, and it
reinforces her negative appraisals—she believes that her life is not worth living if she cannot even
leave her own home.

The memory of the event, fragmented as it is, also drives her maladaptive strategies. Because she
cannot tell a clear story, she has come to believe that if she tries to get help, she will not be
able to explain herself, and so she avoids medical care entirely. The memory also triggers the
negative appraisals, because when she tries to piece together what happened, she lands on the moment
she froze, and she tells herself that she violated her own values. And the memory itself acts as a
trigger, because the gaps in her mind feel like a kind of threat—she is afraid of what she might
have done or not done during the blank seconds, and this fear brings the intrusive memory back more
often. Her husband has noticed that she flinches when he hugs her, and he has stopped initiating
affection, which makes Leilani feel even more disconnected. She has come to believe that she belongs
nowhere, not even in her own marriage.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was a moment in the forest last week. She was leading a
group of schoolchildren on a guided walk, and one of the children ran toward her with sudden,
excited movements. Leilani dropped to the ground, covering her head, and the children stared at her
in silence. She could not explain what happened. She could not finish the sentence. That night, she
told her husband that she was afraid of herself, and he made an appointment for her. She is most
afraid that therapy will force her to remember the gaps, and that what she finds there will confirm
that her life is not worth living. But she is also, quietly, hoping that someone can help her find a
way back to the person she used to be.
