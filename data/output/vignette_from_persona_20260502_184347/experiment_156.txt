way his own voice sounded strange and distant when he tried to call out. He remembers the
paramedic’s gloved hand on his shoulder, and how he flinched so hard he nearly hit his head on the
stretcher rail. That flinch has never really stopped. Now, eight months later, he reacts with alarm
when his wife, Elena, touches his arm to get his attention at the dinner table, and he has come to
believe that his body no longer belongs to him in the way it once did.

found himself mute, his throat tight, the images flooding back without language. What comes to him
instead are sounds: the screech of tires, the low groan of twisting metal, the sharp pop of the
airbag deploying. These auditory fragments rise up without warning, often in the middle of a dance
class when a student’s sneaker squeaks against the floor or when the theater door slams shut. Each
time they surface, he is flooded with a shame so acute it feels physical—a hot, crawling sensation
under his skin. He has come to believe that the accident happened to him because of the sort of
person he is: careless, distracted, someone who deserved to be punished. This conviction has taken
root in him like a splinter he cannot remove. He is certain that he can never feel normal emotions
again, that something essential inside him has been permanently shattered, and that no matter how
carefully he lives, bad things will keep finding him because he is fundamentally the kind of person
they happen to.

Because Carlos is convinced that catastrophe is always waiting, he has begun to scan for exits as
soon as he enters any room or building. He does it automatically now—a quick, practiced sweep of the
eyes that locates every door, every window, every possible escape route. The habit has become so
ingrained that he cannot stop it, and the more he scans, the more vividly the memory of the crash
returns: the blind panic of being trapped, the metal folding in around him, the certainty that he
was about to die. Each memory reinforces his belief that he is helpless, that his character is
flawed, that the world is a place where the next disaster is simply a matter of time. This loop
tightens daily.

To manage the constant threat, Carlos has developed strategies that he knows, on some level, are not
helping. He suppresses his thoughts about the trauma with tremendous effort, pushing them down the
moment they surface, but the suppression itself seems to make the memories return more forcefully

started staying up until very late, sometimes until three or four in the morning, because the hours
before dawn feel safer—quieter, emptier, less likely to surprise him. The sleep deprivation leaves
him exhausted and emotionally brittle, which in turn deepens his conviction that he is broken beyond
repair. In the worst moments, he deliberately tries to leave his body, to blank out, to float
somewhere above himself where the fear cannot reach him. But dissociation only fragments his memory
further, making it harder to integrate what happened, and leaving him feeling more alienated from
himself and from the life he used to lead.

The consequences have been concrete. Carlos has stopped teaching the advanced class because the
dance studio has no back exit, only the single front door, and he cannot bear the trapped feeling

home, that he flinches from her touch, that he spends his evenings staring at the ceiling instead of
sleeping beside her. She asked him last week if he was angry with her, and he could not find the
words to explain that he is not angry—he is terrified, and ashamed of being terrified, and convinced
that he is no longer the man she married.

What finally brought Carlos to seek help was a moment in the grocery store last Tuesday. A child
dropped a jar of pasta sauce, and the sound of glass shattering sent him to his knees in the aisle,
his hands over his ears, his heart slamming against his ribs. A stranger helped him up. He could not
stop apologizing. He is afraid that next time, he will not be able to get back up at all. He is
hoping, more than he can say, that someone can help him find a way back into his own body, into his
