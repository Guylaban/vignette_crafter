For three years, Kwame had worked as a logistics coordinator for an international humanitarian
organization in a conflict zone in the Sahel. He was good at his job—calm under pressure, efficient,
able to keep supply chains moving through checkpoints and across contested roads. Then, on a routine
convoy to deliver medical supplies to a rural clinic, his vehicle hit a buried improvised explosive
device. The blast tore through the armored hull. Two colleagues in the rear cabin were killed
instantly. Kwame, in the front passenger seat, was thrown against the dashboard and lost
consciousness. When he came to, the world was smoke and screaming. He crawled out of the wreckage,
pulled one wounded driver clear, and waited—for what felt like hours—for extraction. No one came to
hold the perpetrators accountable. The attack was never claimed. The people responsible faced no
consequences. Back in the United States now, married and working as a social media manager, Kwame
had come to believe that what happened to him was deeply unfair, and that nothing had been done
about it. This sense of profound injustice sat inside him like a stone.

It was this conviction—that the world had let him down and that he could not trust the systems meant
to protect him—that made him feel perpetually unsafe. He had come to believe that he could not rely
on himself to stay out of danger, because his own judgment had failed him that day. He had chosen
the route. He had approved the timing. And now, every time a car backfired on the street outside his
office, his body seized. He was easily startled, jumping at the sound of a dropped pen or a
colleague clearing their throat. He found it difficult to concentrate on the endless stream of posts
and metrics in front of him because his attention was constantly pulled toward the periphery of his
vision—scanning doorways, monitoring the expressions of people who walked too close to his desk. He
was superalert, watchful, on guard, even in the most mundane settings.

This hypervigilance led Kwame to adopt strategies that only deepened his sense of threat. He avoided
thinking about the bombing during the workday, but the effort of suppression left him drained and
irritable. At home, he would find himself detaching from the memories—watching his own recollections
as if they were a film playing on a screen, disconnected from his body. This emotional numbness
frightened him, and the more he tried to push the memories away, the more his nervous system stayed
on high alert. He began checking his surroundings constantly, even in his own apartment: scanning
the parking lot before getting out of the car, noting the exits in every room. But the scanning
itself kept his anxiety alive. Each safe moment proved nothing; each unexpected sound proved
everything.

His marriage had begun to fray under the weight of these cycles. His wife would reach for him at
night, and he would flinch. He told her he was fine. He told his boss he was fine. But his
productivity had slipped, and he had started taking longer routes to work to avoid the overpass that
reminded him of the convoy's last turn. A few weeks ago, a delivery truck backfired outside his
office window, and Kwame hit the floor. His hands were shaking for twenty minutes. That was the
moment he understood that the strategies he had built to survive—the avoidance, the scanning, the
detachment—were not protecting him. They were keeping him trapped. He came to therapy because he was
afraid of what he might lose next: his marriage, his job, his sense of himself as someone capable of
moving forward. What frightened him most was the thought that he might never feel safe again, and
that no amount of vigilance could undo what had already happened.
