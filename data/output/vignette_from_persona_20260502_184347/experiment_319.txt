Malia first traveled to the conflict zone at twenty-seven, a year out of graduate school, eager to
put her training in crisis intervention to use. She was assigned to a field hospital on the
outskirts of a city that had been under siege for months. What she remembers now is fragmented—the
way dust coated everything, the constant hum of generators, the smell of burnt metal and something
sweet she could never identify. But the parts she cannot reach are the ones that haunt her most.
There are entire stretches of those weeks that feel like a locked room in her mind. When she tries
to deliberately recall what happened during the worst of the shelling, her thoughts skid away, and
she finds herself pushing the memory back before it can fully form. It is as if her own brain has
walled off the worst of it, and she both resents and relies on that protection.

Now, at thirty, Malia works as a parole officer in a midsized American city. The job was supposed to
be a return to order—structured meetings, paperwork, predictable routines. Instead, it has become a
daily minefield. The taste of the instant coffee in the breakroom sometimes triggers a visceral
lurch in her stomach; it reminds her of the sweet, cloying drinks she was given by local aid workers
who tried to keep her hydrated. She has learned to bring her own tea. More difficult to manage are
the moments when a client raises their voice in her office. Even calm expressions of frustration—a
clenched jaw, a raised hand in explanation—send a surge of heat through her chest. She has come to
believe that anger is always one step away from violence, that any man who raises his voice is about
to become the men she saw screaming orders in the triage tent.

The victim memories are the hardest. When she reviews a client’s file and reads about a robbery or
an assault, she sees not a crime report but the faces of the wounded she could not save. One man in
his forties, recently released and mandated to meet with her weekly, reminds her of a medic she
worked alongside who was killed by shrapnel. She cannot look at him without her throat tightening.
She has started scheduling his appointments for late in the day so she can go home immediately
afterward.

Over the months, Malia has built a quiet architecture of avoidance. She never eats lunch in the
communal kitchen, where the smell of reheated food might trigger the taste memory. She keeps her
office door closed and conducts meetings with her back to the window, because the sound of a car
backfiring outside makes her flinch. She has stopped attending team meetings where colleagues
discuss their caseloads with passion; she tells herself she is too busy, but the truth is that their
raised voices, even in animated agreement, feel like a threat.

The consequences have accumulated. Her supervisor has noted her withdrawal and asked if she is
overwhelmed. Malia said no. She has stopped seeing friends, because she cannot explain why the smell
of grilled meat at a barbecue makes her excuse herself to the bathroom. Her divorce, finalized a
year ago, was partly a casualty of this silence—her ex-husband could not understand why she would
freeze when he argued about grocery bills.

What finally brought Malia to seek help was a moment last week. She was driving home and passed a
food truck selling street meat. The gustatory memory hit her so hard she had to pull over, her mouth
filling with the phantom taste of that sweet drink, her hands shaking on the wheel. She sat in the
car for twenty minutes, unable to remember how to get home. That night, she searched online for a
therapist who specializes in trauma. She is terrified that the locked room in her mind is going to
break open, and even more terrified that it will stay sealed forever, leaving her stranded in a life
she no longer recognizes.
