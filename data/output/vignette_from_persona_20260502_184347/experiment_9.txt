beeping monitors that never stopped, and the cold panic of a doctor’s face as they inserted a chest
tube. What stayed with him most, though, was the moment he couldn’t catch his breath—a feeling of
being trapped in his own body, certain he was dying. The medical team saved his life, but the
experience left an invisible scar.

In the months that followed, David’s world began to shrink. He noticed his body felt constantly
tense and ready to react to danger, as if a switch had been left on. When he felt strong negative
emotions—fear, anger, or shame—his chest would tighten, and he’d become hyperaware of his breathing.
This physical reactivity made him feel that his body was betraying him, and he came to believe that
any loss of control over his internal state could lead to disaster. To manage this, he began to
adopt strict strategies. He stopped exercising entirely, because even a slight increase in his heart
rate triggered the same suffocating panic he’d felt in the hospital. He also restricted his eating,
controlling every calorie and meal time with meticulous precision, believing that if he could master

ambulances, even the sight of a stethoscope on a TV show—because they brought back the raw terror of
those days.

These strategies, however, backfired. By avoiding exercise and eating so rigidly, David’s overall

trigger that sent him spiraling. He had to excuse himself to the bathroom, gripping the sink,

procedures or physical exertion, the more his body became a source of unpredictable alarms.
