David is a forty-nine-year-old city council member, married for over twenty years, who served a
single deployment in a combat zone nearly two decades ago. The event that haunts him happened on a
routine patrol: an improvised explosive device detonated beneath the lead vehicle in his convoy. He
remembers the shockwave, the heat, the screaming—but only in fragments. When he tries to describe it
to anyone, he loses the thread, unable to hold the sequence together, and the terror of that moment
floods back as if he were still standing in the smoke. He has never been able to piece together a
full account, and that incompleteness has come to feel like a personal failure.

In the years since returning home, David has come to believe that the world is fundamentally
dangerous, a conviction that hardened quietly over time. Even when his wife tells him they are safe
in their own home, he finds it impossible to trust that reassurance. His body remains on alert, as
though it has decided he is still in danger no matter what his mind knows. He feels betrayed by his
own physical reactions—his heart racing, his muscles tensing—because they never seem to get the
message that the war is over. This sense of the body as an enemy has made him hypervigilant; he
scans every room he enters, every face in a crowd, convinced that threat is hiding just out of
sight. He has to be on guard all the time, because relaxing even for a moment feels like an
invitation to catastrophe.

The hypervigilance feeds directly into his sleep problems. He lies awake most nights, his mind
cycling through imagined scenarios of what could go wrong, and when he finally drifts off, fragments
of the explosion surface in nightmares that jolt him back awake. The lack of rest only sharpens his
sense that the world is hostile, and that he cannot let his guard down. On the rare occasions his
wife touches him unexpectedly—a hand on his shoulder, a gentle nudge in bed—he reacts with alarm,
flinching or pulling away before he can stop himself. This startle response has become a source of
shame and distance between them, though he cannot seem to override it.

To manage the constant pressure of his own vigilance, David has developed strategies that he knows
are not healthy but that feel necessary. He puts on loud music or the television at high volume
whenever he is alone, using the noise to drown out the intrusive images and the internal monitoring
of his own thoughts. He spends a great deal of mental energy checking his own mind for signs that he
is losing control, that he is going crazy, because the intensity of his fear sometimes feels like it
must be a precursor to something worse. He has also begun to avoid all forms of physical contact
with others, steering clear of handshakes at council meetings and sitting with his chair angled away
from colleagues. These strategies provide a thin layer of control, but they also keep him isolated
and exhausted.

The more he avoids being touched, the more his memory of the explosion feels fragmented and
incomplete—as if the act of pulling away from contact reinforces the sense that the past is
something he cannot fully hold. And the more he monitors his thoughts and fills his environment with
noise, the more he convinces himself that he is fragile, barely holding it together, which deepens
his appraisal of the world as dangerous and his body as untrustworthy. The terror he feels when
remembering the event is so vivid that it loops back and strengthens his need to distract himself,
creating a cycle he cannot break on his own.

What finally brought David to seek help was a moment at a public town hall meeting last month. A
constituent touched his arm to get his attention, and he wheeled around with such force that the
room went silent. He saw the shock on the woman's face, and later that night, lying awake, he
realized he could not remember the rest of the meeting at all—only the flash of heat and the sound
of the explosion, as if the two moments had merged. He is afraid that he is losing his grip, that
his work and his marriage are slipping away, but he is equally afraid that if he stops being
vigilant, something terrible will finally happen.
