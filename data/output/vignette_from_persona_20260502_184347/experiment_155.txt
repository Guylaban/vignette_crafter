Sister Amara, an 80-year-old pastor from a West African nation, had spent decades shepherding a
small congregation in a Midwestern city. Her life had been one of service, prayer, and community,
until a single morning in a refugee camp, years before she came to America, rewired her world. She
had been leading a prayer group when armed militiamen stormed the compound, herding people at
gunpoint. She remembered the sound of a machete being struck against a metal door—a sharp, ringing
clang that seemed to hang in the air. In the chaos, she was separated from her family and forced to
watch as others were beaten. She survived by staying perfectly still, playing dead among the
wounded, a memory so extreme it sometimes felt unreal, as if it had happened to someone else.

Now, decades later, the memory forced its way into her mind without warning. She would be in her
kitchen, and the clang of a pot would become that machete on the door. She would hear the shouting,
the cries, as vivid as the day it happened. What frightened her most was the feeling of helplessness
that came with it—a deep, gnawing sense that if she let herself think about the event fully, she
would not be able to handle it. This belief had calcified into a rule: do not look back. She had
come to believe that what happened to her was deeply unfair, and that nothing had been done about
it, which left her feeling like an object, not a person, a piece of debris washed ashore by history.

This sense of injustice and dehumanization bled into everything. When she saw a bus, she felt the
unfairness of her past rise up, and she would quickly turn away, telling herself that the world was
a place where such things were allowed to happen. The triggers themselves—a bus riding past, a
sudden movement from a man reaching for his wallet—immediately put her on guard. She watched other
people closely for signs they might harm her, and she frequently looked behind her to check if
someone was following. These hypervigilant habits made the memories more accessible; scanning a
crowd for threats meant she was constantly primed to recall the camp, where every glance had been a
survival calculation.

Her strategies for coping only deepened the cycle. She refused to talk with anyone about the event
or its aftermath, not even her husband. She believed that speaking the words would make the memory
too real, too present. To avoid being noticed, she wore concealing clothing—loose, dark garments
that swallowed her frame—even in the heat of summer. This avoidance of medical or psychological help
meant she never had a doctor explain why her heart raced on a bus, so she simply stopped riding them
altogether. But the avoidance backfired: the more she hid, the more the bus became a forbidden,
potent trigger. Every time she chose to walk instead, the memory of the camp felt closer, not
further away.

Over the months, the cycle tightened. A trigger—a weapon-shaped object in a movie trailer—would
spark her negative appraisals, reinforcing that she was still a helpless victim. This belief then
made her scan more aggressively for threats, which in turn made the auditory memories louder. She
would hear the machete again, and that would prove to her that she could not handle thinking about
it, so she would avoid more aggressively, cutting herself off from her congregation, her friends,
and even her own body's need for care. Her husband noticed she no longer sat near windows in church,
and she had stopped visiting sick members in the hospital, afraid of the medical environment.

What finally brought Sister Amara to seek help was a Sunday service. A young man in the back row
reached into his jacket for a handkerchief, and the sudden movement sent her into a dissociative
state. She felt detached, unreal, as if she were watching herself from above. She could not finish
her sermon. She left the pulpit, went home, and told her husband she was tired. But that night, she
realized she was tired of being afraid. She came to the clinic hoping to find a way to make the
memories feel like the past, not the present. What she feared most was that she would never feel
like a person again—that she would remain an object, forever watching, forever waiting for the next
sudden movement.
