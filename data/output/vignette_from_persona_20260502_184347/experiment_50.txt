Adwoa is an 80-year-old Ghanaian American woman, a widow who spent decades as a policy analyst in
Washington, D.C., a profession that demanded she hold complex, often contradictory, information in
mind and synthesize it into clear arguments. That capacity for intellectual control was shattered on
a Tuesday afternoon six months ago. She was driving home from a community meeting when a drunk
driver ran a red light and T-boned her sedan. The impact was violent. She remembers the screech of
metal, the smell of burnt rubber and, most vividly, the smell of the other driver’s breath—sharp
with alcohol—as he leaned into her window before the ambulance arrived. That scent, and the
specific, oily smell of burnt rubber and metal from the wreck, now feel like landmines in her daily
life.

Since the accident, Adwoa’s mind has developed a terrible new logic. The smell of alcohol on a
stranger’s breath in a grocery store line, or the faint burnt smell from a toaster, instantly and
without warning hurls her back into the passenger seat. The memory does not unfold like a story; it
arrives as a fragmented, sensory assault. She sees the grill of the other car, feels the temperature
of the air change as the window shattered, but the moment of impact itself is a complete blank.
These triggered returns are so overwhelming that she has begun to push the memory away before she
can even fully recall it, a frantic mental slamming of a door. What frightens her most is that these
episodes are not under her control; a simple, sudden movement by a passerby on the sidewalk can have
the same effect, putting her instantly on guard, her heart hammering before she even knows why.

Adwoa has come to believe that the world is now a place of hidden threats, and that her own mind is
unreliable. A specific temperature on her skin—the coolness of an air-conditioned room that matches
the day of the crash—can trigger a flashback, and that flashback, in turn, floods her with a raw,
undiluted fear that feels identical to the moment of impact. She now watches the hands of other
drivers when she is a passenger, scanning for any sudden movement that might precede another
collision. This hypervigilance has a circular, exhausting quality: the more she scans for threat,
the more she notices potential triggers, like the faint smell of exhaust, and the more her memory of
the event feels fragmented and intrusive.

The consequences have been concrete and isolating. As a policy analyst, Adwoa was known for her

room, a temperature that reminds her of the car’s interior. She has declined all invitations to
professional events, telling colleagues she is “slowing down.” Her daughter, who lives nearby, has
noticed Adwoa flinch when she walks into a room too quickly. The smell of a neighbor’s barbecue,
with its faint burnt undertone, recently sent her into a panic in her own backyard. She has stopped
gardening. The world has shrunk to the few rooms in her house where she feels she can control the
temperature and the smells, and where she can see people approach from a distance. It was the moment
she realized she was afraid to open her own front door that finally brought her to seek help. What
she fears most is that her mind, once a tool of precision and order, has become a source of chaos
she can no longer manage.
