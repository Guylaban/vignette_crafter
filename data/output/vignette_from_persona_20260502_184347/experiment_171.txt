last. Sometimes the emotional intensity of that terror would wash over him without any accompanying
image at all, leaving him shaking in his armchair for no reason he could name.

Tran had always been a steady man, someone his crew relied on. But after the exposure, he began to
feel that he could not rely on himself anymore. The world had shown him how quickly safety could
vanish, and he came to believe it was a fundamentally dangerous place. This sense of pervasive
threat meant that even when he knew he was in a safe place — his own living room, his wife’s car —
he could not relax. His concentration frayed; he would lose track of conversations halfway through,
forget which tools he had put down. The conviction that he had no future settled into him like a
low-grade fever, and he started dwelling on how he used to be before the event — the man who climbed

whiff of antiseptic from a cleaning cart, or the faint chemical tang of paint thinner. These
triggers would instantly drag the memory to the surface, and then the film would start rolling
again. When that happened, Tran tried hard not to think about the trauma — he would force his mind
blank, fixate on the ceiling fan, count the seconds. But the suppression never worked for long, and
the failure only confirmed that he could not rely on himself. Because he saw danger everywhere, he
began avoiding unfamiliar places altogether; he could not assess the risk in a new grocery store or
a friend’s basement, so he simply stopped going. At work, he never disagreed or asserted himself
anymore, even when he knew a safety protocol was wrong, because conflict felt like another kind of
threat he could not manage. His crew noticed he had gone quiet, and the foreman stopped giving him
the complex jobs.
