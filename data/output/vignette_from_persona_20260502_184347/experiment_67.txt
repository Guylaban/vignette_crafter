Pacific Northwest. She had grown up in a small island nation in Oceania, and it was there, during a
brief civil conflict when she was a young woman, that she had been caught in a crossfire while
walking home from a relative’s house. The memory of that afternoon came to her not as a single story
but in disconnected pieces that did not fit together: the crack of a gunshot, the sensation of being
pushed to the ground by a stranger, the smell of damp concrete, and then a long silence that felt
like it lasted for hours. She had not been physically injured, but something in her had broken open
that day. When she remembered it now, she felt an intense shame, as though she should have been able
to stop it, as though her helplessness in that moment revealed something fundamental about who she
was.

In the years that followed, Malia built a life far from that street. She became a veterinarian,
married a kind man named James, and settled into a routine of early mornings at the clinic and quiet
evenings at home. But the war zone had never left her. Loud noises—a car backfiring, a door slamming
too hard—sent her memory replaying like a film that played over and over, the same disjointed images
surfacing without warning. She had come to believe that she could not rely on herself, that she
could not stop bad things from happening, and that what had happened to her was deeply unfair and
nothing had been done about it. These thoughts, which she carried like a weight, made the memories
sharper and more frequent, as though her own mind was punishing her for failing to protect herself.

Her work as a veterinarian, which she had once loved, became a landscape of triggers. Unexpected
touch from a frightened animal or a colleague placing a hand on her shoulder sent her heart racing
and brought the fragmented memories rushing back. Driving with people—even her husband—felt
unbearable; she was certain that if she was not in control of the vehicle, something terrible would
happen, and the shame of that fear only deepened her sense of helplessness. She began to avoid
carpooling with coworkers and made excuses to drive separately to appointments. At home, she had
difficulty falling asleep because she needed to stay alert, and even when she knew she was in a safe
place, she was unable to relax. She constantly scanned her surroundings for signs of danger, her
eyes moving from doorways to windows to the faces of strangers, always looking for the threat that
she was certain was coming. This hypervigilance wore her down, and it reinforced her belief that she
was helpless, that the world was unsafe, and that she could not trust herself to handle it.

The cycle tightened over months. Every time a trigger brought the memory back, her sense of
injustice and helplessness grew, and that made her more vigilant, more watchful, more certain that
danger was imminent. The memory itself, fragmented and shame-laden, kept her scanning for threats,
and the threats she perceived—a sudden noise, an unexpected touch—sent her straight back into the
memory. She began to feel as though she was living in two worlds at once: the quiet clinic where she
treated dogs and cats, and the war zone that she carried inside her, ready to erupt at any moment.
What frightened her most was not the memory itself but the feeling that she could not stop it, that
she was at the mercy of her own mind.

What finally brought Malia to seek help was a moment at the clinic when a colleague touched her arm

that someone could help her find a way out of the loop—to stop the film from playing, to stop the
scanning, to stop believing that she was the person who could not protect herself.
