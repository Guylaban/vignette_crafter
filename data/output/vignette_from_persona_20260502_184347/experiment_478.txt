Marco is a 24-year-old QA engineer who lives alone in a small apartment on the outskirts of a
midsized city. He was raised by his mother and grandmother in a close-knit Latin American family,
and he still speaks with them daily by phone. Two years ago, he was on a commuter bus that collided
with a fuel truck on the highway. The bus overturned and caught fire. Marco was one of the few
passengers who managed to crawl out through a shattered window. He remembers the heat on his face
and the sound of someone screaming for help from the back. He tried to go back in, but another
passenger pulled him away. The person who had been calling out did not survive. Marco has not spoken
about that moment with anyone, not even his mother.

In the months after the crash, Marco returned to work and tried to carry on. He told himself he was
fine. But he began to notice that certain things pulled him back into that day with a force he could
not control. A colleague mentioning a highway accident during a stand-up meeting would make his
chest tighten and his mind go blank. He would sit at his desk staring at his monitor, the code on
the screen suddenly illegible, while a detached, unreal feeling crept over him—as though he were
watching himself from somewhere far away. He started to avoid the break room when the news was on,
because any report about a fire or a collision made his stomach drop. Over time, he came to believe
that the world was fundamentally unsafe. He would think, *You can never know who will harm you*, and
the thought had the weight of a certainty. He also became convinced that he had failed. He should
have gone back for that person. He should have done more. The belief settled into him like a stone:
*Nothing good can happen to me anymore.* He stopped making plans for the future. He stopped dating.
What was the point.

Marco’s job as a QA engineer requires him to test software for bugs, but the work itself became a
kind of trap. After a trigger—a news headline, a coworker’s offhand remark about a crash—his mind
would spiral into counterfactual thinking. He would imagine, in vivid and exhausting detail, how
life would have been different if he had never taken that bus, if he had sat farther back, if he had
been faster. The thinking felt compulsive and useless, but he could not stop it. He also began
avoiding anything that reminded him of authority or danger. When he saw a police car parked near his
building, he would cross the street. He stopped watching television altogether, because he could not
predict when a show might include a scene involving an accident. He canceled his streaming
subscriptions. On weekends, he stayed inside.

The cycle tightened. A trigger would activate his appraisals—the sense that he had failed, that the
world was dangerous, that nothing good was ahead—and those appraisals would drive him further into
avoidance. He thought about quitting his job because the open-plan office felt exposed, but he
needed the income. Instead, he wore headphones constantly and kept his eyes on his screen. He
stopped attending team socials. His manager noticed that Marco had become withdrawn and missed
several deadlines, but Marco could not explain why. He was certain that if anyone knew what he was
thinking, they would see him as weak or broken.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment of quiet despair. He had spent a Saturday lying
in bed, running through the same counterfactual loop, and he realized he could not remember the last
time he had felt anything other than dread or numbness. He called a clinic the next morning. He is
afraid of being asked to talk about the crash in detail, but he is more afraid of staying the way he
is—stuck, isolated, and convinced that the best part of his life is already behind him.
