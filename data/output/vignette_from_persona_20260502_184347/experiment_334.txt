Meera was thirty-two years old, a single woman who had built her life around service and scrutiny.
As a city council member in a mid-sized American city, she was used to navigating crowded town
halls, tense negotiations, and the unpredictable energy of public meetings. That world ended on an

She was standing near the stage when the blast tore through a food vendor’s tent, sending a fireball
rolling across the park. In the chaos that followed—screaming, the smell of burnt metal, people
running in every direction—Meera found herself pinned beneath a collapsed table, her leg trapped,
unable to move as flames licked at the grass a few yards away. A stranger pulled her out, but in
those minutes, she had been certain she was going to die.

In the months after, Meera could not shake the feeling that the world had become a place where
disaster was always waiting. She began to believe that safety was an illusion, that she had to be on
guard all the time, that you never knew what could happen next. This conviction grew into a
constant, exhausting vigilance. She came to see the world as fundamentally dangerous, and that
belief shaped everything she did. Because she was certain that danger could strike at any moment,
she started scanning her environment constantly—checking for anything unusual, any person who seemed
too close or too still. She became especially careful around people she did not know. At council
meetings, she would study the audience before speaking, noting exits, watching hands and faces. She
found herself planning escape routes everywhere she went: before sitting down in a restaurant,
before entering a colleague’s office, before stepping into an elevator. She could not feel
comfortable in unfamiliar places at all, so she avoided them entirely, sticking to a narrow circuit
of home, city hall, and one grocery store she knew by heart.

Nighttime was worse. Meera found it harder to feel safe after dark, and she began to dread evening
events. She would leave community meetings early, claiming fatigue. She stopped attending the late-
night hearings that had once been part of her routine. The vigilance she maintained during the day
became unbearable at night, when shadows and silence amplified her sense of threat. And because she
was always scanning, always planning, always avoiding, her sense of danger never receded. Each time
she checked her surroundings and found nothing, she felt only a temporary relief, followed by the
renewed certainty that the next moment would be different. Her hypervigilance toward people—watching
for anyone who seemed aggressive, erratic, or simply out of place—kept her in a state of low-grade
alarm that fed back into her belief that the world was unsafe. She was constantly on edge, and that
edge only sharpened her conviction that she had to be more careful.

The consequences were concrete. Her colleagues noticed she was distracted, less available. She
stopped attending neighborhood walks and street festivals, events that had been central to her work.

begun to feel trapped in her own life, unable to trust the spaces and people she had once navigated

on emergency preparedness funding, she froze when a constituent raised a question about gas line
safety. Her mind went blank. She could not speak. She excused herself and did not return.

Now, sitting in a therapist’s office, Meera was terrified that she would never feel safe again. But
she was also exhausted—too tired to keep scanning, too tired to keep planning escape routes, too
tired to believe that her vigilance was keeping her alive. She hoped, more than anything, that
someone could show her how to stop.
