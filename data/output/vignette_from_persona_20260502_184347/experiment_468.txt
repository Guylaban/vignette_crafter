hours—that feel like a black hole in his mind. The memory comes only in fragments: a hand reaching

the memory away before it can fully form, as though his brain has learned to slam a door before the
nightmare can walk through.

the world is fundamentally unsafe. His shattered beliefs are not abstract—they are lived, daily. He
no longer trusts that a quiet emergency room will stay quiet, that a patient’s stable vitals will
remain stable. He is certain that violence can erupt at any moment, and that he will fail to stop

devastating is breathlessness. When he runs up the stairs to the trauma bay, when a mask is placed

is not the person he thought he was. He is weak, broken, no longer fit to be a doctor.

falls before a code blue, the still air of a patient’s room late at night. The memory itself feeds
this loop. When a fragment surfaces—the soldier’s pale face, the wet sound of a breath that would
not come—he is flooded with shame and fear, which only deepens his conviction that he has lost
himself. He has come to believe that remembering fully would destroy him, so he blocks it, but the
blocking leaves him with only disjointed pieces that feel more like accusations than memories. Each
piece reinforces his negative appraisals, and each appraisal makes the next trigger more potent.

His work has become a minefield. He avoids the trauma bay when he can, trading shifts with

takes the stairs instead of the elevator, not for exercise but because the enclosed space and the

with colleagues have frayed. He was once the youngest doctor everyone admired; now he is the one who
makes excuses, who disappears during handoffs, who seems distant and cold. He knows they talk. He

froze, and a senior nurse had to push him aside. No one said anything, but Kwame saw the look on her

shift, afraid of the person he had become. He is hoping—though he is not sure he believes it—that
there is a way back to who he was.
