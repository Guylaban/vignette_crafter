Kiet had been a nanny for thirty-seven years. He had raised other people’s children with a gentle,
methodical patience that families came to rely on, and after his wife passed, the work became the
shape of his days. The trauma that undid him was not his own body but his youngest grandson’s—a
sudden, violent sepsis at eighteen months old that Kiet had witnessed from the waiting room of a
county hospital. For three weeks the child lay intubated, and Kiet sat in a plastic chair, watching
monitors flicker, listening to machines breathe. He could not tell a clear story of what happened
even to himself. The days blurred into a single, suffocating image: the small chest rising and
falling not by its own will. Even now, years later, the memory felt more real and vivid than
memories of ordinary things—more real than his wife’s face, more vivid than the taste of pho. When
it surfaced, it did not feel like a recollection. It felt like it was happening right now, the
antiseptic smell, the beeping, the terrible stillness.

Kiet had come to believe that he had permanently changed for the worse. The person he had become was
unrecognisable to him. He used to be the one who hummed while folding laundry, who let toddlers pull
at his sleeves. Now he moved through the world as though wrapped in gauze. He had also become
certain that people were not what they seemed. The parents who hired him were kind, he knew, but he
found himself watching their faces for hidden impatience, for the moment they would reveal something
cruel. Because he believed he was damaged, and because he believed others might hurt him, he began
to avoid what he most needed. He suppressed thoughts of the hospital room with a fierce, clenched
effort, telling himself *do not think about it*. He kept his mind occupied all the time—podcasts,
radio, television, anything that filled the silence. He also avoided medical care entirely. When his
own doctor called to schedule a routine check-up, Kiet did not return the call. He could not sit in
another waiting room. He could not hear another machine.

The avoidance, however, did not quiet the memory. The more he pushed the image of his grandson’s
tiny chest away, the more it surfaced at night, unbidden, vivid, present. And each time it came
back, it reinforced his belief that he had been permanently broken by that room. The memory and the
appraisal fed each other: he could not escape the feeling of the event because he would not let
himself think about it, and each intrusion confirmed that he was no longer the man he had been. The
cycle tightened over months. At work, he found himself flinching when a child coughed. He began to
check the children’s breathing during naps, pressing a hand to their backs until he felt the rise.
One afternoon, a toddler in his care scraped her knee and cried, and Kiet felt his own chest
constrict so sharply he had to excuse himself to the bathroom. He stood there, palms flat on the
sink, telling himself it was not the same, but the memory came anyway—the beeping, the stillness—and
he could not breathe.

What finally brought Kiet to seek help was a single moment. The youngest child in his current
family, a boy of two, developed a mild fever. The mother mentioned it in passing, and Kiet felt a
wave of cold terror so absolute that he called in sick the next day for the first time in his
career. He sat in his empty apartment, the television off, the silence unbearable. He knew, then,
that he could not keep this up. He was afraid of what would happen if he let himself remember fully,
but he was also afraid of what he had already become: a man who could not care for the children he
loved. He wanted to go back to being the person who hummed while he worked. He was not sure that
