locks again. What frightened him most was not the memory of the explosion itself, but the certainty
that he was no longer the person he had been. He had always thought of himself as someone who would
stop, who would help. The fact that he had not—that he had chosen his own survival over a
stranger’s—meant, he believed, that he could not trust himself in any real crisis. This belief made
the world feel dangerous in a new way: if he could not rely on his own instincts, then every crowded
street, every sudden noise, every stranger who walked too close behind him became a potential
threat. He began checking over his shoulder constantly, especially when leaving the bank at the end

extra shifts, often arriving before the branch opened and leaving after the last customer had gone.

cash or processing deposits, he did not have to think about what had happened, or about the person
he had become. But the overwork came at a cost. He was too exhausted to see friends, and he had
