Kaelani is a 74-year-old divorced editor who came to therapy at the urging of his adult daughter,
after months of increasingly isolated living. The event that shattered his life happened nearly
forty years ago, during his marriage to a woman he had loved deeply. The abuse was not constant, but
it was devastating when it came—usually after she had been drinking, and usually triggered by
something he had said or done that displeased her. He remembers one night in particular, not as a
continuous story but as a series of frozen images: the way her hand looked as it swung toward his
face, the crack of his head against the kitchen cabinet, the smear of blood on the white tile floor.
The memory feels alien to him, as though it belongs to someone else, yet when he recalls it he is
flooded with an intense, burning rage that leaves him shaking.

In the years since the divorce, Kaelani has built a careful life around avoiding anything that might
remind him of her. He works from home as an editor, which allows him to control his environment, but
even that has become difficult. When a client sends back a manuscript with corrections, the
criticism lands like a physical blow. His heart pounds, his jaw tightens, and he feels himself
becoming an object again—something to be used and discarded. In those moments, the memory of that
night rushes back unbidden, the snapshot images playing in his mind like a slideshow he cannot stop.
He has come to believe that the abuse happened because of the sort of person he is: weak, deserving
of punishment, fundamentally flawed. This belief colors every memory he has of the marriage, making
the past feel both vivid and unreal.

To cope, Kaelani has developed a set of strategies that have slowly shrunk his world. When a wave of
anger or shame rises, he immediately distracts himself—turning on the television, picking up a
crossword puzzle, scrolling through news articles he does not really read. He avoids any strong
emotion, because he has learned that feeling too much leads to that rage, and the rage leads to the
memory, and the memory confirms everything he fears about himself. He will not watch movies with
conflict, will not answer the phone if he is not sure who is calling, will not visit the grocery
store during busy hours. He has stopped seeing old friends, because they might ask about his life,
and he would have to feel something. This avoidance has made the memory more intrusive, not less;
the more he pushes it away, the more it surfaces at unexpected moments, and the more he feels like a
stranger to himself.

The cycle has tightened over the years. The less he allows himself to feel, the more convinced he
becomes that he will never feel normal emotions again. The more he avoids reminders of his ex-wife,
the more sensitive he becomes to any perceived criticism or correction, which then triggers the
memory again. He is certain that if he let himself feel the rage fully, he might do something
terrible, so he stays in a state of constant vigilance, monitoring his own emotional state for any
sign of danger. His work has suffered; he has lost several contracts because he could not tolerate
the feedback process. His daughter has noticed that he no longer laughs, no longer argues, no longer
seems present in his own life.

What finally brought Kaelani to therapy was the moment he realized he had not left his apartment in
three weeks. His daughter brought him groceries, and when she asked why he looked so tired, he could
not find the words to explain. He is afraid that talking about the memory will make it worse, that
he will be swallowed by the rage and never find his way back. But he is also afraid of staying where
he is—a man who has become a ghost in his own story, waiting for a past that will not let him go.
