Mara is a 48-year-old rabbi who has led a small, close-knit congregation in a midwestern city for
nearly a decade. Her trauma occurred eighteen months ago, during a community gathering at the
synagogue’s social hall. A deranged individual, shouting anti-Semitic slurs, detonated a crude pipe
bomb in the kitchen adjacent to where Mara was standing. She was not physically injured, but the
blast threw her against a wall, and for several seconds the world was nothing but pressure, ringing,
and the acrid smell of burning wiring. In the chaos that followed, she helped direct people out of
the building, but she has since become consumed by the belief that she did not do enough. She
replays the moments after the explosion, certain that she could have reached the kitchen sooner,
that she might have somehow prevented the attack entirely. “I will never forgive myself for not
doing more to stop what happened,” she has said, her voice flat with a guilt that feels immovable.
This sense of personal failure has crystallized into a broader conviction that nothing good can
happen to her anymore; she feels she is living in a world that has already taken everything of
value.

The guilt and the belief that she must be constantly on guard have driven Mara into a pattern of
withdrawal that has reshaped her entire life. She has stopped attending any social events outside of
her mandatory duties, and she has begun refusing the invitations of friends and congregants who want
to talk about the bombing. When someone brings it up, even with kindness, she feels a spike of
resentment and fear—a sense that the memory is being forced upon her. She has also withdrawn from
her husband physically; she cannot tolerate the vulnerability of sex, which now feels like an
exposure she cannot risk. These strategies have deepened her conviction that she is broken and
isolated, reinforcing the guilt and the belief that she must remain hypervigilant because no one
else understands the danger.

Mara’s daily life is governed by an unrelenting sense of threat that has no off-switch. She scans
her environment constantly, her eyes flicking to doorways, to the faces of strangers, to any bag
left unattended. Even when her husband or her closest colleagues tell her she is safe, she finds it
impossible to believe them—their words feel hollow, disconnected from the reality she carries
inside. This threat is especially acute at night; the darkness feels like a cover for the next
attack, and she finds herself lying awake, listening. The scanning and the nighttime vigilance in
turn feed her negative appraisals: the more she watches for danger, the more convinced she becomes
that danger is inevitable and that she must be especially careful, because you never know what can
happen next. This loop tightens further when she encounters her triggers. An unexpected bang—a car
backfiring, a door slamming—sends her heart racing and her mind straight back to the kitchen of her
synagogue. She has also become acutely sensitive to the feeling of being watched, which she
experiences in crowds or even when she is alone in her study, and she cannot watch or read any news
related to violence or mass casualty events, because it feels like the story is always her own.

Her occupation as a rabbi, once a source of meaning, has become a landscape of constant exposure.
She cannot lead a service without scanning the faces in the pews for anyone who seems out of place.
She has begun asking her husband to drive her to the synagogue for evening meetings, and she avoids
walking through the parking lot alone. The checking of her surroundings has become so automatic that
she cannot stop it, and each scan confirms her belief that the world is unsafe. This has made her
distant during counseling sessions with congregants, and she has noticed that her colleagues have
stopped asking her to collaborate on community events. She has become isolated within the very
community she once led.

What finally brought Mara to seek help was a small, ordinary moment. Her husband, trying to comfort
her, placed a hand on her shoulder as she was washing dishes. The touch was gentle, but she flinched
so violently that she dropped a glass. She saw his face fall, and she realized that she had become a
stranger to him. She is afraid that she will never feel safe again, and she is terrified that the
guilt she carries will never lift. But she is also, beneath that, hoping to find a way back to the
life she had, to the person she used to be.
