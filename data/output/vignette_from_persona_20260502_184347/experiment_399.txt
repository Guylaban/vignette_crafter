Tien was thirty-five when he finally walked into a therapist’s office, though he had been living
inside the aftermath of a single afternoon for nearly three years. The event had happened in a
windowless room in a provincial city he had been visiting for work. He had been an accountant then
too, sent to audit a regional branch, and a local official had taken exception to the discrepancies
he found. What followed was not a beating in any simple sense—it was methodical, humiliating, and it
lasted for hours. Tien had been stripped, bound to a chair, and subjected to electric shocks to his
genitals, his fingertips, the soft tissue behind his ears. His captor had asked questions, but Tien
had the sense that the answers did not matter; the point was the demonstration of total control. He
had soiled himself. He had begged. He had dissociated so completely that he watched the ceiling
tiles as though from a great height. When it ended, he was released without explanation, and he flew
home the next day.

In the months that followed, the memory of that room did not behave like a normal memory. It arrived
in fragments, but not in sequence—he could not say whether the first shock came before or after the
man had laughed, and the jumble of images played over and over like a film stuck on a loop. What
troubled Tien most was that the memory felt alien, as though it belonged to someone else. He would
think, *That did not happen to me*, even as his hands began to shake. This sense of unreality made
it harder to push the images away, because they did not feel like his own past, and so he could not
claim them or contain them.

Tien had always been a cautious man, but after the event, his caution curdled into something else.
He became convinced that harm could come from anyone, at any time, without warning. A stranger
asking for directions on the street would send his heart racing; he would hear the official’s voice
in the stranger’s tone. When a colleague mentioned a future project—a conference, a promotion, a
retirement plan—Tien felt a cold dread, because talking about the future meant assuming safety, and
safety was an illusion he could no longer afford. He had come to believe that he had permanently
changed for the worse, that the man in the chair was the real Tien and the competent accountant was
the impostor. And he was haunted by the thought that somebody else—anybody else—would have stopped
it, would have said the right thing, would not have frozen and wet himself and begged. That
appraisal, that he was weaker than others, made the memory return more vividly: he would see the
ceiling tiles again, hear his own voice pleading, and the loop would tighten.

The causal weight of his triggers on his appraisals was heavy in his daily life. Every interaction
with a stranger, every invitation to plan ahead, renewed his conviction that the world was dangerous
and he was inadequate to meet it. And because he believed he was permanently damaged and uniquely
weak, the memories came more frequently—each flashback confirming what he already knew about
himself, which in turn made the next trigger more potent. By the time he sought help, Tien had
stopped accepting new clients, had stopped attending team meetings that required small talk, and had
told his ex-wife that he could not discuss their daughter’s college plans because the future was not
something he could think about. She had called it emotional neglect; he had called it survival.

What finally brought Tien to the clinic was not insight but exhaustion. He had been avoiding
strangers for so long that his professional isolation had become untenable. His supervisor had
noticed. Tien sat in the intake chair with his hands pressed between his knees, and he said, very
quietly, that he was afraid of what would happen if he kept going this way—not that he would hurt
himself, but that he would disappear entirely, that the man in the chair would be all that was left.
