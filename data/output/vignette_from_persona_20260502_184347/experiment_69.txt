Kwame was fifty-seven when the accident happened, a bomb disposal technician with two decades of
service who had learned to read pressure and silence the way others read a clock. The call was
routine — a suspicious package in a warehouse on the industrial edge of town — but the package
wasn’t a bomb. It was a corroded industrial cylinder, and when Kwame’s cutter breached the casing, a
fine aerosol of solvents and organophosphates billowed into his face before his respirator seals
could catch up. He spent the next six hours in the ER, stripped down, scrubbed, and monitored for
seizures, his lungs burning and his vision doubling in waves. The smell of antiseptic followed him
home, clinging to his clothes, his hair, the back of his throat.

In the weeks that followed, Kwame found he could not walk into his own garage without his chest
tightening. The warehouse had smelled like metal and old oil, but the hospital smell — that sharp,
chemical cleanliness — was the one that hooked into his gut. He started avoiding medical settings
entirely. When his wife suggested a routine physical, he told her he felt fine. When a colleague
mentioned an ambulance siren on the news, Kwame left the room. The loud noises were worse than the
smells, though. A truck backfiring on the street would send him into a cold sweat, his hands flying
to his belt where his cutters used to hang. He came to believe that any sudden, sharp sound could be
the beginning of another exposure — that the air itself might turn toxic again without warning.

His solution was to stay busy. Kwame began working double shifts, then triple shifts. He volunteered
for every field assignment, every late-night disposal, because the moment he stopped moving, the
thoughts caught up with him. Being alone in a room — even his own living room — felt like waiting
for something to go wrong. So he didn’t stop. He worked until his hands shook and his wife stopped
asking him to come home for dinner. Overwork became his anchor: as long as he was in motion, he was
safe. But the cost was that he never slept, never rested, and never let his guard down. His body
stayed locked in a low-grade alarm state, and his scanning became automatic. Every room he entered,
he mapped the exits. Every unexpected touch on the shoulder made him spin around, fists half-
clenched. He had come to believe that danger was everywhere, and that only constant vigilance kept
it at bay.

The cycle tightened over months. The more he scanned for threats, the more he noticed triggers — a
whiff of bleach from a janitor’s cart, a distant bang from a construction site — and each trigger
sent him deeper into avoidance. He stopped going to the grocery store. He stopped answering the
door. His wife began to sleep in the guest room because he would flinch and gasp when she rolled
over in bed. Kwame knew his reactions were extreme, but he was certain that if he stopped preparing,
if he stopped scanning, the next exposure would be the one that killed him. The only thing that felt
safe was the work itself, and even that was starting to feel like a trap.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at his desk. A junior technician dropped a metal
clipboard. The sound was sharp, sudden, and Kwame dove under his desk, heart hammering, convinced he
had heard a detonator. He stayed there for fifteen minutes, unable to move, while his colleagues
stared. That evening, his wife told him she couldn’t watch him disappear anymore. Kwame is afraid of
what he will find if he stops running, but he is more afraid of losing the life he has already
stopped living.
