The assault happened three years ago, in the parking garage beneath the county office building where
Marcus worked as a social worker. A man had followed him from the stairwell, pressed a knife to his
ribs, and forced him into the maintenance alcove. Marcus remembered the concrete dust on the floor,
the hum of the ventilation fan, the way his own voice had sounded thin and far away when he tried to
speak. He had not screamed. He had not fought. He had done exactly what the man said, and afterward,
when he drove home on the expressway, his hands had not stopped shaking for two hours.

What stayed with him was not the physical memory alone—though the terror returned whole and
undiminished whenever he thought of it, a wave of cold panic that left him breathless—but the shame.
He could not find words for what had happened, even now. In sessions with his own therapist, he
would start a sentence and stop, the shape of the event dissolving before he could hold it. He had
come to believe that the fact he could not describe it meant something was wrong with him, that a
real man would have fought back or at least been able to talk about it afterward.

In the months that followed, Marcus began to notice that certain spaces felt wrong. Enclosed
areas—the supply closet at work, the elevator in his apartment building, the narrow hallway outside
his office—made his chest tighten. He took the stairs instead, even when his knees ached. But the
stairs themselves became difficult: he found himself looking behind him constantly, checking the
landings, listening for footsteps. What frightened him most was the possibility that someone might
be following him and he would not know until it was too late. He started arriving at work an hour
early so he could park in the open lot three blocks away, even in winter rain.

The shame made the vigilance worse. When he caught himself scanning the parking lot during his lunch
break, he would think: *This is what weak people do.* And then the memory would surface again—the
concrete dust, the knife, his own silence—and the terror would spike, and he would need to check
again, to be sure. He ruminated for hours about what he would do if he ever saw the man again, how
he would hurt him, how he would make him pay. These fantasies gave him a brief sense of control, but
they also kept the event vivid, and afterward he would try to push the whole thing out of his mind,
telling himself to stop being ridiculous.

The checking and avoiding became a structure around his life. He stopped going to unfamiliar
neighborhoods entirely—he could not assess the danger, could not know where the exits were, could
not trust that he would be safe. When a meeting ran late or a client kept him waiting, he assumed
something bad was happening: a breakdown in the system, a threat he had missed, a repeat of that
moment when his life had stopped being his own. His wife noticed that he had become short-tempered,
that he no longer wanted to go to the grocery store with her, that he spent his evenings staring at
the television without watching it.

What finally brought him to seek help was a Tuesday morning in the office supply closet. He had
needed a new notepad, and he had stood at the door for five minutes, unable to open it, his heart
hammering, his mind filling with the image of the alcove. He had walked away and told his supervisor
he was feeling unwell. That night, sitting in his car in the driveway, he realized he could not keep
living this way—afraid of a closet, afraid of a hallway, afraid of his own thoughts. He wanted to
stop checking. He wanted to stop planning revenge. He wanted to be able to remember what happened
without falling apart. But he was not sure he knew how.
