The night the flood came, Elena had been awake, listening to the rain hammer against her apartment
windows. She was a physical therapist then, as she is now, living in a ground-floor unit in a
Houston suburb. The water did not rise slowly. It surged through the walls, through the door seams,
carrying mud and debris. She had to climb onto her kitchen counter, then onto the refrigerator, her
phone already dead, the current black and oily below her. She remembers the smell—wet earth,
gasoline, rot—and the sound of things knocking against the walls in the dark. She was rescued by a
neighbor in a boat just before dawn, but something in her had already been washed away.

In the weeks that followed, Elena found she could not look at a glass of water without her stomach
turning. The nausea would rise first—a sudden, slick queasiness that had no clear cause—and then the
memory would arrive, not as a full picture but as a wave of dread and cold, the feeling of the water
rising against her ribs. She had come to believe that the memory did not belong to her. It felt like
a film she had once watched, a story about someone else, yet the grief it carried was hers alone,
heavy and inexplicable. Sometimes she would be standing in the grocery aisle and the grief would hit
her without any image at all, just a crushing weight that made her want to sit down on the floor.
She began to dwell on who she used to be—the woman who could sleep through a thunderstorm, who did
not flinch at a sudden noise, who felt safe in her own home.

Elena now lives in a third-floor apartment in a different part of the city, but she has not truly
settled. She only feels safe in a very limited number of places: her bedroom, the small coffee shop
two blocks away where the barista knows her name, and the quiet corner of the clinic where she
works. Anywhere else, her body stays on high alert. A car backfiring, a door slamming, the clatter
of a dropped tray in the clinic cafeteria—each sound makes her jump, her heart hammering, her
attention narrowing to a single point of threat. This constant vigilance has worn her down. She has
trouble falling asleep, and when she does, she wakes at the slightest creak of the building, certain
the water is coming back.

The safest place is her bedroom, and she has learned to stay there. She avoids going out unless she
has to. She tells herself it is practical—there is nothing she needs—but the truth is that every
outing carries risk. A patient in the clinic mentioning a vacation to the lake, the name "Houston"
spoken on the television in the waiting room, the sound of a toilet flushing in the next stall—any
of these can trigger the nausea, and the nausea brings the memory, and the memory brings the grief,
and then she is gone. She has learned to leave her body deliberately, to blank out when the feeling
becomes too much. She will stare at a spot on the wall until her mind goes white, and when she comes
back, she is disoriented, sometimes tearful, and the memory feels even more alien than before. This
dissociation, in turn, makes the triggers harder to resist. The next time she hears a name that
reminds her of the flood, the nausea comes faster, and the cycle tightens.

At work, Elena is professional and competent. She guides her patients through their exercises with
patience, her hands steady. But she has stopped taking lunch breaks in the staff lounge because
someone left a news article about hurricane season on the table. She has stopped going to the
clinic’s monthly team meetings because they are held in a basement room. Her supervisor has noticed
her absences and has asked if everything is all right. Elena said yes, but she knows she is not all
right. What finally brought her to seek help was a Tuesday morning in July, when a summer
thunderstorm rolled in and the sky turned green. She felt the nausea, then the alien memory, then
the grief, and she realized she had been standing in her patient’s room, frozen, a towel in her
hand, unable to move or speak for nearly a minute. She is afraid of losing her job. She is afraid of
never feeling like herself again. But what frightens her most is the thought that the water is still
rising, and that no amount of staying home will keep it out.
