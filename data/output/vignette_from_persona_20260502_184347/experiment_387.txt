him. A park ranger for seven years, she had spent that morning teaching a group of schoolchildren

the hours afterward but isolated, photographic details: the way his water bottle lay on its side in
the dust, the specific angle of his boot, the weight of his hand when she held it in the morgue.

she feels a grief so overwhelming it presses the air from her lungs—a raw, childlike sorrow that
makes no distinction between then and now.

In the months since, Malika has come to believe she is no longer capable of doing the things that
defined her. She used to be the person who could read a landscape, who could stay calm when a hiker
sprained an ankle miles from the trailhead. Now she feels dead inside, hollowed out, and she is
certain she has permanently changed for the worse. These appraisals drive her behavior in ways she
does not fully recognize. Because she believes she is no longer capable, she has quietly dropped
important responsibilities: she stopped volunteering for the backcountry patrol, she let her first-

counts. She has come to believe that being still with her own mind is dangerous, so she keeps it
occupied all the time—podcasts during her shifts, audiobooks while she drives, a crossword puzzle at
the dinner table. She avoids relaxing because relaxation feels like an invitation for the memories
to surface. When her husband suggests they sit on the porch and watch the sunset, she finds a reason
to go inside.
