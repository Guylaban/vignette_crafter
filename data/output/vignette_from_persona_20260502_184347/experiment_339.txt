For 55-year-old David, a counselor in a community mental health clinic, the event that reshaped his
life occurred during a group therapy session two years ago. A client, mid-rage, hurled a metal chair
across the room. It struck a wall heater, which sparked and ignited a pile of papers. Smoke filled
the room—thick, acrid, chemical—before David could wrestle the client to the ground and evacuate
everyone. He remembers the sound of the chair, the heat, and most of all the smell: burnt plastic
and insulation, a smell that still invades his present without warning.

In the weeks that followed, David struggled to make sense of his own reactions. He had always prided
himself on being calm in a crisis, but now his body refused to cooperate. His heart would race at
the whiff of a gas stove, or when a colleague tossed a jacket onto a chair. He came to believe that
his body was betraying him—that it kept reacting as if he were still in that room, even when he was
perfectly safe. This belief made him increasingly vigilant. He had to be especially careful, he told
himself, because you never knew what could happen next. At work, he started scanning rooms for
projectiles and exit routes before every session.

David’s memory of the event became strange to him. Sometimes, when he least expected it—while
driving, or lying in bed—he would feel the full terror of that moment, the adrenaline spike, the
helplessness, but without any clear image or memory attached. It was just the feeling, raw and
disembodied. Other times, the memory would surface with a sensory clarity that felt unreal: he could
smell the smoke and burnt plastic, and yet the entire scene felt as though it belonged to someone
else, as if he were watching a movie about a stranger. He was certain that if he thought about the
event deliberately, he would not be able to handle it, so he began monitoring his own thoughts
constantly, searching for any sign that he was losing control. He avoided thinking about the therapy
room entirely.

These strategies only deepened the memory’s alienation. The more he pushed the event away, the more
it came back as a fragmented, intrusive sensation—the smell, the fear, the feeling that it wasn’t
really his story. And each time that smell returned, it yanked him back into the past, reinforcing
his belief that danger was always near. He started canceling plans with friends, telling himself he
needed to stay home where it was safe. At the clinic, he asked to be reassigned to administrative
duties, avoiding group rooms and the smell of burnt coffee from the break-room microwave.

The cycle tightened. A whiff of smoke from a neighbor’s barbecue would trigger a rush of fear, and
David would spend the rest of the evening checking his own thoughts for signs of panic. The more he
monitored, the more the alien, emotion-laden memory intruded. He had come to believe that his mind
was untrustworthy, that thinking about the event would unravel him completely. His avoidance of
friends left him isolated; his avoidance of thinking left him exhausted.

What finally brought David to seek help was a moment in the grocery store, when a customer dropped a
glass jar near the deli counter. The sound—sharp, unexpected—sent him into a full panic attack in
the cereal aisle. He stood there, shaking, smelling phantom smoke, feeling the terror of that room.
He realized he could no longer manage this alone. What frightens him most now is the thought that
his own mind is permanently broken, that he will never feel safe again. But beneath that, what he
hopes for is simply to stop being a stranger to his own memory.
