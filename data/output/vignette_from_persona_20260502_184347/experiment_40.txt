Priya was sixty-six years old, a retired midwife who had spent forty years in a busy city hospital,
and she still could not sleep through the night without waking to the sound of boots on gravel. The
sound came from a single night in 1983, when she was a young nurse deployed with a field hospital
unit near a contested border. She had been walking back from the latrine in the dark, carrying a
small torch, when a military police patrol stopped her. They were not hostile at first—just three
men in uniform, their faces half-shadowed under berets, asking for her identification. She
remembered the way the senior officer’s eyes moved slowly down her body, and how he smiled when she
fumbled for her papers. He told her to open her jacket. She stood there, alone, in the cold, while
he searched her with his hands, the other two watching. She did not scream. She did not tell anyone
afterward. She told herself it was nothing, that he was just doing his job, and she buried it so
deep that for thirty years she almost believed it.

But after her retirement, when the quiet hours stretched too long, the memory began surfacing in
fragments. The trigger could be something as ordinary as a man in a postal uniform walking up her
driveway, and suddenly her chest would lock and her vision would narrow. She would feel watched,
observed, as though eyes were on her from every window. She could not be alone with any man she did
not know—not the plumber, not the neighbor’s son who came to collect donations, not even the elderly
cashier at the grocery store who always smiled too long. Her husband, Raj, was the only male
presence she could tolerate, and even then she sometimes flinched when he came up behind her.
Authority figures in uniform—police officers, security guards, even the crossing guard near the
park—made her cross the street, her breath shallow, her pulse hammering. These triggers led directly
to a pervasive sense of threat. She was hypervigilant in public, scanning every face, every pair of
hands, every sudden movement. A car door slamming too loudly, a man jogging past her on the
sidewalk—these put her immediately on guard. She could not relax even at home; she sat with her back
to the wall, facing the door, unable to let her attention drift.

To manage this constant sense of danger, Priya developed strategies that took over her life. She
threw herself into work—even retired, she took on volunteer shifts at a community clinic, staying
late, taking extra patients, keeping her mind occupied so she would not have to sit with the fear.
She made sure never to be alone with another person, always arranging meetings in open spaces or
with Raj beside her. She was vigilant for dangerous people, scanning crowds for anyone who moved too
quickly or looked at her too long. But these strategies, meant to protect her, only made the
triggers more frequent. The more she avoided being alone, the more she noticed how many situations
required being alone with someone. The more she worked, the more exhausted she became, and
exhaustion lowered her threshold for fear. The more she watched for danger, the more she saw it. Her
hypervigilance became a self-sustaining loop: a trigger would spike her threat level, she would
deploy a strategy to feel safe, and the strategy would expose her to more triggers. She stopped
going to the grocery store. She stopped answering the phone. Raj did most of the errands now, and
she could see the worry in his face, but she could not explain why she was afraid—not fully, not
without saying out loud what had happened in that field.

What finally brought her to seek help was an incident at the clinic. A young man in military
fatigues—a reservist, just visiting a patient—walked past her in the hallway, and she dropped a tray
of instruments, her hands shaking so badly she could not pick them up. The clinic director suggested
she take some time off. Priya sat in the parking lot for an hour before she could drive home, and
that night she told Raj she needed to talk to someone. She was most afraid of being seen as weak, of
having her long career reduced to this one moment. But she was also afraid of staying the same—of
spending the rest of her life crossing the street, of never again feeling safe in her own skin.
