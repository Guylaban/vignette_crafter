Rajesh is a 64-year-old South Asian man who has worked the night shift in a warehouse for nearly two
decades. He is married, with adult children who live in another state, and he has always prided
himself on his reliability and physical stamina. That changed two years ago, when he was held
captive for twelve hours in a shipping container by a group of men who had broken into the
warehouse. They bound his hands and feet, gagged him, and left him in the dark. For hours, he lay on
the cold metal floor, unable to move or call for help, listening to the sounds of rats scurrying
nearby. He remembers the way the darkness felt absolute, and how his mind kept returning to a single
thought: that no one would find him until it was too late. He was certain he could hear a snake
slithering somewhere in the shadows, though he never saw one. When a security guard finally opened
the container at dawn, Rajesh was trembling and covered in sweat. He told himself he was fine, that
he had survived, and that he could put it behind him.

But he could not. Over the following weeks, Rajesh found that the time of day had become a trigger
he could not escape. Every evening, as the clock approached the hour when he had been taken, his
body would tense and his heart would pound. The onset of darkness, especially waking up in the
middle of the night, brought the same visceral dread. He began to avoid going to sleep, lying in bed
with the lights on, his eyes fixed on the ceiling, until exhaustion finally pulled him under. When
his wife asked why he was so restless, he would say he was just having trouble sleeping, but
privately he had started asking her, again and again, whether he was safe, whether the doors were
locked, whether she thought he was okay. She would reassure him, but the relief never lasted. He
also found himself running his hands over his arms and legs, checking for cuts or bruises, as though
his body might be hiding an injury he had not noticed. These maladaptive strategies—the avoidance of
sleep, the constant reassurance-seeking, the body monitoring—became his nightly routine, and they
only deepened his sense of helplessness. Lying awake, he would tell himself that he could not stop
bad things from happening to him, that his life was not worth living anymore, and that if he allowed
himself to think about the event, he would not be able to handle it.

The triggers also kept his threat system on high alert. At night, Rajesh found it harder to feel
safe than during the day; he was superalert, watchful, and on guard. He frequently looked behind him
to check if someone was following him, even in his own home. When he heard a creak in the hallway,
his mind would leap to the image of the container door sliding shut. The hypervigilance was
exhausting, but he could not turn it off. His negative appraisals—the belief that he was helpless,
that his life had lost its value, that his own thoughts were dangerous—fed directly into this state
of threat. He was certain that he was not safe, that he could not protect himself, and that the
world was full of hidden dangers he would not see coming. This conviction made his nights
unbearable.

Over the months, the cycle tightened. His maladaptive strategies—the sleepless nights, the endless
checking, the pleas for reassurance—left him depleted and ashamed. He began to believe that he was
weak, that he had failed to handle the event, and that he would never recover. These negative
appraisals, in turn, made the triggers more potent. The same time of day now carried not just fear
but a crushing sense of hopelessness. The dark no longer just frightened him; it confirmed that his
life was not worth living. And because he avoided sleep, he was awake to experience the triggers,
night after night, in a relentless loop.

Rajesh finally sought help after his wife found him in the living room at three in the morning,
sitting in the dark with his eyes wide open, his hands trembling. He told her he was checking his
body for a wound he was sure was there. What frightened him most was not the memory of the
container, but the thought that he would never feel safe again, and that the darkness would always
be waiting.
