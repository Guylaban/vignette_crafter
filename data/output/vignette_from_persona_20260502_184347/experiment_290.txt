Malia was forty-six when her husband, Kevin, a firefighter, was killed in a structure collapse. The
official report called it a catastrophic accident, but what Malia carried was not a story—it was a
sequence of frozen images. She remembered the phone call not as a conversation but as the sound of
her own voice saying “no” over and over, and then the silence of the kitchen, and then the picture
of his boots by the door. Those moments had become snapshots in her mind, each one a still frame she
could turn over and examine, and when they returned, she felt so overwhelmed by emotion that she had
to stop whatever she was doing—once, mid-transit, she had to pull her car to the shoulder and sit
with her hands gripping the wheel until the crying passed.

In the months that followed, Malia, a forensic analyst, found herself arriving at the lab later and
later. She had always taken pride in her precision, but now the thought of sitting at her
workstation, running data that required sustained attention, felt impossible. She had come to
believe that her life had been destroyed by the trauma—not just changed, but hollowed out. Nothing
felt meaningful anymore. She had lost her faith in people, in life, in the idea that effort
mattered. What was the point of analyzing evidence when the worst thing could happen without
warning? This belief drove her avoidance: she began staying up until very late, delaying sleep
because the moment she closed her eyes, the images came. She also stopped eating properly,
restricting food as a way of coping, telling herself she didn’t deserve comfort. The less she ate,
the more exhausted she became, and the more exhausted she became, the harder it was to push the
memories away. The memories, in turn, reinforced her conviction that her life was over, creating a
loop she could not name.

She had also started to hear things in her head—the crackling of the dispatch radio, the tone of the
chief’s voice when he said Kevin’s name. These auditory fragments would surface during the day,
triggered by something as ordinary as a siren outside her office window. Her job demanded she work
with case files that sometimes involved first responders, and she had begun avoiding those
assignments, transferring them to colleagues without explanation. Her supervisor noticed but said
nothing. At home, Kevin’s family stopped calling as often; Malia had stopped answering, and they
interpreted her silence as coldness. She knew this, but she could not bring herself to explain. She
was certain that no one could understand, and that certainty kept her isolated.

The isolation deepened the meaninglessness. By the time she sought help, she was sleeping three
hours a night, eating one small meal a day, and struggling to complete even the simplest reports.
What finally brought her to a therapist’s office was a moment at her desk: she had been staring at a
photograph from a crime scene—a kitchen, a pair of boots—and she had been unable to move for forty
minutes. She was afraid that if she did not find a way out, she would lose her job, and then she
would have nothing left. But what frightened her most was the thought that even if she tried,
nothing would ever feel real or worthwhile again.
