against the wall beside his head, while another screamed in his face, his breath thick with alcohol.
Kofi froze, unable to move or speak, certain he was going to be beaten or killed. A security guard
eventually intervened, but by then the damage was done.

In the weeks that followed, Kofi found he could not shake the memory. It came to him not as a story
he could tell, but as a series of vivid, frozen snapshots: the bottle arcing through the headlights,
the man’s contorted face, the sour smell of beer. When these images surfaced, he became so
overwhelmed by a wave of pure terror that he would have to stop whatever he was doing, his mind
going blank, unable to find any words for what he had experienced. He began to avoid any situation
that might involve disagreement. At work, he stopped voicing opinions in meetings, even when his
input was critical to a project. He had come to believe that the institutions that should have
protected him—his own city’s police, the security company at the site—had let him down, and that
this proved he could not rely on anyone. This conviction deepened his sense of helplessness; he was
certain that he could not stop bad things from happening to him, and that even the slightest upset
would now be more than he could handle.

thought he might pass out. The sound of the bottle clinking against the table was enough to send the
snapshot memory flooding back. He fled to the bathroom, trembling, and spent the rest of the night
convinced he was not safe. Even when his friends told him the bar was fine, he found it impossible
to believe them. He started jumping at ordinary sounds—a door slamming, a car backfiring—and any
