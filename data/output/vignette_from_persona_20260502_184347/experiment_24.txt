Amina had spent fifty-two years as a midwife, guiding mothers through the most vulnerable moments of
life, her hands steady, her voice calm. The accident happened on a rain-slicked highway outside of
Chicago, on her way home from a delivery. A driver ran a red light and T-boned her sedan at full
speed. She remembers the sound—metal screaming, glass shattering—and then the silence of being
pinned, the car folded around her like a fist. For nearly an hour, she was trapped in the wreckage,
the crushed door pressed against her ribs, the ceiling lowered to inches above her face. The sirens
came, then the jaws of life, then the hospital. Her body healed, mostly. Her right shoulder never
fully recovered, and she walks with a slight limp now. But it was the aftermath she had not
anticipated.

In the months that followed, Amina found herself unable to sit in a room with the door closed. She
would stand and open it, even in winter, even in her own home. Her husband, Kwame, grew
frustrated—he did not understand why she could not just relax. What he did not see was the way her
chest would tighten, how her breath would go shallow, how she would suddenly feel the weight of the
car pressing in again. A locked bathroom door at a friend’s house sent her into a cold sweat. She
had to leave the dinner party without explanation. At work, she began to avoid the supply closet, a
small room with a heavy door that clicked shut. She started delegating that task to younger
colleagues, telling herself she was too busy. But the real reason was that she could not bear the
feeling of enclosure, the way it triggered the memory of being trapped, and with that memory came a
cascade of thoughts she could not control. *Somebody else would not have gotten into this
situation,* she would think. *My body has been permanently damaged. My life has been destroyed.*

The sirens were worse. Every time an ambulance passed the clinic, Amina’s hands would tremble. She
would freeze mid-step, her heart hammering, waiting for something terrible to finish happening. The
sound pulled her back to that roadside, to the helplessness of lying in twisted metal while
strangers shouted. She began to dread her commute, flinching at every intersection where a car might
run a light. She started taking back roads, adding twenty minutes to her drive, but even then she
could not escape the sight of a dented fender or a scraped bumper. Each image of a car hit by
accident reignited the thought that her body had been permanently damaged, that her life had been
destroyed. And the more she believed that, the more vigilant she became. She scanned every parking
lot for danger. She startled at the pop of a car door closing in the street. She lay awake at night,
replaying the crash, listening for sirens that were not there.

The cycle tightened. The more hypervigilant she became, the more triggers she noticed. A locked
door, a siren, a crumpled car—each one confirmed her belief that she was fragile, that her body was
ruined, that someone else would have handled the accident better. And each confirmation made her
more watchful, more jumpy, more exhausted. Kwame began to sleep in the guest room because she woke
him with her tossing and her sudden gasps. At work, she found herself irritable with patients,
short-tempered with a mother who asked too many questions. She had always been patient; now she felt
brittle, as if the smallest pressure might shatter her.

What finally brought Amina to seek help was a moment in the delivery room. A patient had a difficult
labor, and for a few seconds the room felt close, the door was shut, and a monitor began to beep—not
a siren, but close enough. Amina felt her vision narrow, her hands go numb. She excused herself and
stood in the hallway, shaking. She realized she could no longer trust herself to do the work she
loved. She was terrified that she would freeze during an emergency, that she would fail a mother
because of what had happened to her. She came to the clinic hoping to find a way back to herself,
but privately she feared that the damage was permanent, that the woman who had delivered hundreds of
babies was gone, and that nothing could bring her back.
