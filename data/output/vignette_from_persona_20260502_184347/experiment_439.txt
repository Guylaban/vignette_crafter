Leilani is a 65-year-old cybersecurity analyst who was widowed five years ago. The traumatic event
that unraveled her sense of safety happened two years ago, during a routine drive home from a late
shift. A driver ran a red light and T-boned her sedan at an intersection. The impact crumpled the
driver’s side door, and for several minutes she was trapped, the smell of gasoline and hot metal
filling the car. She felt a searing pain in her left ribs and hip, and as she waited for rescue, she
became convinced her body was failing her—that she could not trust it to keep her alive. That
conviction has not left her.

In the months that followed, Leilani found herself going over and over why the event happened to
her, circling the question without ever arriving at an answer. She came to believe that what
happened was deeply unfair and that nothing had been done about it—the other driver had been
uninsured and the legal process had gone nowhere. This sense of injustice fed her rumination, and
she began to avoid anything that could stir up feelings, whether negative or positive, because both
felt equally dangerous. She tried to erase the memory of the crash entirely, pushing it away
whenever it surfaced, but the memories were stubbornly sensory. When she thought about the event,
she could smell the gasoline again, and she would feel a phantom ache in her ribs and hip, as though
her body were still pinned in the car. The memories were mostly pictures and images—the headlights
bearing down, the dashboard crumpling—and they returned without warning, especially at night.

Leilani had always been a light sleeper, but after the accident she found it difficult to fall
asleep because she felt she needed to stay alert. She woke during the night to check whether she was
safe, and she found it harder to feel safe at night than during the day. The sensory memories,
particularly the smell and the pain, would trigger a surge of vigilance, and she would lie in bed
scanning the dark for threats. She had also come to feel that others could see what happened to her
just by looking at her, which made her avoid eye contact with colleagues and neighbors. This sense
of exposure deepened her belief that her body was unreliable, and she began to monitor herself
constantly for signs of danger—her heartbeat, a twinge in her ribs—which only made the memories more
vivid.

Her job as a cybersecurity analyst required her to sit in a cubicle under fluorescent lights, and
she found herself scanning the office for exits, certain that a crash or an attack could happen at
any moment. The threat she felt was constant, and it drove her to more rumination: she would replay
the accident in her mind, trying to find a way she could have prevented it, which only made the
memories more entrenched. She avoided leaving her house after dark, and she stopped attending the
weekly dinners with her late husband’s family, telling herself she was too tired. The avoidance cost
her those relationships, but she was certain it was necessary.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was a night when she woke to the smell of gasoline, her
heart pounding, and found herself on the floor beside her bed, her body braced as if for impact. She
had tried to erase the memory for two years, and it had only grown stronger. She is afraid that she
will never feel safe again, but she is also exhausted by the effort of staying vigilant. She hopes
that someone can help her find a way to stop the cycle, though she is not sure she believes it is
possible.
