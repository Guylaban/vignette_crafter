Mazen was seventy-eight years old and had spent fifty years on rooftops across the greater Detroit
area, a wiry man with a quiet, unshakeable competence that had earned the trust of every contractor
he worked for. That competence had also nearly killed him. On a humid afternoon in late August of
2019, he was patching a chemical plant’s roof when a corroded vent pipe burst beneath him, releasing
a plume of toxic methylene chloride directly into his face. He remembered the sweet, chloroform-like
smell, the instant burn in his throat, the way his chest seized and his vision swam before he lost
consciousness. What he could not deliberately recall, now, were the minutes that followed — the
sound of his own fall, the face of the foreman who dragged him out, the paramedics working over him.
He had tried, in the first weeks after, to reconstruct those minutes, but the memory was a locked
room whose contents he could sense but not see. What came instead, without warning, was the full
emotional impact of the event — a sudden, suffocating wave of terror and helplessness that would
crash over him while he sat in his living room or stood at the kitchen counter, with no image
attached, no trigger he could name.

Mazen’s wife, Leila, noticed the changes first. He could no longer sit through the evening news; the
briefest mention of industrial accidents or chemical spills sent a hot spike of dread through his
chest, and within seconds the locked-room memory would force its way into his mind — not the missing
minutes, but the moment just before, the sweet smell and the burn and the certainty that he was
about to die. He found himself avoiding the television altogether, and then avoiding the grocery
store when he knew the news would be playing on the overhead screens. He stopped visiting his
daughter’s house, where his three grandchildren lived. It was not that the children reminded him of
the accident directly; it was that their presence, their noise and unpredictability, made his body
feel keyed up and watchful, and that state of vigilance seemed to invite the intrusive memories. He
had come to believe that being around children was dangerous, that their sudden movements or cries
would somehow trigger a flashback, and so he told Leila he was too tired for visits. She did not
argue, but her silence felt heavy.

What frightened Mazen most was his inability to relax. Even at home, in the bedroom he had shared
with Leila for forty-three years, he could not settle. The slightest unexpected touch — her hand on
his shoulder as he washed dishes, a pat on the back from a neighbor — made him flinch with an alarm
that felt primal and shameful. He had begun sleeping in the recliner in the living room, because the
bed felt too exposed, and even there he lay awake for hours, his mind circling the locked room, the
emotional waves, the certainty that something was still wrong with his body. He was certain that his
nervous system had been permanently damaged, that the chemical had rewired him, and that any moment
of safety was an illusion.

His work had suffered first. He had retired early, unable to climb a ladder without his hands
trembling, unable to tolerate the smell of tar and sealant. Then his relationships suffered. His
son-in-law stopped inviting him to family dinners. His old crew called less often. Mazen knew, with
a bitter clarity, that he was the one withdrawing, but the knowledge did not change his behavior.
The memories made him vigilant, and the vigilance made him hyperaware of every possible trigger: a
child’s laugh on the street, a headline on a phone screen, a familiar face from the job site. Each
of these seemed to loop back into the memory, tightening the spiral. He had come to believe that the
only way to keep the terror at bay was to shrink his world until nothing could reach him.

It was Leila who finally made the appointment. She had found him standing in the garage, staring at
a can of paint thinner, his face wet with tears. He had not been able to explain what he was feeling
— only that the smell had brought the emotion back, the full intensity, without any image, and he
had been certain for a moment that he was dying all over again. He agreed to see a therapist because
he was afraid of what would happen if he did not. What he wanted, more than anything, was to stop
feeling like a stranger in his own body. What he feared was that the chemical had changed him so
deeply that no amount of talking could undo it.
