Leilani was twenty-one, a nurse, and she had been home from her deployment for eleven months when
the sirens started following her. The first time was on a Tuesday afternoon, driving home from the
hospital; an ambulance passed her on the highway, and she was suddenly back in the forward operating
base, the air-raid warning screaming overhead. Her heart slammed against her ribs, and her hands
locked on the steering wheel so hard that her knuckles went white. She had to pull over. She sat
there shaking, the sound of the siren fading into the distance, but the feeling of it—the certainty
that something was about to explode—stayed with her for hours.

The memory of that day in the combat zone came back not as a story she could tell, but as a wave of
rage and overwhelm that rose up without warning. She had been on a routine supply run when the
mortar hit. The blast, the dust, the screaming. She remembered the medic beside her, the way his
face went slack. She remembered the rage. It was a hot, helpless fury at the enemy, at the command
who had sent them through that corridor, at herself for not seeing it coming. When the memory
returned, she felt so overwhelmed by emotion that she had to stop whatever she was doing. She knew
what had happened, but she could not find the words to describe it. The words simply were not there.

The rage was the hardest part. It did not stay in the past. It leaked into the present. A loud
noise—a car backfiring, a door slamming—and she would be on her feet, fists clenched, ready to fight
someone who was not there. Her boyfriend, Marcus, learned not to startle her. He learned to announce
himself before entering a room. Even so, there were evenings when she snapped at him for no reason,
her voice sharp and cruel, and she saw the hurt in his face before she could take it back. She hated
that. She hated herself for it. But the scanning for danger was constant now, and it wore her out.
She could not stop checking her surroundings, could not stop watching the faces of strangers in the
parking lot, could not stop listening for the wrong sound in the silence.

She began to avoid things that might make her feel happy or hopeful. It was not a conscious decision
at first, but she noticed that whenever she let herself relax—a movie with Marcus, a walk in the
park—the crash afterward was worse. The happiness felt like a trap. So she stopped. She stopped
making plans. She stopped saying yes to invitations. She also stopped exercising, even though she
had been a runner before the deployment. The first time she tried to jog after coming home, her
heart started pounding, and the pounding felt exactly like the seconds before the mortar hit. She
could not separate the sensation from the memory. So she stopped running.

The scanning for danger made everything worse. Every time she caught herself checking a room, every
time she flinched at a siren, the memory of the attack came back stronger. It was as if the
hypervigilance itself was the trigger, a feedback loop that tightened with each rotation. The more
she scanned, the more she remembered. The more she remembered, the more she scanned. She could not
break it. The rage and the overwhelm fed on each other, and the scanning fed on both. She started to
believe that if she stopped watching, something terrible would happen. That she would be caught off
guard again. That the next explosion would not miss.

The sirens were everywhere. She worked at a hospital in the city, and ambulances came and went all
day. Every siren was a hook that pulled her out of the present and dropped her back into the dust
and the noise. She began to dread her shifts. She avoided the emergency department entrance. She
took the long way around the building, even though it made her late. She could not explain it to her
supervisor. She could not explain it to anyone. She just knew that the sound undid her, and that the
undoing was getting faster, not slower.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment in the break room. A coworker dropped a metal
tray, and Leilani was on the floor, her back against the wall, her hands over her ears, her heart
pounding so hard she thought she might die. She did not remember getting there. She did not remember
the seconds in between. She only knew that she could not keep living this way—that the rage and the
fear and the scanning and the memory had become a cage she could not find the door to. She was
terrified that she was losing herself. She was terrified that Marcus would leave. She was terrified
that the next time a siren went off, she would not come back.
