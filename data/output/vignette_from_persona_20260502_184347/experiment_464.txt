Ravi was twenty-two when the blast happened. He had been stationed at a small diplomatic outpost in
a contested region, tasked with cultural liaison work that he had believed in deeply. One afternoon,
during a routine market visit with a local translator, a suicide bomber detonated himself fifty
meters away. The sound was not a bang but a physical force that seemed to enter through his ears and
press against his brain from the inside. He remembers the smell most vividly—a mix of diesel, burnt
fabric, and something sweet, like overcooked sugar, that he later learned was human tissue. He was
not physically injured, but he was covered in a fine gray dust that he could not stop tasting for
days. His translator, a woman named Amina who had shown him photographs of her children, lost her
left leg below the knee. Ravi carried her to a military vehicle while she screamed, and he has not
been able to describe that journey to anyone since. When he tries to tell his girlfriend, or his
therapist at the embassy, the words disappear. He knows what happened—every detail is brutally
clear, the sequence of events playing behind his eyes like a film he cannot pause—but his mouth goes
dry, and he shakes his head, and the story stays inside.

Now, two years later, Ravi is back in Washington, assigned to a desk job reviewing visa
applications. He is grateful for the quiet, but the quiet is never truly quiet. A car backfiring on
Pennsylvania Avenue sends him under his desk. The smell of hand sanitizer in a colleague’s
office—sweet and chemical—makes him nauseous and disoriented, the market rushing back in a wave of
olfactory recall so vivid he can feel the dust on his skin. He has come to believe that there is
something fundamentally broken in him, that his presence in that market somehow caused the attack,
that he should have sensed the danger. He has lost his faith in the diplomatic mission he once
cherished; he now sees every stranger who approaches his desk as a potential threat, even when they
are just delivering interoffice mail. He cannot trust safety, no matter how many times his
supervisor tells him the building is secure, no matter how many times his girlfriend holds his hand
and says it is over.

Ravi has developed a set of strategies to manage the terror, though he would not call them that. He
exercises compulsively, running ten miles before dawn every day, pushing through shin splints and
exhaustion because the physical pain distracts from the images that crowd his mind. He has started
smoking marijuana most evenings, telling himself it helps him sleep, though it actually makes his
memories more fragmented and intrusive, the smell of smoke sometimes triggering the olfactory recall
of the market. He wears concealing clothing—oversized jackets, hoods, dark colors—even in summer,
believing that if he is less noticeable, he will be less of a target. These routines have cost him.
He has stopped attending social events with his girlfriend, who is beginning to withdraw. He has
been written up for arriving late to work, because his morning runs take longer as he checks and
rechecks his route for threats. The more he exercises, the more his body aches, and the more his
body aches, the more he feels the flashbacks pressing in, as if the physical strain opens a door to
the memory. The more he uses marijuana, the more his memory intrudes, unbidden, the smell and the
sound arriving together. He is caught in a loop he cannot name.

What finally brings Ravi to a clinician is a panic attack in a conference room. A colleague opened a
bottle of whiskey to celebrate a trade deal, and the alcohol smell hit Ravi like a second explosion.
He fled, hyperventilating, and spent an hour locked in a bathroom stall, certain he was dying. His
supervisor has mandated a fitness-for-duty evaluation. Ravi comes to the first session with his
hands in his pockets, his hood up, his eyes fixed on the floor. He is afraid that if he starts
talking, he will never stop, and that if he stops, the memories will swallow him whole. He is also
afraid, deep down, that the clinician will confirm what he already suspects: that there is something
about him that made the event happen, and that there is no future worth planning. But he is here,
and that is the only thing he is sure of.
