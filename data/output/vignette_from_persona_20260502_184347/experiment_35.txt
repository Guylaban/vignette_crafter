then, a widow of five years who had finally allowed herself to trust again. She had been a social
worker for four decades, a woman who had built a career on reading people, on knowing when someone
was safe. But the man she had been seeing for six months, a retired accountant named Frank, had a
temper that surfaced only behind closed doors. What she remembered now, five years later, was not a
sequence of events but a series of frozen images: his hand against the wall beside her head, the
look in his eyes that was nothing like the man who brought her tea, the door slamming as she fled
into the night. She could not put those pictures in the right order. The grief that washed over her
when she recalled that moment felt as fresh as the night itself—a raw, bewildering sorrow for the
woman she had been before, for the trust she had given so freely.

In the months that followed, Eleanor found herself unable to sleep. She would lie awake listening
for sounds she had not noticed before: the creak of her own floorboards, the click of a lock, the
distant slam of a neighbor's door. Each sound, each unexpected male voice in the grocery store line,
brought back one of those frozen pictures—Frank's face, the door, the cold night air. She had come
to believe that the world was not the place she had thought it was. Her beliefs about safety, about
people, had been completely destroyed. She felt dirty, as though the contamination of that night had
seeped into her skin. She was certain now that people could not be trusted, that her judgment had
been fatally wrong. Every time a man she did not know well stood too close, or a door closed with
too much force, she felt those beliefs confirmed all over again.

She began to avoid situations that brought on nightmares. She stopped napping in the afternoon,
because that was when the dreams came most vividly. She tried to erase the memory entirely, pushing
the images away when they surfaced, telling herself it had not happened the way she thought. She
spent long hours thinking about why this had happened to her, turning over her own history like a
stone, searching for the moment she had misread the signs. But the more she tried to erase the
memory, the more the grief returned, and the more she felt certain that she was fundamentally
contaminated. She had come to believe that her own judgment was broken, and that belief made her
scan every room for exits as soon as she entered. At eighty, she still worked part-time as a social
worker, but the office had become a minefield. Every male colleague who walked into her office,
every client who was brought in by a male relative—she was watching the door, measuring the distance
to the hallway, calculating how fast she could leave. The hypervigilance exhausted her, but she
could not stop. Being superalert felt like the only thing keeping her safe.

The avoidance itself made things worse. Because she avoided the afternoon naps, she was more tired,
and her mind was more prone to drift into those frozen images. Because she scanned for exits, she
noticed every door that opened, and each one triggered the memory again. She had stopped visiting
her son's house because his teenage son slammed his bedroom door. She had stopped going to the
senior center because the men there were friendly, and friendliness now felt like a trap. Her world
had shrunk to her apartment, and even there, she slept with the lights on, watching the front door.
What frightened her most was not the memory itself but the feeling that she was still in that
moment, still vulnerable, still unable to trust her own mind. She had come to therapy because her
daughter had found her crying at the kitchen table at three in the morning, unable to explain why
she was afraid to go to bed. Eleanor wanted to stop being afraid, but she was terrified that if she
let go of the vigilance, something terrible would happen again.
