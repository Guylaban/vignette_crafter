things—houses, roads, a family. He had been widowed for eight years, and for most of that time, he
had lived alone in a small apartment he’d bought with his wife’s life insurance. What he had never
told anyone was that for the last three years of their marriage, his wife had hit him. It had
started as a slap during an argument about money, then escalated to closed fists, a lamp thrown at
his back, and once, a kitchen knife held to his throat while she screamed that he was useless. He
had never fought back. He had stood there, frozen, telling himself he deserved it because he
couldn’t keep a steady job, because he drank too much, because he was a weak man. The night she
died—a car accident, not his fault—he had felt nothing but relief. Then the guilt came, and with it,
something worse.

The first trigger had been a woman at the hardware store. She had raised her arm to point at a
shelf, and Makani saw the curve of her bicep, the same shape as his wife’s. His chest tightened, and
he felt a wave of shame so strong he had to lean against a pallet of cement bags. He left without
buying anything. Soon, other triggers crept in: a coworker named Leilani who had the same laugh as
his wife, the sound of a raised voice on a jobsite, a man talking about a domestic dispute he’d seen
on the news. Every time someone mentioned the event—even in passing, even about someone else—Makani
felt his stomach drop and a voice inside him whisper, *You let it happen. You’re a weak person.* He
had come to believe that he could not rely on himself to handle anything difficult, that he was
fundamentally broken. The emotional numbness he felt, the deadness inside, only confirmed it. If he
could not even feel sorrow for his own wife, what kind of man was he?

Makani began to avoid conflict at all costs. On the construction site, when a foreman barked at him,
he simply nodded and walked away, even when he was right. He stopped going to the bar after work
because someone might start an argument. He also started spending money he didn’t have—buying a new
drill he didn’t need, ordering takeout every night—because the act of buying something, of clicking
“confirm,” drowned out the voice for a few minutes. But the spending itself made him feel weaker,
more out of control, and that only deepened his belief that he could not trust himself. The cycle
tightened: the triggers made him feel weak, the weakness drove him to avoid and spend, and the
avoidance and spending proved he was weak.

His threat monitoring had become constant. He startled at the sound of a dropped hammer, a car
backfiring, a door slamming. He woke at 3 a.m. every night to check the locks, to make sure no one
was in the apartment. And as soon as he entered any room—the grocery store, the doctor’s waiting
room, the construction trailer—his eyes would find the exits before he even knew he was looking.
This hypervigilance made him more sensitive to triggers: scanning the exits meant he noticed every
person, every raised hand, every potential threat, and each one brought the memory back. The threat
also drove his maladaptive strategies directly. When he startled, he would immediately check the
nearest exit and then, if he was in a store, buy something—anything—to calm down. He stayed near the
door at all times, even at his own kitchen table, and he never sat with his back to the room.

Over the months, the consequences accumulated. He had been passed over for a promotion because he
never spoke up in meetings. His daughter, who lived in another state, called less often because
every conversation felt like a minefield of triggers. He was spending nearly a third of his paycheck
on things he didn’t need, and his savings were gone. What finally brought him to the clinic was a
panic attack in the hardware store aisle, triggered by a woman’s forearm. He had dropped to his
knees, gasping, and a store manager had called an ambulance. Now, sitting in the waiting room,
Makani was most afraid that the therapist would tell him he was too old to change, that this was
just who he was now. But beneath that, what he really hoped was that someone might finally tell him
he wasn’t weak—that he had survived something terrible, and that surviving was not the same as
failing.
