him. There are gaps he cannot fill no matter how hard he tries.

startle responses left him flushed and ashamed. He began watching his colleagues closely, scanning
their faces for any flicker of hostility, certain that someone might be planning to harm him. What
frightened him most, though, was the conviction that others could see what had been done to him just
by looking at him. He became convinced there was something fundamentally wrong with him as a person,
that he had acted in ways during the captivity that violated who he believed himself to be—he had
begged, he had wept, he had given them the names of sources. He dwelled endlessly on how the event
could have been prevented, replaying decisions he had made at the border crossing, each revision
more punishing than the last.

One afternoon in the grocery store, a child began to cry two aisles over. Dara’s chest tightened
instantly; the sound seemed to pierce through him, and he felt dizzy, the fluorescent lights
swimming. In that moment, the fragmented memory surged back—not as a clear narrative, but as a
visceral wave of heat and dread. He abandoned his cart and walked out, certain that the crying child

a therapist’s office, afraid that talking about what happened will make the gaps close into
something worse, but even more afraid of living the rest of his life in a world where a child’s cry
can undo him.
