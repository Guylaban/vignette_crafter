Mai is a 61-year-old sculptor who lives with her husband in a quiet suburban neighborhood outside of
Portland. She has worked with clay and stone for over thirty years, her hands finding comfort in the
familiar weight of materials that yield slowly to pressure. But for the past fourteen months, since
the evening she witnessed a man collapse and die on the sidewalk outside her studio—a sudden cardiac
arrest, the paramedics later said, though Mai never learned his name—her hands have been unreliable.
She cannot hold a tool without her palms growing slick with sweat, and the studio, once her
sanctuary, now feels like a place where something terrible could happen at any moment.

The event itself played out in fragments. She remembers the sound of footsteps behind her as she
locked up for the night, the way she turned and saw a man in his forties clutching his chest, the
sharp, sweet scent of his cologne as he stumbled past her. She remembers the terrible stillness that
followed, the way his body folded onto the pavement, and the paramedics who arrived too late. But
when she tries to tell her husband what happened, the words dissolve. She cannot find the thread.
The memory feels more real than anything else in her life—she can smell that cologne on a summer
breeze and it is as if she is back there, frozen on the sidewalk—yet she cannot form a coherent
story of what she saw. The gaps in her own memory frighten her. She has come to believe that there
is something wrong with her, that she is defective for not being able to hold the event in her mind
the way other people might.

Mai now avoids unpredictable situations entirely. She no longer walks to the grocery store after
dusk. She leaves the studio before the evening rush, and she has stopped attending the weekly life-
drawing group she ran for twenty years because the walk home takes her past the spot where the man
died. The scent of cologne—any cologne—sends her heart racing. When she catches a whiff in a store
or on a stranger in a waiting room, her chest tightens, her vision narrows, and she feels the same
physical reactivity she felt that night: her stomach drops, her hands shake, and she is certain she
is about to see something unbearable again. She has noticed that she is more easily startled now,
jumping at the sound of footsteps behind her, especially after dark. Nighttime has become
unbearable. She lies awake listening for sounds, certain that danger is closer in the dark, that she
cannot let her guard down until the sun rises.

The world has come to feel like a dangerous place. Mai scans every room she enters for exits, for
people who look like they might be in distress. She has come to distrust strangers entirely—she is
convinced that anyone could collapse at any moment, and that she will be the one to witness it
again. This vigilance exhausts her, yet she cannot stop. She has noticed that the more vigilant she
becomes, the more her memories intrude: a footstep behind her in the hardware store and suddenly she
is back on that sidewalk, smelling cologne, watching a man fall. And the more vivid the memory
becomes, the more dangerous the world feels, and the more she believes that something is
fundamentally wrong with her for being unable to move past it.

Her husband has grown frustrated. He does not understand why she will not go out for dinner, why she
flinches when he walks up behind her, why she has stopped inviting friends over. Mai cannot explain
it to him because she cannot explain it to herself. She only knows that the cologne she once
associated with her father now makes her want to flee, that footsteps behind her make her freeze,
and that she has begun to wonder if she will ever feel safe again. She came to therapy because she
found herself crying in the studio one afternoon, unable to touch the clay, unable to remember why
she had ever loved it. She is afraid that the woman who used to work with her hands is gone, and she
does not know how to find her way back.
