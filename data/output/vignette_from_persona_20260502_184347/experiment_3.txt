Kofi is a 55-year-old bomb disposal technician who was deployed to a coastal village in West Africa
after a catastrophic earthquake triggered a tsunami. He spent thirty-six hours pulling bodies from
the wreckage and debris, working in chest-deep water that kept rising without warning. What stayed
with him most was the sound—the roar of the water followed by the screams, then the silence, then
another wave. He had been trained to stay calm in controlled explosions, but this was chaos without
a timer, without a plan, without any way to predict what would come next. In the months that
followed, he found he could only recall fragments: the smell of wet concrete, a child’s shoe
floating past, the way his hands shook as he tried to lift a collapsed wall. The rest felt missing,
like a film reel with whole sections cut out. He would start to remember, and then something in him
would slam the door shut before the full picture could arrive. And when the fragments did surface,
what rose in him was not sadness but a white-hot rage—at the ocean, at the government that had sent
him, at himself for not saving more people.

Back home in Chicago, Kofi returned to work but found the world had become a landscape of hidden
dangers. Loud noises—a truck backfiring, a door slamming—sent his body into a full defensive posture
before his mind could even register what had happened. His heart would pound, his muscles would
lock, and for a split second he was back in the water. He started reacting as if he were in danger
even when nothing was actually wrong, and the unpredictability of daily life—traffic jams, sudden
schedule changes, a colleague raising his voice in a meeting—felt unbearable. The more his body
reacted before he could think, the more he came to expect danger everywhere, scanning every room for
exits, every conversation for the first sign of conflict. Shouting was the worst; it triggered a
cascade of physical alarm that left him breathless and furious, and the memory of the tsunami would
surge back, incomplete but overwhelming, and the rage would return.

To manage this, Kofi began avoiding anything that could be stressful. He stopped attending team
briefings, stopped meeting friends, stopped driving on unfamiliar roads. He kept his mind occupied
all the time—podcasts, audiobooks, constant music in his earbuds—so there was no silence for the
memories to break through. But the constant mental distraction exhausted him, and when he was too
tired to maintain it, he would overeat in the evenings, numbing himself with food until he could
fall asleep. This avoidance, however, only made the world feel smaller and more dangerous. The less
he confronted unpredictable situations, the more threatening they seemed when they did arise. A
sudden noise on a quiet street would make him flinch, and the flinch itself would trigger a
flashback—a full sensory return to the wave—and then the rage again, and then the shame of having
lost control. He began to see threats everywhere, and every threat triggered a memory, and every
memory made him more vigilant, and every moment of vigilance wore him down further.

His wife, Amina, noticed he had stopped laughing. He no longer wanted to go to their daughter’s
soccer games because the crowd noise felt unpredictable. He had started eating dinner alone in the
basement, listening to a history podcast at full volume so he wouldn’t have to hear the television
from upstairs. He was certain that if he let his guard down for even a moment, the memories would
flood back and he would drown in them. What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at the
grocery store: a child dropped a glass jar, and Kofi hit the floor, covering his head, his heart
slamming against his ribs. A security guard had to help him up. He could not stop shaking. He knew
then that the world he was living in—a world of constant alarm, of fragmented rage, of hiding from
his own mind—was no longer sustainable. He was afraid of what he might do the next time a sound
surprised him, and he was desperate to find a way back to the person he had been before the water
rose.
