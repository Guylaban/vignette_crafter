For nearly twenty years, Daniel had made his living high above the street, laying shingles and
patching roofs across suburban neighborhoods outside Boston. He was 43, married, practical, and used
to thinking of himself as the man who kept working no matter the weather. That image of himself
fractured the day he was downtown delivering materials near a transit station when an explosion tore
through the block. He remembered the flash first and then the heat, a violent burst of air that
seemed to punch through him. In the smoke and confusion, his chest slammed with terror so hard he
thought his heart might give out. There was a scorched, chemical smell mixed with burning, and for a
few moments he was convinced the whole street was on fire. He got clear physically, but what stayed
with him was the sense that danger could erupt without warning and that his own body had become part
of the alarm.

In the weeks afterward, Daniel noticed that certain sensations pulled him straight back toward that
day. If he was climbing a ladder at work and his heart started pounding from exertion, he would
suddenly go rigid, as if that familiar racing in his chest meant something catastrophic was about to
happen. The smell of burnt material on a torch-down roofing job, or even a faint smoky odor drifting
from a neighbor’s grill, could stop him mid-motion. In those moments the memory did not come back as
a story he could tell. It hit him all at once, with such force that he had to shut it down. He would
feel a sharp ache in the same parts of his body that had hurt after the blast, and when his wife
asked what had just happened, he would say he knew exactly what he had seen but could not get the
words out.

Because those moments felt unbearable, Daniel began organizing his days around not letting his mind
settle. He kept the radio on in the truck, took extra jobs, scrolled on his phone during breaks, and
stayed busy at home fixing things that did not really need fixing. When burnt or smoky smells caught
him off guard on a roof, he would immediately redirect himself into measurements, tools, anything
concrete that could pull him away from what was beginning to surface. He also stopped watching news
coverage, action films, or anything about bombings or attacks. He told himself there was no point in
filling his head with more of the same; privately, he knew that even brief reminders could set off
the flood he was trying to outrun.

His body, meanwhile, never seemed to stand down. Daniel moved through the world braced and watchful,
scanning rooftops, traffic, crowds, and sudden noises with a level of alertness that exhausted him.
He sat in restaurants facing the door. On jobs in the city he tracked sirens and backfiring engines
before anyone else seemed to notice them. This constant readiness made the trauma memory feel close
to the surface. When he was already wound tight, it took very little for an image or bodily flash of
the explosion to break through, and once it did, he was overwhelmed too quickly to stay with it for
long.

Over time the memory itself became another trigger. He learned that even the beginning of it—the
first whiff of smoke in his mind, the first bodily echo of pain, the first sense of his heart
surging—could set off the same urgent alarm as something happening in front of him. That left him
more determined to distract himself the instant anything related to the attack appeared. The more he
cut away from it, the less able he felt to describe what had happened, and the more frightening it
seemed whenever it forced its way back in. At work, this had started to cost him. On one recent
roofing job, the odor from heated tar made him step away so abruptly that his coworker asked if he
was sick. Daniel shrugged it off, but afterward he avoided that part of the job and stayed on edge
for the rest of the day, embarrassed and irritated that his body had reacted before he could think.

At home, his wife had begun to notice that he was never really at rest. Even in the evenings he kept
himself occupied, flipping channels until he landed on something harmless, leaving the room if a
report about an attack came on, going out to the garage rather than sitting still. He did not tell
her that what frightened him most was how quickly nothing could become something: a pounding
heartbeat, a burnt smell, a flicker of memory, and suddenly he was back in danger. He finally came
for help after a recent incident on a roof when his heart started racing from the climb and he
froze, certain for a few seconds that another attack was unfolding. What he wants is simple enough
to say, even if the rest is not: to work without feeling ambushed by his own body, and to be able to
come home without carrying the blast with him.
