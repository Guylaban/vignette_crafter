been walking through on his lunch break. He worked as an urban planner for the city, and that day he
had gone to the station to observe pedestrian flow around a redevelopment site. What remained with
him was not a story he could tell from beginning to end, but a series of sharp pictures: a flash

without the child in sight, gray dust hanging in the air as alarms screamed. Even in the first weeks
afterward, when colleagues asked what happened, Arjun would stop and lose the thread. He could not
place what came first and what came after; the images arrived without order, and the more he tried
to force them into a coherent account, the more jumbled and unmanageable they felt.

In the months after the bombing, Arjun had come to believe that something essential in him had been
damaged. Before, he had taken pride in being steady, analytic, and effective under pressure, the
kind of planner who could walk a crowded block and instantly see patterns, conflicts, and solutions.
Now he found himself thinking, with shame, that he was no longer capable of doing the things that
had defined him. He also became convinced that if he allowed himself to think directly about the
attack, he would not be able to handle what came up. That conviction shaped the way he related to
his own memory. He kept turning away from the event in his mind, and when it intruded anyway it
appeared as disconnected visual fragments rather than a narrative he could hold onto. He would tell
himself not to go there, not to look too closely, and the result was that the attack remained a set
of frightening images he could not put in order even for himself. He felt isolated in this, set
apart from coworkers and friends who seemed able to discuss difficult events and move on, while he
was left with scenes he could neither fully face nor fully explain.

Arjun’s way of coping became increasingly rigid. He began exercising compulsively, waking before
dawn to run hard for miles and spending long evenings pushing through punishing workouts. He
described it less as enjoyment than as a way to outrun the agitation in his body and prove to
himself that he still had discipline. He also started organizing his life around avoiding anything
that might feel stressful. At work he stopped volunteering for public meetings, especially those
held near transit hubs, and steered himself toward desk-based tasks with minimal uncertainty. He
took circuitous routes across the city to avoid the concourse where the bombing occurred and, more

time was spent dwelling on who he had been before the attack—more confident, more social, less
fragile by his own account.

Over time, these strategies deepened the very conclusions he had drawn about himself. The more Arjun
narrowed his world to prevent stress, the more his life seemed to confirm that he was no longer
capable. Passing up site visits and high-stakes meetings left him feeling unlike the professional he
had trained to be. His excessive exercise became another private standard he could fail against: if
he missed a run or felt tired, he took it as evidence of weakness rather than strain. And his
constant comparisons with his pre-trauma self hardened his sense that the real Arjun was gone. He
became increasingly cut off after his divorce, declining invitations from friends and keeping
conversations superficial at the office. In his mind, other people still belonged to ordinary life,
while he lived outside it.

By the time he sought help, the loop had tightened. On several recent mornings, after arriving at
work and seeing planning maps of the transit district spread across a conference table, he found the
same stark images breaking in—the flash, the dust, the red backpack—without sequence, without
context, as if the event were happening in separate frames. He would look away, tell himself not to
think about it, and feel ashamed that he still could not simply master his own mind. What finally
brought him to treatment was a missed presentation on a major station redesign project, work he
would once have considered central to his identity. He told the therapist, quietly, that he was
frightened not only of the memories but of what they seemed to say about him: that he had become
someone smaller, more avoidant, and permanently apart from other people. At the same time, he still
hoped there might be a way to face what happened without being overwhelmed by it, and to find some
version of himself that did not feel lost.
