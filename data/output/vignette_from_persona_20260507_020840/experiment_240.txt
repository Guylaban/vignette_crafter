the danger were still hunting her, long before her mind could catch up to recognize it. It had begun
six months ago with the sudden, violent death of her younger cousin, Marcus. They had been driving
back from a community outreach project when a freight truck crossed the median. Amara remembered the
sound first—a deafening, metallic shriek that now lived in her ribs. When she tried to recall the
moments after the impact, her mind simply slid away, pushing the memory out before she could fully
grasp it. She knew exactly what had happened, yet she was utterly unable to find the words to
describe it to anyone, not even the grief counselors who had tried to speak with her. What
frightened her most was not the past, but the way it refused to stay there. On crowded subway rides,
when the brakes squealed or a passenger bumped her shoulder, the scene would drop over her like a
heavy coat. She was not remembering Marcus; she was back in the cabin, smelling diesel and spilled
soda, feeling the sudden, absolute absence of his voice. In the weeks that followed, Amara found
herself trapped in a relentless loop of self-reproach. She repeatedly blamed herself for what
happened, certain that her own careless planning or impulsive nature had put Marcus in the way of

trauma that kept resurfacing. She also stopped consuming any media that touched on sudden loss.

her body would finally stop bracing for a crash that had already happened.
