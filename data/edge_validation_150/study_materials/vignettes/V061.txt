He is 51 years old, an Oceanian man who has spent most of his adult life working as a welder, a trade he takes pride in because it has always given him a sense of usefulness and control. Before the collision, he describes himself as steady, practical, and not given to "making a fuss" about pain or fear. He has been married for many years, and his life has been structured around early starts, physical work, and the familiar rhythm of coming home tired but satisfied.

The traumatic event happens on an ordinary day that still feels, to him, like it should not have become the day that changed everything. He is driving when another vehicle hits him with violent force. In the split second before impact, he sees the other car coming and has no time to react. He remembers the sound first - the explosive crash of metal, glass, and his own body jerking against the seatbelt - and then a strange silence that follows, as if the world has been knocked out of him. He thinks he is dying. His chest feels crushed, his breathing becomes shallow, and he is intensely aware of the smell of fuel, dust, and broken plastic. Even after help arrives, he is disoriented and terrified, unable to make sense of where he is or whether he is still trapped in danger.

In the days afterward, he tries to push through in the way he always has, but the event keeps returning to him in flashes that feel immediate rather than remembered. A screech of tires on the road near his house can send his body into a sudden surge of panic. He says his heart starts racing before he has fully understood what he is hearing, and he feels as if he is back in the collision, bracing for impact. At night, he wakes abruptly from vivid dreams in which he is again unable to stop the car or escape the crash. Sometimes he jerks awake already sweating, with his jaw clenched and his hands gripping the sheets. He is embarrassed by how easily his body reacts, because he has always seen himself as someone who can stay calm under pressure.

He begins avoiding anything that reminds him of the accident. He no longer drives unless it is absolutely necessary, and when he must get into a vehicle, he sits rigidly and watches every approaching car. He chooses back roads when he can, not because they are better, but because there is less traffic and fewer chances for something to go wrong. He avoids the stretch of road where the collision happened and finds excuses not to talk about the event in detail. Even riding as a passenger leaves him tense and irritable. His wife notices that he scans the road constantly, flinches at sudden braking, and becomes short-tempered if another driver is too close. He does not like being driven, but he dislikes even more the feeling that he cannot trust what is happening around him.

At work, the changes are painful because welding used to give him a sense of mastery that he now struggles to access. He can still do the tasks, but he notices his concentration slipping when his body is on alert. If a loud bang echoes in the workshop, he startles hard and then feels irritated at himself for overreacting. He finds it harder to settle into the focused, exacting attention his work demands. At times, he catches himself checking exits or imagining collisions when he should be concentrating on a seam. He pushes through the day, but by afternoon he is often drained in a way that feels bigger than fatigue.

He also becomes more distant in ways that are hard for him to explain. He is less interested in social gatherings and feels cut off from the easy version of himself he used to be around his wife and family. He is not exactly sad all the time, but there is a flattened, watchful quality to him, as if part of him has stepped back from ordinary life. He feels on edge much of the day, and his sleep remains poor, leaving him irritable and less patient than before. Small frustrations can provoke anger he does not fully understand. He dislikes feeling weak, and that dislike often turns inward into frustration and shame.

As he tells his story across sessions, it becomes clear that the collision has not only frightened him; it has altered the way he moves through the world. He still goes to work, still tries to be dependable, still fulfills the role he has always carried, but he does so with a tightened body and a mind that is constantly checking for danger. The accident is over in time, but it remains active in him, arriving in his dreams, in his reactions to traffic, in the uneasy vigilance that now follows him through each day.
