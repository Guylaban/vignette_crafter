Amina is a 42-year-old African American woman who came to treatment after years of trying to get through her days by staying busy and drawing as little attention to herself as possible. She is divorced and works long shifts in a factory, where the press of bodies on the line, the clang of equipment, and the unpredictability of sudden noises have become part of the strain she carries rather than just the backdrop of her job. In the first sessions, she spoke carefully about having been tortured earlier in her life, describing it in fragments that felt to her both vivid and impossible. She remembered being held in a confined place with other people nearby, close enough that she could feel another person's presence against her skin and hear voices using names connected to the men involved. The weather at the time had been damp and heavy, the air close, and there were smells that stayed with her more than any clear sequence of events. When she tried to tell it now, she often paused and said it sounded unreal, "like something from somebody else's life," even as certain details came back with punishing clarity.

What remains most potent for Amina are not orderly memories but moments that seize her. If she hears a name that resembles one she associates with that period, she feels a jolt before she has time to place why. On hot, wet days with the same oppressive feel in the air, she notices her chest tighten and her body brace as if something is about to happen. If someone steps too close to her in a grocery line or on the factory floor, she can feel herself go rigid, her attention narrowing immediately to exits, footsteps, and the distance between herself and the other person. She says her body "decides before I do." Even ordinary sounds can set this off. At home, a noise in the hallway or the creak of the building makes her jump hard enough that she has to steady herself. She listens intently for signs that someone might be entering, especially at night, and she is often not convinced by her own reassurances afterward.

When Amina thinks about what happened, smell is often the thing that comes first. She can suddenly catch, in her mind, the same scent from that place, and for a moment it is as if the present has dropped away. Yet at the same time she struggles to experience the memory as fully hers. She described it as watching a terrible story that she knows intellectually belongs to her life but does not feel connected to her in the normal way. This strangeness frightens her almost as much as the recollections themselves. She has come to believe that the world is fundamentally dangerous and that safety depends on constant alertness. Just as painful is her sense that she is no longer capable in the way she once was. Before seeking help, she measured herself against the woman she believes she used to be and found herself wanting. She also no longer trusts her body. Because it surges into alarm so quickly, she experiences it as unreliable, as though it might betray her at any moment.

Over time, Amina built her life around trying not to stir these reactions. She keeps her mind occupied nearly all the time, filling silence with television, chores, phone games, extra shifts, anything that prevents her thoughts from settling. On days off, she avoids sitting still because stillness makes room for images and smells to return. She is deliberate about clothing, refusing to wear anything that resembles what she associates with the time of the torture. She also leaves the house in heavy makeup, applying it carefully and consistently. She did not describe this as vanity; it is closer to armor, a way of facing the world with a covered surface. At the factory, these habits shape her routines. She chooses tasks that let her stay focused and mentally occupied, but the closeness of coworkers on the line is difficult, and when someone leans in to speak over the machinery she can flinch so visibly that colleagues notice. Sudden bangs from equipment make her startle, and she has become embarrassed by how often this happens. The accumulation of these moments has left her more isolated at work and more withdrawn outside it.

What finally brought Amina to seek help was not a single new event but the exhaustion of living this way and a recent stretch of humid weather that made it harder to keep herself steady. She found herself increasingly on edge at home, listening for intrusions, and increasingly tense at work whenever people came near her. She said she was afraid that this was now simply who she was: a woman who could not trust the world, her body, or her own mind. At the same time, she came in holding onto a quieter hope that the memories might begin to feel less unreal and less overpowering, and that she might one day move through ordinary spaces without constantly preparing for danger.
