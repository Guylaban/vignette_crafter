Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was thirty-four when he came to his first appointment, referred by his primary care physician after his wife, Elena, had finally insisted he see someone. He sat with his shoulders pressed against the back of the chair, positioned so that he could see the door of the consulting room without turning his head. He had noticed the exit the moment he walked in-a habit so automatic now that he could not remember when it had started, though he knew exactly when it had become necessary.

Six years earlier, Marcus had taken a leave of absence from the fire department to work as a relief worker in a conflict zone in the Levant. He had been there for four months, helping to coordinate medical supplies and assist with evacuations in a region where the distinction between civilian and combatant had become meaningless. On his forty-third day, the compound where he and twelve others were sheltering came under sustained fire. He had been in the courtyard when the first rounds hit. He remembered the sound-not loud but precise, almost clinical-and then the chaos of bodies moving without coordination, people calling out names that went unanswered. A colleague beside him fell. Marcus dragged him toward what he thought was cover, but the angle was wrong; there was no real shelter. He spent eighteen minutes in that courtyard, certain that each second would be his last, listening to rounds strike concrete and flesh with equal indifference.

He was not physically injured. When the firing stopped, he helped carry the wounded to a vehicle. He finished his rotation. He came home.

For the first year back, Marcus told Elena that he was fine, just tired. He returned to the fire department. But something had shifted in how he understood space and safety. He had come to believe that threats could materialize anywhere, that ordinary environments contained hidden dangers, that strangers-people he passed on the street, customers at the grocery store, even the new neighbor who had knocked on his door to introduce himself-might pose a risk he could not anticipate or control. His mind had developed a kind of scanning protocol: entering any room, he would immediately locate the exits, assess sightlines, note potential obstacles. In the firehouse, this felt almost professional; in his own living room, it felt like a betrayal of the life he was supposed to be living.

Within months, Marcus had stopped going to the gym. He had always lifted weights three times a week, a ritual that steadied him. But gyms were enclosed spaces with limited exits, full of strangers, and the vulnerability of exertion felt intolerable. He told Elena it was just a scheduling issue. Then he stopped going out for evening walks through their neighborhood. The open streets felt worse in some ways-too many people, too many angles of approach. At home, he positioned himself always with a view of the door. He began to tense when Elena tried to hold him, when she wanted to celebrate small things-a promotion at her law firm, their anniversary-because joy felt dangerous, a kind of exposure. When she reached for him with genuine happiness in her face, something in him recoiled. He had come to believe that positive feelings made you careless, that they softened your vigilance, that they were a luxury he could not afford.

Elena had watched him disappear into hypervigilance over five years. She saw how his shoulders never fully relaxed, how he would sometimes grip her arm too tightly when a car backfired, how he had become a ghost in their marriage-present but sealed off, always listening for a threat that never came. When she tried to talk to him about it, he would acknowledge that yes, he knew it was irrational, but knowing and feeling were different things. The rational part of him understood that their quiet suburban street was safe. The part of him that had spent eighteen minutes in that courtyard knew better.

Marcus came to therapy because Elena had given him an ultimatum, but also because he was exhausted. He was afraid of what his hypervigilance might cost him-his marriage, his career, his mind. He was hoping that someone could help him understand why safety, even when objectively present, still felt like a luxury he had forfeited.
