Mateo, a 24-year-old QA Engineer, had always been a meticulous and reliable presence, but his life irrevocably shifted eighteen months ago. He had been enjoying a vibrant concert in a packed stadium when, without warning, a section of the upper tier groaned and then gave way. In the terrifying crush of concrete and screaming, Mateo found himself pinned, watching helplessly as people, including a family with young children just meters away, were engulfed in the chaos. He survived with minor physical injuries, but the indelible image of the collapse and his inability to intervene became the defining moment of his existence.

In the months that followed, Mateo found his world shrinking. Any discussion of accidents, even trivial ones like a fender bender a colleague mentioned during a lunch break, would instantly pull him back to the stadium, triggering a chilling dissociative feeling where he would begin to feel detached and unreal, as if observing his own life from a distance. These moments were often accompanied by a crushing sense that he should have protected others and he failed, a belief that gnawed at his core. He would replay the event endlessly in his mind, consumed by counterfactual thinking, wondering how life would have been different if he had moved just a few feet to the left, or if he had simply stayed home that night.

His conviction that he should have protected others and he failed became a lens through which he viewed every interaction and every news headline. This pervasive negative appraisal made him profoundly anxious, particularly about any situation where he might be perceived as responsible or, worse, helpless again. As a result, he began to actively avoid police, crossing the street if he saw a patrol car, haunted by the memory of the overwhelming official presence after the disaster and the questions he couldn't answer. He was certain that you could never know who would harm you, a terrifying uncertainty that permeated his daily life.

The world outside his apartment, once a source of curiosity and engagement, now felt like a minefield. When Mateo encountered media or news related to any kind of accident or disaster, even distant ones, he would quickly change the channel or close the browser, unable to bear the visceral pang it caused. This strategy of avoiding media became second nature, a desperate attempt to shield himself from reminders of the trauma. However, this avoidance only reinforced his belief that nothing good could happen to him anymore, making him feel that any effort to engage with the world was futile, further deepening his isolation. His work, which required him to meticulously identify potential flaws in software, became increasingly difficult as his concentration wavered, and the fear of making a mistake, of failing again, became crippling.

Mateo finally sought help when he realised his life had narrowed to an unbearable point. He was living in a perpetual state of vigilance, convinced that danger lurked everywhere and that he was powerless to stop it. He understood that his strategies, though offering momentary relief, had trapped him in a cycle of fear and despair. What frightened him most was the thought that this detached, hopeless existence was all that remained for him, but a small part of him still hoped to find a way back to a life where good things might, against all odds, still be possible.
