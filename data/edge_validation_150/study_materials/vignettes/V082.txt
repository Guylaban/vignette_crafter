Mark had been a firefighter for a decade before he took the two-month leave to volunteer with a relief agency. He was used to controlled chaos, to running towards danger that had a name and a shape. The war zone was different. The danger there was formless, an atmospheric pressure. The traumatic event itself wasn't a dramatic firefight but a moment of sudden, incomprehensible violence. He was organizing medical supplies in the concrete basement of a field clinic when a car bomb detonated in the street outside. The world became a concussion of sound and force. The lights went out, the air filled with thick, choking dust, and the ceiling sagged, raining down debris. The only way out was a narrow, partially collapsed stairwell, and the panicked rush of strangers in the pitch-dark felt as dangerous as the failing structure itself.

When he returned home to Chicago, his wife, Chloe, noticed he was different. He was quieter, and the easy-going physicality he once had was gone, replaced by a tense stillness. At first, the changes were subtle. In restaurants, he would insist on a table near the door. At the movies, he would only take an aisle seat at the back. He told himself it was just being tactical, an extension of his professional training, but the behaviour soon hardened into a rigid rule. The simple act of entering a room became an exercise in threat assessment; before he could sit down, he had to locate every possible exit, his mind mapping escape routes he would never use. This constant rehearsal of disaster prevention sent a powerful, unending message to his nervous system: you are never safe. The world, his brain concluded, was a place that required a perpetual exit strategy.

This deep-seated conviction that threat was imminent began to poison every aspect of his life. At home, the place he once recharged, he felt perpetually on guard, startling at the sound of a delivery truck in the street or the rattle of the pipes. He would walk the house at night, checking locks he knew were secure. The feeling of safety had been hollowed out, replaced with a low hum of anxiety. His strategies, meant to keep him safe, were instead the very things convincing him he was in constant danger. He started avoiding the fire station's gym, telling his crew he was nursing a bad knee. The truth was that the sensation of a pounding heart and ragged breath-the very feelings of exertion and life-had become indistinguishable from the memory of clawing his way up that collapsed stairwell, gasping for air. By avoiding exercise, he denied his body any chance to relearn that a racing pulse could be benign, cementing the idea that any internal arousal was a herald of doom.

The most insidious change was his relationship with happiness. One evening, Chloe shared some good news about her job, her face lit with a joy he hadn't seen in months. He felt a flicker of warmth in his own chest, a genuine moment of shared happiness, but it was immediately followed by a cold wave of panic. Letting his guard down felt like an invitation for another blind-sided attack. He pushed the positive emotion away, his expression going blank. He saw the light in Chloe's eyes dim, replaced by a familiar look of hurt confusion. This emotional suppression kept him locked in a state of hypervigilance, which he interpreted as necessary readiness. His attempts to control his world were shrinking it, and the person he loved most was now on the outside of the fortress he had built. He came to therapy after his captain gave him a formal warning about his performance in training drills, threatening to move him to a desk job. The thought of losing the one identity he had left, of no longer being a firefighter, was more terrifying than any threat he could imagine. He sat in the consulting room, a man trapped inside his own alarm system, quietly asking if it was possible to ever feel safe again.
