Ethan was twenty-five when he deployed overseas with an aid organization. He had wanted to make a difference, to be useful. That ended on a Tuesday afternoon in a field clinic on the outskirts of a city he now struggles to name out loud. A ceasefire had been agreed upon, and the clinic was busier than usual. Ethan was helping to triage a steady stream of civilians when the shelling started again, closer than anything he had ever experienced. He remembers the building shaking, dust filling his throat, and the sudden, piercing sound of children screaming and crying. A mother had thrust an infant into his arms just before a blast wave knocked him sideways. He hit the ground hard, the baby pinned between his chest and the packed dirt. In the silence that followed, all he could hear was his own heart hammering in his ears, a frantic, trapped animal trying to escape his ribcage. He was certain he was going to die. He remembers the rough hands of a colleague grabbing his vest, yanking him upwards without warning and shouting in his face, pulling him from the rubble. The memory of that touch-unprepared, forceful, life-saving-is burned into his skin more vividly than almost anything that has happened since.

Four years later, Ethan works as a jeweler in a quiet, well-lit shop in a Midwestern mall. He tells himself he chose the work because it requires precision, patience, and a steady hand-everything that his internal world is not. He sets up his bench in the far corner of the workshop, his back pressed firmly against the solid drywall. From here, he can see the door, the security gate, and every customer who walks into the showroom. He has come to believe that this constant scanning is just being careful. He watches people's hands, noticing bulges in jacket pockets that could conceal a weapon, or the way a man holds his phone, as if he might be holding something else entirely. What frightens him most is how a customer's sudden, gentle touch on his shoulder to ask for a ring sizing can send electricity through his entire nervous system, leaving him breathless and rigid for several seconds before he can compose his face.

The symptoms arrived not as clear memories, but as ambushes. A child crying in the mall's food court does not trigger a thought of the clinic; it triggers a full-body plunge into that dust-choked silence, his chest tightening, his palms slicking with sweat before he even knows why. Sometimes, at night, he wakes up drenched, his heart detonating in his chest, feeling the pure, unadulterated terror of impending death, but with no image attached. He lies there, trembling, unable to connect the feeling to any reason at all. The memories themselves have a strange, unnerving quality-they feel more real than the ordinary moments of his day. He can recall the precise sensation of grit under his fingernails with more clarity than what he had for breakfast this morning.

To manage this constant state of alarm, Ethan has developed several rules that he follows rigidly. He forces himself to think only positive thoughts. I am safe now. I survived. It could have been so much worse. He repeats this to himself like a mantra against the chaos in his head. He reminds himself that other people endured far worse. But these reframings feel hollow, a thin veneer over a deep well of dread that refuses to drain. They do not stop the scanning, and they do not stop the double-checking of the shop's locks. His colleagues tell him the mall is patrolled and secure, that their little shop is safe, but he finds it impossible to believe them. Their reassurances sound like a foreign language that he has forgotten how to speak.

The consequences have been accumulating quietly. He has become distant from his friends, who find him jumpy and unreachable. He no longer dates, terrified of the vulnerability of someone touching him in the dark, or seeing his face when a memory strikes. At work, a customer recently became frustrated with his slow, deliberate hesitation before handing her a ring to try on-a moment he spent scanning her open purse. His boss took him aside afterward, asking if he was okay. It was this conversation that made Ethan realize that his strategies were beginning to show.

He is here, now, in this office, because he is exhausted. He is hoping to find a way to make the memories feel less real than his actual life, and to stop the constant, grinding state of alert. But what he fears most is that if he stops checking, if he stops forcing the positivity, the terror he felt in that field clinic will swallow him whole. He is beginning to suspect that the very strategies he built to save him are the things keeping him trapped inside that moment. He wants to know if there is a way out.
