Kareem is a 56-year-old Lebanese American man who works as a social media manager for a mid-sized retail brand. He is married and lives with his wife in a suburb outside Detroit. He presented for an initial consultation reporting significant distress that had persisted for over a year following an encounter with police outside a convenience store near his workplace.

The incident occurred on a weekday afternoon when Kareem had stepped out during his lunch break. Officers responding to a reported disturbance in the area stopped him without warning, and within seconds the interaction escalated. He was forced to the ground, a knee pressed into his back, his face against the concrete. He remembered the metallic taste of blood where his lip split against the pavement, the specific texture of the sidewalk against his cheek, and the sound of his own voice - thin and strange - saying that he had done nothing wrong. The officers were eventually called away to the actual disturbance nearby, and Kareem was released without charges or explanation. He drove back to the office. He told no one what had happened.

In the weeks that followed, the memory returned with a physical completeness that unsettled him deeply. The taste of blood would arrive without warning - during a meeting, while eating dinner with his wife - and with it came a wave of rage so total that he would have to leave the room. He had come to believe that if he allowed himself to fully revisit what happened, something in him would break open irreparably, and so he had developed a habit of cutting the memory short, redirecting his attention before it could reach its conclusion. The memory remained fragmented as a result, neither resolved nor distant.

His work as a social media manager placed him in daily contact with exactly the kind of content he had come to fear most. Monitoring platforms meant encountering footage of police encounters, protest coverage, and news threads about use of force - all of it arriving without warning in his feed. He had quietly restructured his workflow to assign that category of content to a junior colleague, offering a professional rationale he knew was thin. He had also stopped watching television with his wife in the evenings, citing tiredness, and had not read a newspaper in months. What had once been a shared ritual - the two of them watching a drama series after dinner - had quietly disappeared from their life together. His wife had stopped suggesting it.

Kareem was unable to relax even at home, which he himself described as bewildering. He knew, in an abstract sense, that his living room was safe. He knew his neighbourhood was quiet. But he had come to believe, at a level that felt more fundamental than reasoning, that the world was a dangerous place in ways that were not visible until they were immediate. He had begun identifying exits in every room he entered - restaurants, his office, the waiting room of his dentist - before he could settle into a chair. He scanned faces in public spaces with a vigilance that exhausted him. He was certain that people were not what they seemed, that authority and normalcy were surfaces over something less predictable and more violent.

What disturbed him most privately was the conviction that he had violated something essential in himself during the encounter - that in lying still, in not resisting, in driving back to work and saying nothing, he had acted in ways that went against who he was. He had not been able to articulate this to his wife. He had not been able to articulate it to anyone.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at a company offsite when he stood in a hotel conference room and could not identify a second exit. The anxiety that rose in him was so acute that he had to step into a corridor and stand against the wall for several minutes. A colleague had noticed. Kareem had offered an excuse about low blood sugar, but driving home that evening he understood, with unusual clarity, that he could not continue managing this alone. He came to the first session hoping, above everything else, to be able to sit in a room without planning how to leave it.
