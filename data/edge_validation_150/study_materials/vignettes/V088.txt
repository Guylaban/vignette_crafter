He is a 56-year-old Middle Eastern man who comes across in session as composed and careful, the kind of person who has learned to monitor himself closely before he speaks. He is married, works as a social media manager, and is used to spending his days tracking reactions, anticipating what will land badly, and smoothing conflicts before they spread. That same alertness now seems to organize much of his life, though it has a different weight than the professional vigilance he once saw as a strength.

The event that changed things happened during a protest in his city. He had gone out that day with a sense of civic responsibility, wanting to stand with others and witness what was happening. He describes the air as tense even before the first sirens, the crowd shifting, people calling to one another, the sudden charge of fear moving through the street. He remembers seeing police advance and, in the confusion, trying to step back and make himself small. He is clear that he was not behaving aggressively, that he had his hands visible, that he believed compliance would protect him. Instead, he was struck, shoved to the ground, and hit again while he tried to cover his head. What stays with him is not only the pain but the humiliation of it, the shock of realizing that the uniformed people he had assumed would restrain themselves were the source of danger. He says that in those moments he felt himself leave his body, hearing fragments of shouting and the sound of boots, then the hard pressure of fear in his chest as he wondered whether he would be arrested, injured badly, or disappear into something he could not control.

Afterward, he returns home changed in a way that initially feels hard to name. He is physically intact but begins moving through the days as though he is still waiting for the next blow. At home he startles at loud voices from the street. A car door slamming can make him freeze. He finds himself scanning sidewalks, flashing lights, and uniformed figures whenever he leaves the apartment. In the weeks that follow, he stops walking certain routes and avoids public gatherings entirely. Even ordinary errands become strategic: he notes exits, watches who is behind him, and chooses times when the streets are quieter. He says he is not afraid in the simple sense; he is braced. The distinction matters to him, because fear sounds temporary, while bracing sounds permanent.

His work as a social media manager becomes both refuge and trap. He has always lived online to some extent, reading public response, shaping language, thinking several steps ahead of an audience. After the assault, the screen is harder to tolerate. Videos of protests, police interactions, and even unrelated clips of shouting or pushing can send his body into a surge of heat and panic. At the same time, he cannot fully step away from the internet, because his job requires him to stay current, respond quickly, and manage public tone. He describes staring at comment threads too long, reading hostile remarks repeatedly, as if he can somehow master the threat by tracking it more carefully. Instead he ends the day exhausted, irritable, and ashamed of how often his attention drifts. He makes more mistakes than before, rereads messages several times, and worries that colleagues can see how unsettled he is.

At home, his wife notices that he is present but distant. He does not talk much about what happened, partly because he does not want to burden her and partly because the memory feels too hot to handle once spoken aloud. He becomes less affectionate and more easily startled by sudden touch. Sleep is broken by vivid dreams in which he is back on the pavement, unable to get his arms under him while voices close in. Some nights he wakes already tense, with his heart racing and his jaw clenched, as if his body has been standing guard while he sleeps. He often lies awake replaying the scene, searching for the moment when he might have done something different. That self-questioning turns into anger, then into a kind of grief he has trouble naming. He mourns not only the injury to his body but the loss of his earlier confidence in public life, the belief that he could move through the city as a citizen rather than a target.

He is aware of how much this has narrowed him. He says he still goes to work, still answers emails, still jokes with his wife at dinner, but there is a constant background noise of caution. He feels older than he did before the assault, as though something fundamental in him has been taught to expect harm. Even so, he keeps trying to function, telling himself that if he can remain organized, useful, and calm on the outside, perhaps the fear will stay contained. What emerges in session is a man who has survived an act of violence by becoming more watchful than he ever intended, and who now moves through his days with that vigilance folded tightly into everything he does.
