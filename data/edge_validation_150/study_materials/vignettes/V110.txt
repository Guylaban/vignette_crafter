Amina sits at the end of the assembly line, her gloved hands moving with mechanical precision as she screws caps onto plastic bottles. The factory hums around her-conveyor belts grinding, air compressors hissing, supervisors barking orders in a language she understands but does not trust. She has worked here for three years, ever since the resettlement agency placed her in this city. The job is monotonous, but that is what she needs. Monotony means nothing unexpected. Monotony means safety.

But safety is a fragile concept for Amina. She does not remember exactly when the guards came for her-it was during the second year of the civil war, when the military targeted anyone with a university education or a political opinion. She was a community health worker then, not involved in politics, but her brother had been seen at a protest. That was enough. They came at night, dragged her from her home while her husband watched. She was thirty-six years old. She has not lived a single day since then without the memory of those seven weeks.

The torture was systematic. They used electricity on her feet and made her listen to recordings of her children screaming-though she later learned the children were never taken. The interrogator, a man named Colonel Tshibangu, would whisper to her in French, "You are nothing but meat now." She learned to separate her mind from her body, to float above the pain. That dissociation still visits her at work. Sometimes the factory lights flicker, and she is no longer at the production line-she is back in that room, smelling diesel and blood. Her hands go cold. The screw caps fall from her fingers.

Her supervisor, a middle-aged man named Dave, has noticed. He thinks she is slow or lazy. He has written her up twice for "lack of focus." Amina cannot explain that when a co-worker drops a metal tray, the clang sounds exactly like the guards closing the cell door. She cannot explain that she counts the exits in every room, that she has memorized the security guard's patrol pattern, that she never sits with her back to the door. She cannot explain that she vomited last week in the bathroom because the floor cleaner smelled like the disinfectant they used on her wounds.

Divorce came three years ago. Her husband could not understand why she flinched when he touched her, why she never wanted to talk about the past, why she cried silently every night. He said she was broken. She agreed. She has not dated since. The only intimacy she tolerates is with her youngest daughter, now seventeen, who lives with her in a small apartment. Amina watches her daughter sleep sometimes, making sure she is still breathing.

At night, the triggers follow her home. A car backfire on the street throws her to the floor, heart hammering, before she remembers it is not gunfire. She avoids public transport because crowded buses feel like being trapped. She has not been to a market in two years-too many people, too many voices, too much potential for ambush. The only place she feels slightly at ease is the factory, because the noise is predictable and the routine never changes.

But even there, the hypervigilance never stops. Her neck aches from constantly turning her head. She has memorized the fire exits and the locations of the heavy tools she could use as weapons. She does not sleep more than four hours a night. When she closes her eyes, she sees the Colonel's face. She hears his laugh.

Today, a new machine was installed on the line. It makes a rhythmic thumping sound-thump-thump-thump-like someone walking toward her in boots. Amina grips her screwdriver so hard her fingers go white. She forces herself to breathe. In. Out. In. Out. She tells herself she is safe. But safety, like trust, like sleep, like life before the cell, is something she no longer fully believes in.
