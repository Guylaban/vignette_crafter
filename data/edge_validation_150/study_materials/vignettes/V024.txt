Farah traces the beginning of the end of her old life to the scent of oud. It was a heavy, sweet, and woody fragrance worn by the local dignitary she was assigned to translate for at a checkpoint outside Fallujah. She remembers the oppressive heat, the dust kicked up by passing vehicles, and the easy smile of the young corporal standing beside her, a girl from Ohio barely twenty years old. Farah's memory of the moments that followed is not a story but a collection of broken shards. There is the scent of the oud, a sudden, concussive roar that felt like the world tearing in half, and then a searing white light. The next piece she can grasp is the taste of grit in her mouth and a high-pitched ringing in her ears. The space where the dignitary and the young corporal had been standing was simply gone. She has tried, over the years, to force the pieces together, to fill the gaps between the sound and the silence, but her mind refuses. It feels, to her, like watching a film with crucial frames missing; she knows the beginning and the devastating end, but the sequence of how one became the other is a void.

Now, fifteen years later, that void governs her life. As an auditor, Farah's job requires her to visit corporate clients, a task that has become an exercise in managed terror. Last month, walking through the cavernous, glass-walled lobby of a downtown firm, she felt a familiar panic rise in her throat. The wide, open space felt like a kill zone, exposing her from every angle. She found herself automatically mapping the exits, her eyes darting to security guards, her entire body tensed for a threat only she could perceive. She has come to believe she only feels safe in her small, second-floor apartment, the one place with no open sightlines from the street. Her world has shrunk to the path between this sanctuary and her own office, and even that short walk is fraught. She constantly glances over her shoulder, certain she is being followed, her heart hammering against her ribs if footsteps behind her quicken their pace.

The triggers are unpredictable, ambushing her in the most mundane moments. A car backfiring on the street below her window can leave her crouched on the floor, shaking, for minutes. The scent of a colleague's perfume, if it shares a note with that dignitary's oud, can hijack her senses entirely, plunging her back into the dust and the ringing silence. The mornings are often the worst. Nightmares, vivid and suffocating, leave a residue that clings to her long after she wakes. She'll sit on the edge of her bed, the phantom smell of smoke in her nostrils, her body drenched in a cold sweat, convinced the danger is not a memory but a premonition. She sees this as a profound betrayal. Her body, she feels, is her enemy, constantly sounding an alarm for a war that ended long ago, trapping her in a state of high alert she cannot switch off.

This constant vigilance has cost her dearly. Her ex-husband called it living with a ghost. Her manager has gently noted her declining performance and her strange reluctance to take on clients in newer, open-plan office buildings. In her quiet moments, Farah is consumed by a loop of relentless questions. If she had been more observant, would she have seen the threat? If she had positioned herself differently, could she have shielded the young corporal? She has settled on a devastating conclusion: the event happened because of the kind of person she is-not sharp enough, not brave enough. This belief has calcified into a core of self-loathing. It was after a particularly bad panic attack at a client's office, triggered by a fire drill's alarm, that she finally sought help. She walked into the clinic not with hope, but with the quiet desperation of someone who can no longer bear to live inside her own skin, terrified that she is broken beyond repair.
