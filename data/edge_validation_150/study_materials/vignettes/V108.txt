Caleb is a 26-year-old detective, married, who presented for psychological evaluation following a prolonged period of physical abuse perpetrated by his former romantic partner prior to his current marriage. He scored a 72 on the PCL-5, indicating severe posttraumatic symptomatology. The abuse had unfolded across nearly two years and was characterised by escalating violence - his partner would drink heavily before attacks, and the smell of alcohol on her breath had become, for Caleb, the sensory herald of what was coming. She had targeted his body methodically, leaving bruises and lacerations that he had learned to conceal beneath long sleeves at work. The sounds of her voice during those episodes - a particular low, controlled tone she used just before striking him - had burned themselves into his memory in ways he could not yet fully articulate when he first sat down with the clinician.

In the months after leaving that relationship and entering his current marriage, Caleb found that the world had not simply reset. The memories of the abuse were vivid and detailed in a way that felt almost hallucinatory - he could recall the specific quality of light in the apartment, the texture of the floor beneath him, and most disturbingly, he could hear her voice in his head with a clarity that made him doubt, momentarily, that he was alone. These memories were not retrieved voluntarily; they forced themselves into his awareness without permission, arriving most violently when something in his environment carried a trace of the past. The smell of alcohol - encountered regularly when Caleb interviews suspects or processes scenes involving intoxicated individuals - would hit him and the memory would open like a wound, auditory and visual and visceral all at once. Seeing injuries on others, which as a detective he encounters with grim regularity at crime scenes, carried the same detonating quality. Even nausea, arising from stress or fatigue, was enough to pull him back - his body had learned to read its own distress as a signal of danger, and the memory would arrive uninvited. These triggers did not merely surface the past; they confirmed something darker. Each intrusion reinforced his growing conviction that people are not what they seem, that the person who had claimed to love him had hidden cruelty beneath familiarity, and that this kind of concealment was not exceptional but structural to human relationships. He had lost his faith - not in any formal religious sense, but in the basic legibility of other people and in the idea that ordinary life could be safe. He felt, increasingly, like a permanent outsider, someone who had been inducted into a knowledge that others did not carry and that separated him irrevocably from them.

These appraisals, once settled, fed back into the memory itself. Believing that people are fundamentally unknowable made the past feel not like an aberration but like a revelation, and the memories returned with the added weight of that meaning - more frequent, more confirming, more difficult to metabolise. In response, Caleb had developed a set of strategies that had accumulated quietly over months. He had stopped allowing himself to relax, reasoning - with a logic that made complete sense to him - that the moments he had been most hurt were moments when he had been unguarded. He numbed his emotional responses with a deliberateness he had come to think of as professional composure. He avoided being touched, which had created a visible and painful distance from his wife, who had begun to ask questions he did not know how to answer. These strategies, however, had begun to generate their own costs. The hypervigilance required to never fully relax meant his body remained in a state of chronic arousal, and that arousal itself - the nausea it produced, the hypersensitivity to sensory input - became a trigger in its own right, pulling the memory forward again. The numbing and the withdrawal, meanwhile, deepened his sense of being an outsider, confirming to him that he was no longer capable of ordinary connection, which only hardened his distrust and his loss of faith.

What finally brought Caleb to seek help was a moment at work. He had been interviewing a domestic violence survivor and found himself unable to continue - not because of professional inadequacy, but because he had begun to shake. His wife had found him that evening sitting in the car in the driveway for forty minutes, unable to come inside. He told the clinician he was most afraid that nothing would ever feel safe again - and most hoping, quietly, that he was wrong.
