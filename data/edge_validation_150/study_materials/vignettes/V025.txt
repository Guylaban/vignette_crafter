The accident happened two years ago, on a Friday afternoon in late October. James, then twenty-eight, was finishing a kitchen renovation in a suburban split-level when his foreman, a man named Rick who had taught him how to set a miter saw, stepped backward into the path of a circular blade. James saw it happen in a single, soundless instant-the way the saw kicked, the way Rick's arm opened, the blood that sprayed across the fresh white subfloor. He remembers the taste of the air afterward: copper and sawdust and the metallic tang of his own spit, a taste he cannot shake no matter how many times he brushes his teeth. He remembers the way Rick's fingers looked, splayed and wrong, and the wet sound of the ambulance doors closing. He has never been able to put any of it into words. When his wife asks what happened, he opens his mouth and nothing comes out. The memory lives in his body, not in language.

In the weeks that followed, James began to notice that certain things would bring the whole scene back without any warning. A customer would reach across him to point at a blueprint, and suddenly he would be back on that subfloor, the copper taste flooding his mouth, the image of Rick's arm sharp and real across every sense at once. He learned to brace himself before anyone touched him, but it never helped. The more he tried to prepare, the more the memories seemed to ambush him. He started to believe that his life had been destroyed by what he saw-that he had permanently changed for the worse and could not rely on himself anymore. These thoughts made him more sensitive to the world around him. He began scanning every job site for signs of injury, for bandages or blood or the way a coworker's hand might look wrong. Every visible wound he saw became a fresh trigger, and every trigger confirmed what he already believed: that he was broken, that the damage was inside him now and would never heal.

James started to avoid thinking about the event altogether. He would catch himself about to replay it in his mind and would deliberately blank out, trying to leave his body or dissolve the thought before it could take hold. This worked for a few minutes, sometimes an hour, but the effort exhausted him and left him feeling hollow, which only deepened his sense that he had permanently changed for the worse. He also began to eat excessively at night-pizza, bags of chips, anything that might fill the hollow feeling and quiet the images. He would eat until he was numb, and then he would feel disgusted with himself, which confirmed again that he could not rely on himself, that his life was destroyed.

At work, he started to avoid certain jobs. He told his boss he could not do bathroom remodels anymore because of the dust, but really it was the way a bathroom's tight space reminded him of the corner where Rick had fallen. He stopped accepting jobs in medical offices altogether; the sight of a bandage on a patient's arm could send him reeling. His wife noticed he was pulling away. She would reach for his hand in the car, and he would flinch before he could stop himself, and then he would see the hurt on her face and feel the copper taste rise again, and he would dissociate until they got home. He knew she was confused, maybe angry, but he could not explain. The words were not there.

Last month, a new apprentice on a framing crew cut his palm on a nail gun. James saw the blood and immediately tasted copper, saw Rick's fingers, felt the full sensory reliving pull him under. He dropped his hammer and walked off the site without a word. That night, he told his wife he needed help. What frightens him most is the idea that he will never get back to who he was-that the man he used to be is already gone, and that every trigger, every memory, every meal he eats to quiet the images is just another step further from him. He is hoping therapy can give him the words he has never been able to find.
