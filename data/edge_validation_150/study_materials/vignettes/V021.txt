John, a 71-year-old male of Oceania ethnicity, has been living with the aftermath of witnessing a traumatic event that occurred several months ago. John works as a cashier at a local grocery store and is married to his wife, Mary. On that fateful day, John was working his usual shift when he witnessed an armed robbery unfold right in front of him. The thief, who barged into the store with a gun, demanded cash from the register while brandishing his weapon at both customers and employees alike. Despite his efforts to remain calm and comply, the intensity of the situation left deep psychological scars that have only intensified over time. The trauma has left John with a variety of triggers that cause him significant distress. He is particularly sensitive to sounds associated with babies crying or doors slamming open. These auditory triggers remind him of the sudden, startling movements he witnessed during the robbery and evoke feelings of discomfort and fear. As a cashier, his work environment often exposes him to these triggers. The sound of customers opening and closing freezer doors can sometimes bring back memories of the gun being drawn that day. This has led John to develop a habit of avoiding areas where there might be more noise or movement, which in turn affects his ability to perform certain tasks at work. John's hypervigilance and threat monitoring are evident in his constant state of alertness, especially when he is out in public places. He watches others closely for any signs that they might harm him. In the grocery store where he works, this has meant paying close attention not just to customers but also to other employees. The unexpected movement from a colleague reaching for something on a high shelf can cause John to react as if danger were imminent. This heightened state of awareness makes it difficult for him to relax and enjoy his work. The memory of the traumatic event itself is fragmented, with gaps that leave John feeling like he only remembers parts of what happened. When he tries to recount the incident, either to Mary or even just in his own mind, he often loses track of the details and cannot piece together a coherent narrative. This sense of incompleteness exacerbates his distress because it makes it difficult for him to process and integrate the experience fully. John's negative appraisals reflect deep-seated beliefs that have developed since the event. He feels emotionally numb, describing himself as feeling "dead inside." The trauma has led him to question the reliability of institutions meant to protect him, such as law enforcement and security measures in public spaces. John also believes that no one responsible for the incident has faced any consequences, which adds another layer of frustration and anger to his emotional state. Over time, these symptoms have taken a toll on both John's work performance and his relationship with Mary. He finds it increasingly difficult to concentrate at work due to his hypervigilance and avoidance behaviors. His tendency to withdraw from social interactions has strained his marriage, as he struggles to engage in conversations or activities that might trigger uncomfortable memories. What finally prompted John to seek help was a particularly distressing incident where the sound of a baby crying outside the store triggered an intense panic attack. This episode left him feeling overwhelmed and desperate for relief from his symptoms. In therapy, John hopes to regain some sense of emotional connection with Mary and find ways to cope more effectively with the triggers that have been disrupting his life. John is most afraid of being unable to manage his reactions in public spaces, particularly at work. He longs for a return to normalcy but also harbors hope that through therapy he can start to feel less numb and regain some control over his responses to triggers.
