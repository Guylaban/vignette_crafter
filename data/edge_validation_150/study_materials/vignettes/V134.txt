She is 47 and works in social ministry, a role that has always mattered to her because it lets her stay close to people who are struggling. Before the trauma, she describes herself as organized, warm, and reliable, someone who could move from one household visit to the next, carrying forms, food bags, and a calm presence. She is married, and for many years her home has felt like a place of routine and shared responsibility. That sense of steadiness changes after she is raped.

The assault occurs unexpectedly, and what stays with her is not only the violence itself but the suddenness of it, the way the world narrows in an instant. In the weeks afterward, she feels as if she is moving through her days with part of herself missing. She remembers fragments with painful clarity: a smell, a sound, the pressure of panic in her chest. Other parts feel blurred or unreal, as if her mind refuses to hold them in full view. She starts to measure time differently, separating life into before and after.

She continues working because the people she serves still need her, but going out alone becomes an effort she hides from others. On the bus, she scans faces and exits. When she enters unfamiliar buildings for ministry visits, she notes whether the door locks, whether there is a second way out, whether anyone is standing too close. She tells herself she is being careful, yet her body remains tense from the moment she wakes. The smallest unexpected sound can send a sharp wave through her, and she notices that she has begun to flinch at footsteps behind her.

At home, sleep stops feeling restorative. She falls asleep only after a long period of checking the locks, the windows, and the hallway light, and even then she wakes abruptly, sweating, with her heart racing. Some nights she wakes from dreams that are not complete memories but carry the same terror, leaving her disoriented and fearful. In the dark, her mind starts replaying what happened, not in a straight line but in repeated flashes that invade her without permission. She tries to push them away by focusing on her breathing or counting slowly, but they return, often when she is least prepared, such as while washing dishes or folding laundry.

She begins to avoid anything that feels connected to that night. Certain streets are no longer part of her route. She turns down invitations to evening gatherings. She stops wearing some clothes because they make her feel exposed, and she becomes uneasy when people stand too close, even in crowded church halls where she once moved easily among others. Intimacy with her husband becomes complicated. She wants closeness, but her body reacts with fear, and this creates shame she has trouble naming. She often feels detached during moments that should feel comforting, as if she is there and not there at the same time.

Her mood changes in ways that are hard for her to explain to people who know her as giving and composed. She feels irritable and on edge, and she notices herself snapping over small things, then crying afterward in private. She feels guilty for becoming harder to live with. At times she blames herself for not having prevented the assault, even though she knows, rationally, that the responsibility belongs entirely to the person who hurt her. Still, the self-blame returns, especially when she is tired. She also feels less able to trust her own judgment. Decisions that used to come naturally now take extra effort, as though she is always second-guessing whether she is safe.

At work, she continues to help families in crisis, but she does so with more strain than others can see. She can still sit with a grieving mother or listen to someone describe hunger, eviction, or illness, yet afterward she feels drained in a way that is different from ordinary fatigue. The suffering of others now reaches her more sharply, as if her own wound has left her less protected. She sometimes steps into a restroom during the workday to steady herself, pressing both hands against the sink until the panic eases.

Over time, she becomes more aware of how much effort it takes to appear okay. She smiles when expected, prays when she can, and keeps showing up, but inside she remains alert for danger. Even in safe settings, she feels unable to fully relax. The trauma has altered not only her sense of safety but also her relationship to her own body, her home, her work, and the future she once assumed would unfold in familiar ways.
