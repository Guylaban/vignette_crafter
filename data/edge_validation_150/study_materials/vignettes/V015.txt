Adaeze Okonkwo is a 77-year-old Nigerian-American woman who has spent the better part of four decades in public service, rising through local government to hold a seat on her state's legislative assembly. She is known in her district as composed, formidable, and precise - qualities that have served her well in chambers where she has often been the only woman, and frequently the only Black woman, in the room. She came to therapy quietly, scheduling her intake appointment through a private line, and she sat for several minutes before speaking. What she finally said was that she was tired of being afraid in her own home.

The abuse began approximately two years ago, during what Adaeze describes as a period of prolonged deterioration in her marriage of forty-one years. Her husband, whose own health had been declining, became increasingly volatile. On the evening that brought her to the present moment, he struck her repeatedly in their kitchen - an argument that had begun at the dinner table escalating without warning. She remembers the sound of her own breath leaving her body. She remembers the tile floor. She remembers blood - her blood - on the white cabinet door beside the sink, and the particular quality of the silence that followed when he stopped. She called no one that night. She cleaned the kitchen herself.

In the weeks that followed, Adaeze continued to appear at committee hearings and press briefings. She wore concealer. She answered questions. But the kitchen had become a place she moved through quickly, and the cabinet door had been repainted before the month was out. Blood, encountered anywhere - a paper cut during a legislative session, a news photograph, a scene in a television programme her aide had left playing - produced in her an immediate, wordless recoil that she had not been able to explain or contain. During a floor session some months after the incident, a colleague raised his voice in debate - not at her, not even near her, simply across the chamber - and she felt her hands go cold. She had come to understand that anger, even when directed nowhere near her, carried a charge she could not reason away. The proximity of other people in enclosed spaces had become its own difficulty; she found herself calculating exits in meeting rooms she had walked into for years without a second thought.

At home, night had taken on a different texture entirely. She was certain, in a way she could not fully articulate, that the hours between midnight and dawn were simply less safe than daylight, and she lay awake in the bed she still shared - out of what she called practicality - monitoring sounds. She startled at the house settling. She had begun, without consciously deciding to, checking over her shoulder when she walked down hallways, including the hallways of the statehouse where she had worked for over a decade.

What troubled her perhaps most deeply was what was happening to her memory of that night. She knew something was missing from it - whole stretches that her mind seemed to have misplaced - and when she tried to reach toward those gaps, she found herself turning away before she arrived. The memories she did access came accompanied by guilt so acute it felt like a physical weight, a guilt she could not entirely account for and could not put down. She had come to believe that she had been permanently altered by what happened - that the woman who had walked into that kitchen was not the woman who walked out of it, and that the distance between those two versions of herself was not closeable. She was certain she had lost access to something essential in herself, some capacity for ordinary feeling that had simply gone. And she trusted almost no one with any of this, a fact that had quietly hollowed out relationships she had maintained for decades.

What finally brought her to seek help was a vote she nearly missed - not because of scheduling, but because she had sat in her car in the statehouse parking garage for forty minutes, unable to account for the time. She came to therapy hoping, she said, to be functional. What she was most afraid of was that functional was the most she had left to hope for.
