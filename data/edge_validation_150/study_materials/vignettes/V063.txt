Mateo describes his mind as a program running a constant, frantic diagnostic. Before the collapse, his job as a QA engineer was a source of pride. He found satisfaction in stress-testing systems, in finding the single point of failure in a million lines of code before it could cause a problem. He liked order, predictability, and the elegant logic of a well-designed system. His life, at 22, mirrored that. He had a great job right out of college and a new apartment on the 14th floor of the Champlain Towers South, a concrete symbol of his ascent.

He remembers the sound first. It wasn't an explosion, but a deep, guttural groan from the bones of the building, a sound he had never heard and yet recognized instantly as fundamentally wrong. It was followed by a sharp crack, like a cosmic thunderclap directly overhead. He had been on his couch, scrolling through his phone. The floor beneath him didn't just shake; it dropped. He speaks of a single, terrifying second of weightlessness before the violent chaos began. The world became a roaring avalanche of concrete and dust. He recalls the sensation of being tossed inside a metal drum filled with rocks, the darkness absolute and suffocating.

When he came to, he was pinned by a piece of drywall, his leg twisted at an unnatural angle. The air was thick with a chalky dust that clogged his throat and burned his eyes. For hours, the only sounds were the groans of stressed metal, the distant, muffled cries of others, and the frantic beating of his own heart. He was one of the lucky ones, pulled from the rubble with only a fractured tibia and deep lacerations.

In the two years since, the physical wounds have healed, but the internal diagnostic never stops running. His world is now a system full of critical bugs, and he is the only one who seems to see them. When he walks into a room, he doesn't see the decor; he scans the ceiling for stress fractures. He finds himself unconsciously pressing his hand against support columns in parking garages, testing for stability. He lives on the ground floor now, in a small apartment complex built in the 1980s, a fact he researched extensively. He needs to know he can get out. He hasn't been in an elevator since the collapse.

His work, once a source of comfort, has become a source of torment. The logic he once admired now feels like a cruel joke. He'll be staring at a screen, testing an application's resilience, and suddenly he's not seeing code, but a blueprint of his old building. The low hum of the office server becomes the groan of failing concrete. He has to get up and walk to the bathroom, splashing cold water on his face until the image of twisting rebar subsides. He tells his colleagues he has migraines.

Sleep offers little escape. He often jolts awake, heart pounding, convinced the bed is shaking. He lies in the dark for hours, listening for any sound that is out of place, his body braced for an impact that never comes. The dust is the worst sensory memory; he sometimes feels its phantom grit in the back of his throat, a dry, suffocating reminder.

He's tried to date, but it feels impossible. How do you explain to someone that you can't go to a restaurant on the third floor, or that the sound of a passing garbage truck makes you flinch so hard you spill your drink? He feels an immense distance from his peers, whose biggest concerns seem to be career moves and weekend plans. They live in a world that feels solid and dependable. Mateo's world is provisional, temporary, and liable to break apart without warning. He survived the failure of one system, and now he can't shake the terrifying certainty that everything else will eventually fail, too.
