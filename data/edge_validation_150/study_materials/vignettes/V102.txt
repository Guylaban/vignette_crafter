The flood came at night, while Derek was asleep in his first-floor apartment in the small Missouri town where he'd grown up. He woke to water already lapping at the edge of his mattress, the sound of it rushing under the door, the terrible groan of the building shifting. He scrambled onto his dresser, then through the window into the black water, grabbing a tree branch that kept him from being swept away. For hours he clung there, listening to people scream, watching cars and debris surge past in the dark. He was certain he would die. When rescue came at dawn, he was hypothermic and shaking, but alive. What stayed with him, though, was not just the terror of that night-it was the way the world had turned, in an instant, into something that wanted to kill him.

In the months afterward, Derek tried to go back to his life. He worked as a salesperson at a regional auto dealership, a job that required him to drive to client meetings across three counties. But he found himself avoiding routes that passed near rivers, creeks, or even large drainage ditches. Rainy days became unbearable. The first time a spring thunderstorm rolled in, he pulled his car over on the highway, hands locked on the wheel, heart hammering, certain he could hear water rising even though the road was dry. He had come to believe that any stretch of low ground, any patch of woods that looked like the ones that had lined the flooded streets, could trigger the same helplessness. What frightened him most was that he didn't always see the flood in his mind when it happened. Sometimes he would just feel the terror-the cold, the panic, the certainty of drowning-without any image at all, as though his body remembered what his mind could not.

There were parts of that night he could not retrieve no matter how hard he tried. He knew he had been in the water for hours, but the sequence was gone. He remembered the tree branch, the screams, the gray light of dawn-but there were gaps, whole stretches of time that felt erased. And when he did remember, the terror came back undiminished, as fresh as if he were still clinging to that branch. These memories, or the absence of them, made him feel like a stranger to himself. He used to be someone who could handle things, who knew who he was. Now he felt hollow, untethered, as though the person he had been had drowned in that flood and left only this anxious, watchful shell behind. Nothing felt meaningful anymore. His job was just motion. His relationships, never deep, had become almost nonexistent. He had stopped returning calls from friends because he couldn't explain why he spent his evenings pacing his new second-floor apartment, checking the locks, listening for the sound of water.

The world had become a landscape of threats. Every low-lying road, every overcast sky, every moment of vulnerability felt like the beginning of another disaster. He woke at night to check whether he was safe, though he knew logically that his apartment was on a hill. He was hypervigilant during the day, scanning the horizon for storm clouds, monitoring the ground beneath his feet for any hint of dampness. This constant watchfulness only deepened his belief that he could not rely on other people-they hadn't been there in the water, they didn't understand the danger that still lurked everywhere, and they couldn't protect him. He had learned that safety was something he had to enforce himself, and he was failing.

The cycle tightened over the course of a year. A trigger-a drive past a creek swollen by rain-would bring the memory rushing back, and with it the appalling realization that he was not the person he used to be. That sense of lost identity would sharpen his vigilance, and his vigilance would confirm that the world was unsafe, which reinforced his feeling that nothing mattered anymore. And then the vigilance itself would lead him to notice more triggers: a patch of moss on a curb, the smell of wet earth, the sound of a storm drain gurgling. Each new trigger pulled him back into the memory, and the memory reminded him of who he had become. He had stopped going on sales calls that required crossing bridges. His manager had noticed. His commissions had dropped. He was running out of excuses.

What finally brought Derek to seek help was not the fear, but the exhaustion. He was tired of being on guard, tired of the way his own mind ambushed him with feelings he couldn't name, tired of the hollow, meaningless days. He sat in the therapist's office with his hands clasped, and said, quietly, that he just wanted to feel like himself again-though he wasn't sure that person even existed anymore.
