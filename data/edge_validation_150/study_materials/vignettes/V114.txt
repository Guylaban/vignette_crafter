Omar, a 27-year-old married physical therapist from a Middle Eastern family, had been assaulted by a man who cornered him after a late shift, close enough that the stranger's expensive cologne clung to his clothes and the room afterward seemed to hold the smell in its air. In the days that followed, Omar found that the scent of a specific perfume in a shop aisle or on a patient's jacket could hit him with the force of the memory itself. What made it worse was the meaning he attached to those moments: he would suddenly feel ashamed of what had happened to his body, and the shame quickly hardened into the fear that he would never be able to feel normal emotions again, or trust himself to know what was safe. At times he tried to push the whole thing away by pretending it had happened to someone else, as if it belonged to another man entirely, but that distancing left him feeling even less steady in his own skin and more certain that he could not rely on himself. The same happened with sexual situations. Intimacy with his wife, which had once felt reassuring, now brought back the conviction that his body had been marked in a way he could not undo, and the closeness itself became one of the moments that made the assault feel vividly present again. Even pain had become a messenger of threat for him; a sore shoulder after work or a twinge in his back could send him scanning his body for signs of injury or danger, and each search seemed to confirm how vulnerable he believed he had become.

As those appraisals deepened, Omar became increasingly jumpy and on guard. A sudden touch on the arm from his wife in the kitchen, or an unexpected movement beside him in the clinic, could make him flinch as if he were in immediate danger, and the smallest noise in the hallway could leave him braced and tense. He began to interpret these reactions as further proof that something was fundamentally wrong with him, and that belief fed the alarm he felt in ordinary moments. The more ashamed and untrusting he felt, the more he noticed every scent, every sensation, every sign of intimacy as a possible warning. Cologne in the elevator at work, physical discomfort during a session, or the prospect of sex at home would pull his attention straight back to his body and to the assault, and he would immediately start checking himself for tension, pain, or that awful sense that he was about to lose control. He learned to cope by avoiding thinking about what had happened, but the effort to keep it out of mind never really worked; when the memories pressed in, he would detach and tell himself it had happened to someone else, only to find that the dissociation left him feeling more exposed and more easily startled when someone moved too quickly near him. In practice, the strategies meant to protect him also kept his alarm system switched on. He monitored his body so constantly that he was always waiting for the next sign of danger, and that vigilance made him even more reactive to touch, movement, and the smell of perfume, each of which had become a cue that something terrible might be about to happen again.

Over the following months, Omar's life narrowed around these reactions. At work as a physical therapist, he found himself tense when patients shifted unexpectedly on the table or reached out without warning, and he often had to mask the jolt that ran through him. He started keeping more distance from close physical contact and from anything that reminded him of the assault, especially situations involving intimacy at home. He finally sought help after his wife told him that he seemed to be disappearing from their life, present in the room but far away, and after he realized that his constant scanning and startle responses were leaving him exhausted and afraid of his own body. What he most feared was that he would stay ashamed, emotionally numb, and unable to trust himself; what he most hoped for was to feel safe in his body again without having to keep checking it every minute.
