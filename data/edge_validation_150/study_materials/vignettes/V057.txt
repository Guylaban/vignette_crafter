Fatima is a 38-year-old married woman of Jordanian descent who works as a housekeeper at a mid-sized hotel in the outer suburbs of Phoenix. She presented for psychological assessment reporting a PCL-5 score of 61, following an assault that occurred approximately fourteen months ago and from which she describes herself as never having fully recovered.

On a Tuesday afternoon shift, Fatima was cleaning a room on the fourth floor when a male guest entered the room behind her and locked the door. What followed was a prolonged physical assault during which he restrained her arms and forced her to remain in the room despite her attempts to leave. She recalls with terrible precision the sound of his voice rising - the way his shouting filled the small space and seemed to leave no air for her - and the sensation of her wrists being held against her will, her body made to stay where she did not choose to stay. Other parts of that hour are gone entirely. She cannot account for stretches of time that she knows passed, and the gaps themselves disturb her, as though the missing pieces contain something she is not yet able to hold. She has described the memory as a series of photographs with whole pages torn out between them, and she has come to feel that she is only ever working with a partial account of what happened to her - that the full truth of it exists somewhere she cannot access.

In the months that followed, Fatima returned to work twice before stopping altogether. The hotel corridors, the sound of a room door closing behind her, the sight of a housekeeping cart - each of these had become intolerable. More specifically, any moment in which someone else's instruction or presence constrained what she could do with her body would produce an immediate and overwhelming response. Her supervisor, a well-meaning woman, once asked her firmly to finish a room before her break, and Fatima had to leave the building. She understood, on some level, that this was a reasonable workplace request. What she could not explain, even to herself, was that being required to stay somewhere she had not chosen to stay no longer felt like a scheduling matter. She has not returned to hotel work since, and the loss of income has placed significant strain on her marriage, with her husband taking on additional shifts and the two of them speaking less than they once did.

At home, Fatima moves through her days in a state of sustained alertness. She listens for the sound of the front door, for footsteps in the hallway, for any indication that someone might be entering the house without her full awareness. She positions herself in rooms so that she can see the entrance. When a neighbor's television carries raised voices through the shared wall, she goes rigid. Shouting - even the muffled, ordinary kind - lands in her body before her mind can contextualize it. On several occasions the memory has returned with a physical dimension she had not anticipated: a tightening across her wrists, a pressure in her shoulders, sensations she recognizes as belonging to that afternoon even when nothing in her present environment has changed. Concentration has become difficult in a way that compounds her distress; she begins tasks and loses them, reads a sentence and finds it gone.

She has avoided the news stories covering a subsequent assault at the same hotel chain. She cannot say exactly what she fears she will feel if she sees coverage of another victim, only that the thought of it produces something close to dread.

What finally brought Fatima to seek help was her youngest daughter's school play last spring, at which another parent raised their voice during a parking dispute outside the auditorium. Fatima spent the performance in the lobby, unable to go back inside. She told her husband afterward that she did not recognize herself anymore. She arrived at her first session hoping, above all, to feel safe in her own home - and afraid, most deeply, that the parts of the memory she cannot reach are the parts that would finally explain why she is not.
