Leena, a 38-year-old stockbroker of South Eastern Asian heritage, found her carefully constructed world shattered when a former client, whose obsession had escalated over months, finally broke into her apartment. She had been home with her partner that evening, an ordinary Tuesday night that quickly spiraled into terror. The man, reeking of alcohol, had smashed the front door in with a deafening crash, his shouts echoing through their home as he threatened her, her partner, and the future she envisioned. He had destroyed cherished items, his rage amplified by the alcohol, leaving behind a chilling sense of violation and vulnerability. The police had eventually intervened, and he was arrested, but the echoes of that night remained, profoundly altering Leena's perception of safety and reality.

Now, months later, Leena lives with a pervasive sense of unease. When a loud noise erupts unexpectedly - a car backfiring on the street below her office, a sudden shout from a colleague on the bustling trading floor - her body seizes. Her heart hammers against her ribs, her breath catches, and for a terrifying moment, she is back in her apartment, the sound of the door splintering and the man's furious voice consuming her. It feels as though the event is happening right now, the original terror flooding her system with an intensity that leaves her breathless and disoriented. Even when she attempts to recount the story to her partner, or simply reflects on it herself, the sheer horror of it brings back the same visceral fear she felt at the time, making it almost impossible to process. Sometimes, she finds herself questioning if it even truly happened, the extremity of the event making it feel surreal, like a nightmare she can't quite believe was real.

Her professional life, once a source of pride and control, has become a minefield. The trading floor, a place where quick decisions and clear thinking are paramount, is a constant assault on her senses. The cacophony of ringing phones, shouting brokers, and the relentless click of keyboards often triggers her, each sharp sound a potential reminder of the home invasion. She has started requesting to work from a quieter back office more frequently, ostensibly for focus, but in reality, it's an attempt to escape the unpredictable loud noises that plunge her into a state of panic. This shift has subtly impacted her visibility and her ability to engage directly in the fast-paced environment, leading to a noticeable dip in her deal flow.

Beyond the office, triggers lurk everywhere. The smell of alcohol, even a faint whiff from a passing stranger or a colleague celebrating after work, sends a jolt of alarm through her. It's the same cloying scent that had permeated her apartment that night, signaling danger and aggression. She meticulously avoids bars and restaurants where alcohol is prominent, often making excuses to her partner to stay home or choose different venues. What frightens her most, however, are the unexpected pangs she feels when she sees what she perceives as "happy families" - a young couple pushing a stroller in the park, a colleague sharing stories of their children's achievements, or even an advertisement for a family holiday. These moments, once innocuous, now evoke a profound sense of loss and vulnerability, reminding her of the future that felt so threatened and fragile after the attack, a future she now struggles to envision with the same unburdened joy.

Over the past few months, Leena's avoidance behaviors have become ingrained habits. She takes circuitous routes home to avoid certain streets, checks her locks multiple times before bed, and has become withdrawn from social gatherings, particularly those involving her partner's extended family. Her partner, though supportive, has expressed concern about her increasing quietness and her reluctance to discuss their future plans, which once brought her so much joy. The cumulative toll of these internal battles and external adjustments finally pushed Leena to seek help. She arrived at her first therapy session exhausted, admitting she was tired of feeling like she was constantly on high alert, and deeply afraid that the life she once knew, the person she once was, might be irretrievably lost.
