Minh, a 22-year-old UX designer of Vietnamese American background, walked into the clinic with his shoulders hunched forward, one hand pressing lightly against the side of his neck. He had asked to come in later in the day because mornings were unpredictable-he never knew when the feeling would hit. During the initial sessions, he spoke in a quiet, careful voice, as if testing whether the room was safe. He had not always been this way. Two years earlier, Minh had been living in a ground-floor apartment in a coastal city when a slow-moving hurricane stalled overhead, pushing floodwaters through his neighborhood faster than anyone had anticipated.

He remembered the way the water felt at first-ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then chest-deep-and the metallic, muddy taste that filled his mouth as he struggled to keep his head above the rising current. That taste, he said, would sometimes return without warning. He would be sitting at his desk working on a wireframe, and suddenly his mouth would fill with the sensation of brackish water, and he would gag. At other times, a wave of pure terror would wash over him with no image attached-just the feeling of drowning, of being crushed, of not being able to breathe. Those episodes left him shaking and confused, because his mind presented no story, only the raw panic. The memory of the event itself felt like something that had happened to someone else. Minh would describe the hours he spent clinging to a collapsed shelf, his neck twisted at an awkward angle as he tried to stay afloat, but then he would stop and say, "It doesn't feel real. I can't believe that actually happened to me."

The physical aftermath of that prolonged strain was a persistent tightness in his neck. Over time, the neck pain became a trigger. Whenever the ache returned-when he slept wrong or sat too long at his computer-his mind would jump back to the hurricane, and he would feel physically ill again, nauseated and dizzy, as if the flood were still around him. He began to avoid anything connected to the disaster: news reports, weather alerts, even conversations about storms. But the neck pain was inescapable. It turned into a daily reminder that his body had not forgotten.

What changed Minh more than the memories, however, was what he came to believe about other people. During the flooding, he had seen neighbors push past him without helping, and later, in the shelter, he overheard people exaggerating their losses to get more aid. He started to feel that people were not what they seemed-that friendliness was a mask for self-interest. In the months that followed, he withdrew from his small circle of friends. He stopped responding to texts and turned down invitations. He felt painfully separate from others, certain that no one could understand what he had been through, and that he no longer belonged anywhere-not in his old life, not among his family who had not been there, not in the city he still lived in but now distrusted.

Minh's work as a UX designer, which had once been a source of pride, became a minefield. His office was open-plan, and the sound of a colleague coughing or dropping a cup could send him into a state of hypervigilance. He started arriving late, leaving early, and eventually requested to work remotely full-time. His manager expressed concern, but Minh could not explain. He felt isolated in a way that was hard to articulate: he was surrounded by people who talked about deadlines and design systems, while he was stuck in a loop of physical pain and disbelief.

What finally brought him to seek help was a series of sleepless nights where the emotion-without-image episodes intensified, and his neck pain became a constant companion. He was afraid that he was losing his grip on his career and his sense of self. He hoped, he said, to find a way to stop feeling like a stranger in his own life. But part of him was terrified that talking about it would make the memories more real-and that was a possibility he could not face alone.
