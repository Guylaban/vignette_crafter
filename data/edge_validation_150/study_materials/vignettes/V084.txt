Frank, a 47-year-old bus driver, had spent two decades navigating the city's streets with a calm stoicism. That changed six months ago. During a routine traffic stop for a supposed broken taillight, he was pulled from his personal car, mistaken for a suspect in a nearby robbery. The encounter was brief but brutal, ending with him pinned to the pavement, the officer's knee on his back, before the mistake was realized. There was no apology, just a gruff "you're free to go." The physical bruises faded, but the incident left an imprint that reshaped his world.

Initially, it was just the jumpiness. A siren would wail in the distance, and Frank's hands would tighten on the large steering wheel of his bus. Soon, the sight of a police cruiser parked by the side of the road was enough to trigger a hot surge of anger that made it difficult to concentrate on the traffic ahead. This constant state of threat began to bleed into his perception of everything. Living at that pitch of alertness, his mind automatically scanned for and snagged on any potential sign of authority, turning his daily bus route into a landscape dense with reminders of his vulnerability. After months of simmering irritability and hypervigilance, Frank came to the conclusion that this must be what the world was really like: a hostile place where danger was always lurking. This raw, ever-present sense of threat solidified his belief that people, in general, simply can't be trusted. This new conviction that the world was hostile primed him to see threats everywhere, meaning the previously routine sight of a police car was no longer just part of the city's backdrop, but a specific and immediate warning.

This new conviction governed his life. Convinced that the world was a dangerous place, he began checking the doors and locks at his apartment three, four, sometimes five times before he could even think about sleeping. His belief that people were untrustworthy transformed his bus from a moving community into an isolated capsule. He stopped making small talk with the regulars on his route and started taking his breaks alone in the depot, avoiding the easy camaraderie of the staff room. The more he isolated himself, the more he felt the truth of his own conclusion: he had lost his connection to his community, a ghost driving the city streets. This profound sense of disconnection only served to amplify his vigilance; his avoidance of others made him feel more exposed when he had to interact, ratcheting up his baseline sense of threat.

The cycle has now become self-sustaining and vicious. A sudden, intrusive memory of the officer's face flashing in his mind will cause him to instinctively raise a hand to his cheek, as if to shield himself from a blow that isn't coming. This action, a fleeting attempt to hide his face, deepens his shame and sense of alienation. The constant, exhausting feeling of being on high alert drives him to blast the television the moment he gets home, a wall of sensory distraction to keep the silence-and the thoughts-at bay. In the rare moments of quiet, perhaps after turning down the TV to check the locks again, the intrusive memories seem to rush in to fill the void. His conviction that people are untrustworthy also seems to prime him, making even a brief mention of law enforcement on the radio feel like a direct, personal threat.

Last week, his supervisor called him in. There had been complaints: an angry outburst at a passenger who asked for directions, and a report of him driving erratically after passing a police checkpoint. He was given a formal warning and a recommendation to seek support. Frank sat in the office, unable to explain that the world felt like a minefield, that his concentration shattered every time he saw a uniform, that he felt utterly alone in his fear. He came to therapy not with hope, but with the quiet desperation of a man afraid of losing the last piece of structure in his life: his job. He doesn't know if he can ever feel safe again, but he is terrified of what will happen if he doesn't try.
