Eleanor, at seventy-eight, had always prided herself on her sharp intellect and unwavering dedication as a public defender, a career she had passionately pursued for over fifty years. Her days were still filled with complex legal battles and the intricate lives of her clients, a world that demanded her full attention. But in the quiet moments, and increasingly, even in the bustling courthouse, her world had become a landscape of apprehension.

It began over a year ago, on a Tuesday evening when Eleanor was one of the last to leave her downtown office. She had been engrossed in a particularly challenging appellate brief, the kind that demanded late nights. As she walked through the nearly empty parking garage, her mind still replaying legal arguments, a sudden, jarring force from behind shoved her against the concrete wall. Before she could fully process what was happening, a hand snatched her purse, and the figure vanished into the shadows as quickly as they had appeared. Physically, she sustained only a few bruises, but the shock of the unexpected assault, the feeling of being utterly helpless and unaware until it was too late, left an indelible mark.

In the weeks and months that followed, Eleanor found herself increasingly on edge. What frightened her most was the memory of the attacker appearing without warning, a silent, unseen threat. This experience began to reshape her daily movements. Now, whenever Eleanor walked down a corridor, whether in the courthouse or the grocery store, she would frequently look behind her, a quick, almost involuntary glance over her shoulder, just to check if someone was following, if the space behind her was truly empty. It was a habit born of a deep-seated conviction that danger could always emerge from the unseen, a constant, low thrum of vigilance.

This heightened awareness extended to the people around her. In courtrooms, during client interviews, or even simply waiting for a coffee, Eleanor found herself watching other people closely. Her gaze would linger on their hands, their expressions, their movements, searching for any subtle sign that they might pose a threat, a flicker of aggression or an unexpected gesture that could escalate. This intense scrutiny, while offering a fleeting sense of control, was exhausting, often leaving her drained even before her workday truly began. The world had become a place where potential threats lurked in plain sight, and her job was to identify them before they could act.

The assault had also left her with a profoundly altered nervous system. The slightest unexpected noise or sudden movement would send a jolt through her. A door slamming unexpectedly, a book falling from a shelf in her study, or even her husband placing a hand on her shoulder without warning, would cause her to jump, her heart pounding in her chest. She was always feeling jumpy or easily startled, a constant state of readiness for a threat that rarely materialized but always felt imminent. These reactions, sometimes accompanied by a sharp intake of breath, were embarrassing, particularly in professional settings, and she often found herself apologizing for her overreactions.

Her husband, Arthur, had noticed the changes. Their evening walks, once a cherished routine, were now often cut short because Eleanor found herself too anxious about the dimly lit paths, constantly scanning their surroundings. At work, her intense vigilance, while protective, sometimes made it difficult to fully immerse herself in her cases. Her concentration would waver, pulled by the perceived need to monitor her surroundings, leading to longer hours and an accumulating sense of fatigue. She had always been meticulous, but now, the energy she used to dedicate entirely to her clients was split, diverted to the constant task of self-preservation.

It was after a particularly jarring incident in the courthouse, when a bailiff accidentally dropped a heavy file, sending Eleanor flinching visibly and nearly crying out, that she decided she could no longer manage alone. The persistent fear, the constant scanning, and the relentless jumpiness were not just inconveniences; they were slowly eroding her ability to live her life fully and with the peace she deserved. She sought help, hoping to find a way to reclaim the sense of security that had been so violently taken from her, and to once again navigate the world without the constant, exhausting burden of impending dread.
