Priya was sixty-six, a widow for nearly a decade, and had worked the evening shift at a big-box retail store for the last eight years. Three years ago, a young male coworker cornered her in the stockroom as she was packing up returns. He had been smoking in the back alley moments before, and the smell of cheap tobacco clung to his clothes. When he grabbed her, she saw his face in the fluorescent light, then a blank-a skip in time-and then the image of a metal shelf pressing into her back while he bore down. She did not scream. Afterward, she walked home, showered, and told no one because she was certain no one would believe a sixty-three-year-old grandmother over a twenty-four-year-old. The police were never called. The man was transferred to another location, and she never saw him again, but what remained was a set of photographs in her mind, frozen moments: his hand on her wrist, the pattern of the linoleum, the orange glow of the exit sign above the door.

In the weeks that followed, Priya noticed that the snapshots did not fade. She could bring up each picture at will-the exact angle of his chin, the smoke smell-but other parts, like whether she had said anything, were gone completely. She began to flinch when male customers leaned too close to the counter. That was the first trigger: the physical presence of a man in her personal space. Then came the smells. A whiff of cigarette smoke from a car in the parking lot would stop her mid-sentence, and the memory-the picture of his face through the haze-would flood back with such force that she would have to grip the counter until her knuckles turned white. And then the moral violation: a customer yelling at a cashier, a supervisor demeaning a stock boy-any display of someone mistreating another person would ignite a thought that burned through her: This is what happens. People misbehave. No one stops them. Each trigger fed directly into a loop: the memory arrived, and with it came the appraisal that nothing in her life had meaning anymore, that she could not handle even the smallest upset, that what had happened was deeply unfair and no one had ever been held accountable. She would stand behind the register, trembling, convinced that she was a fragile shell who could break at any moment.

That belief made her hypervigilant. She started scanning for triggers before they could surprise her. She would check the gas station across the street for smoke before stepping out of the store. She would note the nearest male coworker and calculate how many steps to the break room door. The negative appraisals themselves-this is unfair, I cannot cope-sharpened her attention to every potential violation around her, so that a teenager's loud joke became proof of a world without justice, and a forgotten cigarette butt on the pavement felt like a direct attack. At the same time, the memory of the assault-the photographic clarity of each moment-made those triggers feel inevitable. Whenever she recalled the smell, her brain seemed to register every trace of smoke in her environment as a present danger, as though the memory itself were actively updating her threat radar. She began to avoid being alone with any male colleague, taking longer routes through the store, skipping her usual coffee break if she saw a man in the break room.

Her supervisor noticed she was pulling away from teamwork. Priya stopped attending the monthly employee appreciation lunches because the room was always full of men. Her daughter called less often, weary of Priya's short answers. The loneliness deepened the belief that nothing mattered, which in turn made her even more sensitive to every small injustice she witnessed at work. By the time she walked into the clinic, she had lost eight pounds and was sleeping with the bedroom light on. She said she was tired of feeling like a photograph that could not be put away, and that she wanted to believe the world could still hold meaning. But she was certain, as she folded her hands in her lap, that no one could ever truly understand what had been taken from her.
