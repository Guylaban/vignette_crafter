Sanjay, a 47-year-old park ranger of South Eastern Asian descent, presented with a pervasive sense of dread and an inability to shake off the past, nearly five years after his young son miraculously recovered from a life-threatening illness. The memory of his son, then just four years old, suddenly developing a dangerously high fever and seizures that led to an emergency hospitalization, remained vivid and relentless. He recalled the terrifying rush to the emergency room, the sterile, antiseptic smell of the hospital corridors that now seemed permanently etched into his olfactory memory, and the overwhelming fear as he watched his small child hooked up to machines, often appearing dizzy and disoriented. What haunted Sanjay most was the agonizing period in the pediatric intensive care unit, where he became certain that a particular doctor, Dr. Aris, had delayed a critical diagnosis, nearly costing his son his life.

In the years that followed, Sanjay found himself increasingly trapped in a cycle of rumination. He constantly replayed the events of that week, dwelling on what he perceived as Dr. Aris's negligence and the systemic failures that put his son at risk. This intense focus on what others had done to him, and the burning desire to get even, consumed his waking hours. He would spend hours meticulously reconstructing scenarios, imagining how he could expose Dr. Aris, how he could seek justice for the suffering inflicted upon his family. This relentless dwelling on revenge and other-blame rumination kept his mind in a constant state of agitation and vigilance. This constant mental replaying of perceived wrongs sharpened his awareness, making him acutely sensitive to any sensory input or bodily sensation that might remotely connect to the traumatic event. By repeatedly revisiting the sensory details of the hospital and his son's physical symptoms in his mind, Sanjay inadvertently primed himself to interpret any similar sensations or smells in his present environment as immediate and potent signals of renewed threat.

It was this pervasive mental state, this continuous replay of injustice and anger, that made Sanjay exquisitely sensitive to certain triggers in his daily life. His mind, primed by the constant replay of perceived wrongs, was always on high alert for anything that might signal a recurrence of danger or a reminder of the trauma. For instance, while out on patrol in the expansive park, if he experienced even a slight feeling of dizziness or lightheadedness, his heart would race, and a cold sweat would break out. This somatic sensation, amplified by his ruminative state, would immediately transport him back to the terrifying vulnerability of his son's illness, making him fear he was losing control, just as he had felt then. Similarly, the faint, lingering smell of hand sanitizer from a visitor or the antiseptic scent of cleaning products used in the ranger station would instantly trigger a wave of intense fear and anger, vividly recalling the hospital and fueling his conviction that he was still surrounded by potential threats or reminders of past harm. Even the subtle scent of alcohol, perhaps from a cleaning solution or an accidental spill, would immediately evoke the memory of the hospital, tightening the knot of anxiety in his stomach and reinforcing his belief that the world was still a dangerous place, filled with reminders of the past injustice.

Sanjay's preoccupation with the past and his heightened reactivity began to take a toll. He found himself avoiding interactions with colleagues or family members if there was any chance the conversation might drift towards his son's illness or other medical topics, fearing being reminded of the event by others would disrupt his fragile composure. He grew quiet, withdrawn, and increasingly isolated, often choosing to work alone on remote trails to minimize contact. His wife, worried by his emotional distance and frequent outbursts of anger, gently urged him to seek help. Sanjay finally came to therapy, exhausted by the relentless mental battle, admitting that what frightened him most was the possibility that he would never truly escape the shadow of that week, and that his anger would eventually consume him entirely.
