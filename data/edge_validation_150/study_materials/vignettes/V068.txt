Chukwu's Vigil

Chukwu Okafor had been a bomb disposal technician for thirty-four years, and for most of that time, he had felt invincible. The precision, the protocol, the calm focus required to approach an unknown device-these had defined him. His wife Amara had learned not to ask him about the details; she knew only that he came home whole, and that was enough. But on a Tuesday in March, during a routine call to a chemical plant in Newark, something went wrong. The device had been rigged with a secondary charge, and when it detonated, Chukwu was close enough to catch the full force of the blast and the toxic cloud that followed. He remembered the burning in his lungs, the acrid taste, the moment he understood his body was being poisoned from the inside. The hospital stay lasted six weeks. The physical injuries healed. The chemical exposure, the doctors said, would require monitoring but had not caused permanent organ damage. Yet something had shifted in Chukwu that no scan could measure.

The workplace itself had become dangerous in his mind now. He returned to the bomb disposal unit three months later, cleared by occupational health, but the moment he entered the facility-the fluorescent hum, the metallic smell of the equipment room, the sudden bark of a colleague's radio-his body would flood with adrenaline. He had come to believe that the workplace was where catastrophe lived, and his mind would not let him forget it. Loud noises, particularly the sharp crack of equipment being moved or doors slamming, would send him into a state of high alert; each sound felt like the precursor to another detonation. The smell of hospital antiseptic, which clung to his clothes after his mandatory annual physical, would trigger the same cascade of fear and dread that the chemical exposure had. These triggers-the workplace itself, sudden loud sounds, anything that smelled of hospitals-had become so potent that Chukwu found himself constantly scanning his surroundings for signs of danger whenever he encountered them. His eyes would dart to exits, his shoulders would tense, his breathing would shallow. He was always looking for the next threat, the next way out.

To manage the panic, Chukwu had developed a pattern of avoidance that had slowly consumed his life. He could not bear to be alone with his thoughts, so he began staying at the facility longer and longer, volunteering for extra shifts, arriving early and leaving late. Work became both the source of his fear and his refuge from the silence of his own mind. When he was at home, he would find tasks-repainting the garage, reorganizing the shed, anything to keep moving. Amara would find him in the basement at two in the morning, sanding wood that had already been sanded. He avoided hospitals entirely now, refusing even routine check-ups, and when Amara had suggested they visit her sister who was recovering from surgery, Chukwu had invented an excuse rather than face the antiseptic corridors and the memory of his own vulnerability. He also could not tolerate being alone in a closed room; if Amara left the house, he would follow her from room to room, or he would leave entirely, driving aimlessly until she returned. His avoidance of these situations-the solitude, the medical spaces-had begun to feed his sense of threat. The more he avoided, the more dangerous these situations seemed. Staying late at work meant he was exhausted and hypervigilant; the hypervigilance meant he could not relax even during those long hours, so he would push himself harder, volunteering for more shifts, deepening the cycle. He had become someone who could not sit still, could not be alone, could not trust his own safety.

The consequences had rippled outward in ways Chukwu had not anticipated. His performance reviews, once exemplary, had begun to slip; supervisors noted that he was making small errors in his paperwork, that he seemed distracted and tense. Amara had stopped inviting friends over because Chukwu's constant vigilance-the way he would startle violently if someone knocked on the door, the way he would position himself to face the entrance of every room-made visitors uncomfortable. When his nephew had placed a hand on his shoulder in greeting, Chukwu had flinched so sharply that the boy had stepped back, hurt and confused. Now, at sixty-two, Chukwu sat in the therapist's office, his jaw clenched, his eyes still scanning the room for exits. He had finally come because Amara had given him an ultimatum: get help or lose her. What terrified him most was not the memory of the blast itself, but the certainty that his body would betray him again, that the danger was still present, waiting in the next loud noise, the next hospital smell, the next moment of silence. He was hoping-though he could barely admit it-that someone could teach him how to stop running.
