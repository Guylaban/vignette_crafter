Marcus

Marcus was fifty-four when his partner of eight years, David, began hitting him. The violence had escalated over months-first shoves during arguments about money, then fists during what Marcus had thought were private moments, locked away from the world in their apartment in Portland. One evening in March, after Marcus had mentioned wanting to spend a weekend with friends, David had cornered him in the bedroom. What followed was a blur of impact and helplessness: fists against his ribs, his own voice pleading, the specific taste of blood in his mouth-metallic, warm, unmistakable-and the overwhelming realization that he could not stop what was happening. His body was not his own. His will meant nothing. When David finally stopped and left the room, Marcus lay on the floor tasting that blood for what felt like hours, though it was probably minutes, understanding with absolute clarity that he had lost something fundamental: the belief that his own body was safe, or that his choices mattered when someone else decided otherwise.

He had reported the assault to police, and David had been arrested. But the trauma had not ended with the legal intervention. In the weeks that followed, Marcus found himself unable to be alone. The silence of an empty room became unbearable-not because of sadness, but because of what filled that silence. When he tried to deliberately think through what had happened, to make sense of it for the police report or for himself, the memory seemed to slip away, fragmentary and resistant. Yet when the memory came unbidden-triggered by a sound, a smell, a particular angle of light-it arrived with photographic clarity, as vivid as if he were watching it happen again on a screen behind his eyes. Worse, he could taste the blood. Not metaphorically. When the intrusive memory surfaced, the taste was there, coating his mouth, and he would find himself running to the bathroom to rinse his mouth out, knowing even as he did it that there was nothing physical to wash away.

The fear of being alone had become the organizing principle of his life. He stopped going home to his apartment after work. Instead, he would drive directly to the homes of colleagues from his civil service office, or to bars, or to his sister's house-anywhere there were people. His supervisor had begun to notice that Marcus was arriving late to work, distracted, sometimes having clearly not slept. He had taken to staying at the office late into the evening, finding reasons to linger, to chat with anyone still there, because the moment he left the building and headed home, panic would rise in his chest. The thought of sleeping alone in an unfamiliar place-a hotel, a friend's guest room-was worse still. He had tried it once and had spent the night awake, every sound a potential threat, his body rigid with the certainty that he had no control over what might happen next.

Being alone with another man triggered something primal in him. Even in professional settings-a one-on-one meeting with a male colleague, or standing in an elevator with a man he didn't know-Marcus felt his body tense, his breathing shallow. He had come to understand, though he had not articulated it clearly to anyone, that his body had learned a lesson: men could take what they wanted. Control was an illusion. And so he kept himself constantly surrounded by people, a human shield against the possibility of being trapped again. His relationship with his current partner, Elena, had become strained by his refusal to sleep beside her, his need to leave gatherings early to find crowds, his inability to simply be present in quiet moments.

It was Elena who had finally insisted he seek help. She had found him in the kitchen at three in the morning, having abandoned their bed again, and had asked him plainly: "Are you going to let what he did destroy us too?" The question had landed differently than he expected. He had come to therapy not because he believed he could recover-he was not certain what recovery would even look like-but because he could no longer bear the taste of blood, or the weight of constant vigilance, or the slow erosion of the one relationship that still mattered to him.
