James

James had been driving the number 42 crosstown route for nineteen years when Officer Patricia Dominguez pulled him over on a Tuesday afternoon in March. The stop itself was routine-a broken taillight-but what happened in the four minutes that followed would reshape how he moved through the world. Officer Dominguez approached his window, and James, a Black man in his mid-forties with no criminal history, rolled it down and kept his hands visible on the steering wheel, a habit born of a lifetime of caution. When she asked for his license, he reached slowly toward the glove compartment. She shouted a command he didn't fully process. His hand was still moving. She drew her weapon, and in the chaos that followed-her voice, his confusion, the weapon's presence-she fired once. The bullet entered his right shoulder. James remembered the sound more than the pain, remembered the way his vision tunneled, remembered thinking he was going to die on the side of Maple Street with his hands up.

He survived. The shooting was investigated, deemed a tragic mistake, and Officer Dominguez was placed on administrative leave. The city offered a settlement. But James had been fundamentally altered. In the weeks after his discharge from the hospital, he returned to driving the 42, telling himself he could manage it. Within two weeks, he could not. The sight of a police car in his mirror sent his heart into his throat. The sound of a siren made him grip the wheel so hard his knuckles blanched. He began taking side streets, adding forty minutes to his route to avoid the precinct on Fifth Avenue. His supervisor noticed the inconsistency. His passengers noticed the irritability-the way he snapped at someone who asked him a simple question about the next stop, the way his jaw clenched when someone's bag bumped against him.

By June, James had begun covering his face with his hands whenever the intrusive memory arrived unbidden-and it arrived often, sometimes multiple times a day. A backfire from a car would trigger it. A raised voice on the street. The memory would crash through his consciousness with such vividness that he could smell the gun oil, could feel the impact, and his immediate response was to hide his face as though the memory itself might see him. He started wearing sunglasses on the bus, even on overcast days, a barrier between himself and the world. He stopped going to the community center where he had played basketball three nights a week for fifteen years. The thought of being around people-of being watched, of being vulnerable-became intolerable. He told himself it was temporary, that he would go back once things settled, but weeks became months and the absence became permanent.

At home, James found himself checking the locks on his windows and doors repeatedly, sometimes five or six times after he had already locked them. He knew it was excessive, but the compulsion felt necessary. The world had shown him that safety was an illusion, that authority could turn on you in an instant, that nowhere was truly secure. He had come to believe that people could not be trusted-not police, not strangers, not even the neighbors he had known for years. He had lost something essential, some thread that connected him to the community he had lived in his whole life. That loss manifested as a grinding loneliness and a profound alienation from the city itself.

To manage the noise in his head, James began playing loud music constantly-rap, rock, anything that would drown out his thoughts. His apartment became a fortress of sound, a sensory cocoon that kept the intrusive memories at bay while he was inside it. But the moment he stepped outside, the vulnerability returned. His concentration at work deteriorated. He missed turns. He struggled to remember passenger requests. His dispatcher had begun documenting his errors.

It was his sister, Keisha, who finally insisted he seek help. She had not seen him laugh in eight months. She had watched him withdraw from the family, from his nieces' birthday parties, from the church he had attended since childhood. When she confronted him in September, James broke down-not from sadness, but from a rage he could no longer contain. He raged at the system, at the unfairness, at his own helplessness. Keisha held firm and gave him a therapist's number. James called it because he was afraid of what he might become if he did not, and because somewhere beneath the mistrust and the hypervigilance, he wanted his life back.
