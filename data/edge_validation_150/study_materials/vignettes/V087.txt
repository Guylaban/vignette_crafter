Amir is a 27-year-old physical therapist who came to therapy reluctantly, at his wife's urging, after months of what he called "not being himself." He is a tall, broad-shouldered man with a carefully neutral expression, but his hands tremble slightly as he sits down, and he chooses the chair farthest from the door. He speaks in a low, deliberate voice, often pausing to swallow before continuing.

The event that changed everything happened two years ago, on a Friday evening after a long shift at the outpatient clinic where he worked at the time. A male colleague, someone Amir had trusted and considered a friend, asked him to stay late to "review a case." When they were alone in the treatment room, the colleague stepped close, and Amir caught the sharp, woody scent of a new cologne-something expensive, something unfamiliar. Before Amir could move away, the colleague's hand was on his shoulder, then sliding down his back. Amir froze. He did not scream. He did not push back. He stood there, his body rigid, as the colleague's hands moved lower, as the cologne filled his nostrils, as a dull, grinding pain spread through his lower back-an old injury flaring under the pressure of being bent over the treatment table. When it was over, the colleague straightened his lab coat, smiled, and said, "I think we understand each other now." Amir walked out of the clinic in silence, drove home in silence, and never told anyone what happened. He quit the job two weeks later, citing "personal reasons."

In the months that followed, Amir began to notice changes he could not explain. He found himself scanning every room he entered, cataloging exits, noting the positions of men who stood too close. The startle response was the first thing he could not hide. A hand on his shoulder at a grocery store made him flinch so violently that the other person stepped back, apologizing. His wife, reaching for his arm in bed, would find him already tensed, already pulling away, his heart hammering before his mind could catch up. When she asked what was wrong, he said he was just "tired." But he was not tired-he was vigilant, constantly, exhaustingly vigilant. He began to monitor his own body for signs of threat: a twinge in his lower back sent a spike of alarm through him, and he would press his hand against the spot, checking, rechecking, as if the pain itself were a predator. He developed a habit of wearing loose, thick clothing, even in summer, because the sensation of fabric brushing his skin was easier to tolerate than the thought of someone's hand finding bare flesh.

At work, in his new job at a different clinic, Amir was a competent and well-liked therapist. But he found himself avoiding certain patients-those who wore strong cologne or perfume. He would rearrange his schedule, trade cases with colleagues, invent reasons why he could not treat a particular person. When a patient mentioned a sexual topic, even in passing, Amir's mind would go blank, and he would nod along while feeling as though he were watching himself from a great distance. He had learned to do this: to pretend the event had happened to someone else, to imagine a stranger's body on that treatment table while his own floated somewhere above. It was the only way he could get through a day without collapsing.

The shame was the heaviest part. He could not shake the feeling that his body had betrayed him-that the pain in his back, the way he had frozen, the fact that he had not fought back-all of it meant something about who he was. He began to believe that he could no longer trust himself to handle even ordinary situations. If he could not protect himself from a colleague in a well-lit room, how could he protect himself from anything? He told his wife, in a rare moment of honesty, that he felt like a stranger in his own skin, that he had lost access to the normal emotions he used to take for granted-joy, anger, even sadness. Everything felt flat, distant, like watching a movie of his own life.

What finally brought Amir to therapy was an incident three weeks ago. His wife, trying to be playful, snuck up behind him in the kitchen and wrapped her arms around his waist. He spun around, shoved her away, and shouted before he could stop himself. She stumbled against the counter, not hurt, but her face crumpled with a hurt and confusion that Amir could not bear to see. He slept on the couch that night, and the next night, and the night after that. He is here now because he is terrified that he will lose the only person who still believes in him. He does not know if he can ever feel safe again, but he is willing to try-if only to stop the look in her eyes from haunting him.
