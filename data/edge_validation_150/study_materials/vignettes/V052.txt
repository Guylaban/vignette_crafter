Mrs. Amina Johnson is a 77-year-old African woman who has been married for over five decades. She served as a respected politician in her community, known for her strong advocacy and leadership skills. Her life took a dramatic turn when she experienced severe physical abuse at the hands of her romantic partner. Since that traumatic event, Amina has struggled with a range of symptoms that have significantly impacted her daily functioning and personal relationships. The trauma began one evening during an argument with her husband, where he physically assaulted her in their home. The sight of blood and the overwhelming sense of vulnerability were seared into her memory. Now, any sign of anger, even when expressed calmly by others, triggers a wave of fear within Amina. She often feels jumpy and easily startled, especially at night when she finds it particularly difficult to feel safe. The visual cues associated with violence-such as seeing blood or the physical proximity of someone who resembles her abuser-can quickly bring back fragmented memories of the assault. These memories are incomplete, leaving her feeling like parts of what happened remain unprocessed and missing. When these fragments do surface, they often come with intense feelings of guilt, leading Amina to push them away before she can fully recall the details. Amina's persistent fear is compounded by her belief that she has been permanently damaged for the worse. She worries that she will never be able to feel normal emotions again and that no one can truly be trusted. These negative appraisals have become a constant presence in her life, reinforcing her sense of vulnerability and mistrust. For example, when Amina sees blood while helping her granddaughter with an injury, it triggers a flashback that leaves her feeling overwhelmed and jumpy. The memory is incomplete and fragmented, causing her to feel a surge of guilt and pushing the image away before she can fully process what happened. This cycle perpetuates her sense of threat and mistrust. In her political role, Amina's triggers have made it increasingly difficult for her to participate in public events or meetings where angry expressions are common. She often finds herself looking behind her to check if someone is following her and feels particularly unsafe when the environment seems hostile. This constant vigilance has strained her relationships with colleagues and friends who cannot understand why she reacts so strongly to seemingly minor triggers. Amina's belief that people can't be trusted exacerbates her sense of threat, making it harder for her to feel safe in public settings or even within the comfort of her own home at night. Her fear of being physically harmed again is reinforced by these negative appraisals and fragmented memories. She often wakes up startled from nightmares where she relives moments of the assault, feeling like she can never truly escape the trauma. The combination of her triggers, threat responses, memory issues, and negative beliefs has created a self-reinforcing cycle that feels inescapable. Amina is now seeking help because she realizes that these symptoms are impacting her ability to function effectively both personally and professionally. She hopes to find ways to process her memories more fully and regain a sense of safety and trust. What frightens Amina most is the feeling that she will never be able to move past this trauma, that it will continue to define her life indefinitely. However, she is also hopeful that with support, she can begin to heal and reclaim aspects of herself that were lost during those painful moments.
