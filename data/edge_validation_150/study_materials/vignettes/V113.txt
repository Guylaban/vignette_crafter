Malia had deployed as a relief worker to a fractured province where the landscape shifted from quiet villages to sudden crossfire without warning. She remembered the triage tent with perfect, brutal clarity: the sharp scent of floral cologne layered over the copper tang of blood, a man grabbing her arm with a frantic, jerking motion as he demanded priority, and the way the canvas flapped violently in a wind that sounded like incoming mortars. Other stretches of those weeks remained entirely blank, as if her mind had simply cut the film. When she tried to recount the deployment to her husband, her thoughts tangled. She would start with the supply route, then lose the thread, staring at her hands until the words dissolved. She could not piece it into a story she could trust, let alone share. The gaps and the fragments simply existed side by side, neither yielding to the other, leaving her to navigate the present without a clear map of the past. Over the following months, the war zone did not stay in the past. It followed her into quiet rooms. She found herself scanning every crowd for the telltale tension of a hostile glance, her eyes constantly tracking shoulders and hands. Unfamiliar neighborhoods felt like uncharted terrain; without knowing sightlines or escape routes, her chest tightened, and she turned back. At home, her husband's spontaneous hug sent a jolt through her that left her breathless. Even the faint whiff of floral perfume on a stranger at the grocery store could freeze her mid-stride, her pulse hammering as if the triage tent had materialized in the aisle. She stopped attending the community church gatherings she had attended for years. The stained glass and pews felt like open doors to unpredictable threats. She kept her distance, citing work schedules instead of the quiet panic that rose in her throat. She also avoided clinics and counseling offices. The waiting rooms felt like places where danger might be formally documented. She kept driving past them, telling herself she was simply too busy to sit in a doctor's chair. Her job as a park ranger gave her routine, but it also became a ground where her habits took root. On patrol, she mapped every trailhead in her mind, refusing to lead groups into secondary valleys where visibility broke down. When a visitor rushed past her to take a photograph, she flinched so sharply her radio clattered to the dirt. The forest, which should have been a place of quiet, now felt like a space she could no longer assess. She missed her extended family gatherings, slipping away to her truck before the singing and embraces could begin. She told her husband she was exhausted, but what frightened her most was the quiet certainty that she no longer recognized the woman she used to be. She had made choices in that tent that sat like stones in her chest, choices that clashed with every principle she had clung to back home. She was certain she had broken in ways that could not be fixed, and that the distance between her and the life she remembered had become permanent. Some nights, sitting in the dark, she wondered if waking up at all served any purpose. What finally brought her to the clinic was not a single breaking point, but the slow accumulation of empty chairs at her family table, the way she counted exits before sitting down at dinner, and the hollow realization that her husband's hand resting on hers no longer felt like safety. She was hoping someone could help her untangle the threads without forcing her to speak the parts she could not hold. Most of all, she was afraid of being trapped inside her own alertness, of never again being allowed to simply be still.
