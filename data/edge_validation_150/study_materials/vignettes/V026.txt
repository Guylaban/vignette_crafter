He is 70 now, a widowed dentist who still goes into his office four days a week, wearing the same pressed white coat and carrying himself with the same careful steadiness his patients have trusted for decades. People know him as gentle, meticulous, a man with calm hands. What they do not see is how much of his day is organized around avoiding the feeling that something catastrophic is about to happen again.

The event took place almost twenty years ago, when he was in his early fifties. He had been working in a busy clinic in a large Latin American city, seeing patients back-to-back before a holiday weekend. Near midday he stepped out to buy coffee from a stand across the street. He remembers the heat, the smell of diesel, a woman laughing into her phone. Then the blast came. For a moment he does not understand what he is hearing; it seems too large to belong to ordinary life. The ground shudders, glass bursts outward, and he is thrown sideways against a parked car. When he looks up, the storefront next to the coffee stand is gone. There is smoke, screaming, and a strange silence inside his own head. Later he learns it was a terrorist bombing aimed at a government office nearby.

He goes back toward the clinic because that is the only thought he can form: my patients, my staff. He is bleeding from cuts on his face and forearm, but he keeps moving. Inside, the waiting room is chaos-dust, broken ceiling tiles, a child crying, one of his assistants on the floor holding her ankle. For hours he helps where he can, using whatever training and instinct he has, pressing towels against wounds, calling out names, trying to sound calm. That night at home, his wife cleans blood from behind his ear while he sits at the kitchen table unable to stop shaking. He tells her he is fine. He does not sleep.

In the weeks that follow, he returns to work quickly. He says this is what a man must do, what a professional must do. But the city no longer feels familiar. Every unattended bag, every motorcycle backfiring, every crowded waiting room seems charged with danger. He begins scanning windows and exits wherever he goes. On the bus he positions himself near the door. In restaurants he insists on sitting with his back to the wall. He stops buying coffee from street vendors. When his wife suggests they go downtown for dinner, he makes excuses.

At night he dreams in fragments: shattered glass in a patient's hair, smoke rolling under a doorway, the sound of screaming just before he wakes. Sometimes in the dream he is back outside and sees the woman with the phone turning toward him, alive one second and then gone. He wakes soaked in sweat, heart pounding so hard he presses a hand to his chest. His wife used to reach for him, but he often jerked away before he was fully awake. He started sleeping lightly, listening for noises in the street, and by morning he was exhausted and ashamed of how frightened he felt.

The years pass, but the fear settles into him rather than fading. In the dental office he remains competent, even admired, yet certain parts of the job become quietly difficult. The high whine of the drill can blend in his mind with the ringing in his ears after the explosion. If a tray crashes to the floor, his whole body jolts before he can stop it. He laughs it off with patients, blaming old age, but afterward he feels embarrassed and irritable. He keeps the radio on for distraction, though too much news about bombings or political violence makes him abruptly turn it off. His staff knows he dislikes surprises; he insists they announce visitors before opening his office door.

When his wife develops cancer and later dies, his world narrows further. Grief strips away the person who had helped him stay tethered to ordinary routines. Alone in the apartment, he notices how often he checks the locks, how hard it is to settle at night. He leaves the television on for company but avoids channels showing international news. His children, now adults, tell him he seems distant. He wants to explain that it is not distance exactly, but vigilance, as if some part of him never left that sidewalk.

He still works because the structure steadies him. In the operatory, when he leans over a patient and asks them to open wider, his hands remain precise. Yet he is always monitoring the corridor, the street below, the possibility of sirens. Some mornings he sits in his parked car outside the clinic for several minutes before going in, watching pedestrians and delivery trucks, waiting for the feeling in his stomach to loosen. He tells himself he has survived worse. Even so, when he hears a sudden bang from construction next door, his mouth goes dry and he is back in the smoke, already bracing for impact before he can remind himself where he is.
