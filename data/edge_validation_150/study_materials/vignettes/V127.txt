Javier, a 54-year-old parole officer, had been a pillar of his community, his career defined by a steadfast belief in justice and the rehabilitation of others. That conviction was shattered two years ago during an off-duty incident at a local park. He and his wife had witnessed several officers aggressively detaining a young man for a minor infraction. As Javier, in his professional capacity, attempted to de-escalate the situation, identifying himself, he was shoved, verbally threatened, and had his badge number aggressively taken by one of the officers, who then accused him of obstructing justice. The experience was a profound shock, a betrayal by the very institution he had dedicated his life to. Despite his formal complaint, the incident was quickly dismissed internally, leaving Javier with a gnawing sense that what happened to him was deeply unfair and nothing had been done about it. He had come to believe that the people and institutions that should have protected him had, in fact, let him down completely.

Recalling the event often felt like trying to grasp smoke. He found himself pushing the memory away before he could fully recall it, particularly the moments when the officer's face was inches from his, spitting threats. When he did try to piece it together, the story felt unreal, so extreme that he struggled to believe it had actually happened to him. He often described feeling like he was only remembering parts of what happened, with the rest missing, leaving a fragmented and unsettling picture.

Since then, the world had become a place of constant vigilance for Javier. His work as a parole officer, once a source of purpose, now felt like a minefield of potential triggers. Any instance of people misbehaving, whether a parolee acting out during a check-in or a citizen arguing loudly in public, would instantly make his heart begin racing or pounding, a familiar and terrifying physical sensation that he now associated with impending danger. The inherent stress of his job had also become a potent trigger; any increase in pressure or workload would send him spiraling into anxiety.

His senses were constantly on high alert. Even in the quiet of his office, an unusual silence would make him feel something dangerous was about to happen, his mind immediately conjuring worst-case scenarios. He had become profoundly jumpy or easily startled, flinching violently at the unexpected ring of a phone or the sudden closing of a door. This hyper-vigilance extended to his environment; he constantly scanned his surroundings for signs of danger, his eyes darting from face to face in a crowd, meticulously checking every corner of a room during a parole visit.

The changes had taken a toll on Javier's life outside of work too. His wife had noticed his increasing distance and preoccupation. He used to enjoy their evening conversations, but now he often retreated into himself, finding the normal flow of family life overwhelming. At work, his colleagues sometimes commented on his withdrawn demeanor, mistaking his relentless scanning for aloofness. He found himself avoiding certain routes to parolees' homes, especially those that passed by police stations or offered too much open visibility, adding significant time and stress to his daily commute. He felt increasingly isolated, unable to reconcile his professional identity with the profound sense of betrayal he carried.

The recent anniversary of the incident, coupled with a particularly challenging case involving complex police interactions, had amplified his symptoms to an unbearable degree. Javier felt utterly exhausted by the constant vigilance, the perpetual feeling of being on the edge of catastrophe. He finally sought help, not just for the pervasive anxiety, but because what frightened him most was the thought of living the rest of his life feeling constantly on guard, unable to trust the very system he had once believed in, and increasingly estranged from the people and work he once cherished.
