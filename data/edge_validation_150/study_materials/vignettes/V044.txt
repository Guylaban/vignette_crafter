Elise Vance, an American of German descent, stood in her kitchen, the dispatch radio still humming through the morning static, and watched the rain streak the window. Eighteen months had passed since the intersection, since she had reached into the crumpled driver's side to stabilize the steering column, and since a secondary bracket she hadn't anticipated had sheared away. She had moved too fast, misjudging the weight of the twisted steel, and the frame had dropped hard across the man's leg. The memory did not play out as a sequence of events. It remained as a few distinct moments, each a rigid picture in her mind: the yellow work light catching the fluid on his jeans, the exact angle of the crushed metal, the sudden absence of his breathing when she finally guided him onto the stretcher. The moment had stayed, and everything after it had fractured. Over the following weeks, fragments of the incident would surface without warning-a specific crack of metal from a construction site nearby, a patient's sharp gasp during a tourniquet application-and then vanish as quickly as they came. When she tried to sleep, the terror she had felt in that exact second returned, identical in its intensity to the night of the crash. She found it harder to feel safe once the sun went down. Night felt thinner, more exposed. She would lie awake, tracking the settling of the house, convinced that any sudden noise would signal another emergency she could not prevent. During her shifts, her mind was locked in a constant, exhausting scan. She tracked every doorframe, every patient's hands, every shifting weight on the rig. She was always on guard, bracing for the next wrong turn, the next collapsing frame. Alongside this hypervigilance, she struggled to follow written protocols. She began losing her place in medication charts, misreading dosage cards, and forgetting radio transmission steps mid-sentence. Elise stopped talking about the accident. When her husband, David, asked about her shift, she would change the subject, focusing on the grocery list or the weather. When questions lingered, she turned to the television, the radio, or the relentless scrolling on her phone, filling the quiet with anything but the event. She also began relying on a glass of wine each evening and the prescribed sleep aid her primary doctor had given her, waiting for the chemical fog to finally dull the edges. The silence between them grew. She and David stopped sharing their evenings together; he began sleeping in the guest room, not out of anger, but because he noticed how tightly she wound herself each time he touched her shoulder, how she flinched at the sound of a closing car door downstairs. The unspoken distance deepened, leaving her alone with the weight of the rig and the bay. Now, at thirty-three, Elise sits on the edge of her bed, keys already packed in her bag, knowing she cannot drive herself to work tomorrow. The concentration lapses have nearly cost her a patient during a recent transport, and the nights have become so long she can no longer tell where fear ends and fatigue begins. She finally called the clinic this morning, not because she expects a clean slate, but because she is terrified of the moment she might misplace a dose card, or a sudden fragment will surface during a critical maneuver, or she will simply stop feeling safe in her own body. What she hopes for, more than anything, is to remember the man on the stretcher without the terror that follows, to sleep through the night without counting every floorboard creak, and to finally lay down the weight of a mistake that never stopped demanding her attention.
