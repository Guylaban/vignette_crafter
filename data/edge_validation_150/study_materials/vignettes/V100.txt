Daniel Mercer had been proud of the work he did as a prosecutor, the kind of North American man who was used to reading facts carefully, building a case, staying composed under pressure. That confidence changed the night a bombing tore through the downtown civic plaza where he had been walking to meet a colleague after court. He had heard the bang first, a violent crack that seemed to split the evening open, and then saw people scattering, glass raining down, sirens rising somewhere beyond the smoke. In the aftermath, amid the chaos and the smell of spilled alcohol from overturned cups and shattered bar fixtures near the plaza, Daniel kept thinking he should have done something more useful, more decisive, more protective. He had stood frozen long enough to shame himself for it, and afterward the thought returned with a blunt certainty: he had failed to protect people who were right there in front of him, and he would never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened.

Since then, Daniel's memories of that night had not settled into a story he could tell cleanly, even to himself. He would start to describe the scene and lose the thread, because what came back was not orderly recollection but fragments that felt more real and vivid than ordinary life: the flash, the smoke, someone screaming nearby, his own body jolting toward cover. The memory did not sit in the past for him; it could feel as though it was happening again in the same instant, with the same sharpness and urgency. That quality of the memory fed his belief that he had been inadequate in a moment that demanded action. If it could still come back with that force, then it seemed to him it must mean something about him-that he had been too passive, too trusting, too slow-and that thought settled into a broader suspicion that people were not what they seemed.

Over the following months, the very situations that brought the event back also brought that self-condemnation with it. When Daniel came home from long hearings exhausted, or when he caught a whiff of alcohol on someone's breath at a reception, or when a car backfired on the street with an explosion-like bang, his body would tense before he could reason with himself. In those moments, the old conclusion returned immediately: he should have protected others and failed, and there was something dangerously deceptive about the world around him. The smell, the noise, even the drained heaviness of fatigue seemed to confirm that he was still vulnerable and that danger could appear anywhere, disguised as ordinary life. What frightened him most was not only the sudden surge of fear, but the way it seemed to prove his worst judgments true.

The more Daniel's memory returned in that vivid, uncontained way, the more it sharpened those appraisals. He would be standing in his kitchen with his wife, trying to answer a simple question, and a fragment of the bombing would rush in so completely that the present felt secondary. In those moments he would think again that he had not acted enough, and then another thought would follow almost immediately: if he could not trust his own memory to give him a clear account of what happened, then maybe he could not trust his judgment about anyone else either. That uncertainty made him watch people more closely, searching for signs of deceit in faces, voices, and intentions. He noticed himself scanning for exits in restaurants, starting at sudden noises, and feeling most on edge when he was tired enough that his mind seemed less able to hold itself together.

In turn, those vivid recollections kept making the triggers more potent. An alcohol smell in a crowded hallway could pull him back into the plaza with painful force; a slammed door at work could make his muscles lock before he had even identified the sound; a run of late nights in the prosecutor's office could leave him so depleted that the memory arrived more easily and with less warning. His job had become a difficult place to endure, because the courthouse corridors, conference rooms, and evening events often involved the exact cues that set off the memory and the guilt that followed it. He began shortening his days, avoiding post-work gatherings, and keeping his distance from situations where he might smell liquor or hear a sudden bang. That shrinking of his world made him feel even more like someone who had failed in a public way and was now retreating from the demands of life.

By the time he sought help, Daniel felt trapped between the vividness of what happened and the harsh conclusions he had drawn from it. He could not make the memory feel past, and because it kept returning with such force, he found himself more alert for danger and more certain that other people could not be taken at face value. He came to therapy at his wife's urging after she noticed how often he came home drained, on edge, and withdrawn from the routines they once shared. What finally brought him in was the fear that he was becoming defined by the night of the bombing: by the sounds that jolted him, the smells that unsettled him, the exhausted states that made everything worse, and the conviction that he had failed when it mattered most. He was hoping for a way to stop reliving it; what he feared most was that, without help, he would never stop believing he should have done more.
